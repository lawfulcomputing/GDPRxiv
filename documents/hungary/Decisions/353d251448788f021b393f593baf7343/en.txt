D E C I S I O N 
 
 
The National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Authority) before […] 
(address: […]; hereinafter: Applicant) at the request of the Applicant against […] (headquarters: […]; hereinafter: Respondent 1) regarding the unlawful processing of the Applicant's personal data, as well as the improper fulfillment of the access request, and against […] (headquarters: […]; hereinafter: Respondent 2) (Respondent 1 and Respondent 2 hereinafter: Respondents) regarding the unlawful transfer of the Applicant's personal data, in the initiated data protection authority procedure, the Authority makes the following decisions: 
 
I. 
The Authority, partially granting the Applicant's request, establishes that Respondent 1 has violated Article 6(1) and Article 15(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council (hereinafter: GDPR) concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, as well as the repeal of Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation). 
 
II. The Authority, partially granting the Applicant's request regarding the violations established in point I, finds Respondent 1 liable. 
 
III. The Authority rejects the Applicant's request to establish that Respondent 2 unlawfully transferred the inheritance transfer order containing the Applicant's personal data to Respondent 1. 
 
IV. The Authority, partially granting the Applicant's request, establishes that Respondent 2 has violated Article 15(1) of the GDPR. 
 
V. The Authority, partially granting the Applicant's request regarding the violation established in point IV, finds Respondent 2 liable. 
 
VI. The Authority grants the Applicant's request to order Respondent 1 to delete the personal data at Respondent 1, and instructs Respondent 1 to delete the personal data of the Applicant processed for the purpose of enforcing the Applicant's claim from the claims management register, and to confirm the deletion to the Authority within 30 days from the date of the decision becoming final. 
 
The Authority warns Respondent 1 that, pursuant to Section 61(6) of the Infotv., the data subject to the disputed data processing cannot be deleted or destroyed until the expiration of the deadline for initiating legal action against the decision or until the final decision of the court in the case of initiating administrative litigation. 
There is no administrative remedy against this decision, but it can be challenged in administrative litigation with a lawsuit addressed to the Metropolitan Court within 30 days from the communication. The lawsuit must be submitted electronically to the Authority, which will forward it along with the case documents to the court. A request for a hearing must be indicated in the lawsuit. IV. Decision  

IV.1. Lawfulness of the data processing of the Applicant 2, the legal basis for data processing  

(31) The Authority considers it necessary to first examine whether the data transfer by Applicant 2 to Applicant 1 was lawful due to the chronological order of the data processing contested by the Applicant.  

(32) Applicant 2 exercises public authority during inheritance proceedings, thus, in accordance with Article 6 (1) of the GDPR, it cannot base its data processing on its own or a third party's legitimate interest during the exercise of public authority; the legal basis for the data processing necessary for the performance of these duties may be Article 6 (1) (c) or (e) of the GDPR.  

(33) According to Article 6 (1) (c) of the GDPR, data processing is lawful if it is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject. According to Section 2 (1) of the Hetv., inheritance proceedings are subject to the provisions of the Hetv. unless otherwise provided by the Hetv.
10

provides that the rules of the Civil Procedure Act (Pp.) must be applied with deviations arising from the specific characteristics of non-litigious civil proceedings. Considering that the Act on Inheritance (Hetv.) does not contain specific rules regarding the access to documents, the rules of the Pp. shall apply. According to Section 162 (5) of the Pp., information about the proceedings can be provided to those who have a legal interest in the conduct of the proceedings or its outcome. The presiding judge of the court, or in inheritance proceedings, the notary public – after verifying the legal interest associated with this – shall permit the inspection of documents, the preparation of copies or extracts thereof, and the provision of necessary information.

(34) This means that if a third party, typically a creditor, proves that they have a claim against the deceased, then based on Section 162 (5) of the Pp., the acting notary must provide access to the inheritance distribution order, as the creditor can enforce their claim against the heirs in this manner. The Authority finds it necessary to note here that the mere fact that a creditor did not register as an inheritance creditor during the inheritance proceedings, for example, because they were unaware that the debtor had died, does not result in the loss of rights, meaning that the claim can still be enforced in civil litigation or non-litigious proceedings, provided that in this case, they cannot contest the satisfactions made to other inheritance creditors that occurred before their registration, in terms of maintaining the order and the proportion of satisfaction for those belonging to the group.

(35) The Authority holds the view that Applicant 1 has adequately demonstrated to Applicant 2 that they had a claim against the late [Applicant's grandmother], meaning that they had a legal interest in the outcome of the proceedings, as they can enforce their claim against the deceased's heir. The fate of the inheritance can be understood from the inheritance distribution order as a whole, namely who received what share of the inheritance, who renounced the inheritance or a part thereof, and to whom the inheritance was distributed. With this information, the creditor can decide whether they wish to enforce their claim through civil litigation or by means of a payment order.

(36) To enforce the claim legally, it is necessary to know the debtor's identification data and address [Pp. Section 170 (1) b); Fmhtv. Section 20 (1) a)]. Notaries are not authorized to decide on behalf of creditors against whom they can enforce their claims, meaning that the inheritance distribution order must be issued in such a way that it does not negatively affect the enforcement of the claim, i.e., it must include the identification data and addresses of potential obligors, however, for example, the masking of the tax identification number is expected based on Article 5(1)(c) of the GDPR, considering that it is not significant for the enforcement of the claim.

(37) Based on all of the above, the Authority establishes that Applicant 2 lawfully provided the inheritance distribution order to Applicant 1, based on the legal obligation under Article 6(1)(c) of the GDPR – which was established for them by Section 162 (5) of the Pp. – together with the personal data of the Applicant (name; mother’s name; date of birth; address).

VI.2. Legality of Applicant 1's data processing

(38) The Authority holds the view that the legality of certain data processing operations carried out by Applicant 1 should be examined individually. On one hand, Applicant 1 became acquainted with the personal data contained in the inheritance distribution order and stores them in a copy received from the inheritance distribution order. On the other hand, Applicant 1 used the personal data of the Applicant contained in the inheritance distribution order for the purpose of enforcing the claim, recorded them in their claim management system, and sent a payment reminder to the Applicant.

(39) The Authority holds the view that in the letter written by Applicant 1 to Applicant 2, as well as in the attached documents titled "Notification of Assignment" and "Declaration of Succession,"
11

sufficiently substantiated the existence of the claim, the interest associated with its enforcement, that is, considering Section 162 (5) of the Civil Procedure Code (Pp.), as well as the explanations provided in the previous subsection, Respondent 1 could lawfully access the personal data of the Applicant as stated in the inheritance transfer order based on its legitimate interest.

(40)  
According to the inheritance transfer order, the late [grandmother's] estate was fully transferred to the Applicant by the acting notary public; however, the basis for the transfer was not inheritance but a gift based on an agreement, the prerequisite of which was the notary's determination of the intermediate acquisition by the Applicant's mother, […].

(41)  
Respondent 1, considering the commentary related to Section 94 of the Inheritance Act (Hetv.) – in the inheritance transfer order, the notary referred to Section 94 of the Hetv. as the basis for his decision – concluded that in the case of a division agreement, the basis for the transfer is always inheritance. This led to the conclusion that based on the legal provision indicated in the inheritance transfer order, the basis for the transfer of the estate to the Applicant could only be inheritance, a presumption further supported by the fact that the estate was fully transferred to the Applicant. In his view, a full transfer of the estate can only occur to the heir, thus he considered the Applicant to be the heir of the late [grandmother]. Based on all this, he concluded that based on legitimate interest, he could lawfully process the Applicant's data as an heir for claim management purposes, and therefore sent a payment request to the Applicant.

(42)  
Respondent 1 processed the Applicant's personal data based on Article 6(1)(f) of the GDPR, referring to the legitimate interest associated with the enforcement of the claim. He stated that his legitimate business interest was to collect the purchased claim from the debtors and, if necessary, to enforce his claim through legal means. For this reason, since the collection of the claim had not been completed, and since in his view the Applicant should be considered the heir of the late [grandmother], and thus the debtor of the claim, he did not comply with the Applicant's request for the deletion of personal data. In this context, he also referred to Article 17(3)(b) of the GDPR.

(43)  
The Authority first deems it necessary to state that the inheritance transfer order, as a document issued by a notary public, qualifies as a public document under Section 323(1) of the Pp. According to Section 323(3) of the Pp, a public document proves the reality of the data and facts certified by it with full evidentiary force. Furthermore, according to Section 2(2) of the Hetv., the notary's procedure in the inheritance proceedings has the same effect as that of a first-instance court, meaning that the inheritance transfer order has the same effect as a judgment of a first-instance court, and the contents thereof are considered res judicata, which can only be contested in the context of legal remedies, provided that the laws allow for such.

(44)  
Based on this, the Applicant's mother can be considered the heir of the late [grandmother], not the Applicant, since the agricultural lands constituting the objects of the estate were transferred to her on the basis of a gift resulting from an agreement. This fact is not altered by the possibility of interpreting the referenced legal provisions in such a way that the Applicant could be considered an heir.

(45)  
Therefore, considering that the Applicant is not regarded as the heir of the late [grandmother] according to the inheritance transfer order, Respondent 1 could not lawfully process the personal data of the Applicant for claim management purposes, as he is not the successor of the original debtor, and no performance can be expected from him. Consequently, it can be established that Respondent 1 has no legitimate interest in processing the Applicant's personal data for the purpose of claim enforcement; therefore, the Authority finds that Respondent 1 unlawfully processed the Applicant's personal data for this purpose, in violation of Article 6(1) of the GDPR.
12

(46) The Respondent 1 denied the Applicant's request for deletion by referring to Article 17(3)(b) of the GDPR; however, it argued that in the absence of personal data, it would not be able to legally enforce its claim against the Applicant, and thus it processes the personal data necessary for the enforcement of the claim based on a legal obligation. According to the Authority, Respondent 1 should have referred to Article 17(3)(e) of the GDPR at this point, as it may have needed the data for the submission, enforcement, or defense of its legal claims. However, as the Authority established above, Respondent 1 unlawfully processed the Applicant's personal data for the purpose of enforcing its claim, as it cannot be considered an heir based on the inheritance transfer order, and thus should have deleted the Applicant's personal data from the claims management register at the request of the Applicant based on Article 17(1)(d) of the GDPR.

(47) Nevertheless, according to Respondent 1's reference, the inheritance transfer order is also considered an accounting document under Section 166(1) of the Accounting Act, given that it supports the accounting of an economic event (its record) and records the change in the obligated person's status. According to the Authority, data that cannot be deleted from documents qualifying as accounting documents under Section 166(1) of the Accounting Act, as per Section 169(6) of the Accounting Act, means that all personal data contained in these documents can be retained. The mandatory retention period lasts until the end of the 8th year from the closure of the case – which, according to Respondent 1, occurred in 2022. It should be emphasized, however, that while Respondent 1 is obliged to retain and store the inheritance transfer order and the personal data contained therein, it cannot perform any further operations on the personal data. Based on all of this, the Authority concludes that Respondent 1 lawfully retains and stores the Applicant's personal data in the inheritance transfer order in accordance with Article 6(1)(c) of the GDPR.

IV.3. Fulfillment of the Applicant's access request by Respondent 1

(48) In the access request dated April 14, 2022, the Applicant requested information from Respondent 1 regarding the personal data processed by Respondent 1, their source, the purpose of data processing, the legal basis, duration, the name and address of the data processor, and all activities related to data processing, as well as the legal basis and recipient in the case of data transfer. The Applicant specifically requested information regarding the content of the inheritance transfer order, including the source of the personal data contained therein and the legal basis for the processing of the data.

(49) Respondent 1 responded to the request, referencing the Information Act – based on which Respondent 1 should have recognized it as an access request – on April 22, 2022, adhering to the one-month deadline stipulated in Article 12(3) of the GDPR. In its response, it did not address all the data processing circumstances the Applicant wished to know, as it only provided information about the source of the Applicant's personal data, as well as stating that the Applicant is the obligated party for its claim – which can be considered the purpose of data processing – and that the processing of personal data does not require the Applicant's consent.

(50) According to Article 15(1) of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to receive information about the scope of their personal data being processed and the circumstances of the data processing if their personal data is being processed. Respondent 1 did not inform the Applicant about which specific personal data it processes, did not specify the legal basis for its data processing, nor the duration; it only referred to the purpose of data processing, and did not provide any information regarding the potential involvement of a data processor, nor about any data transfer and its recipient. Based on this, the Authority concludes that Respondent 1 violated Article 15(1) of the GDPR by not providing the Applicant with the appropriate information regarding the processing of their personal data.
13

IV.4. Fulfillment of the Access Request by Respondent 2

(51)  
The Applicant submitted an access request to Respondent 2, stating that the inheritance transfer decree contained personal data, and therefore requested that Respondent 2 indicate the legal basis on which the inheritance transfer decree was issued and the personal data contained therein was sent to a third party. Furthermore, the Applicant requested that “if you were contacted regarding the debts of the late [grandmother], please also send me the balancing of interests concerning me, according to which my personal data was issued as an interested party not listed as an heir in the decree.”

(52)  
In its decree dated May 19, 2022, Respondent 2 informed the Applicant about the background of the data transfer and that the inheritance transfer decree was sent to Respondent 1 based on the likelihood of the creditor's legal interest, as it was considered that Respondent 1 could be regarded as an interested party in the inheritance proceedings. It further stated that according to Article 6(1) of the GDPR, the processing of personal data is lawful if it is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject or if the processing is necessary for the purposes of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or a third party. In this context, it referred to Section 90 of the Inheritance Act, which states that the inheritance transfer decree must be sent to the interested party in the inheritance proceedings.

(53)  
The Authority holds the view that the response given by Respondent 2 to the access request was not entirely clear, as it referred to both point (c) and point (f) of Article 6(1) of the GDPR, whereas, as a public authority, it cannot base its data processing necessary for the performance of its public tasks on point (f) of Article 6(1) according to the provisions of the GDPR. The mention of this legal basis hindered the Applicant's ability to understand precisely on what legal basis their personal data was transferred to Respondent 1.

(54)  
Moreover, it is also unclear whether the legal provision referred to by Respondent 2 as a legal obligation, namely Section 90 of the Inheritance Act, can be invoked in the specific case, as – as the Applicant also disputed – it is not clear whether Respondent 1 can be considered an inheritance creditor, since during the ongoing inheritance proceedings, they did not declare their creditor claim – they acquired the claim itself only after the conclusion of the inheritance proceedings – thus, they do not meet the definition according to point (e) of Section 6(1) of the Inheritance Act. This, of course, does not affect the existence or legitimacy of the claim.

(55)  
Furthermore, in response to the Authority's request, Respondent 2 has already stated that the legal provision prescribing the data transfer is Section 162(5) of the Civil Procedure Act, which the Authority has accepted as such.

(56)  
Based on all of the above, the Authority finds that Respondent 2 violated the Applicant's right of access under Article 15(1) of the GDPR by not providing clear information to the Applicant regarding the legal basis on which their personal data in the inheritance transfer decree was transferred.

IV.6. Interpretation of the Applicant's Request

(58) The Applicant requested the determination of their right of access ("failure of the Respondents to fulfill their obligation to respond") on the grounds that the Respondents' response did not contain a balancing of interests. In the Authority's view, the existence and adequacy of the balancing of interests should be assessed within the framework of the lawfulness of data processing; therefore, the Authority did not evaluate it in the context of ensuring the right of access. Furthermore, considering that in the case of Respondent 2, the legal basis for data processing was Article 6(1)(c) of the GDPR, there is no need for a balancing test supporting the lawfulness of data processing, while it was still possible to decide on the lawfulness of the data processing of Respondent 1 even in the absence of such a test.

IV.7. Legal Consequences

(59) The Authority partially upholds the Applicant's request and finds that Respondent 1 unlawfully processed the Applicant's personal data for the purpose of enforcing a claim, thereby violating Article 6(1) of the GDPR.

(60) The Authority partially upholds the Applicant's request and finds that Respondent 1 violated Article 15(1) of the GDPR by not fully responding to the access request. Due to these violations, the Authority holds Respondent 1 accountable under Article 58(2)(b) of the GDPR.

(61) The Authority partially upholds the Applicant's request and finds that Respondent 2 violated Article 15(1) of the GDPR by not providing clear information to the Applicant regarding the legal basis for the transfer of their personal data. The Authority holds Respondent 2 accountable for this violation under Article 58(2)(b) of the GDPR.

(62) The Authority, partially upholding the Applicant's request, instructs Respondent 1 under Article 58(2)(g) of the GDPR to delete the personal data processed in their claims management registry for the purpose of enforcing the Applicant's claim and to credibly prove to the Authority that this has been done.  
(63) According to Section 75/A of the Information Act, the Authority exercises its powers as set out in Article 83(2)-(6) of the General Data Protection Regulation, taking into account the principle of proportionality, particularly in that, in the case of a first-time violation of the provisions regarding the processing of personal data – as defined in legislation or in a binding legal act of the European Union – the Authority primarily acts by warning the data controller or processor regarding the remedy of the violation, in accordance with Article 58 of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
(64) The Authority does not find it necessary to impose a data protection fine against either Respondent 1 or Respondent 2. Both Respondents have violated the provisions of the GDPR for the first time. The Authority also does not find it necessary to apply a fine against Respondent 1, as according to Respondent 1's statement addressed to the Authority dated August 23, 2022, they refrained from further enforcement of the claim against the Applicant and removed the case from their registry, thus taking steps to remedy the consequences of the unlawful data processing.
