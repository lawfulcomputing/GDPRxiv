D E C I S I O N  

The National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Authority) issues the following decisions in an official data protection authority procedure initiated ex officio against the Foundation Without Tears for Sick Children (headquarters: 1193 Budapest, Vécsey utca 33., hereinafter: Obligor or Foundation) due to the presumed violation of the provisions of the General Data Protection Regulation:  

1.  
The Authority establishes that the Obligor violated Article 12(1) of the GDPR during the period specified in point III.4 of the reasoning, as well as Articles 13(1)-(2) by failing to provide adequate general information to the data subjects regarding the processing of their personal data during phone calls.  

2.  
The Authority establishes that the Obligor violated Article 12(2) of the GDPR during the period specified in point III.4 of the reasoning, by not facilitating the exercise of the data subjects' rights during the phone calls.  

3.  
The Authority establishes that the Obligor violated the principle of transparency under Article 5(1)(a) of the GDPR regarding the data processing conducted during the period specified in point III.4 of the reasoning, by not providing adequate, actual information to the data subjects regarding the legal basis for the data processing, or by providing misleading information to the data subjects about the legal basis, and that the data subjects did not receive information regarding the data processing either during the phone calls or on the Obligor's website.  

4.  
The Authority establishes that the Obligor violated Article 6(1) of the GDPR concerning the data processing specified in point III.5 of the reasoning, as it processes the personal data of the data subjects without a valid legal basis.  

5.  
The Authority establishes that the Obligor violated Article 12(1) and Articles 13(1)-(2) of the GDPR concerning the data processing specified in point III.5 of the reasoning, by not providing information to the data subjects regarding the processing of their personal data during the phone calls.  

6.  
The Authority, based on Article 58(2)(d) of the GDPR, instructs the Obligor to modify its practice related to the solicitation of donations through phone calls in accordance with the explanations provided, so that it complies with the provisions of the GDPR, meaning that it should only continue the data processing if the appropriate purpose, legal basis, necessity of balancing interests, and other conditions are fulfilled for lawful continuation; in other cases, the database should be deleted, and the Authority should be notified immediately of the measures taken. According to Section 61(6) of Act CXII of 2011 on the Right to Informational Self-Determination and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Infotv.), the data affected by the disputed data processing cannot be deleted or rendered invalid until the expiration of the deadline for initiating legal action against the decision or until the court's final decision in case of initiating administrative litigation.

**REASONING**  
I. Procedure and clarification of the facts  

1. Background cases  
(1)  
The Authority received several reports in which the reporters complained that they received unsolicited calls from the Obligor on their secret phone numbers multiple times, urging them to donate, and despite requesting the Obligor to delete their personal data during the calls and in writing, the phone calls continued despite verbal confirmation of deletion and, in some cases, email confirmation.  
(2)  
Based on the reports, several investigations were initiated against the Obligor, and the Authority also initiated an investigation ex officio under case number NAIH/2020/4620.  
(3)  
Based on the facts uncovered in these background cases, it was established that the legality of the processing of personal data stored in the Obligor's database and the data processing practices was not sufficiently clarified, thus the legal basis for data processing, its purpose, the adequacy of informing the data subjects, and the assurance of the exercise of rights by the data subjects were not adequately ensured.

III.  
Decision  
1. Subject of the current data protection authority procedure  
(25) Several complaints were received regarding the Obliged's data processing in 2019 and 2020, in which the complainants objected that they received unsolicited phone calls urging donations from the Obliged multiple times, despite having requested the deletion of their personal data during the call and in writing (via email), which the Obliged confirmed verbally and in writing. Based on the established facts, in all cases, the complainants had previously supported the activities of the Foundation with their donations, meaning they were listed in the Foundation's database as previous donors.  
(26) Based on the information uncovered in the preliminary investigation cases, the Authority established that the legality of the processing of personal data stored in the Obliged's database was not sufficiently clarified, and the legality of its data processing practices (the legal basis for data processing, the purpose, the adequacy of informing the data subjects, and the adequacy of ensuring the exercise of rights by the data subjects) is also questionable. Therefore, the Authority initiated an ex officio data protection authority procedure to examine the legality of the Obliged's data processing practices related to telephone solicitations for donations.  
(27) Considering the subject of the data protection authority procedure specified in the previous point, and also considering that according to the Obliged's statement, as of October 3, 2021, it no longer uses call center services for the purpose of collecting new donors, the Authority's current procedure did not extend to examining the legality of the data processing of the data stored in the call center's own separate database that was previously commissioned.  
(28) According to the records in the Authority's decision NAIH-4572-1/2023 regarding the initiation of the data protection authority procedure, the data processing period examined in this data protection authority procedure extends from the applicability of the GDPR to the date of initiation of the current data protection authority procedure (March 31, 2023).  

2. Qualification of those involved in data processing  
(29) According to the definition in Article 4(7) of the GDPR, a data controller is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or any other body that determines the purposes and means of processing personal data, either alone or jointly with others, while according to point 8, a data processor is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or any other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller.  
(30) According to the 7/2020 EDPB guidelines regarding the definitions of data controller and data processor (hereinafter: 7/2020 guidelines), the data controller
16  
Among the conceptual elements described in the previous section, the term "determines" refers to the influence of the data controller on the data processing through the exercise of decision-making authority. The data controller is the entity that determines the key elements of data processing. To establish this, it is necessary to examine the specific data processing operations and to understand who determines them, that is, to investigate why the specific data processing occurred, and who decided that the data processing should be carried out for a specific purpose.  
(31) A crucial building block of the definition of the data controller is the controller's influence on the purposes and means of data processing. Determining the purposes and means involves deciding why and how the data processing takes place. In the case of a specific data processing operation, the data controller is the actor who determines why the data processing should occur—namely, for what purpose, or why—and how this purpose can be achieved, that is, what means should be employed to achieve the purpose. According to Article 4(7) of the GDPR, a legal entity that influences the processing of personal data participates in determining the purposes and means of the processing. The data controller must decide both on the purpose of the data processing and on its means. The data controller cannot agree solely on the determination of the purpose; it must also make decisions regarding the means of data processing. In contrast, a party acting as a data processor can never determine the purpose of data processing.  
(32) The Obligor stated during the examination of the preliminary investigation cases that the Obligor qualifies as a data controller, while the call center and the courier service qualify as data processors.  
(33) According to the established facts, the Obligor exclusively determined during the foundation's telephone campaigns which data processing operations it wished to carry out using which personal data, which phone numbers would be called on its behalf, and the method of outreach was also selected by the Obligor, as it commissioned a call center, […] to conduct telephone outreach during the period up to October 3, 2021, while in the subsequent period, it exclusively continued the telephone outreach through its own volunteers.  
(34) Furthermore, the Obligor uses the services of [Company3] for the delivery of donations to the Obligor and simultaneously for the delivery of thank-you letters to the donors. In this process, the Obligor provides the data to the courier service by uploading the data into the courier service's program, thus making it available to the courier service.  
(35) Accordingly, based on Article 4(7) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the Obligor is considered a data controller in relation to the fundraising through telephone outreach, which was carried out with the services of the call center as a data processor and the courier service as a data processor.  

3. The Obligor's data processing practice related to fundraising telephone calls  
(36) During the examination of the data processing practice of the Obligor in relation to fundraising telephone calls within the investigated data processing period, the Authority separately evaluated the period when the Obligor also conducted telephone outreach by commissioning a call center, that is, the period up to October 3, 2021, and the subsequent period when the Obligor conducted telephone outreach exclusively through its own volunteers.

4. Period of phone calls made using a call center  
4.1. The data processing practice of the Obligor during the period up to October 3, 2021  
(37) The Obligor engaged in fundraising activities by contacting both individuals and companies, organizations via phone calls.  
(38) According to the Obligor's statement, initially, the phone numbers used for the calls were collected from publicly available data on the internet, such as online directories and data of companies found online. The Obligor's volunteers worked exclusively from the database created in this manner, contacting by phone those individuals who had previously made donations to the Obligor.  
(39) The Obligor periodically contacted, with the help of its volunteers, by phone those who had previously donated and were stored in its records, to request their support again.  
(40) According to the Obligor's statement, until January 2019, it exclusively conducted phone outreach through its volunteers based on its own database, which recorded those who had previously made donations.  
(41) From January 2019, in addition to the outreach by volunteers, the Obligor also commissioned a call center to carry out its phone campaigns. The call center used both the Obligor's database for its phone outreach and its own database.

(43) The call center forwarded the data of those individuals who expressed their intention to donate to the Obligor. Subsequently, the Obligor's volunteers contacted these individuals, who had previously made donations, by phone 1-2 months later based on the data received from the call center, effectively integrating these contacts into the existing database.

In light of this, the Obligor terminated its cooperation with the call center on October 3, 2021, and from that date onwards, it only maintains contact with those who had previously made donations through its volunteers.  
(45) The Obligor indicated its legitimate interest as the legal basis for its data processing under Article 6(1)(f) of the GDPR, while the purpose of the data processing, according to its statement, is to maintain contact with supporters, inform them about the Obligor's activities, and conduct fundraising.  
(46) The Obligor's volunteers called previous supporters to thank them for their past support, informed them about the Obligor's current campaigns, and requested further support.  
(47) According to the Obligor's statement, the information of the individuals was provided during the call by the call center, whereby the call center staff introduced the Obligor's activities to the individuals wishing to donate, provided the Obligor's contact information, and requested the necessary personal data from those intending to donate: name, address, phone number, which personal data was sent to the Obligor in a tabulated format.

4.2. Legal Basis for Data Processing  
(49) According to the definition in the GDPR, the name, address, telephone number, and other data processed in relation to these are considered personal data of the data subject, while any operation performed on the data, such as collection, storage, and use of the data, is classified as data processing.  
(50) For the legality of data processing, the data controller must have a legal basis in accordance with Article 6(1) of the GDPR.  
(51) Regarding the legal bases, the Authority emphasizes that a data processing operation can fundamentally have only one valid legal basis. It is unacceptable for the data controller to assign different legal bases to the same data processing purpose—in this case, maintaining contact with supporters, informing about the Obligor's activities, and fundraising—since the simultaneous application of multiple legal bases violates the principles of transparency and fair procedure.  
(52) According to the Obligor's statement, the personal data of the data subjects is processed based on the legitimate interest legal basis under Article 6(1)(f) of the GDPR "for the purpose of enabling the data subject to be a donor to support the Foundation's mission and to provide opportunities for this as often as possible."  
(53) At the same time, the thank-you letter delivered to the data subjects expressing their intention to donate—based on the attached sample letters—contained information stating (See (5) paragraph /xvii. point) that the legal basis for data processing is the consent of the data subject, which is expressed by the data subject accepting and receiving the thank-you letter.  
(54) The Obligor attached a copy of its interest balancing test dated January 1, 2020. According to the document presented, the Obligor's operations require the occasional and preferably continuous collection of donations. Without addressing citizens and presenting the Foundation's activities, people cannot decide whether they wish to donate for the stated purpose. Based on the "interest balancing" outlined in the attached document, the telephone outreach is also an opportunity for conversation between the caller representing the Obligor and the individual, which is an important and trust-building method, allowing the data subject to receive immediate answers to their questions. Therefore, the data controller has a fundamental and legitimate interest in establishing contact and communication, and the telephone call is the most appropriate method for this. According to the provisions of point 3 of the document, "we can only act accurately, reliably, and in a trust-building manner with the data provided by the donor if we coordinate their data and contact details with the donor themselves, and they consent to the data processing by voluntarily providing this information."  
(55) Although there was no information available regarding data processing on the Obligor's website for a significant part of the specified period, as of March 1, 2021, the data processing information available on the Obligor's website mentions "legitimate interest, consent to the processing of personal data, and performance of a contract" as legal bases for data processing.  
(56) The Authority examined the existence of legitimate interest under Article 6(1)(f) of the GDPR based on the data controller's statements and the submitted interest balancing. It found that the document lacked an assessment of the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, an analysis of the impact of data processing on the data subjects, and a demonstration and justification of why the interests of the Obligor take precedence over these. The interest balancing essentially involves the data controller.
19  
The activity was presented, but the comparison of the interests of the data subjects and the data controller was not conducted at all, nor were they identified. Accordingly, the balancing of interests presented for the examined period did not meet the requirements expected by the General Data Protection Regulation, thus the Obligor did not demonstrate, based on Article 5(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation, that its data processing was lawful, transparent, and had an appropriate legal basis.  
(57) Based on the above, during the examined period, the legal basis for data processing was not clearly indicated, neither towards the data subjects nor in the Authority's proceedings. The Obligor primarily identified the legitimate interest as the legal basis for its data processing; however, both in its communication with the Authority and in the attached balancing test, two legal bases appeared, in some cases indicating them in parallel concerning the data processing. Furthermore, the data processing information that became available on the Obligor's website in the meantime indicated three legal bases for its data processing: legitimate interest, consent, and performance of a contract. In the Obligor's statements and the documents it attached, the data processing conducted for the purpose of locating donors was also conflated with the data processing related to maintaining contact with those who had previously made donations. The two data processing activities, however, cannot be fully separated, as during a period, repeated contact with previous donors also occurred using a call center, alongside the outreach to the Obligor's volunteers, and simultaneously the call center also recruited new donors based on its own database, and the personal data of the "recruited" new donors were subsequently added to the database of previous donors.  
(58) As described above, consent also appears as the legal basis for data processing, according to the information indicated on the thank-you letter delivered via courier, as the legal basis for data processing is the consent of the data subject, which is expressed by the data subject accepting and receiving the thank-you letter.  
(59) According to Article 4(11) of the General Data Protection Regulation, one of the most important conditions for valid consent is that it must be preceded by appropriate information, and the data subject must give their consent to the processing of their personal data through a statement or an unambiguous declaration of confirmation.  
(60) The (42) preamble of the General Data Protection Regulation also states that the data controller must provide a pre-formulated consent declaration, which must be made available in an understandable and easily accessible form, and its language must be clear and unambiguous, and must not contain unfair conditions.  
(61) The guidance on consent under Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Guidance No. 5/2020, as well as the guidance WP259 on consent issued by the Data Protection Working Party established under Article 29 of the Data Protection Directive, confirm that a statement or an act expressing confirmation unambiguously is a prerequisite for valid consent.  
(62) Data processing based on the consent of the data subject can only occur if the data subject consents to the processing of their personal data through an unambiguous confirmatory act. This confirmatory act fulfills the data subject's right to informational self-determination: based on appropriate information, the data subject considers whether to give consent to the specific data processing or not. Consent will be unambiguous if the data controller ensures that the data subject must take an active step for the data processing to occur. Any statement or act that clearly indicates that the data subject has given their consent to the intended processing of their personal data is acceptable for this purpose.
20  
(63) According to the Authority's position, the receipt of the thank-you letter cannot be considered as the valid consent of the data subject for the further processing of their personal data for purposes not specified on the thank-you letter, meaning it cannot be regarded as a specific, clear expression of the data subjects' will. For the Obligor to process the data subjects' personal data for purposes beyond the specific one-time donation—such as further communication with supporters, information about the Obligor's activities, and fundraising—explicit consent from the data subjects is required.  
(64) The Authority holds that the legality of the processing of personal data of the data subjects can be established only if the data controller has a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data for all processing purposes. The fairness and legality of processing the personal data of the data subjects for additional purposes can be determined only if the data subject has received adequate information and the opportunity to decide regarding the processing of their provided personal data for which processing purpose it will be used, meaning the data subject has sufficient information regarding the circumstances of data processing before making the decision to give consent, which was also clearly lacking in this case. The data subjects cannot be denied the option to freely decide, alongside the donation, whether they wish to receive further information about the Obligor's activities or whether the Foundation will contact them in the future via phone regarding current fundraising campaigns.  
(65) According to Article 7(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, in the case of consent as a legal basis, the data controller must also be able to demonstrate that the data subject has consented to the processing of their personal data, which obligation—considering the principle of accountability—includes that the data controller must prove that the individual elements of consent (adequate information, voluntariness, clarity of consent) were properly fulfilled during the acquisition of consent, as well as that it has met the additional requirements set forth by the regulation in connection with consent. These conditions were evidently not fulfilled during the Obligor's data processing for the reasons outlined above. Based on all of this, the Obligor's data processing in this manner—namely, further communication and contact with donors through phone calls—cannot have a lawful basis under Article 6(1)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation, as all of its conceptual elements are missing.  
(66) Based on the above, the Authority established for the period specified in point III./4. that the Obligor violated Article 5(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation and, in this regard, Article 6(1), as it processed personal data unlawfully and transparently without a valid legal basis.

(80) According to Article 12(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the data controller has an active obligation to provide information to the data subject in a manner that corresponds to the communication channel being used.  
(81) Based on the above, it can be established that the Obligor did not provide the essential information listed in Article 13(1) and (2) of the GDPR to the data subjects during the phone call; the data subjects could only learn from the call that they were being contacted on behalf of the Obligor. Furthermore, other information such as the source of the phone number, the possibility to object, legal basis, etc., was not provided by the Obligor to the data subjects, thereby preventing the data subjects from effectively exercising their rights.  
(82) In the Authority's view, at the beginning of the phone call – rather than at the very end – a brief informational statement should be provided to the data subjects regarding the most important information related to data processing, particularly indicating the source of the called number. This brief data processing notice should refer to where the data subjects can find the detailed data processing notice on other platforms.  
(83) Based on the above, the Authority established for the period specified in III./4 that the Obligor did not provide adequate general information to the data subjects regarding the processing of their personal data during the phone calls, thereby violating Article 12(1) and Articles 13(1)-(2) of the GDPR.  
(84) The obligation to provide transparent information and communication imposed on the data controller in Article 12 of the GDPR also extends to measures related to the exercise of the rights of the data subjects. Accordingly, the data controller must take appropriate measures to ensure that information related to the data subject's rights to access, rectification, erasure, restriction, data portability, and objection is provided in a transparent and easily accessible manner.  
(85) Data subject requests must be fulfilled based on the conditions set out in Article 12 of the GDPR, that is, as a general rule, within one month. The preamble (59) states that measures must be provided to facilitate the exercise of the rights of data subjects granted by this regulation, including the provision of mechanisms that allow the data subject to request and, where applicable, receive such information free of charge.
23  
particularly access to personal data, its rectification and deletion, as well as exercising the right to object.  
(86) The information provided to data subjects must be easily accessible, which primarily means that the data subject should not have to search for the information; it must be immediately visible to them where and how they can access it.  
(87) According to the Obligor's statement, the exercise of data subject rights was provided to data subjects in such a way that they could send their data subject requests to the Obligor's email address or by postal mail. However, the Obligor stated that it could not accept deletion requests made via SMS or indicated during a phone call to the Obligor's volunteer, as these did not reach the Obligor, and the telemarketing software used by the contracted call center could not receive SMS messages, only initiate outgoing calls and receive incoming calls.  
(88) Based on the above, it can be established that the data subjects did not receive information about the possibility and method of exercising their data subject rights during the phone call; they only had access to the information available from the greeting text of the phone call – especially if they did not wish to donate – regarding who the call was on behalf of. Furthermore, considering that there was no information available on the Obligor's website regarding data processing for an extended period, the data subjects were limited in exercising their data subject rights based on the information received and available.  
(89) Therefore, the Obligor did not facilitate the exercise of the data subjects' rights, thereby violating Article 12(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
(90) According to Article 5(1)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the processing of personal data must be lawful and fair, and conducted in a transparent manner for the data subjects. It must be transparent to natural persons how their personal data is collected, used, accessed, or otherwise processed, as well as the extent to which their personal data is processed and will be processed. The data processing, by not providing the data subjects with actual information regarding the legal basis for processing in accordance with the provisions of the law, which provided information, could have been misleading for the data subjects, as it was not clear to the data subjects providing donations on which legal basis their personal data was actually being processed, whether on the basis of legitimate interest or consent. By this behavior, the Obligor violated the principle of transparency as set out in Article 5(1)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
Furthermore, the fact that the data subjects, on the one hand, did not receive information about the most important information related to data processing during the phone call, and on the other hand, there was no information available on the Obligor's website for a long time that would have ensured the data subjects' awareness and facilitated the exercise of their rights, constitutes a significant deficiency in information, resulting in a violation of the fundamental requirement of transparent data processing. Therefore, the Obligor's above behavior violated the principle of transparency as set out in Article 5(1)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation.

5.1. The Obligor's data processing practice during the specified period  
(91) According to the Obligor's statement, telephone fundraising during the specified period occurred in two ways. The Obligor, through its volunteers, seeks out its previous donors.
24  
The Obligor requests support via phone and seeks out companies and organizations. According to the Obligor's statement, it does not conduct fundraising through outreach to unknown private individuals.  
(92) In addition to the above, donations can also be made via direct transfer to the Obligor's bank account or by offering 1% of personal income tax.  
(93) The Obligor utilizes courier services during the collection of donations, specifically the services of [Company3] mentioned earlier, whose task is to deliver thank-you letters to donors and to ensure the delivery of the offered cash or in-kind donations to the Obligor.  
(94) According to the Obligor's statement, it processes the data of supporters based on legitimate interest, and to support this, it has attached a copy of the interest balancing assessment dated December 1, 2022.  
(95) According to the Obligor's statement, it has developed a new procedure for handling requests from data subjects during the specified period, and information regarding data processing has also been placed on the Obligor's website.  

5.2. Legal Basis for Data Processing  
(96) According to the Obligor's statement, it processes the data of supporters based on legitimate interest, as it is in the Obligor's legitimate interest to call for support from potential supporters, namely individuals who have previously made donations, in order to achieve its goals and finance the supported objectives.  
(97) In connection with the legitimate interest legal basis, it is important to emphasize that it does not serve as a justification for the data controller to process personal data at any time and for any reason in the absence of other legal bases. Although it may seem to be the most flexible legal basis, its application entails significant responsibility for the data controller—not only in the narrow sense of processing personal data but also in fulfilling related guarantees. Appropriate consideration, planning, and guarantees must ensure, among other things, that the data subject is aware of the data processing and can object to it, as after the data processing—especially in the case of short-term or one-time data processing, such as a call—all rights of the data subject may become void.  
(98) Closely related to the legitimate interest legal basis is the principle of accountability set forth in Article 5(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation, which imposes an obligation on the data controller to fulfill administrative burdens that ensure the transparency, accuracy, and fairness of personal data processing. This is not merely about "paperwork," but rather a substantive task.  
(99) It is neither the responsibility of the data subject nor the Authority to identify and justify the purpose of data processing and the legitimate interests on behalf of the data controller during an official procedure. The data controller must specifically justify, assess, and establish guarantees for the purpose and legitimate interests for which it intends to process personal data, broken down to the level of data and purpose. The failure to do so is solely the responsibility of the data controller. After determining the lawful purpose, but before commencing data processing, the data controller must assess whether the data processing is necessary to achieve the purpose. In this context, it must be examined whether there are alternative solutions available that could achieve the intended purpose without the specific data processing or with less restrictive means compared to the specific data processing.
25  
If the data controller can reasonably achieve the same result through a less restrictive means or tool regarding the rights to personal data, necessity does not exist, and thus the legal basis under Article 6(1)(f) is not applicable.  
(100) According to the Obligor's statement, it has a legitimate interest in soliciting support from potential donors to achieve its objectives and finance supported goals. It states that the rights of the data subjects and the interests of the Obligor have been weighed against each other, and a balancing test has been conducted to substantiate the legality of the data processing. According to the Obligor's statement, the purpose of data processing is to solicit donations from potential supporters.  
(101) Regarding the telephone inquiries conducted by the Obligor for the purpose of soliciting donations, as indicated in point 2 of the attached balancing test, the purpose of data processing is to "contact potential donors by phone or email to invite them to donate for its public benefit objectives. The purpose of the data processing conducted for the inquiry is to establish a future supporter relationship or to maintain an existing donor relationship." The Authority notes in this regard that the email address included in the records submitted by the Obligor, which would allow for communication with existing donors via email, is not recorded, thus the indication of this mode of communication among the purposes of data processing is not valid.  
According to the information available from the balancing test, the purpose of data processing identified by the Obligor is, according to the Authority's findings, on the one hand, to seek new donors, and on the other hand, to re-establish contact with previously existing donors, meaning that the Obligor continues its telephone inquiries for two different data processing purposes.  
In point 7 of the Obligor's balancing test, the Authority believes that further purposes can be identified, as stated in the referenced point, namely that "without data processing, it would not be possible to assess donor habits and develop services," regarding which the Obligor did not provide specific information in its statements to the Authority; however, these can be identified as additional data processing purposes.  
(102) In the present case, the balancing test sent by the Obligor in response to the Authority's inquiry, which is presented in point (ii) of paragraph (9), according to the Authority's position, did not support that the data processing is in the legitimate interest of the Obligor, as the Obligor did not identify all purposes when planning its data processing, and consequently, a purpose-by-purpose assessment did not take place; thus, the Obligor did not examine the necessity of data processing concerning all purposes.  
(103) In terms of necessity, specific facts must be evaluated considering the given situation – why and how the data processing solves the intended purpose, and on the other hand, an analysis of why other reasonably available means are not suitable for achieving the goal. In this context, the Obligor merely stated that the goal cannot be achieved in another way, but did not demonstrate why other available means are not suitable for achieving the goal for objective reasons.  
(104) The balancing test submitted by the Obligor also did not substantively and specifically address why and to what extent the data processing constitutes a limitation on the privacy of the data subjects. It completely lacked the information that if the data subjects support the Obligor with a donation, they will simultaneously be included in the Obligor's donor database, based on which the Obligor will repeatedly contact the data subjects every 2-3 months to invite them to donate again.
26  
In the balancing of interests, among the guarantees undertaken by the data controller, the Obligor stated that "the Data Controller ensures the right to object to data processing to the widest possible extent, and in the event of an objection, terminates the data processing without further consideration." However, the Obligor did not provide the right to object to the data subjects during the first phone call, nor was the attention of the data subjects specifically drawn to the right to object during the phone call. According to the attached script, which is described in point (7)/xii., during the phone call, the data subject is only informed about where the data processing information and information about the Obligor can be found on the website. The data processing information available on the website highlights under point 7, after the section "Data processing related to the facilitation of donations and donations," that "The Foundation respectfully draws the attention of Data Subjects to the fact that they may object to the processing of their data in cases of data processing based on the legitimate interest of the Foundation regarding inquiries from the Foundation, and may request that the Foundation no longer contact them. In this case, we kindly ask that they primarily contact the Foundation in writing (via email or postal letter)." However, the Authority's position is that data subjects cannot be expected to search the Obligor's website for information regarding what further data processing may be involved in supporting the Obligor with a donation after the phone call and donation.  
Essentially, data subjects cannot expect that the Obligor will contact them again after the donation to encourage them to donate again, and that the Obligor will repeatedly do this every few months until the data subject actively objects against it – specifically in the manner indicated by the Obligor, via email or postal letter – considering that the data subjects received no information about this.  
This is further supported by the fact that previous complaints received by the Authority were related to the Obligor or that shortly after their donations, they were contacted again to encourage them to donate, or that they were called again every 2-3 months without even recalling a donation made years earlier.  
(105) In the complete absence of a substantive assessment of the interests and expectations of the data subjects, the Obligor could not identify and compare its legitimate interest with the rights and freedoms of the data subjects – which is a legal requirement for the least disturbance of the broader category of "private sphere" beyond the specific rights of the data subjects – thus it could not draw correct conclusions at the end of the balancing of interests. In the balancing of interests, it must be substantiated that the identified legitimate interest takes precedence over the rights and freedoms of the data subjects in a given case. This involves not only explaining why the data controller finds the data processing "good" and "necessary," but also considering all aspects of the data processing, and then evaluating them individually and collectively. It must also be explained why no other means can be chosen, why the given data processing is the most suitable for achieving the goal, and what guarantees the data controller provides to the data subject for the enforcement of their rights. This did not happen in the present case.  
As a result of all this, the interests of the data subjects are excessively harmed such that the legitimate interest identified by the data controller is no longer balanced with the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, thus the legitimate interest of the Obligor does not stand.  
(106) Upon reviewing the balancing of interests, the Authority also noticed that in point 7 titled "Balancing of Interests," when describing the group of data subjects, it stated that "The data subjects may be donors who have previously consented to the processing of their data for such purposes [...]," meaning that the Obligor continues to phrase it as if it were processing personal data based on consent, whereas according to its statement, the previous
27  
The legal basis for the data processing related to repeated telephone inquiries to donors is legitimate interest. The Authority emphasizes that a data processing operation can fundamentally have only one valid legal basis. It is unacceptable for the data controller to assign different legal bases to the same data processing purpose, as the simultaneous application of multiple legal bases violates the principle of transparency.  
(107) Based on the above, the Authority established for the period specified in point III./5. that the Obligor violated Article 6(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, as it did not substantiate the existence of its legitimate interest, thereby processing the personal data of the data subjects without a valid legal basis in connection with its telephone inquiries soliciting donations.  

5.4. The obligation to provide appropriate information during communication with data subjects  
(108) According to the Obligor's statement regarding the period specified in III./5., it contacts its previous donors by phone through its volunteers and requests their support, as well as reaching out to companies and organizations.  
(109) According to the attached script, the text spoken at the very beginning of the call contained information regarding data processing, with the following content:  
“Good day, I am XY calling from the Tear Drop Foundation. More information about the Foundation and the data processing notice can be found at the following website: www.konnycsepp.hu/ If you have any questions regarding data processing, you can also reach us by email.”  
(110) Based on the script, the Authority found that accurate information regarding the data controller was not provided during the call, and the website mentioned during the call indicated an inaccessible address, as the Obligor is actually reachable at https://konnycseppnelkul.hu/, meaning that the called parties could not identify the data controller.  
(111) According to Article 12(1) of the GDPR, it is the responsibility of the data controller to take appropriate measures to ensure that all information regarding the processing of personal data mentioned in Articles 13 and 14, as well as all notifications under Articles 15-22 and 34, are provided to data subjects in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, clearly and understandably formulated.  
(112) The system of appropriate information in the GDPR serves to ensure that the data subject is aware of which personal data will be processed, by which data controller, for what purpose, and how. This is essential for the data subject to be in a position to effectively exercise their rights.  
(113) The brief information provided during the call must include the most important information regarding the planned data processing, including information about the identity of the data controller and the availability of the detailed notice. It is not the responsibility of the data subject to obtain information from other sources; it must be readily and reasonably available.  
(114) According to Article 12(1) of the GDPR, the data controller has an active obligation to provide information in a manner that corresponds to the communication channel being used.

(115) Based on the examined script, it can be established that it does not provide any information regarding the most important details mentioned earlier, and while it attempts to fulfill the information obligation by mentioning a website, it refers to a non-existent website.  
(116) In light of the above, the Authority determined for the period specified in point III./5 that the Obligor did not provide information to the affected individuals regarding the processing of their personal data during the phone calls, thereby violating Article 12(1) and Articles 13(1)-(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  

IV.  
Legal Consequences  
(117) Considering that the Obligor is currently still applying the data processing practice of making phone calls soliciting donations, it is necessary to require, pursuant to Article 58(2)(d) of the GDPR, that it align its data processing with the provisions of the GDPR. Data processing may only continue for appropriate, valid, and specific purposes, with a valid legal basis, and with proof of the maximum assurance of the rights of the data subjects; otherwise, the Obligor must demonstrate the cessation of data processing to the Authority within the deadline specified in the operative part.  
(118) Based on Section 75/A of the Infotv., the Authority exercises its powers set out in Articles 83(2)-(6) of the GDPR with consideration of the principle of proportionality, particularly by addressing the first-time violation of the regulations regarding the processing of personal data – as defined in legislation or in a binding legal act of the European Union – primarily through a warning to the data controller or data processor, in accordance with Article 58 of the GDPR.  
(119) In this context, the Authority considered all circumstances of the case based on Article 83(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation and Section 75/A of the Infotv. Given the circumstances of the case and the nature of the data processing, the Authority determined that a warning is not a proportional sanction in the case of the violation uncovered during this procedure; therefore, the Authority decided to impose a fine, taking into account Section 61(1)(a) of the Infotv. and Article 58(2)(i) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
(120) Regarding the necessity and amount of the fine, the Authority took into account that the Obligor's last published net sales revenue, according to the official report published on the civil organizations' birosag.hu website, was 82,804,000 HUF in 2022 (94,832,000 HUF in 2021; 96,182,000 HUF in 2020). The possible maximum fine, based on Article 83(5)(a) and (b) of the General Data Protection Regulation, is 20 million EUR.  
(121) The determination of the amount of the fine was based on the following considerations:  
(i) The Obligor's revenue in HUF falls within the revenue bracket below 2 million euros.  
(ii) In the case of violations falling under the fine category according to Article 83(5) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the maximum fine ranges between 0 and 8,000 EUR (approximately 3,000,000 HUF).  
(iii) The fine amount of 1,000,000 HUF established for the Obligor is approximately 1.2% of the annual global market turnover (revenue).  
(122) In determining the amount of the data protection fine, the Authority considered the following as mitigating circumstances:  
(i) The Obligor acted negligently regarding the established violations. The GDPR requires at least minimal expertise from the data controller when processing personal data.
29  
wishes to process data. Therefore, if a violation occurs due to lack of expertise, it is considered at least negligent. In this case, there is no evidence indicating intent based on the activities of the Obligor and the circumstances revealed in the case. (GDPR Article 83(2)(b)).  
(ii) The Authority has not previously established a relevant data protection violation against the Obligor (GDPR Article 83(2)(e)).  
(iii) The Authority took into account that the Obligor is a civil organization whose declared purpose is primarily to improve the medical equipment and general infrastructure of hospitals and clinics for the recovery of sick children, as well as to provide material and psychological assistance and support to sick children receiving hospital care and other needy individuals. The Obligor's goal also includes supporting sick children's foreign medical treatments and surgeries in material and other ways (GDPR Article 83(2)(k)).  
(123) In determining the amount of the data protection fine, the Authority considered the following aggravating circumstances:  
(i) The Obligor processes a significant number of personal data of affected individuals (the attached copy of the register contains more than 21,000 affected individuals) related to fundraising, for an unclear purpose and legal basis (GDPR Article 83(2)(a)).  
(ii) The Obligor's data processing was aimed at contact-type personal data (name, phone number, address), and the Obligor also records the amount donated and the date of donation related to the affected individuals (GDPR Article 83(2)(g)).  
(124) In making the decision regarding legal consequences, the Authority did not consider the points of Article 83(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation that were not detailed above to be relevant in this case.  
(125) The amount of the data protection fine was determined by the Authority in the exercise of its discretion based on legislation.  
(126) Based on the criteria set forth in this Section IV and all circumstances of the case, the Authority deemed the amount of the fine to be proportionate from both specific and general prevention perspectives. This assessment does not bind the Authority in other cases; the legal consequence – as highlighted in Guidelines paragraphs 145-146 – is determined in each case based on individual circumstances.  
(127) The Authority publishes the decision with the name of the Obligor in consideration of the Obligor's activities and the affected individuals.
