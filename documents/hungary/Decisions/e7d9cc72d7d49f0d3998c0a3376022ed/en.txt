D E C I S I O N  

The National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Authority) issues the following decisions in the official data protection authority procedure initiated ex officio against […] Limited Liability Company (hereinafter: Obligor) due to the presumed violation of the provisions of the General Data Protection Regulation:  

1.  
The Authority establishes that the Obligor has not substantiated the existence of a legitimate interest regarding the processing of personal data stored in its database, and during the procedure, it has not justified any other legally existing legal basis; therefore, the Obligor processes the phone numbers and other personal data of the data subjects in its database without a legal basis, thereby violating Article 6(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (hereinafter: General Data Protection Regulation or GDPR), which concerns the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, as well as the repeal of Directive 95/46/EC.  

2.  
The Authority establishes that the Obligor has not demonstrated the compliance of the processing of personal data stored in its database after the applicability of the GDPR on May 25, 2018, nor has it taken appropriate substantive measures for its review, thereby violating the principle of accountability under Article 5(2) of the GDPR.  

3.  
The Authority establishes that the Obligor, by failing to inform the data subjects of the most important information regarding data processing during the first contact with the data subjects, in consideration of the provisions of Article 14 of the GDPR, and by not providing adequate and accurate information about all essential circumstances of data processing in the data processing notices available on its website, has violated the principle of transparent data processing under Article 5(1)(a) of the GDPR, and in this regard, Articles 12(1) and 14(1)-(3) of the GDPR.  

4.  
Due to the violations established in points 1-3, the Authority instructs the Obligor, pursuant to Article 58(2)(d) of the GDPR, to document the deletion of the phone number and associated personal data stored in its database, acquired from […] Ltd. Among its activities, the Obligated Party also engages in telephone information and market and public opinion research, which requires a database containing contact information. During the outbound calling activity, the Obligated Party does not transfer the personal data of the individuals to its client – whose text is spoken during the phone call initiated towards the individual concerned.  
The impact of data processing on the individuals concerned can be twofold: (i) if the information conveyed is relevant to the individual, the data processing cannot be interpreted as having a negative effect; (ii) if the individual receives information that is not relevant to them, it does not affect them, noting, however, that this is an automatic outbound calling service, during which the individual encounters a machine-generated voice recording. The effect may only manifest in that if the individual is not interested in the text conveyed during the call, they must hang up the phone or press the call end button. According to the data controller's position, this action, which takes approximately one to two seconds, does not have a substantial effect on the individual, thus there cannot be a claim of disproportionate impact on the individual, regardless of the fact that the data processing activity may momentarily, to a small extent, disturb the individual in their fundamental rights.  
In summary, the Obligated Party has determined that its legitimate interest is based on the freedom of enterprise enshrined in the Fundamental Law, which is also reinforced by the Civil Code, and that the data processing does not have a disproportionate effect on the individual. Therefore, it concluded that its data processing based on legitimate interest is considered lawful, as the "exercise of the fundamental right of the Obligated Party does not significantly affect the individual that their phone rings and, in case the text conveyed is irrelevant to them, they must terminate the phone call with a single motion."  
The Obligated Party also took into account that its outbound calling activity does not target the same database on a daily basis.

(46) According to the preamble (171) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the General Data Protection Regulation repeals Directive 95/46/EC. Data processing that commenced before the application date of the General Data Protection Regulation must be brought into compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation within two years from the date of its entry into force. If the data processing is based on consent under Directive 95/46/EC and the data subject provided their consent in accordance with the conditions set out in the General Data Protection Regulation, there is no need to obtain the data subject's permission again for the data controller to continue processing the data after the application date of the General Data Protection Regulation. Decisions made by the Commission under Directive 95/46/EC, as well as authorizations issued by supervisory authorities, remain in effect until they are amended, replaced, or repealed.  
(47) According to Article 2(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the General Data Protection Regulation applies to the processing of personal data that is carried out by automated means, as well as to the non-automated processing of personal data that forms part of a filing system or is intended to form part of a filing system.  
(48) According to Article 4(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, "personal data" means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ("data subject"); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, particularly by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.  
(49) According to Article 5(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, personal data must be:  
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject ("lawfulness, fairness, and transparency");  
According to paragraph (2), the data controller is responsible for compliance with paragraph (1) and must be able to demonstrate such compliance ("accountability").  
(50) Under Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation, the processing of personal data is lawful only if at least one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party, or for taking steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;  
d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller;  
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, particularly if the data subject is a child.  
The first subparagraph, point (f) does not apply to the processing of personal data by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.  
(51) According to Article 12(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the data controller shall take appropriate measures to provide the data subject with all information regarding the processing of personal data referred to in Articles 13 and 14, as well as any information pursuant to Articles 15-22 and 34, in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language, particularly when addressing any information to children. The information shall be provided in writing or by other means – including, where appropriate, by electronic means. Upon request of the data subject, oral information may also be provided, provided that the identity of the data subject is otherwise verified.

1. **Subject of the current data protection authority procedure**  
(70) The Authority, as stated in point (26) of this decision, established in the preliminary investigation procedure under number NAIH-7678/2021 that the Obligor processes personal data in an independent data controller capacity regarding the storage of a database containing the phone number owned by it and related personal data such as residential postal code, residential municipality, and residential county.  
(71) The Authority states in connection with the investigation procedure number NAIH-4949/2022 that its jurisdiction does not extend to determining the validity of civil law contracts; however, based on the details outlined above, the Authority can ascertain that, from the perspective of the Authority's procedure, it is not of decisive significance whether the contract between the Obligor and […] has been terminated, since the Obligor did not act in accordance with the client's instructions, did not process the phone numbers designated by the client for the automated calls, and independently decided on the essential conditions of data processing against the client's will. Therefore, based on Article 28 (10) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the Obligor has become an independent data controller and is responsible for the data processing related to automated phone calls conducted using its own database between March 29, 2022, and April 1, 2022, regardless of the civil law qualification of the contract.  
(72) In the referenced preliminary investigation procedure NAIH-7678/2021, since the Obligor performed the political calling activity based on the specific assignment using the phone numbers stored in its own database, the Authority also made findings regarding the legality of the personal data stored in the Obligor's database. However, considering that the subject of the preliminary investigation procedure was the legality of the data processing related to a specific calling activity of the Obligor, the Authority examines the legality of the processing of personal data stored in the Obligor's database within the framework of the current data protection authority procedure.  

2. **Legality of the processing of personal data stored in the Obligor's database**  
(73) The phone number of a private individual, as well as the personal data stored in connection with it (name, phone number, residential postal code, residential municipality, and residential county), qualifies as personal data under Article 4 (1) of the GDPR. The general provisions of the GDPR, sectoral regulations, and, where applicable, the provisions of the contract with the service provider apply to their processing. If personal data is processed in any way, it is necessary to define the purpose and have an appropriate so-called legal basis for data processing, which can refer to the legal bases under Article 6 (1) of the GDPR in general cases of personal data processing.  
(74) The Obligor indicated legitimate interest as the legal basis for the processing of personal data acquired from […] Ltd. through a sale.  
(75) In the balancing of interests attached to this, the Obligor stated that it is an economic entity engaged in telephone information, market, and public opinion research, which requires databases containing contact data for the exercise of its activities. a) if the data subject receives appropriate and relevant information, then the data processing cannot be interpreted as having a negative effect, since the information reaches its destination regardless of whether it may also affect the fundamental rights of the data subject;

(82) In relation to the legal basis of legitimate interest, the Authority emphasizes that it is not intended for the data controller to process personal data at any time and for any reason by referring to this basis in the absence of other applicable legal bases. Although it may seem to be the most flexible legal basis, its application entails significant responsibility for the data controller, not only in the narrow sense of processing personal data but also in assuming compliance with other related guarantee obligations. Appropriate consideration, planning, and guarantees must ensure, among other things, that the data subject is aware of the data processing and can object to it, as all rights of the data subject may become void after the data processing—especially in the case of short-term or one-time data processing, such as a telephone call.  
(83) Closely related to the legal basis of legitimate interest is the principle of accountability set forth in Article 5(2) of the GDPR, which imposes an obligation on the data controller to fulfill administrative burdens that ensure the transparency, accuracy, and fairness of personal data processing. It is not merely about "paperwork," but rather a substantive task, which is particularly true for data processing activities that involve heightened risks, including those related to political opinions and associated public opinion research objectives.  
(84) The Obligor's balancing of interests merely established that the Obligor needs personal data to carry out its activities; however, it did not substantiate that the data processing serves the legitimate interest of the Obligor. Based on the consistent practice of the Authority, the European Data Protection Board, and the Court of Justice of the European Union, the legitimate interest under Article 6(1)(f) of the GDPR is not equivalent to merely a lawful economic interest.  
(85) The data controller must specifically justify, broken down by data and purpose, why it wishes to process personal data for what purposes and what legitimate interests it has. After determining the lawful purpose, the data controller must examine, even before commencing data processing, whether the processing is necessary to achieve the purpose. In this context, it must be assessed whether there are alternative solutions available that could achieve the intended purpose without the specific data processing or with less restrictive means compared to the specific data processing. If the data controller can reasonably achieve the same result through a less restrictive means of accessing personal data, necessity is not present, and thus the legal basis under Article 6(1)(f) cannot be applied.  
(86) In assessing necessity, specific facts must be evaluated considering the particular situation—how and why the data processing resolves the intended purpose, and on the other hand, analyzing why other reasonably available means are not suitable for achieving the purpose. This was completely absent from the Obligor's interest balancing, which was undated and unsigned.  
(87) The Obligor's interest balancing also did not substantively address why and to what extent the data processing restricts the privacy of the data subjects, why the data processing is effective, and why it is the least restrictive means to achieve the purpose. In the complete absence of a substantive assessment of the interests and expectations of the data subjects, the Obligor could not identify and compare its legitimate interest with the rights and freedoms of the data subjects—which is a legal requirement for the least interference with the broader "private sphere" beyond the specific rights of the data subjects—thus it could not draw correct conclusions at the end of the interest balancing. The data controller must also substantiate in the interest balancing that the identified legitimate interest takes precedence over the rights and freedoms of the data subjects in the specific case. In this regard, it is necessary not only to explain why the data processing is "good" and "necessary," but also to consider all aspects of the specific data processing.

This general right to object clearly expresses the data subject's objection under the General Data Protection Regulation to any data processing for marketing or research purposes concerning their phone numbers, to which they have not specifically consented in advance. In the case of the phone numbers managed by the Obligor, it has not been demonstrated that there are no phone numbers in its database for which the subscriber has objected to the use of their personal data for research and marketing purposes, as the Obligor's legitimate interest cannot exist in relation to these phone numbers.  
(89) Based on the above, the Obligor did not substantiate the existence of its legitimate interest in the processing of personal data stored in its database during the balancing of interests, nor did it demonstrate any other legally valid legal basis during the proceedings; therefore, the Authority established that the Obligor processes the phone numbers and other personal data of the data subjects in its database without a legal basis, thereby violating Article 6 (1) of the GDPR.

IV.  
Legal Consequences  
(114) The Authority, noting that the Obligor has not substantiated a legally existing legal basis for the processing of personal data stored in its database, following the applicability of the GDPR from May 25, 2018, has not substantiated the compliance of the processing of personal data still stored in its database, nor has it taken appropriate substantive measures for its review, and has not provided information to the data subjects recorded in its database either at the first contact or generally provided adequate, truthful information to the data subjects regarding the most important information about the processing of their personal data, instructed the Obligor, based on Article 58(2)(d) of the General Data Protection Regulation, to document the deletion of the personal data stored in its database, specifically the phone number acquired from […] Ltd. "kt.a." (previously referred to in the sales contract as: […] Ltd.) on May 7, 2018, through a sale, and the associated personal data!  
(115) Based on Section 75/A of the Infotv., the Authority exercises its powers as set out in Articles 83(2)-(6) of the General Data Protection Regulation, taking into account the principle of proportionality, particularly that in the case of the first violation of the provisions regarding the processing of personal data – as defined in legislation or in a binding legal act of the European Union – the remedy for the violation is primarily addressed by warning the data controller or data processor, in accordance with Article 58 of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
(116) In this context, the Authority considered all circumstances of the case based on Article 83(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation and Section 75/A of the Infotv. Given the circumstances of the case and the nature of the data processing, the Authority determined that a warning would neither be proportional nor serve as a deterrent sanction in the case of the violation uncovered during this procedure; therefore, the Authority decided to impose a fine in accordance with Section 61(1)(a) of the Infotv. based on Article 58(2)(i) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
(117) Regarding the necessity and amount of the fine, the Authority took into account that the Obligor's last published net global market turnover (net sales revenue for the year 2022) was approximately 150,000,000 HUF according to the public e-reporting registry. The possible maximum fine, based on Article 83(5)(b) of the General Data Protection Regulation, is 20 million EUR.  
(118) The Authority considered the contents of the European Data Protection Board's Guidelines 4/2022 (hereinafter: Guidelines) regarding the determination of the amount of the fine, which contains the calculation criteria for administrative fines under the General Data Protection Regulation. In this regard, the determination of the fine amount was based on the following criteria:  
(i) The Obligor's revenue in HUF is in the revenue bracket below 2 million euros.  
(ii) According to the Guidelines, in the case of violations falling under the fine category of Article 83(5) of the General Data Protection Regulation – in the case of a violation assessed as relatively low in severity – the static maximum fine ranges between 0 and 8,000 EUR (approximately 3,000,000 HUF).  
(iii) The fine amount of 1,500,000 HUF determined for the Obligor is approximately 1.008% of the annual global market turnover (revenue).

(119) In determining the amount of the data protection fine, the Authority took the following mitigating circumstances into account:  
(i) No circumstances indicating the intentionality of the Obligor emerged regarding the established infringement (Article 83(2)(b) of the GDPR).  
(ii) The Obligor's data processing was aimed at relational types of personal data, and primarily did not extend to the processing of exact address data (Article 83(2)(g) of the GDPR).  
(iii) The Obligor committed a data protection infringement for the first time (Article 83(2)(e) of the GDPR).  
(120) In determining the amount of the data protection fine, the Authority considered the following aggravating circumstances, noting that it had already assessed the point in Article 83(2)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation in relation to the severity of the infringement, and thus did not take it into account again:  
(i) The Obligor's liability for the infringement is ascertainable, considering that it did not verify the legality of the database at the time of acquisition, nor the actual provision of information to data subjects by the selling company (Article 83(2)(d) of the GDPR).  
(ii) The Authority observed the likelihood of the unlawful nature of the Obligor's general data processing practices during an ex officio investigation initiated based on multiple complaints from data subjects related to the Obligor's activities, which ultimately resulted in the current data protection authority procedure (Article 83(2)(h) of the GDPR).  
(iii) The data processing was also aimed at the Obligor's profit-making, as the Obligor conducts automatic telemarketing activities (public opinion research activities) based on commissions, i.e., within the framework of its economic activities. The lack of appropriate information placed the data subjects in a situation where they could not adequately understand and exercise their rights, thus their data protection risk was even greater (Article 83(2)(k) of the GDPR).  
(121) In making the decision regarding legal consequences, the Authority did not consider the points of Article 83(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation that were not detailed above to be relevant in this case.  
(122) The amount of the data protection fine was determined by the Authority in the exercise of its statutory discretion.  
(123) Based on the criteria set out in this Section IV and all circumstances of the case, the Authority deemed the amount of the fine to be proportionate and deterrent from both the specific and general prevention perspectives. This assessment does not bind the Authority in other cases; the legal consequence – as highlighted in paragraphs 145-146 of the Guidelines – is determined in each case based on individual circumstances.
