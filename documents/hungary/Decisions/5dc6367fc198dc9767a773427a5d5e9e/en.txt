DECISION  

The National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Authority) makes the following decisions in the official data protection authority procedure initiated ex officio to examine the legality of the data processing practices of Pitagorasz Oktatási Stúdió Kft. (Company Registration No. 01-09-309194; hereinafter: Client1) regarding market research and direct marketing purposes – in which the Authority involved […] Zrt. (hereinafter: Client2) as a client:  

1.  
The Authority establishes that Client1 did not prove the existence of a legitimate interest regarding the processing of the name and address data of minor data subjects requested from the personal data and address register; therefore, Client1 processed the requested personal data without a legal basis, thereby violating the principle of accountability under Article 5(2) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (hereinafter: General Data Protection Regulation or GDPR) concerning the protection of natural persons in relation to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, as well as Article 6(1) of the GDPR.  

2.  
The Authority establishes that Client1 violated the principle of purpose limitation under Article 5(1)(b) of the General Data Protection Regulation during the data processing related to postal inquiries made in 2020 and 2021.  

3.  
The Authority establishes that regarding the processing of name and address data requested by Client1 in 2020 and 2021, the data processing violated the principle of fair processing under Article 5(1)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation concerning the purpose of data processing.  

4.  
The Authority establishes that in connection with the data processing related to Client1's postal inquiries, Client1 violated Article 12(1) and the provisions of Article 14(1)(a) and (c), as well as Article 14(2)(b), (c), and (e) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  

5.  
The Authority establishes that Client1 violated Article 6(1) of the GDPR concerning the data processing related to newsletter sending.  

6.  
The Authority established that regarding Client1's data processing related to newsletter sending, Client1 violated Article 12(1) of the GDPR, as well as points (c) and (e) of Article 13(1) and point (d) of Article 13(2).  

7.  
The Authority established that by not deleting the personal data of data subjects who withdrew their subscriptions despite their withdrawal of consent, Client1 violates point (b) of Article 17(1) of the GDPR.  

8.  
The Authority instructs Client1, based on point (d) of Article 58(2) of the GDPR, to document and permanently delete the data of those data subjects stored in its database who unsubscribed from the newsletter, as well as those for whom the newsletter sending was unsuccessful.  

9.  
Due to the above data protection violations, the Authority obliges Client1 to pay a data protection fine of 500,000 HUF, that is, five hundred thousand forints.  

10. The Authority terminates the seizure of databases ordered in its decisions NAIH-8386-1/2022. and NAIH-8386-2/2022.  

11. The Authority terminates the ex officio initiated data protection authority procedure against Client2.  

Client1 must provide written proof of compliance with the obligation set out in point 8 above within 30 days following the expiration of the deadline for legal remedies against this decision, along with supporting evidence, to the Authority. According to Section 61(6) of Act CXII of 2011 on the Right to Informational Self-Determination and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Infotv.), the data affected by the disputed data processing cannot be deleted or destroyed until the expiration of the deadline for initiating legal proceedings against the decision, or until the court's final decision in the case of an administrative lawsuit.  

The fine specified in point 9 above must be paid within 30 days from the date this decision becomes final to the Authority's centralized revenue collection account (10032000-01040425-00000000 Centralized Collection Account IBAN: HU83 1003 2000 0104 0425 0000 0000). When transferring the amount, reference must be made to "NAIH-373/2023 FINE." If Client1 fails to meet the deadline for paying the fine, it is obliged to pay late fees. The amount of the late fee corresponds to the statutory interest, which is equal to the central bank base rate valid on the first day of the calendar half-year affected by the delay.  

In case of non-payment of the fine and late fee, or failure to comply with the obligation in point 8 above, the Authority orders the enforcement of the decision.  

No procedural costs were incurred in the procedure.  

There is no administrative remedy against this decision, but it can be contested in an administrative lawsuit within 30 days from the notification to the Metropolitan Court of Budapest. The lawsuit must be submitted electronically to the Authority, which will forward it along with the case documents to the court. A request for a hearing must be indicated in the lawsuit. For those not fully exempt from personal fees, the fee for the administrative lawsuit is 30,000 HUF, and the case is subject to the fee exemption. Legal representation is mandatory in proceedings before the Metropolitan Court of Budapest.  

The Authority publishes this decision on its website, indicating Client1's identification data.

During the analysis of the saved database, the Authority observed the following facts:  
(45) The database is of a so-called relational type, which means that the relationships (relations) between the individual tables can determine how the individual data points relate to each other, whether there is a logical connection between them, whether they are interconnected, or whether one data point depends on another. These interconnections are described by the logical framework of the database, known as its schema.  
The schema of the received database—although the naming of the tables, column names, and the clear logical relationships between them would justify it—does not contain such logical relationships. As a result, the actual internal relationships of the database and its mode of use cannot be fully mapped; they can only be inferred if the interconnections between the individual tables allow for it.

(99) In the case of data processing based on Article 6(1)(f) of the GDPR, the data controller must conduct a balancing of interests. This is supported by the (47) recital of the GDPR, which states that the existence of a legitimate interest legal basis must be justified by a balancing of interests. In this context, the data controller must examine the necessity of the data processing. The processing of personal data must be directly related to the intended purpose: the purpose must be examined precisely, through a complex and fact-based analysis, particularly considering whether there are less restrictive means to achieve the intended goal and whether there are alternative means that are less restrictive of the data subjects' privacy. The legitimate interest of the data controller and any third party must be defined as precisely as possible, also based on a fact-based, specific examination relevant to the given data controller. The interest must be real and current. A fundamental element of the balancing of interests is the assessment of the interests and expectations of the data subjects, taking into account the status, legal and factual situation of the data subjects, as well as their reasonable expectations regarding the data processing. The balancing of interests involves a detailed analysis of why the legitimate interest of the data controller proportionately restricts the rights of the data subjects and why the primacy of the data controller's interest over the rights of the data subjects follows from the established facts. In the absence of such a balancing of interests—considering the violation of the principle of accountability—there can be no lawful and transparent data processing. The balancing of interests must clearly define the identity of the data controller, and it is the responsibility and duty of the data controller to accurately document and justify the above.  
(100) According to the Authority's consistent and established practice, the data controller is responsible for the lawfulness of the data processing it performs. Due to the nature of the legal basis under Article 6(1)(f) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the data controller must be able to specify exactly which legitimate interest justifies the processing of a specific personal data and, in light of this interest, why the data processing is necessary, while also being able to demonstrate that this interest takes precedence over the data subject's legitimate interest and their right to the protection of personal data.  
(101) The Authority points out that, based on the accountability principle requirement formulated in Article 5(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation, which essentially establishes the objective responsibility of the data controller and heightened due diligence requirements, the data controller has the obligation to demonstrate that the conditions for the lawfulness of data processing are met—
23  
The processing of personal data shall be continuous from the commencement of data processing. The fact that the personal data of the data subject is being processed and that this is not detrimental to them does not imply adequate balancing, meaning that the data controller has fully considered the data subject's perspectives, interests, fundamental rights, and freedoms. The application of interest balancing always comes with the requirement that data controllers must ensure the incorporation of specific guarantees. Finally, it must be determined why the legitimate interests of the data controller or a third party proportionately limit the rights of the data subject. Interest balancing must be conducted prior to the data processing, not retrospectively. The data controller can only reasonably trust that they comply with the legal requirements of data processing in all respects if they can also fulfill this burden of proof and demonstrate the existence of these conditions to the Authority, the adjudicating court, and the data subject in a detailed and sufficiently certain manner.  
(102) It follows that if the data controller cannot prove that the data processing contested by the data subject complied with data protection requirements during the examined period, they do not meet the principle of accountability, thereby violating Article 5(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation. Data controllers must implement all data processing operations in such a way that they can demonstrate at any moment how they complied with data protection regulations. The principle of accountability, therefore, is not only interpretable in general, at the process level, but also applies to all specific data processing activities concerning the personal data of a specific data subject.  
(103) According to Article 5(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation, based on accountability, the data controller is obliged to document and record the data processing in such a way that its legality can be proven subsequently. An important requirement arising from the principle of accountability is that the documentation must be prepared concerning the data controller and its data processing; mere literal adoption of legal texts does not clarify why the specific data processing is necessary.  
(104) The Authority's position is that it is unacceptable for Client1 to state in their declaration that they based their data processing on the legitimate interest legal basis, yet did not conduct any interest balancing according to their declaration, and therefore could not demonstrate to the Authority the existence of their legitimate interest.  
(105) Based on the above, the Authority established that Client1, by failing to demonstrate the existence of their legitimate interest regarding the processing of the name and address data of the minor data subjects requested from the personal data and address registry, processed the requested personal data without a legal basis, thereby violating the principle of accountability under Article 5(2) of the GDPR and, in this regard, Article 6(1), as they processed the personal data of the minor data subjects unlawfully and without a valid legal basis.

3.3 Fairness of Data Processing  
(121) The description of the purpose must be made clearly, obviously, and in understandable language, so that the data subjects are aware of all essential circumstances of the data processing, the specific purposes, and the scope of the data corresponding to those purposes, as well as...

the processing of their personal data. This expectation placed on the data controller follows from the principle of transparency and fair data processing as set out in Article 5(1)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
Compliance with the purpose limitation principle consists of two main parts: on the one hand, it involves the selection of a clear and lawful purpose, and on the other hand, it requires the processing of personal data in a manner compatible with that purpose or purposes.

- A specifically defined purpose, declared before the commencement of data processing. In this case, this was completely absent during the postal inquiries made to the data subjects, and the information provided by Client1 in the inquiry letters was misleading and incorrect.

(159) In the absence of adequate information, the data subject is understandably not in a position to properly exercise their rights and to give informed consent to something they do not fully understand.  
(160) In the case of data processing based on Article 6(1)(a) of the GDPR, the data controller is obliged, according to Article 4(11) of the GDPR, to provide information prior to the commencement of data processing and before obtaining consent, which allows for informed consent to be given.  
(161) Consent from the data subject for data processing can only be valid if it is requested for specific purpose(s) – which can be specified separately for each purpose – and if adequate information is provided beforehand, placing the data subject in a position to make an informed decision regarding the granting of consent, and meeting all other validity requirements set forth in the GDPR. Article 12(1) of the GDPR explicitly imposes an obligation on the data controller to provide assistance to the data subject so that they can exercise all their rights in an informed manner.  
(162) The obligation to provide information, as elaborated above, does not merely represent a "paperwork" obligation under the GDPR. The purpose of the information is to place the data subject in a position to make appropriate decisions regarding the exercise of their rights. If this is not objectively achieved for the average data subjects, it will not comply with the GDPR.  
(163) Since Client1 obtained personal data related to newsletter subscriptions – the personal data provided on the designated platform during the subscriptions – from the data subjects, taking into account the provisions of Article 13 of the GDPR, the information regarding the processing of their personal data must be made available to the data subjects at the time of obtaining the personal data, in accordance with Article 13(1) and (2).  
(164) According to the Authority's findings, the information provided by Client1 regarding data processing is not clear to the data subjects; the information available on the newsletter subscription platform is not entirely consistent with the information contained in the data processing notice available during the examined data processing period:  
(i) The purpose of data processing indicated in the pre-formulated consent statement available on the subscription platform (marketing and direct business acquisition purposes) is not the same as the purpose of data processing specified in the data processing notice regarding information specifically related to newsletter sending, where the primary purpose indicated for sending newsletters is "facilitating communication," and additionally, the personalized provision of certain services and the transmission of new and current information are also indicated as purposes.  
(ii) The mandatory data to be provided on the subscription platform is not the same as the categories of processed data indicated in the data processing notice regarding newsletter sending; furthermore, based on the content of the database provided to the Authority during the fact-finding process, Client1 stores a much broader range of data types.  
(iii) In the data processing notice, Client1 also referred to the provisions of the Infotv. when specifying the legal basis for data processing, and the notice also referenced laws that are no longer in effect, such as the laws considered when processing users' data. Additionally, the address of the Authority in the data processing notice refers to a location that has been outdated for years, indicating that it has not been updated for years. The Authority has stated in numerous decisions and opinions that compliance with the GDPR is not a
34
not only at a specific point in time but rather a regularly recurring, continuous compliance obligation. The Authority further notes that in the current data protection authority procedure, the data protection notice modified by Client1 during the procedure still indicates the Authority's headquarters from years ago.  
(iv)  
The notice described the data processor used by Client1 during its data processing; however, since December 2020, Client1 has not been using the service of the company specified in the notice for newsletter sending and data storage, but rather the service of Client2.  
(165) According to the Authority's findings, the data processing notice published by Client1 during the examined data processing period is also not transparent. At the beginning of the notice, the data controller highlighted the legal basis for data processing, its purpose, and what data is processed, then after unnecessary definitions taken from legislation regarding the adequacy of the information, it again informed about the legal basis for data processing—misleading the data subjects by listing multiple legal bases—however, it could not be determined which data processing activity this information referred to, as the notice subsequently listed the various data processing activities it performed without an overview, highlighting the legal basis for data processing, its purpose, the scope of data processed in relation to it, and the duration of data processing. Therefore, data subjects could only find more specific information regarding data processing for a specific purpose when reviewing the complete, multi-page notice.  
(166) Considering that the legal basis chosen by Client1 was the consent of the data subject, it must be based on appropriate prior information, among other things, to be valid.  
(167) According to Article 12(1) of the GDPR, it was Client1's obligation to take appropriate measures to provide the data subject with all information regarding the processing of personal data mentioned in Articles 13 and 14, and each notification under Articles 15-22 and 34 in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, clearly and comprehensibly formulated in its information, which the data subjects intending to subscribe to the newsletter can access before subscribing.  
(168) In light of the above, the Authority established in the operative part that Client1 violated Article 12(1) of the GDPR and points (c) and (e) of Article 13(1) and point (d) of Article 13(2) as it did not provide transparent and clear, accurate information to the data subjects regarding the newsletter subscription and newsletter sending during the examined data processing period.  

3.5.3. Client1's data deletion practice in relation to newsletter sending  
(169) According to Client1's statement, based on the details outlined above, there is an option to subscribe to the newsletter. According to Client1's statement, the newsletter sending continues until the data subject unsubscribes. Each newsletter contains an unsubscribe link; to prove this, Client1 attached a text sample of each newsletter sent in the academic year, that is, samples of three newsletters.  
(170) The Authority, while analyzing the database saved by Client2 and made accessible to the Authority—as detailed in point I.4—found that its table named […] contains the data of subscribed readers, thus including the email address, the email of the subscriber at the time of subscription.
35  
the title, the dates, a unique session that served to identify the subscriber in encrypted hash format at the time of subscription, the subscriber's group to which the subscription was requested, their ID, as well as whether they are active or deleted.  
The "Active" column of the data table indicates the status of the subscribers, which can have 3 different values: "0", "1", and "2".  
According to the Authority's observation, the data of subscribers marked with "0" in the column, meaning inactive, is accessible in the database with the same content as that of active subscribers, meaning that the email addresses and IP addresses of these subscribers can also be found within the table.  
(171) Client1, in response to the Authority's related question regarding how it acts when a previously subscribed individual unsubscribes from the newsletter, or in the case of multiple unsuccessful email deliveries to a subscriber, confirmed in its statement that when a previously subscribed individual unsubscribes, the Webgalamb mailing system automatically deletes the subscriber immediately upon the unsubscribe click, such that they will not be aware of the identity of the unsubscribing individual. In the case of unsuccessful email delivery, the Webgalamb system indicates this fact, and this data is also permanently deleted and removed from the database.  
According to Client1's statement, the referenced data table's "Active" column indicates "0" for inactive subscribers, while "2" indicates those subscribers whose sent email bounced back due to some error. According to its statement, this data will be permanently deleted.  
(172) According to Article 17(1)(b) of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to request the controller to delete personal data concerning them without undue delay, and the controller is obliged to delete personal data concerning the data subject without undue delay if the data subject withdraws their consent on which the processing is based under Article 6(1)(a), meaning in this case unsubscribing from the newsletter.  
(173) According to the principle of accuracy set out in Article 5(1)(d) of the GDPR, the controller must take all reasonable measures to ensure that inaccurate personal data is erased or rectified without delay, in relation to the purposes of processing.  
(174) The Authority's position is that if during the newsletter sending Client1 notices that the newsletter sending was unsuccessful due to the unavailability of a certain email address, it must ensure the immediate deletion of both the previously subscribed individual's email address and the personal data processed by Client1 in connection with it.  
(175) Based on the analysis of the database and Client1's statement, the Authority finds that contrary to Client1's statement, the data with "0" and "2" active field values in the referenced data table is still accessible in the database table named […] which means that the data of the individuals marked as "inactive" with a "0" value and those related to bounced emails with a "2" value are all present in the database, with the email address stored for all of them, while the IP address at the time of subscription is also stored in the data table for all but 2 individuals.  
(176) Based on the above, Client1 continues to process the personal data of individuals who have withdrawn their subscriptions, despite the withdrawal of their consent, thereby violating Article 17(1)(b) of the GDPR.
**IV. Legal Consequences**

(177) The Authority, considering that Client1 continues to process the personal data of the individuals who have withdrawn their subscriptions in its database despite the withdrawal of their consent—i.e., after their unsubscription—and also continues to process the personal data of those individuals for whom the newsletter delivery was unsuccessful, instructed Client1, based on Article 58(2)(d) of the GDPR, to permanently delete the personal data of those individuals stored in its database who have unsubscribed from the newsletter, as well as those for whom the newsletter delivery was unsuccessful.

(178) Based on Section 75/A of the Infotv., the Authority exercises its powers as set out in Articles 83(2)-(6) of the GDPR, taking into account the principle of proportionality, particularly by addressing the first-time violation of the regulations regarding the processing of personal data—defined in legislation or in mandatory legal acts of the European Union—primarily by warning the data controller or data processor in accordance with Article 58 of the GDPR.

(179) In this context, the Authority considered all circumstances of the case based on Article 83(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation and Section 75/A of the Infotv. Given the circumstances of the case and the nature of the data processing, the Authority determined that a warning is not a proportional sanction for the violation uncovered during this procedure; therefore, the Authority decided to impose a fine, taking into account Section 61(1)(a) of the Infotv. in accordance with Article 58(2)(i) of the General Data Protection Regulation.

(180) In terms of the necessity and amount of the fine, the Authority took into account that the last published net global market turnover (net sales revenue) of the Obligor, according to the public e-reporting registry, was 55,839,000 HUF in 2022 (for 2021: 44,647,000 HUF; for 2020: 13,874,000 HUF). The possible maximum fine, based on Article 83(5)(a) and (b) of the General Data Protection Regulation, is 20 million EUR.

(181) The Authority considered the contents of the European Data Protection Board's Guideline 4/2022 (hereinafter: Guideline) regarding the determination of the amount of the fine, which contains the calculation criteria for administrative fines under the General Data Protection Regulation. In this regard, the determination of the fine amount was based on the following criteria:
(i) Client1's revenue in HUF falls within the revenue bracket below 2 million euros.
(ii) According to the Guideline, for violations categorized under Article 83(5) of the General Data Protection Regulation—considered relatively low in severity—the static maximum fine ranges between 0 and 8,000 EUR (approximately 3,000,000 HUF).
(iii) The fine of 500,000 HUF determined for Client1 represents approximately 0.895% of the annual global market turnover (revenue).

(182) In determining the amount of the data protection fine, the Authority considered the following mitigating circumstances:
(i) Client1 acted negligently regarding the established violation (Article 83(2)(b) of the GDPR).
(ii) The Authority has not previously established a relevant data protection violation against Client1 (Article 83(2)(e) of the GDPR).
