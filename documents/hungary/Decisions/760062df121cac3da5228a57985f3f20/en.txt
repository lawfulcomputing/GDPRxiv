H A T A R O Z A T  

The National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Authority) […] represented by its legal representative (address: […]; hereinafter: Representative) regarding the minor child, […] (date and place of birth: […]; mother’s name: […]; hereinafter: Data Subject) submitted an application to the Authority on May 16, 2023, against […] Bank Zrt. (registered office: […]; company registration number: […]; hereinafter: Applicant) concerning the unlawful processing of the Data Subject's personal data, the Authority makes the following decisions:  

I.  
The Authority rejects the Data Subject's request for the Authority to determine that the Applicant unlawfully processed their personal data.  

II.  
The Authority rejects the Data Subject's request for the Authority to instruct the Applicant to delete the unlawfully processed personal data.  

III.  
The Authority rejects the Data Subject's request for the Authority to instruct the Applicant to provide the Representative with copies of documents unlawfully processed concerning the Data Subject.  

There is no administrative remedy against this decision, but it can be challenged in an administrative lawsuit filed with the Metropolitan Court within 30 days from the notification. The lawsuit must be submitted electronically to the Authority, which will forward it along with the case documents to the court.1 A request for a hearing must be indicated in the lawsuit. For those not fully exempt from personal fees, the administrative lawsuit fee is HUF 30,000, and the case is subject to the fee registration right. Legal representation is mandatory in proceedings before the Metropolitan Court.

II. Facts
(6) The Respondent initially stated, in general, regarding the banks' obligation to copy documents, that the supervisory bodies overseeing banks, such as the Hungarian National Bank (hereinafter: MNB) or the Hungarian State Treasury (hereinafter: Treasury), formulate expectations for banks that make it unavoidable for banks to be able to substantiate with documents and records in every case that they comply with the relevant legal provisions during their financial service activities, such as loan granting or the evaluation of support related to state subsidies.

(7) The Respondent further stated that in accordance with the relevant legal regulations and the MNB's relevant recommendations, the bank branch employees conducting sales cannot be placed in a position to make lending-related or credit evaluation decisions. In order to ensure prudent and transparent operation of banks, the protection of depositors' money, and the preservation of the stability of the banking sector, banks cannot establish internal processes that result in commission-incentivized bank branch clerks making credit evaluation decisions, meaning that the bank branch clerks receiving the verifications are not authorized to make decisions related to the suspension of repayment or the awarding of child-rearing support. To ensure prudent and proper banking operations, situations must also be avoided where bank branch employees may inadvertently handle data.
3

In connection with misinterpretation and concealment, the credit assessment or the disbursement of the loan or state aid may be influenced or unlawfully ensured. Based on all of this, the high level of documentation requirements arising in the banking sector, which is expected by supervisory authorities from banks, stems from the fundamental principle that the prudent operation of banks and the possibility of internal abuses must be avoided in order to maintain the stability of the banking sector.  
(8)  
The Applicant also deemed it necessary to state that banks are not entitled to override or supplement legal provisions regarding loans related to state aid. Banks may decide whether to provide a particular state aid or not, but if they choose to provide the given state aid, they cannot deviate from the legislator's regulation, even if the provision concerning the relevant legal provision is unintelligible, inapplicable, or inappropriate. Therefore, the Bank, even if a particular legal provision is difficult or impossible to interpret from a data protection perspective, still maintains the application of the relevant laws, such as the concepts or solutions defined in the Babaváró Act.

IV. Decision  

IV. The legality of the data processing requested  

(33)  
The Babaváró Regulation names several forms of support. The supported couples are entitled, on the one hand, to interest support regarding the loan taken – which interest support can be lost under various conditions, and a reimbursement obligation arises regarding the interest support already disbursed by the Treasury – and, on the other hand, they are entitled to suspend repayment after the 12th week of pregnancy, including the period following childbirth, or if they jointly adopt a child. The supported individuals are entitled to a second,
7

In the case of having a second or third child, individuals are entitled to non-repayable childbearing support, the amount of which is 30% of the outstanding debt from the baby waiting loan for the second child, and for the third child, it corresponds to the total remaining loan debt. Childbearing support can be requested similarly to the suspension of repayment after the 12th week of pregnancy, including the period following childbirth, or in the case of adoption.  
(34)  
In the specific case, the Representative and his wife requested the suspension of repayment from the Applicant on […] 2022, considering that the 12th week of pregnancy had been completed, and then on […] 2023, they confirmed to the Applicant that their child, the Affected, had been born, which obligation was prescribed by Section 22 (2) of the Baby Waiting Regulation. Regarding the suspension of repayment, Section 14 (4) of the Baby Waiting Regulation states that the request for suspension must be submitted to the financial institution. The application must be accompanied by the documents specified in Section 9 (2) points b) and c). The financial institution determines the fulfillment of the eligibility criteria for suspension based on the submitted certificates.  

(35)  
Section 14 (4) of the Baby Waiting Regulation thus refers back to the documents specified in Section 9 (2) points b) and c), which reference is a general legislative drafting technique aimed at avoiding unnecessary repetitions. That is, for the purpose of the request for suspension of repayment, only the part of Section 9 (2) points b) and c) that names the documents to be submitted is significant, not the fact that Section 9 (2) points b) and c) aim to trigger other legal effects.  

(36)  
Consequently, it is of no significance whether the Representative and his wife applied for, and if so, where, family support benefits; this does not affect the scope of documents to be submitted to support the request for suspension of repayment, which is defined by Section 14 (4) with reference to Section 9 (2) points b) and c), which for biological children are as follows: pregnancy care book or a document issued by the treating physician containing the information specified in Annex 2 of the Baby Waiting Regulation; birth certificate of the biological child, official identification proving their address, and tax identification.  

(37)  
Furthermore, Section 5 (2) of the Baby Waiting Regulation explicitly authorizes financial institutions to manage the names, birth names, names of their mothers, places and times of birth, citizenship or stateless status, addresses, and tax identification numbers of children born after the loan application is submitted by the supported couples for the purpose of evaluating the loan application, concluding the loan agreement, disbursing the loan, and verifying lawful utilization. The documents copied by the Applicant contain only this data, and the copies serve to verify the authenticity and correctness of this data. The Representative only claimed in his submission that the page containing the personal identification number of the Affected's official identification proving their address was also copied by the Applicant, but he could not substantiate this with anything, thus the Authority establishes based on the documents provided by the Applicant that the personal identification number of the Affected was not managed by the Applicant, and no copy was made of it by the Applicant.  

(38)  
Section 14 (4) of the Baby Waiting Regulation explicitly emphasizes that the financial institution determines the fulfillment of the eligibility criteria for the suspension of repayment based on the submitted certificates, meaning that in the absence of these documents, financial institutions must reject requests for suspension of repayment.  

(39)  
It should also be noted that the Applicant, as well as all financial institutions disbursing baby waiting loans, are in an accounting relationship with the Treasury, and Section 23 (8) of the Baby Waiting Regulation...
8

According to paragraph 8, the Treasury may verify the activities related to the baby waiting loan and is entitled to request the necessary information and documents or to inspect them on-site. Furthermore, according to Section 24 (1) of the Baby Waiting Regulation, the credit institution is responsible to the state for any damage arising from a breach of obligations imposed on it under this regulation. This means that the Applicant must manage the documents and records specified in the Baby Waiting Regulation to ensure compliance with the laws and to prove compliance. Thus, based on the above, it is not sufficient for the credit institution to merely present the documents specified in Section 9 (2) b) and c) of the Baby Waiting Regulation to prove eligibility for support.

(40) 

In light of all this, the Authority establishes that the Applicant lawfully prepared copies of the documents containing the data of the Affected Party as per Section 14 (4) and Section 9 (2) b) and c) of the Baby Waiting Regulation, namely the part of the official identification document of the Affected Party that contains the address, their tax identification card, and their birth certificate, and that the personal data contained therein is managed lawfully. The management of the personal data on the document copies by the Applicant is necessary and proportionate to the intended purpose, as the Applicant determines the fulfillment of the eligibility criteria for the suspension based on the submitted certifications; without these, the support applications could not be assessed. Consequently, the Authority concludes that the Applicant lawfully manages the personal data of the Affected Party in accordance with the principle of data minimization under Article 5 (1) c) of the GDPR, and therefore rejects the requests submitted by the Representative on behalf of the Affected Party.

(41) 

The Authority further notes that the Applicant designated Article 6 (1) b) of the GDPR as the legal basis for the management of the personal data of the Affected Party, which states that the processing of personal data is lawful to the extent that it is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for taking steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.

(42) 

However, the Affected Party is not a party to the contract, nor do any rights or obligations arise from the contract for them; the contracting parties concerning the baby waiting loan are the parents of the Affected Party and the Applicant, thus Article 6 (1) b) of the GDPR can only be considered an appropriate legal basis in relation to the supported parties as contracting parties.

(43) 

The processing of personal data of children—whether they are biological or adopted children of the supported parties—occurs so that the parents, the supported parties can prove that they have fulfilled the conditions for the support received from the state in parallel with the baby waiting loan, and that this fulfillment can subsequently be verified by the credit institution to the Treasury. The obligation of the parents to prove compliance with the eligibility criteria for support to the credit institution arises with the conclusion of the baby waiting contract. Although the supported parties must prove compliance to the credit institution, this is significant in terms of the support provided by the state; the fulfillment or non-fulfillment of the conditions for support does not affect the existence of the loan agreement. Conversely, the verification of the fulfillment of the conditions for support is a legal obligation imposed on credit institutions by law, for which they bear liability for damages to the Treasury. Based on all this, it can be stated that the processing of personal data of children occurs based on the legal obligation of credit institutions, and the appropriate legal basis for the processing of their personal data is Article 6 (1) c) of the GDPR.

(44) 

Nevertheless, in the specific case, the fact that the Applicant referred to an inappropriate legal basis under Article 6 (1) of the GDPR during the processing of the Affected Party's data is not significant in terms of the legality and necessity of the data processing. On one hand, the Representative did not dispute the legal basis for the processing of the Affected Party's personal data, only its necessity.
9

On the other hand, the Applicant has an appropriate legal basis for the processing of personal data, regardless of the fact that it indicated an inappropriate point of Article 6(1) of the GDPR as the legal basis for data processing in its statement addressed to the Authority, since according to Article 6(1) of the GDPR, the processing of personal data is lawful to the extent that one of the conditions set out in points a) to f) is met.
