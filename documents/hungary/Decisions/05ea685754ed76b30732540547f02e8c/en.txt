**EXPLANATION**

I.  
Background and Clarification of Facts

1. In this decision, under the designation Client or Obligor, the Authority refers to eKRÉTA Informatikai Zrt. for the factual elements prior to April 20, 2023, and subsequently to Educational Development Informatikai Zrt. Apart from the name change, the other data of the Obligor (headquarters, company registration number, tax number) have remained unchanged according to the stored company extract.

2. The Authority became aware on November 7, 2022, from an article published on an internet news portal – available at https://telex.hu/tech/2022/11/07/kreta-rendszer-e-naplo-kozoktatas-adathalasz-tamadas-adatszivargas-ekreta-informatikai-zrt since 10:30 on November 7, 2022 – regarding an IT attack and data protection incident affecting eKRÉTA Informatikai Zrt. Following this, due to the content of the news and the fact that it had not received a data protection incident report from the Client prior to the publication, on November 8, 2022, it initiated an official inspection regarding the fulfillment of obligations by the Client under Articles 32-34 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

3. The linked article contains several statements, including the following:

4. "A phishing attack occurred a few weeks ago against the developer company of the Public Education Registration and Study System, i.e., eKRÉTA Informatikai Zrt. The attack was successful, allowing the attacker unauthorized access to the data of the administrative system used in all public education institutions – Telex learned from an independent source. We understand that all data of every student managed in KRÉTA may have been leaked, but not only to this data, but also to other databases of the company, the source codes, and the internal communications of the developers. Our information has been anonymously confirmed from within eKRÉTA Zrt.; according to our source with insight into the development, a phishing attack indeed occurred: a project manager clicked on an infected link in a scam email, and by obtaining their data, access was gained to internal databases, effectively reaching everything within the company's systems."

100. As seen in the above paragraph, the General Data Protection Regulation does not differentiate between data controllers and data processors concerning compliance with data security requirements; the referenced provisions apply equally to data processors as they do to data controllers.

101. The evaluation by the Authority of the data security settings applied by the Obligor and the incident management measures of the Obligor is crucial, as the KRÉTA system's live and test databases, to which the affected user also had access, contain the personal data of all students, educators, and guardians (see the tables in paragraphs 59-60). Among these, there are several pieces of information whose disclosure could particularly adversely affect the data subject, e.g., whether the student is struggling with integration, learning, behavioral, or educational difficulties; whether they have special educational needs; whether they are state care recipients; whether they receive social support or regular child protection benefits. Although not a mandatory element, the student's religion may also appear in the KRÉTA system. Furthermore, in the KRÉTA system, personal data is stored in large quantities and extends to a significant number of data subjects: approximately 225,000 employees, 1.5 million students, and 1.87 million guardians' personal data can be found in the system. In terms of the quantity of personal data, this amounts to approximately 6.5 million for employees, approximately 47 million for students, and approximately 7.5 million for guardians. Therefore, it is of immense importance how the Obligor's staff, as developers, access the development environment and the live and test system databases, as they can reach them at the service level.

102. Based on the established facts, the affected user also performed support activities, and consequently had all the necessary permissions to access the live and test KRÉTA systems and their databases. The Obligor informed the Authority that the affected user had permanent access to the email system, an internal communication-supporting chat system, a separate system storing the source codes of the system, and the support system, and temporarily accessed personal data in the live KRÉTA system in connection with performing support tasks.

105. As soon as the Obligor became aware of the attack in September 2022, it immediately replaced the affected user's machine and deactivated both the original user account and the various permissions, then created a new user account for them. At the same time, the affected user synchronized the login data (usernames and passwords) for both their old and new accounts to a password manager provided as a service by Google Ireland Ltd. (hereinafter: Google), except – according to their statement – for the login data required for NISZ access. The Google password manager works in such a way that the user can save the usernames and passwords necessary to access applications and systems available on other websites in their own Google account. If unauthorized individuals gain access to a user's Google account that stores their passwords in this manner, they can discover all usernames and passwords stored there. Through the synchronization provided by the password manager, if the user enters a different, new password for another website or system compared to what has already been saved, the Google password manager can store this, effectively overwriting the old password with the new one. The attackers could still access the affected user's Google account through an open session, thereby obtaining the passwords for all systems belonging to the Obligor that were also stored in the password manager. The use of the Google password manager thus posed an unmanaged risk to the security of the data stored in the KRÉTA system. The attackers could have already logged into the affected user's Google account before they received a new user account and password for the KRÉTA system, and could have remained logged in thereafter, thanks to the continuous open connection (session) between Google's server and the attackers' computer. Consequently, merely creating a new user account for the KRÉTA system in this case could not have been a sufficient step, as its password was synchronized by the Google password manager following the first successful login by the affected user. According to the Authority's position, for an employee performing such activities, both the synchronization of passwords with Google and the fact that the session is not automatically logged out constitute serious data security flaws, which unacceptably increased the system's exposure, as there are password management software solutions installed only on the specific computer that can securely store users' passwords, like a safe. The passwords stored in these software solutions are generally only accessible through the user's two-factor authentication or extremely strong (i.e., sufficiently long,
22

password (which requires the inclusion of numbers, both lowercase and uppercase letters, as well as special characters) is accessible.

106. Therefore, the attacker - as acknowledged by the Obligor - gained access to the affected user's permissions, and consequently, it can be established that the attacker could also access the data stored in the live KRÉTA system. The fact that the attacker specifically became aware of the personal data stored in the live KRÉTA system could not be proven during the proceedings, considering that if the attacker logged into the live KRÉTA system, they did so with valid permissions, those of the affected user, thus the actions of the attacker cannot be separated from those of the affected user based solely on browsing the log files. It can, however, be treated as a fact that the attacker had the opportunity to access the data stored in the live KRÉTA system. The Obligor did not rule out this possibility in their responses. It is worth mentioning the information cited by the Obligor (see paragraph 46), stating that an external state-owned contractor is conducting an investigation for the Obligor until the end of December 2023, which includes a search for data related to KRÉTA system users on the so-called dark web. In this regard, the Authority emphasizes that even if this search were to conclude that the personal data of KRÉTA system users is not found on the dark web, it would not serve as evidence that the attackers did not extract personal data from the KRÉTA system, as it is not a given that attackers will later publish any information they may have obtained during the attack.

107. A key element of the facts is that the Obligor only set up two-factor authentication for accessing the live KRÉTA system on November 10, 2022, as mentioned in paragraph 32 of this decision.

Two-factor authentication (2FA) is a security method for identity and access management that requires two forms of identification to access resources and data. Two-factor authentication enables businesses to monitor and protect their most vulnerable information and networks. Due to the lack of two-factor authentication, if a username and password pair is known by an unauthorized person, such unauthorized individuals can successfully log into certain systems. Therefore, it is generally considered good practice to use a second factor in addition to the password as a "factor" – for example, sending a one-time code via SMS or using a hardware identifier (so-called token). While a password can be "guessed" with enough attempts, meaning it can be reverse-engineered, a short-lived code or a hardware token possessed only by the authorized person cannot.

108. Due to the obligation to ensure a level of data security commensurate with the degree of risk, the requirement for data security must be strictly enforced in a system such as the Obligor's development environment, since – although personal data from the live KRÉTA system is also stored there in connection with support activities, and not in small numbers – the database of the live KRÉTA system can be easily accessed from there for task performance, which contains a large amount of personal data concerning a significant number of affected individuals, as detailed above.

109. The fact that the incident in question occurred, and that the Obligor did not become aware of its severity on their own but only through the attacker's message, more than a month and a half after the security breach, is significant evidence that the general data protection regulation's data security requirements were not adequately met by the Obligor.

110. During the investigation of the phishing attack detected in mid-September 2022, the Authority's position is that the Obligor - immediately following the detection of the attack in September 2022 -
23

the comprehensive assessment of information security risks related to the affected employee was not conducted, and the response measures were almost exhausted in merely deleting the specific user account and suspending the affected user's permissions. If this investigation had been carried out with due diligence—especially considering the quantity and sensitivity of personal data stored in the live KRÉTA system—the Obligor should have monitored at least the network traffic related to the affected user for a prolonged period, following information security best practices, and should have examined, with the involvement of the given user, which traffic could be linked to their activities and which belonged to unknown—thus presumably unauthorized—users. This would have been necessary also because the Obligor did not have sufficient information regarding whether unauthorized access had occurred, thus a thorough investigation of the causes and potential consequences of the event would have been expected from them. Had this been done, the Obligor would likely have timely detected that unauthorized individuals were present in their systems, as well as precisely what they had access to, and thus could have likely prevented further escalation of the situation.

111. The above clearly highlights a key element of the facts, namely why it would have been of paramount importance and could have prevented the incident if access to the systems used in the Obligor's IT development environment—at least those containing personal data—had required two-factor authentication even at the time of the attack, and that the Obligor should not have only implemented two-factor authentication for accessing the live KRÉTA system in November 2022.

112. It can also be established that the logging conducted by the Obligor regarding the activities of its employees proved to be insufficient for the investigation of the incident, as—just as occurred in this case—if an attacker gained access to an employee's profile, from that point on the attacker could do anything, including extracting all data (including the data in the live KRÉTA system in this case); the attacker's activities could no longer be separated from the activities of the owner of the hacked profile. This represents a serious data security deficiency, as such logging makes it impossible to comply with the requirements set forth in Article 32(1)(b) and Article 32(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation. The Authority reiterates that the Obligor did not present any information during the proceedings that would have had probative value regarding whether data was not extracted from the live KRÉTA system during the attack.

113. Consequently, it can be clearly established that a minimum requirement for the development of a KRÉTA-level system is two-factor authentication, as well as the existence of sufficiently detailed logging for the investigation of any potential security incident. The Authority is not concerned with the exact records contained in the log files conducted by the Obligor, but rather whether the security event can be sufficiently uncovered based on these records. The answer to this latter question is negative in the present case, and two-factor authentication was only implemented after the attack, which significantly contributed to the success of the attack, as well as to the Obligor's inability to timely recognize the fact and extent of unauthorized access, and thus to timely initiate the implementation of risk-reducing measures.

114. The Authority firmly disagrees with the Obligor's position that the incident was merely due to the negligence of one of its employees, and therefore cannot be traced back to serious data security issues. Although the direct trigger of the incident was indeed employee error, with appropriate data security measures, this would not have led to the consequences described in the case. The Obligor's own position contradicts this.
24

that following the awareness of the incident in November 2022, a significant number of (nearly twenty!) system-level changes were implemented, which are of fundamental importance and are expected according to the state of technology (see paragraph 76), whereas if it truly believed that an employee's negligence was merely the trigger of the incident, there would have been no reason for this. Although increasing user awareness is indeed expected from a company of the size of the Obligor, since no business can rely on the assumption that its employees 'will not make mistakes', this only constitutes a small, albeit important, slice of potential data security measures; other data security measures are also necessary, as demonstrated by the measures subsequently taken by the Obligor, which are detailed in paragraph 76.

115. In light of the above, the Authority finds that the Obligor has failed to comply with its obligations under Article 32(1)(b) and Article 32(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation by not adequately considering the risks arising from data processing that could result in the unauthorized disclosure of personal data or unauthorized access to such data during the data security settings of its IT development environment, and as a result, it did not guarantee the continuous confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of the systems and services used for the processing of personal data.

Handling, Risk Classification, and Reporting of the Data Protection Incident

116. According to Article 4(12) of the General Data Protection Regulation, a "data protection incident" is defined as a security breach that results in the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to personal data that is transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed.

117. In the investigation of a data protection incident, the evaluation of the steps taken during the handling of the incident is inseparable from the data security settings of the data controller/data processor's systems and the specific characteristics of the data security environment. Since the incident in question would not have occurred with an adequate data security background, the following findings should be interpreted in light of the Authority's earlier statements regarding data security.

118. The Authority primarily assesses the severity of data protection incidents based on the level of risk posed by the security breach to the rights and freedoms of the affected natural persons. Overall, in line with the previously stated points, it can be concluded that a business developing a system like the KRÉTA system (which contains personal data of millions of natural persons, amounting to tens of millions of data entries, see paragraph 61) and having access to the data stored therein, as described in paragraphs 59-60, must take and investigate even the slightest indication of a security breach as seriously as possible, especially when the possibility/chance of an attack arises in relation to a profile that can also be accessed through the live KRÉTA system.

119. According to Article 32(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the data controller and the data processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of data security appropriate to the risk, taking into account the state of science and technology and the costs of implementation, as well as the nature, scope, circumstances, and purposes of the data processing, and the varying likelihood and severity of the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.
25 

120. The lack of two-factor authentication and the deficiencies in logging performed by the Obligor, as detailed above, are in no way consistent with the state of science and technology in the development environment. The amount of personal data stored in the system developed by the Obligor, and in some cases saved during support activities, clearly defines the nature and scope of data processing. In the case of developing such a system, it is a minimum expectation that all reasonably available data security measures in accordance with the latest state of science and technology must be implemented. The data security measures taken by the Obligor following the occurrence of the incident, as detailed in paragraph 76, were deemed adequate by the Authority; however, this only supports the fact that the Obligor is aware of the level and nature of data security measures that would be expected for a system like the one it is developing, yet this incident had to occur for these measures to be realized.

121. Based on the personal data to which access occurred or could have occurred by the attacker, the data protection incident at the Obligor can be interpreted on two levels according to the Authority's position:

122. The first level of the incident: The attack demonstrably affected the personal data that the affected user saved on their machine in connection with support activities, as well as the data of the Obligor's employees (see paragraphs 45-46). In this case, due to the high number of affected individuals and the large amount of affected personal data, a high-risk data protection incident has demonstrably occurred.

123. The second level of the incident: Potential access to personal data in the live KRÉTA system by the attacker. In this regard, the above section of the decision analyzing the Obligor's data security settings contained findings, but the Authority wishes to emphasize again that the Obligor did not present any information during the proceedings that would unequivocally prove that the attacker did not extract personal data from the live KRÉTA system. According to the Authority's position, based on the information uncovered during the proceedings, it can be reasonably concluded that personal data stored in the live KRÉTA system was also accessed during the attack. Therefore, the occurrence of a high-risk data protection incident in this case is likely.

124. In its response, the Obligor stated that the attack against it likely occurred on September 15, 2022, when the affected employee opened the infected element, a phishing message. Based on the details in paragraph 40, on September 19, 2022, the affected user reported the problem to their superior and the operations group leader; therefore, the Authority considers this day as the date of becoming aware of the data protection incident. Although the Obligor was unable to assess the actual extent and consequences of the attack at that time, it was already aware that it had fallen victim to a phishing attack, and thus, with due diligence, it should have at least presumed the possibility of the consequences mentioned in the previous two paragraphs. The Authority cannot evaluate in favor of the Obligor that it did not make this presumption due to its own negligence and was unable to conduct an internal investigation that could have confirmed the extent of the attack as early as September 2022, which, in fact, was not a difficult-to-detect, sophisticated attack due to being a phishing attack.

125. Since, according to the Authority's assessment, the Obligor became aware of at least the data protection incident mentioned in paragraph 122 on September 19, 2022, it was obliged, under Article 33(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation, to report it to the data controllers without undue delay following its awareness, meaning in this case to the 12.
26

institution (see paragraph 45), to which personal data had been saved in the context of support activities on the affected user's machine.

126. Summarizing the circumstances of the incident mentioned above, it is demonstrable that, for interconnected reasons, a high-risk incident occurred on the part of the Obligor, and it is likely that an exceptionally high-risk incident also occurred. Although the assessment of the risks of data protection incidents is typically the responsibility of the data controller, in this case, due to the nature of the KRÉTA system and the development environment, the Obligor, as the data processor, was only in a position to assess the risks of the incident with knowledge of all its circumstances.

127. In light of the above, the Authority finds that the Obligor violated Article 33(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation, as it failed to report a data protection incident, in connection with which  
a) the fact of the attack (the affected user clicked on the link in the phishing message) was already known to the Obligor on September 19, 2022,  
b) regarding the success of the attack and its consequences - based on the nature and scope of the system - it could have reasonably ensured through appropriate investigative measures that it was aware of the situation without undue delay.

The applied sanction and its justification

128. During the clarification of the facts, the Authority established that the Obligor, in the course of its data processing,  
- violated Article 32(1)(b) and Article 32(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation,  
- violated Article 33(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation.

129. The Authority examined whether it was justified to impose a data protection fine on the Obligor. In this regard, the Authority considered all circumstances of the case based on Article 83(2) of the GDPR and Section 75/A of the Infotv.

130. In this regard, the Authority decided, based on Section 61(1)(a) of the Infotv., as stated in the operative part, and also obliged the Obligor to pay a data protection fine in this decision.

131. In imposing the fine, the Authority took the following factors into account:

132. The Authority considered the following as aggravating circumstances:

133. The processing of data affected by the incident carries an exceptionally high risk due to the quantity and nature of the data (see paragraphs 59-61), therefore the Obligor should have acted with increased caution to ensure a level of data security commensurate with the degree of risk. Nevertheless, the Obligor did not take appropriate measures prior to the incident to ensure the continuous confidentiality of the system used for processing a large amount of personal data affecting a significant number of data subjects. The violation would not have occurred at all, or could have been remedied as early as September 2022, had the expected data security measures been in place. However, based on the established facts, the recognition of the severity of the incident and the actual extent of the data security deficiencies only occurred in November 2022 (Article 83(2)(a) and (g) of the General Data Protection Regulation).
27

134. The Authority established, based on the facts revealed, that the liable party's attributable negligent behavior led to the aforementioned violations. The liable party should have anticipated external attacks due to the recognition of the system it developed and the volume of personal data stored within it. The obligation to implement the technical and organizational measures mentioned in Article 32 of the General Data Protection Regulation was also defined to prevent the success of IT attacks. However, the fact that the measures described in paragraph 76 were only introduced after the incident occurred indicates that the liable party recklessly relied on the absence of attacks and applied data security measures that were inadequate for eliminating and detecting unauthorized access in a fundamentally high-risk data processing context, disproportionate to the risks involved. The security preparedness for the processing of personal data of the category and quantity mentioned in paragraphs 59-61 is expected to be heightened from the liable party, as a profit-oriented enterprise (Article 83(2)(b), (c), and (d) of the General Data Protection Regulation).

135. The liable party has no further influence over what the attacker does with the personal data acquired during the attack, and this circumstance gives rise to a significant degree of uncertainty regarding the further fate of the personal data (Article 83(2)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation).

136. The Authority became aware of the data protection incident not through the liable party but based on news reports published in the media (Article 83(2)(h) of the General Data Protection Regulation).

137. The Authority took the following mitigating circumstance into account:

138. The Authority considered that no similar violation regarding the processing of personal data had previously been established against the liable party, nor had any of the measures mentioned in Article 58(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation been ordered against it in the same matter (Article 83(2)(e) and (i) of the General Data Protection Regulation).

139. Other circumstances:

140. The Authority also noted that the liable party cooperated with the Authority during the investigation of the case, although this behavior was not specifically evaluated as a mitigating circumstance since it did not exceed compliance with legal obligations (Article 83(2)(f) of the General Data Protection Regulation).

141. Article 83(2)(j) of the General Data Protection Regulation was not relevant in this case.

142. In light of the above, the Authority deemed it necessary to impose a fine, considering that merely applying a warning under Section 75/A of the Infotv. was not deemed appropriate.

143. The amount of the data protection fine was determined by the Authority exercising its discretion based on legal provisions.

144. The violations committed by the liable party are classified as violations falling under the penalty category specified in Article 83(4)(a) of the General Data Protection Regulation.

145. In imposing the fine, the Authority also considered the economic weight of the liable party. In this context, it took into account that, according to the liable party's report, between January 1, 2022, and December 2022...

146. Considering the severity of the infringement and the above economic data of the Obligor, the amount of the imposed fine is deemed proportionate to the severity of the infringement according to the Authority's assessment.
