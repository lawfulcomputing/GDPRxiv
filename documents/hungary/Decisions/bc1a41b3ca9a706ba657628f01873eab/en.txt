The National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Authority) […] (address: […] ; hereinafter: Applicant) has received an application on July 25, 2022, regarding the examination of data processing related to cameras installed by […] (address: […] ; hereinafter: Respondent). The Authority makes the following decisions in the data protection authority procedure initiated:  

1. The Authority partially accepts the Applicant's request and establishes that the Respondent has violated Article 6(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (hereinafter: General Data Protection Regulation) concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, as well as the repeal of Directive 95/46/EC.

IV. Decision

IV.1. The identity of the data controller

According to Article 4(7) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the data controller is the natural or legal person […], which determines the purposes and means of processing personal data, either alone or jointly with others […]. Based on the facts established in this case, the Applicant decided on the data processing, the installation of the cameras, and the purpose of operating the cameras, thus he is considered the data controller in terms of camera data processing.

IV.2. The legality of camera data processing

IV.2.1. The distinction between household and non-household data processing

According to Article 4(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the image of the data subject is considered personal data. A data subject is an identified or identifiable natural person. In this context, if a natural person can be identified from a recording, then the image captured is considered personal data, and the act of capturing the image is considered data processing.

The use of a camera—based on its placement and the adjustment of its field of view—may be suitable for monitoring other private areas or public spaces, capturing recordings related to other properties, which may infringe upon the rights of the individuals observed by the camera, as well as their privacy.

According to Article 2(2)(c) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the regulation does not apply, and thus the rules of the General Data Protection Regulation do not need to be applied to the processing of personal data if it is carried out by natural persons solely for personal or household activities (so-called private or household data processing). The General Data Protection Regulation (18) provides examples of personal or household activities, such as correspondence, address book storage, communication on social networks conducted within the framework of personal and household activities, and other online activities. However, it is important to emphasize that—as established by the Court of Justice of the European Union in the so-called Rynes judgment—the exception rule regarding private data processing must be interpreted narrowly. According to this ruling, camera surveillance—inasmuch as it extends to individuals located outside the data controller's private property—does not fall under the aforementioned exception.

This practice has been maintained by the European Data Protection Board in its Guidelines 3/2019 on the processing of personal data through video devices (hereinafter: guidelines). The guidelines state that, in general, the use of a camera surveillance system designed for monitoring one's own property may extend to the boundaries of that property, but it acknowledges that in exceptional cases, there may be situations where the scope of camera surveillance cannot be limited to within one's own property, as this would not provide sufficiently effective protection. Appropriate technical or organizational measures (for example, masking areas that are not relevant to the purpose of the surveillance or...
8  
In addition to the application of filtering the monitored area with IT tools, the individual is entitled to extend the camera surveillance to the immediate vicinity of their own property.  

However, if the individual does not apply solutions that obscure public areas or other private areas within the camera's field of view, or if they operate a camera system that specifically monitors public areas or other private areas, they will become a data controller, and their activities will not be considered private data processing. Therefore, they must comply with all provisions set forth for data controllers in the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). By applying masking, the area monitored by the camera can be narrowed down to the area owned by the data controller. However, if the camera's field of view extends beyond the private sphere of the person conducting data processing with the camera surveillance system—such as to public areas, areas owned in common by a condominium, or areas owned by other third parties—it cannot be considered as a "personal or home" activity falling under the above exception. Consequently, the data processing activity in question falls under the scope of the GDPR, which means that, among other things, the principles set out in Article 5 of the GDPR must be adhered to, and a valid legal basis as required by Article 6 of the GDPR is necessary.  

In the present case, according to the Applicant, both cameras operated by the Respondent overlook the Applicant's property and public areas.  

However, according to the submitted snapshots from August 28, 2022, which show the cameras' field of view, neither camera overlooks public areas. The submitted log files, as well as the logs from events in September 2022, do not contain information indicating that there was any change in the camera settings that would have modified the cameras' field of view at the time of the Applicant's request submission—July 25, 2022.  

According to the submitted snapshots, the camera marked "gate" exclusively monitors the Respondent's internal courtyard, applying masking techniques, so that the public area and the Applicant's private area do not fall within the camera's field of view; thus, the camera surveillance and the related data processing do not extend beyond monitoring the Respondent's own property.  

Consequently, the Authority does not establish unlawful data processing in connection with the camera marked "gate."  

However, the camera marked "small gate" and the related data processing are subject to a different assessment, which the Authority analyzes in point IV.2.2 of this decision.  

IV.2.2. The camera monitoring the Applicant and their property  

According to the snapshot available to the Authority, the camera marked "small gate" overlooks the Respondent's internal courtyard in such a way that it is masked so as not to see public areas and not to monitor the Applicant's property either. The Applicant's statement is consistent with this, as they confirmed that the camera marked "small gate"—among other things—sees the wire fence separating the two plots, which currently has a privacy screen placed on it. In this regard, the Applicant also indicated that it is possible to see through the screen, in their opinion, even with a camera.  

The Authority, upon reviewing the snapshot taken by the camera marked "small gate" submitted by the Respondent, established that there is indeed a privacy screen placed on the wire fence separating the two plots. Nevertheless, as confirmed by the snapshot, it is indeed possible to see through the screen, thus the camera does indeed capture individuals appearing beyond the fence in such a way that they can be identified by the Respondent as a neighbor and as a data controller based on their movement and shape. Therefore, the Respondent does not provide a level of obscuring that would limit the scope of the camera surveillance to the property that is exclusively owned by them.
9

Consequently, it can be established that the field of view of the camera marked as "loophole" extends beyond the private sphere of the Respondent to an area owned by a third party, namely the Applicant, and therefore the data processing cannot be considered as private data processing. For this reason, the data processing activities related to the camera marked as "loophole" fall under the scope of the General Data Protection Regulation, which means that, among other things, the principles set out in Article 5 of the General Data Protection Regulation must be adhered to, and a valid legal basis as per Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation is required.

Although the camera may potentially be suitable for achieving the personal and property protection objectives indicated by the Respondent, the surveillance of the Applicant and their family, resulting in constant data processing that extends to everyone without limitation, cannot be proportionate to the interest in protecting the personal data of the Applicant. Thus, the Respondent has violated Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation by monitoring the Applicant and their property, as the personal data of the Applicant was processed without a valid legal basis.

The amount of the administrative litigation fee is determined by Section 45/A(1) of Act XCIII of 1990 on Fees (hereinafter: Itv.). The party initiating the procedure is exempt from the prior payment of the fee under Section 59(1) and Section 62(1)(h) of the Itv.

According to Section 132 of the Ákr., if the Respondent fails to comply with the obligation set forth in the Authority's final decision, it is enforceable. The Authority's decision becomes final upon communication in accordance with Section 82(1) of the Ákr. According to Section 133 of the Ákr., enforcement is ordered by the authority that made the decision unless otherwise provided by law or government decree. According to Section 134 of the Ákr., enforcement is carried out by the state tax authority unless otherwise provided by law, government decree, or a local government regulation in a municipal authority matter. Based on Section 60(7) of the Infotv., regarding the obligation to perform a specific act, to exhibit specific behavior, to tolerate, or to cease, the enforcement of the decision is carried out by the Authority.
