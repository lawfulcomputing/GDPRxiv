DECISION  

The National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Authority) issues the following decisions in the data protection authority procedure initiated by the application submitted by Bence Tordai (hereinafter: Applicant) against Mediaworks Hungary Zrt. (hereinafter: Respondent), represented by attorney Dr. János Nagy (KASZ: 36065909, cegkapu#19291671), regarding the unlawful processing and disclosure of personal data:  
I.  
The Authority rejects the Applicant's request to determine that the Respondent unlawfully processed the Applicant's personal data.  
II.  
The Authority rejects the Applicant's request for the deletion of personal data unlawfully processed by the Respondent.  
III.  
The Authority rejects the Applicant's request for the imposition of sanctions on the Respondent under Section 61 of Act CXII of 2012 on the Right to Informational Self-Determination and Freedom of Information (hereinafter: Infotv.) against press organs violating the General Data Protection Regulation and possibly other bodies (building authority).  
IV.  
The Authority grants the Applicant's request and establishes that the Respondent failed to inform the Applicant as a data subject regarding the data derived from the e-log, thereby violating Articles 14 (1)-(3) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (hereinafter: General Data Protection Regulation) concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, as well as the repeal of Directive 95/46/EC.  
V.  
The Authority establishes ex officio that the Respondent failed to inform the Applicant as a data subject regarding the image recordings, thereby violating Articles 13 (1)-(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
VI.  
As a consequence of the legal violations specified in points IV and V, the Authority reprimands the Respondent based on point b) of paragraph 2 of Article 58 of the General Data Protection Regulation, considering the disregard for the rights of the data subjects.  

There is no administrative remedy against this decision, but it can be challenged in an administrative lawsuit filed with the Metropolitan Court within 30 days from the communication of this decision. The lawsuit must be submitted to the Authority electronically, which is the case file.

(16) In the present case, the Authority examined the legality of the data processing related to the newspaper article published by the Applicant, as well as whether the Applicant fulfilled their obligation to provide prior information.  
(17) Based on the available evidence, the officially known and public facts, the Authority established the following facts:  
(18) The Applicant is a politician, a member of parliament representing the political party Párbeszéd – Zöldek, and co-chair, thus clearly a public figure.

The Applicant further stated: "We will no longer tolerate that Fidesz's privileged individuals stand above the law, and we will not tolerate that state party bureaucrats like Sára Botond use their official power to cover up the illegal affairs of other Fidesz criminals!", "There is no place in the National Assembly for politicians who disregard both the rule of law and natural resources!". The Articles referred to Metropol as the source of the published photos and the electronic construction log data.

The e-log reportedly also contains that "complete demolition and machine foundation digging" took place. The Articles suggest that it is particularly noteworthy that the Applicant, who has long been the loudest critic of a property investor who has also been building unlawfully in District II, even filed a report regarding the matter.

Applicable Legal Provisions  
(28) According to Article VI (3) of the Fundamental Law of Hungary (hereinafter: Fundamental Law), everyone has the right to the protection of their personal data, as well as to the knowledge and dissemination of public interest data.  
(29) Based on Articles IX (1) and (4) of the Fundamental Law, everyone has the right to freedom of expression. The exercise of freedom of expression must not be directed against the dignity of others.  
(30) According to Article IX (2) of the Fundamental Law, Hungary recognizes and protects the freedom and diversity of the press, ensuring the conditions necessary for the formation of democratic public opinion through free information.  
(31) According to Article 2(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (hereinafter: General Data Protection Regulation), the General Data Protection Regulation must be applied to the processing of personal data that is carried out by automated means or to the processing of personal data that is part of a filing system or intended to be part of a filing system.  
(32) According to Article 4(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, “personal data” means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (“data subject”); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, particularly by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.  
(33) According to Article 4(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation, “processing” means any operation or set of operations which is performed on personal data or on sets of personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage,

IV.3. Scope of Processed Personal Data  
(94) In evaluating the definition of personal data, the following conceptual elements must be examined:  
- the data (“any information”) formal and content criteria;  
- the identified or identifiable natural person (“data subject”);  
- the relationship between the data and the data subject (“relevant”).  
(95) Generally, any information that relates to an individual, or that influences or may influence the individual's situation in some way, is considered personal data. Therefore, information that affects the individual's situation qualifies as personal data. Data concerning the data subject particularly includes the name of the data subject, identification mark, knowledge characteristic of one or more physical, physiological, mental, economic, cultural, or social identities, as well as their image, voice, and physical characteristics suitable for identifying the data subject. Personal data also includes information concerning the individual's private and family life, which falls within the realm of so-called “privacy,” as well as facts or value judgments related to their workplace, economic, or social activities.  
(96) The data subject can be any specific natural person who can be identified or is identifiable based on their personal data—either directly or indirectly. In relation to the data subject, the status of the natural person and the aspect of identifiability must be examined. When assessing whether information qualifies as personal data, attention must be paid to the question of identifiability, i.e., whether the individual can be identified based on the given information. If so, the information is clearly considered personal data.  
(97) A fundamental element of the concept of personal data is the connection between the information and the data subject. The information must be related to the data subject, and its personal data nature remains as long as the connection between the information and the data subject can be restored.  
(98) In the Applicant's Application, they contest the publication of the data from the e-log concerning the construction of their family house, as well as photographs taken of their family house. (99) According to the opinion 4/2007 (WP 136) of the working group under Article 29 regarding the concept of personal data, a piece of data can be considered “relevant” to an individual if its use is likely to affect the rights and interests of that person, taking into account all circumstances surrounding the case.

IV.4. Legality of the Contested Data Processing  
(101) In this case, the Authority examined the legality of the data processing related to the Articles published by the Respondent, as well as whether the Respondent fulfilled their obligation of prior information.

IV.4.1. Legal basis for the recording and publication of personal data  
(102) The General Data Protection Regulation defines in Article 6(1)(a)-(f) which legal bases may be applied in the case of the processing of personal data.  
(103) According to the consistent position of the Authority, the legal basis for data processing related to journalistic activities—unless it concerns voluntary consent—is the legitimate interest under Article 6(1)(f) of the General Data Protection Regulation. The application of this legal basis has also been confirmed by the Curia in its judgment Kfv. 37.978/2021/10. Section 10 of the Media Act establishes the public's right to information, that is, the right to be informed about local, national, and European public affairs, as well as significant events for the citizens of Hungary and members of the Hungarian nation, stating that the press's task is to provide authentic, prompt, and accurate information on these matters and events. In this regard, the court stated in its referenced judgment that this provision of the Media Act does not impose a public interest task on the media outlet, thus the journalistic activities it performs cannot be classified as a public interest task or, in the terminology of Hungarian national law, as a public duty. The court also pointed out that journalistic activity is related to the fundamental right to information and the right to be informed as defined in the Fundamental Law. According to these provisions, the press's task is necessary for ensuring the constitutional fundamental rights protected by the Fundamental Law, which require protection; therefore, the data processing necessary for fulfilling this task is essential for the press to carry out its duties as recorded in the Media Act.  
(104) According to Article 6(1)(f) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the processing of data is lawful if it is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data.  
(105) The Authority first examined whether the Applicant or a third party had a legitimate interest in the data processing. The essential findings supporting the legitimate interest of the Applicant and third parties in data processing are contained in paragraphs 7/2014. (III.7.) AB [39], [40], [42], and [48].  
(106) The right to freedom of expression occupies a prominent place in the fundamental rights system of the Fundamental Law. The opportunity for free speech is essential for the realization of individual autonomy, as the development of personality is entirely inconceivable without anyone being able to freely express their views and thoughts to others without substantive constraints. The free self-expression of free individuals is a fundamental element and meaning of the constitutional order based on the Fundamental Law. On the other hand, freedom of speech is the foundation of a democratic, pluralistic society and public opinion. Without the freedom and diversity of social and political debates, there is no democratic public opinion, and there is no democratic rule of law.  
(107) Freedom of the press—which encompasses the freedom of all types of media—is an institution of freedom of speech. The press, despite the increasingly complex and diverse nature of its activities, is primarily a means of expressing opinions, shaping opinions, and acquiring information essential for forming opinions. The special nature of freedom of speech in this regard also applies to freedom of the press, and the dual justification of freedom applies to it as well: the significance of freedom of the press is justified by both the subjective fundamental right and the constitutional institution of democratic public opinion.  
(108) The special role of freedom of speech entails that, on the one hand, it must only exceptionally yield to other rights or constitutional values invoked for its limitation, and on the other hand, laws restricting free expression must be interpreted restrictively.  
(109) Speech concerning public figures is a central component of political expression. Statements concerning the activities, views, and credibility of those shaping public affairs constitute an essential part of the discussion of public matters. A significant portion of social and political debates consists precisely of the interactions of public figures and the public discourse—
17  
Typically through the press, participants criticize each other's ideas, political performance, and in connection with that, each other's personalities. The press has a constitutional mission to oversee the exercise of public power, which includes presenting and even sharply criticizing the activities of individuals and institutions involved in shaping public affairs. Therefore, despite the fact that the focus of public expression is on public affairs themselves—not on public figures—the majority of statements regarding the personalities of those shaping public affairs necessarily and inevitably fall within the protective scope of political expression. The outstanding constitutional significance of debating public affairs entails that the protection of the personalities of public figures can only be adequately met by a narrower restriction of freedom of speech and press, as derived from the Fundamental Law. It is particularly important from a constitutional perspective that citizens and the press can participate in social and political debates without uncertainty, compromise, and fear. It would counteract this if speakers had to broadly consider legal accountability in light of the personality protection of public figures.  
(110) The Authority established in paragraph (18) of this decision that the Applicant, as a member of parliament and co-chair of a political party, is clearly a public figure. It can also be established that the Articles report on public affairs, specifically examining the credibility of a public figure—the Applicant: whether the Applicant has a moral basis to criticize other public figures for their illegal activities, or whether the Applicant's own alleged illegal conduct—according to the Articles, illegal construction—excludes this. The judgment of the Budapest Court of Appeal states that “Every data subject should have received individual information regarding the provisions of Articles 14-15 of the General Data Protection Regulation. […] The argument of the first-instance plaintiff that […] the rights of the data subjects contained in the general data processing information published on its website replaced the provisions of Articles 14-15 of the General Data Protection Regulation is unfounded. According to Article 14(1) and Article 15(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, the data subject is entitled to information and feedback from the data controller regarding the processing of their personal data, thus the first-instance plaintiff was obliged to engage in active information activities concerning the second to fourth-instance plaintiffs. The argument is insignificant – especially since the reference to the availability of the general data processing information published on the Forbes website was omitted from the information – that the data subjects could have checked the website. The first-instance plaintiff failed to fulfill the above active information obligation, therefore the defendant lawfully established the violation of Articles 14-15 of the General Data Protection Regulation. […] The information provided under Article 14 of the General Data Protection Regulation must contain individualized information about the processed personal data for the data subjects, just as the information regarding the right of access of the data subjects under Article 15(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation. The purpose of individualized information is precisely to provide the data subject with specific – not general in form and nature – information about the processing of their own personal data and their related rights. A publication on the data controller's website
20  
The general information notice does not provide individualized information regarding which personal data the data controller processes about the data subject, what the source of the data is, what the legal basis for data processing is, and, in accordance with the purpose of this data processing, what duration of data processing the data subject can expect. The data subject can exercise their rights and assert their claims based on this individualized information, and can decide which personal data corrections to request from the data controller; furthermore, if there is a need for restriction or deletion of data, which personal data they can initiate the restriction or deletion of.

IV.4.2.2. Information on the recording and publication of drone footage  
(128) The Applicant refers to a violation of Article 14 of the General Data Protection Regulation on the grounds that prior information regarding data processing was not provided in any of the cases listed. The Authority did not agree with the Applicant regarding the legal qualification of the absence of information about drone footage. According to the Authority's position, the footage taken by the Respondent's employee of the Applicant's house qualifies as personal data obtained from the Applicant based on the following.  
(129) In its decision NAIH-3476-1/2022, the Authority referred to the opinion of the working group on transparency under Article 29 of the (EU) 2016/679 Regulation (hereinafter: Opinion) and stated that "The fact that the camera footage and photograph were taken by one of the Respondent's employees does not mean that the personal data was not obtained directly from the Applicant." The Authority based this conclusion on the Opinion, which states that Article 13 of the General Data Protection Regulation applies when data is obtained from the data subject. This includes the following personal data: data consciously provided by the data subject to the data controller (e.g., when filling out an online form); or data collected by the data controller from the data subject through observation (e.g., using automated data collection tools or data collection software, such as cameras, network devices, Wi-Fi tracking, radio frequency identification, or other types of sensors). According to the Opinion, Article 14 applies when data is not obtained from the data subject. This includes personal data obtained by the data controller from various sources, including third-party data controllers; publicly available sources; data brokers; or other data subjects.  
(130) The Authority officially established that by failing to provide information regarding the recording and publication of images taken of the Applicant's house published in the Articles, the Respondent violated Articles 13 (1)-(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation.
