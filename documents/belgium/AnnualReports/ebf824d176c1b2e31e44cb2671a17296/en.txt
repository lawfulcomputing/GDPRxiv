Data Protection Authority - Annual Report 2020.........  
Better safe than sorry!  
For 2020, the Authority had planned changes. But this year also brought its share of unexpected challenges. Despite all the difficulties, we managed to stay the course and continue the transition journey of our Authority.  
The year started off very well, with the approval of our Strategic Plan 2020-2025, the Management Plan 2020, and the commitment of six additional staff members. Given the new oversight responsibilities, these new colleagues were primarily assigned to the Litigation Chamber and the Inspection Service, both responsible for processes aimed at enforcing regulations.  
Very quickly, investigations and decisions followed one another, and sanctions were also imposed in some cases. The year 2020 was also marked by the first provisional measures from the Inspection Service.  
However, the other departments did not remain idle. The number of complaints doubled, and following the installation of a new government in the fall, the number of requests for opinions on legislation and regulations rose again. In the meantime, useful processes and procedures were developed for some new instruments or oversight responsibilities (such as opinions regarding data protection impact assessments). In a bid for efficiency in our oversight mission (our sixth strategic objective), we also realized the idea that data protection should be simple, for example by drastically simplifying the data breach notification form. Since then, notifications have also doubled. The arrival of the coronavirus a few months ago, which necessitated organizing nearly all human interactions electronically, did not divert us from our course. Our plan was indeed very clear: to raise awareness and inform, to act when necessary to enforce regulations, to identify new developments and respond to them, to collaborate with other controllers or key stakeholders, and thus make the DPA an indispensable player, an experienced guide, and a reference center in the field of digital information.  
Thanks to the efforts of all staff members and despite often difficult circumstances (particularly due to a structurally too limited budget), we also achieved our objectives during the second half of the year. In a certain sense, the coronavirus crisis and the increased importance of digital information and communication further highlighted our mission and our new approach.  
We provided opinions not only on legislation and regulations concerning all kinds of new data processing in the fight against the virus but also on the correct or incorrect assessment of risks related to the Coronalert application. As part of our more proactive approach, opportunities were also plentiful. Among other examples, we addressed temperature checks at schools or workplaces, cameras measuring crowd density at the coast, and additional measures taken by local or provincial authorities. We requested information, analyzed it, and thus published transparent guidelines and useful information on our website without delay. "Better safe than sorry," a principle that is also very important in the field of data protection. And to top it all off, we were also able to present all this information attractively, thanks to our new visual identity.  
On behalf of the Management Committee, I would especially like to thank all our staff for facing this tumultuous year 2020 and for giving their best every day. We must hold on a little longer: after the rain, the sun always shines.  
On behalf of the Management Committee,  
D. Stevens, President

Data Protection Authority - Annual Report 2020  
The Data Protection Authority (DPA) is the independent supervisory body regarding the processing of personal data. It was established by the law of December 3, 2017 (hereinafter: "LCA"). It has a much broader range of actions than its predecessor, the Privacy Protection Commission (the "Privacy Commission"). The DPA is a federal body with legal personality, established with the House of Representatives.  

**Competences of the DPA**  
The general mission of the DPA is defined by Article 4 of the LCA as "the supervision of compliance with the fundamental principles of personal data protection, within the framework of this law and laws containing provisions related to the protection of personal data processing."  
The DPA is therefore, in principle, competent not only for monitoring compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter: "GDPR") or general legislation related to data protection (such as the law of July 30, 2018, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data, hereinafter: "LTD"), but it can also exercise oversight of compliance with other more specific legislations and regulations, such as legislation related to cameras.  
However, the DPA's oversight does not cover processing carried out by courts and tribunals as well as by the public prosecutor in the exercise of their judicial functions. The DPA is also not competent for the processing of personal data carried out by police services.  
The DPA is competent regarding actors and bodies in both the public and private sectors and has the ability to impose administrative sanctions autonomously.  

**Composition of the DPA**  
According to Article 7 of the LCA, the DPA is composed of a Management Committee, a General Secretariat, a Frontline Service, a Knowledge Center, an Inspection Service, and a Litigation Chamber. Both the Litigation Chamber and the Knowledge Center also include six external members who contribute their expertise to the work and deliberations of these bodies.

**The Management Committee**  
The competences and activities of the Management Committee are outlined in Article 9 of the LCA and include, in addition to the approval of the annual accounts, decisions regarding the annual budget, internal organization and composition, as well as the annual management plans of the DPA.  
The Management Committee also has significant operational responsibility in the area of citizens' privacy, as according to Article 63, 1° of the LCA, it can decide to refer to the Inspection Service if it finds serious indications of the existence of a practice likely to lead to a violation of the fundamental principles of personal data protection. In 2020, the Management Committee exercised this competence 7 times, generally after the opening of a monitoring file. In only one case was it decided that the indications were insufficient.  
In 2020, the Management Committee met 31 times.  
At the beginning of 2020, the Management Committee approved the "Strategic Plan 2020-2025." The DPA states that its ambition "is to guide citizens, businesses, associations, and public authorities towards a digital world where privacy is a reality for all. This is an ambitious but achievable goal. The DPA believes that the GDPR has initiated an irreversible process to respond to a demand from citizens who are calling for more trust, respect, and integrity."  
Both in the Strategic Plan and in the 2020 Management Plan, this vision is further specified in strategic and operational objectives, or applied through the various tasks and competences of the DPA, with a strong emphasis on the necessity of unity and coherence: "A coherent intervention by the DPA as an independent public institution serving the public interest."

Collaborators and Budget  
In the plan, the DPA identified its talented and motivated collaborators as an important asset for feasibility. They indeed contribute to the effective protection of the privacy of our citizens at the European and international level (for example, by developing guidelines within the European Data Protection Committee, or by collaborating in transnational complaints and investigations). However, given the new competencies and procedures, the pressure on collaborators is significant.  
The success of achieving this envisioned growth trajectory has therefore been subject to the essential condition of an increase in human and financial resources. Specifically, the DPA has proposed a minimalist growth scenario for its staff framework of 6 FTEs (Full-Time Equivalents) in 2020 as well as in 2021, 4 additional FTEs in 2022, and another 2 additional FTEs in 2023 and 2024.  
For the 2020 fiscal year, the House of Representatives approved an increase in the financial resources of the DPA, which allowed for the temporary hiring of 6 additional collaborators. At the end of 2020, the DPA had 67 collaborators, compared to 62 at the end of the previous year, representing an increase of 8%.  
To finance its operations, the DPA had a budget of 8,947,430.92 EUR in 2020 (compared to 8,247,129.147 EUR in 2019, an increase of 8.5%). The operating budget is primarily funded by the actual allocation of 7,992,000 EUR (the amount is included annually in the federal authority's expenditure budget) and by carryover surpluses from previous years. In 2020, the DPA also received grants from the European Commission amounting to 244,327.67 EUR to support the Boost project.  
Despite this increase, a comparative study conducted by PwC at the end of 2020 at the request of the DPA indicated that other European supervisory authorities in neighboring countries had significantly higher human resources and experienced much stronger growth in personnel and budget.  
New Approach of the DPA  
As demonstrated in detail later in this annual report, significant steps have been taken since 2020, not only in establishing a new proactive authority that shares its knowledge and raises public awareness but also in the application of legal and regulatory provisions.  
Proactive Monitoring  
A brand new "monitoring process" has been developed in accordance with Article 19 of the LCA (Law on the Control of Data Protection). These files allow the DPA to actively collect information regarding both technological and societal developments impacting data protection in order to quickly gauge the situation if it becomes aware of potentially risky processing for citizens' data protection rights. The DPA thus fulfills the third objective of its Strategic Plan ("Better data protection through the identification of developments and appropriate responses").

Covid-19, the privacy challenge of the year  
The year 2020 was marked by an unprecedented health crisis, which also had a significant impact on the protection of personal data.  
The DPA was called upon to express its opinion on the establishment of data processing that would have been difficult to imagine before the pandemic, such as the creation of a mobility index based on the telecommunications data of Belgians, or the development of methods and applications for contact tracing of individuals infected with the coronavirus. Finding a way to preserve public health while ensuring compliance with the protection of personal data was not always an easy task, but it was necessary to protect the rights and freedoms of individuals regarding privacy.  
Despite limited resources, the DPA therefore doubled its efforts in 2020 on awareness-raising, notably by creating a thematic Covid-19 file on its website, regularly updated with new guidelines and questions and answers to guide citizens and data controllers during these uncertain times.  
It also provided numerous opinions to parliamentarians and governments regarding normative texts establishing data processing by the State in the context of the health crisis (collection, recording, and use of data, particularly health data, in the context of tracing, vaccination, or monitoring compliance with testing and quarantine, for example).  
Finally, it increased its vigilance, notably reacting to numerous concerning initiatives such as the temperature checks of travelers at Brussels Airport, or a municipal initiative to impose Covid-19 tests. Several control files related to the coronavirus have been opened and are currently under review by the Inspection Service and/or the Litigation Chamber of the DPA.  
Opinions  
Draft normative texts  
Since the beginning of the coronavirus pandemic, numerous normative texts have been drafted and implemented to frame various types of measures, generally temporary, aimed at:  
- either limiting the spread of the virus (through the imposition of measures such as "contact tracing" or monitoring compliance with quarantine obligations);  
- or assisting citizens or organizations whose situation – particularly financial – has been significantly impacted by the crisis (through loans, grants, or exemptions);  
- or further adjusting the functioning of certain State services that, in ordinary times, require close contact between individuals.  
In accordance with Article 36.4 of the GDPR, the Authority must be consulted prior to the establishment of all legislative and regulatory standards of general scope that create, modify, or condition personal data processing. The DPA therefore issues prior advisory opinions on the draft standards that are submitted to it before their vote or implementation.
20  
The APD issued a series of opinions in 2020 regarding such draft normative texts. The complete list of these opinions can be consulted on the APD website (see the "Opinions related to Covid-19" page).  
A large part of these opinions had to be rendered within extraordinary deadlines in order not to delay the implementation of the measures in question.  
These opinions essentially analyze the compliance, by the authors of the draft texts, with the principles of legality, predictability, necessity, proportionality, as well as other principles and rules derived from the GDPR, the law of July 30, 2018, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data, international treaties relating to fundamental rights, and case law (from the Court of Justice of the European Union and the Court of Human Rights). The main shortcomings identified in these opinions relate to (i) the lack of precision in the description of the purposes of the envisaged data processing (leaving open the possibility of reusing often sensitive data by the State for purposes other than those for which they were collected or generated), (ii) the non-exhaustive nature of the categories of processed data (leaving the executive the power to add data to the list of data established by law) and of concerned individuals, and (iii) the absence of determination of third parties to whom the collected and/or recorded data may be made accessible (opening the door to unlimited sharing of health data).  
About a dozen opinions were issued in the context of establishing a high-risk contact tracing system. The multitude of opinions rendered on this unique subject is explained by the fact that this project was initially regulated by a royal decree of special powers, which was later replaced by a bill that itself underwent several amendments (on which opinions were rendered), and was subsequently formalized in a new decree that was integrated into a draft cooperation agreement which, once concluded, had to be ratified through several laws and decrees. Texts confirming the initial royal decree of special powers were also issued, as well as regional decrees defining the concrete organization of regional contact tracing centers. Some of the recommendations issued were followed, while others were not. This constitutes a notable fact compared to the functioning and ordinary activity of the Knowledge Center whose opinions are generally followed.  
The APD also exercised its advisory competence regarding two texts allowing the offering of a digital contact tracing application (Coronalert) and defining its operation. The remarks made mainly concern the centralization at Sciensano of data generated by the application regarding its users.  
The opinions issued regarding measures aimed at granting bonuses, allowances, loans, and other compensatory benefits, as well as those concerning the establishment of videoconferencing, led to more limited remarks, notably due to the fact that the data concerned are less sensitive and do not reveal the health status of citizens. Their collection, storage, and use by the State thus constitute a less significant interference in the right to data protection of individuals, so that these operations are subject to lesser requirements in terms of legal framework.  
Finally, an opinion issued at the end of December concerns a royal decree "regarding the registration and processing of data related to vaccinations against Covid-19." This opinion highlights a series of serious shortcomings, part of which was corrected in the drafting of the cooperation agreement that replaced this royal decree.

Public Authorities  
One of the sectoral priorities included in the DPA's Strategic Plan 2020-2025 concerns compliance with the regulations regarding the protection of personal data by public authorities.  
Public services generally process large amounts of personal data - sometimes sensitive - in the exercise of their missions. This particularly includes access to data included in the National Register, the processing of financial data, and data related to criminal convictions. Citizens often have no choice regarding the sharing of their personal data, as the processing is generally based on a legal obligation or is necessary for the fulfillment of a public interest mission.  
The DPA believes that public authorities must be beyond reproach and that they should be expected to use this data scrupulously. They must indeed lead by example in terms of personal data protection.  
In 2020, the various departments of the DPA therefore paid particular attention to the processing activities of these public sector data controllers, both in their advisory role and in their role as a regulator.  
In this regard, the DPA focused on the following themes:  

- The lawfulness of personal data processing by public authorities (Article 6 of the GDPR)  
The processing of personal data by public services is generally based on Article 6.1 c) (legal obligation) or Article 6.1 e) (public interest mission) of the GDPR. In this regard, both the Knowledge Center in its opinions and the Litigation Chamber in its decisions have emphasized the importance of having a legislative norm that frames the processing in question in a sufficiently clear and precise manner and whose application for the individuals concerned is predictable (Article 6.3 of the GDPR, read in conjunction with Article 22 of the Constitution and Article 8 of the ECHR).  
In this regard, reference can be made to opinions No. 36/2020, 42/2020, 44/2020, 46/2020, 52/2020, and 64/2020 from the Knowledge Center, which concern, among other things, certain measures taken by public authorities in the context of the fight against Covid-19 (see above).  

- Compliance with the principles relating to the processing of personal data (Article 5 of the GDPR) and the principles of "data protection by design" and "data protection by default" (Article 25 of the GDPR)  
The aforementioned principles constitute one of the cornerstones of the GDPR. The DPA emphasizes the importance of compliance with these principles by public authorities when processing personal data and particularly highlights the importance of implementing the principle of data protection by design, which is intended to ensure that the guarantee of personal data protection and the rights of the individuals concerned are "integrated" into the processing and that no more data is processed than is strictly necessary for the intended purposes ("data minimization").

Impact Assessment: Procedure and Form  
Before initiating the processing of personal data, a data controller checks whether it presents a high risk to the freedoms and fundamental rights of the data subjects. In such a case, a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) must be carried out prior to the initiation of the processing.  
If, despite all the measures put in place to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, the DPIA results in a high residual risk remaining, the data controller must consult the DPA prior to the initiation of its processing. The DPIA and, where applicable, the prior consultation are thus tools for compliance (accountability) provided for by the GDPR.  
In order to actively support data controllers in this compliance obligation, the DPA has made tools available on its website. In addition to detailed information and a downloadable DPIA guide, a prior consultation form has also been established. The questions it contains and the various elements required for submitting a consultation request aim to facilitate the procedures for data controllers in the context of this consultation, as well as in their analysis of the high residual risk and, more generally, in the management of personal data processing.  
Data Breach Processing  
In the event of a significant data breach, organizations must notify this breach to the DPA within 72 hours. The notification of data breaches is done via an electronic form, a simplified version of which was published in July. This new electronic form limits the administrative burden on the notifier and contains only the necessary fields for the DPA's assessment of the breach. The form allows the notifier to know what information they need to provide and is also part of a systematic and effective follow-up of notifications by the DPA.

In the context of monitoring and following up on notifications, particular attention is paid to appropriate corrective measures that are applied in a timely manner, as well as to the correct and timely information of the individuals concerned.  
For organizations, assessing a data breach can sometimes be a real challenge. This is why the Data Protection Authority (DPA) has collaborated on the new guidelines from the European Data Protection Board regarding data breach notifications. These guidelines complement the general guidelines on data breaches. International Transfers  
International transfers were once again brought to the forefront in 2020, both by the ruling C-311/18 (known as the "Schrems II" ruling) and by Brexit.  
In the "Schrems II" ruling, the Court of Justice of the European Union invalidated the decision (EU) 2016/1250 of the European Commission regarding the adequacy of the protection provided by the EU-U.S. Privacy Shield on the grounds that the access and use of data transferred from the EU to the U.S. by American authorities under surveillance programs are not limited to what is strictly necessary (failure to comply with the proportionality principle of the GDPR). In contrast, the Court upheld the validity of decision 2010/87 of the European Commission regarding standard contractual clauses for the transfer of personal data to processors established in third countries. However, the Court clarified that if these clauses are not or cannot be complied with in that country and the protection of the transferred data, required by Union law, cannot be ensured by other means (notably by taking additional measures), the exporter established in the EU must suspend or terminate the said transfer. If this is not the case, national supervisory authorities must do so if they deem it necessary.  
The United Kingdom left the European Union and thus became a "third country" with Brexit as of December 31, 2020. However, a trade and cooperation agreement concluded between the European Union and the United Kingdom provides that transfers of personal data to the United Kingdom may temporarily continue from that date for six months or less if the European Commission adopts an adequacy decision for the United Kingdom.  
By publishing information on its website, supporting stakeholders in the development and implementation of transfer tools (binding corporate rules, administrative arrangements, etc.), and closely collaborating with its European counterparts within the EDPB, the DPA continues its mission to support public authorities and businesses to facilitate the transfer of data outside the European Union while ensuring the protection of the rights of the individuals concerned.

The Growing Importance of Audit Files  
The previous annual report already pointed out the significant difference between the inspection tasks and the audit tasks of the Inspection Service.  
In addition to its general inspection mission, the Inspection Service also has a specific audit mission. This aspect is not highlighted in the Data Protection Act, but it directly stems from European legislation. However, it is important to emphasize again that these audits generate a significant workload and that the Inspection Service must therefore allocate its operational resources between inspections and audits. In this regard, it is sufficient to mention the difference in approach between an inspection and an audit. The audit is therefore, to simplify, a somewhat more flexible approach than an inspection.  
This audit mission consists, on the one hand, of a national audit mission that is part of a quadrennial review of several public services and, on the other hand, of an international audit mission (mainly an on-site review of the Member States that are part of the Schengen area). The Inspection Service notes that generally, several of its findings are taken up by the Litigation Chamber. The central mission of the Inspection Service is to ascertain the facts and report on them. The subsequent handling (the organization of the adversarial debate on this matter and the evaluation of the follow-up actions) falls to the Litigation Chamber.  
As a result, certain aspects regularly or systematically recur in the reports of the Inspection Service. In 2020, for example, the Inspection Service focused on the theme of the "data protection officer" ("DPO"). In various investigations, this key player in data protection was subject to detailed analysis. In different cases, the Inspection Service found issues regarding the functioning of the DPO. The DPO does not always report directly to the highest level of the hierarchy, the role is often combined with other internal functions (second line) that may create conflicts of interest or other problems, making it impossible for the DPO to work independently. In some cases, the Inspection Service also found that the DPO did not have the necessary resources.

The Contentious Chamber in broad terms  
The Contentious Chamber is the administrative litigation body of the APD (Article 32 of the LCA) and is tasked with intervening in a repressive manner in cases submitted to it, based on a complaint from a citizen or following an inspection initiated by the APD. The Contentious Chamber also deals with cases submitted to the APD by authorities of European member states within the framework of the single contact point mechanism established by the GDPR, where the APD is the lead authority or concerned authority. The Contentious Chamber has, in this regard,
45  
Data Protection Authority - Annual Report 2020  
several instruments: corrective measures and administrative fines. The procedure before the  
Contentious Chamber is conducted in accordance with the principles of procedural law.  
The Contentious Chamber is composed, in addition to its president, of six external members who are  
appointed by the Chamber based on their particular expertise.  
The Contentious Chamber has a unique structure that does not exist among market controllers  
in Belgium or among other supervisory authorities in the EU. This structure has a hybrid character.  
The Contentious Chamber is an organ of the controller, but the procedure is almost judicial, with  
notably a decision-making process following the exchange of conclusions between the parties and  
often also after a hearing.  
This structure has proven effective, especially due to the decisions that the Contentious Chamber  
has made in the meantime and which are available on the website of the DPA. The Contentious  
Chamber thus made 34 substantive decisions in 2020. The procedure allows for impartial rulings  
and respects legal guarantees.  
The Contentious Chamber operates based on the following principles.  
-  
The philosophy of content. The Contentious Chamber places importance on concrete and relevant  
technological innovation from a societal perspective, which must obviously occur in respect of  
citizens' privacy rights, as formulated in the Strategic Plan. In carrying out its mission, it also  
naturally takes into account the protection of other (fundamental) rights.  
-  
Accessible legal protection for all. The right to lodge a complaint with the DPA is an alternative  
to recourse to civil or administrative courts and must remain easy for citizens. The legislator did  
not, for example, intend for parties to always be assisted by a lawyer. The accessibility of legal  
protection allows for cases to be classified as not pursued for reasons of opportunity due to the  
absence of strategic interest, as long as this complies with the requirements of the case law of the  
Brussels Market Court.  
-  
Impartiality. In examining disputes, any form of bias and partiality must be avoided. The right to  
contradiction occupies a central place in the handling of disputes. Following the case law of the  
Market Court, the Contentious Chamber decided in March 2020 to inform the concerned party of  
its intention to impose a fine.  
-  
Efficiency. The Contentious Chamber must be able to handle cases quickly, while obviously  
respecting procedural guarantees, such as the right to contradiction. Speed of action is particularly  
important in cases with a significant social impact, but also in cases where parties need legal  
certainty. However, the speed of action has become a major source of concern, particularly given  
the lack of resources.  
-  
Transparency. As far as relevant and possible, the Contentious Chamber ensures the transparency  
of principles, procedures, and decisions. In principle, all decisions of the Contentious Chamber are  
published on the website.  
-  
Cooperation. As far as relevant and possible, the Contentious Chamber cooperates with other  
controllers in Belgium and with fellow controllers in the EEA and sometimes outside of it (such as  
the British controller).
46  
The Contentious Chamber is, however, faced with three types of existential problems:  
-  
Problems in terms of quantity and quality. The Contentious Chamber wants and must provide high quality in its decisions, but at the same time, the number of cases submitted is disproportionately high. The Contentious Chamber operates with a very small staff (4 legal experts per language role, 2 to 3 secretarial collaborators) and rules at the end of a generally heavy legal procedure.  
-  
The hybrid and unique nature of the Contentious Chamber (an internal body of the Data Protection Authority, but with an almost judicial role) leads to a disproportionately heavy and therefore slow procedure, which creates a lot of confusion in practice. Although the procedure before the Contentious Chamber has almost judicial characteristics, the Contentious Chamber is not part of the judiciary. As an organ of the supervisory authority, the Contentious Chamber has the responsibility to contribute to a high level of data protection in Belgium and beyond, and in the decisions it makes in cases, it can materialize the priorities established in the Strategic Plan, certainly while respecting the general principles of law. Furthermore, it believes that it must be able to address violations reported to it during the procedure. Transparency is an important principle.  
-  
Recent case law of the Market Court. A significant proportion of the decisions that have been appealed to the Market Court have been annulled, either totally or partially, mainly for procedural reasons. The ruling of February 24, 2021 (2021/AR/1159) is a perfect illustration of this. This ruling compromises the functioning of the procedure within the Contentious Chamber, namely when it is based on a complaint from a citizen. The ruling indeed seems to make it impossible for the Contentious Chamber to consider elements of violation not mentioned in the complaint that it may have become aware of during the procedure.  
Summary of substantive decisions  
Established following the adoption of the Law of December 3, 2017, creating the Data Protection Authority (DPA) and operational from the application of the GDPR on May 25, 2018, the Contentious Chamber remains, in 2020, a young institution. Its work in reviewing the increasing number of complaints addressed to the DPA is becoming more widely known, but the exact contours of its actions and what can or cannot be expected from it remain sometimes misunderstood by the general public and sometimes even by professionals in data protection.  
In 2020, the Contentious Chamber thus, on several occasions, emphasized its role as the litigation body of the DPA responsible for examining the complaints submitted to it, notably under Article 58 of the GDPR and Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union. Its contribution, through the adoption of binding decisions, to the effectiveness of the fundamental right to data protection is essential. Without substituting for the courts of the judicial order or interfering in conflicts that would fall under other areas of law (such as labor law, for example), the Contentious Chamber is there to ensure compliance, in multiple sectors of activity, with the rules applicable to the processing of personal data. It has a range of both corrective measures and sanctions aimed at one objective: that of rapid and effective compliance for the benefit of citizens. In this sense, the Contentious Chamber has considered that it could play an active and supportive role in clarifying the complaints and grievances they raise while respecting the principle of adversarial proceedings. The Contentious Chamber also considered that the withdrawal of a complaint during the procedure or the regularization of a breach after the filing of a complaint did not ipso facto result in the loss of the subject matter of the complaint. This cooperation also enables it to contribute to a harmonized application of the GDPR in the EEA.  
Regularly seized with complaints related to the lack of a satisfactory follow-up to the exercise of their rights by the complainants, the Contentious Chamber has logically adopted a series of decisions reminding, for example, the unconditionality of the right to object in matters of direct marketing or the necessity of erasing data whose processing is no longer necessary for the pursued purpose. Similarly, it has examined several times the quality of "privacy policies" intended to inform the data subjects about the data processed concerning them. The right to de-referencing has also received its first recognition in decision 37/2020 of the Contentious Chamber (see chapter below). As for the right of access, the Contentious Chamber has clarified its contours as well as the scope of the exceptions (41/2020).  
Regarding data controllers, whether in the public or private sector, the Contentious Chamber has, through a number of emblematic decisions, highlighted what their obligations were and how they should be concretized. The profile, obligations, and position of the DPO (as well as the incompatibilities with certain functions) have thus been recalled. Likewise, the obligation to maintain a record of processing activities, the adoption of a privacy policy, or the obligation to conduct a data protection impact assessment have been emphasized. The issue of emails, from the perspective of sending emails, an email management policy, and their closure in case of departure, are themes that have also been addressed. Finally, regarding the implementation by data controllers of the foundational principles of data protection, the Contentious Chamber has had the opportunity to remind the respect owed to the principle of purpose (particularly in the electoral context), the principle of minimization, as well as the principle of accountability which requires the data controller to adopt the necessary measures to ensure that the processing they carry out complies with the GDPR, notably through the adoption of adequate procedures. Lastly, the issue of the lawfulness of processing has been recurrent and reflects a real difficulty for some data controllers to identify the legal basis for processing. The Contentious Chamber has thus, through several decisions, identified what could be the legitimate interests of the data controller and the conditions under which these could be validly invoked. The Contentious Chamber has also recalled what the conditions for consent must be in accordance with Articles 6.1.a) and 7 of the GDPR.

The Right to be Forgotten and the Google Decision  
Among the 2020 decisions of the Litigation Chamber, one decision in particular garnered significant attention. In July 2020, the Data Protection Authority (DPA) imposed a fine of 600,000 euros on Google Belgium for violating a citizen's right to be forgotten and for the lack of transparency in its deletion request form.  
A Belgian citizen had requested the removal of links containing information perceived as negative about him. This request had been denied by Google.  
The Litigation Chamber of the DPA determined that some of these links were necessary for the public interest and should not be removed: the citizen indeed plays a role in public life, and the links concerned an alleged relationship with a political party. The other links contained outdated, unfounded information that could severely damage the reputation of the individual concerned. The DPA believes that these links should therefore have been de-referenced by Google. For the DPA, it is important to note that the facts of the case were clear, leaving Google no reasonable margin for maneuver to decide otherwise.  
Furthermore, Google lacked transparency in its de-referencing form, as well as in its response to the individual concerned.  
The DPA considered itself competent in this matter, since Google claims that the single window mechanism does not apply to data processing concerning the Google search engine. Indeed, the data controller in this case is Google LLC, the Google company based in the United States.  
Although it recognizes that this company is responsible for processing, the DPA deemed that it could impose sanctions against Google Belgium due to the inseparable links that exist between the activities of the two entities.  
Moreover, it considered that in order to ensure a useful effect of the GDPR and to avoid a legal vacuum, Article 3.1 GDPR should be interpreted in such a way that an authority must be able to impose binding measures on the establishment within the Union of a data controller whose headquarters are located outside the Union.

In 2020, 13 monitoring files were also closed.

Admissibility and Competence of Closed Cases  
Before any review or mediation or communication of information, the Authority always conducts an admissibility analysis. The significant difference with the admissibility figures from previous years lies in the fact that they have not been included as such in the statistics, as even in cases of inadmissibility, the citizen generally still receives information from the First Line Service. In 2020, 162 requests (2.68%) were declared inadmissible due to a lack of information from the applicant or the absence of the required signature. This includes 11 information cases, 4 mediation requests, and 147 complaint cases. Furthermore, 155 requests (2.57%) were submitted to the Authority when it was not competent in the matter. In 99 cases, the applicants were redirected to the appropriate authority.  
Types of Data Processed in the Cases

1. Identification data (68.27%)  
2. Electronic identification data (17.06%)  
3. Audio and video recordings (10.75%)  
4. Financial particulars (8.89%)
