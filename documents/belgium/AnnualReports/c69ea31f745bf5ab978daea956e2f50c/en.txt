Data Protection Authority - Annual Report 2019  
The year 2019 was (once again) rich in changes for the Authority. It concluded in a grand manner with the public consultation held in December on our draft Strategic Plan 2020-2025. The final version is now available here.  
The new Authority explains its objectives and how it intends to achieve them over the coming years. In essence, we want to be a beacon in the field of personal data processing, to help guide the digital society of tomorrow. We want to direct citizens, businesses, associations, and authorities towards a digital world where respect for privacy is a reality for all. In our view, this "reality" entails two things.  
First, we want to clarify the rules by issuing opinions and recommendations to data controllers, responding to specific information requests, promoting codes of conduct, and addressing privacy professionals of all kinds, sectoral associations, etc. We also want to simplify compliance with these rules by providing concrete tools to small and medium-sized enterprises. Furthermore, we also intend to clarify abstract rules and principles by applying them to concrete investigations and disputes. Thus, 2019 is also the year in which the Authority imposed its first fines.  
Awareness, monitoring, and sanctioning non-compliance with regulations, proactive oversight, and cooperation are the key terms that will allow the Authority to position itself more than ever as a reference center in data protection and as an effective regulator.  
The numerous new tasks assigned to the Authority, as well as cooperation at the European level, have led to a considerable increase in the workload. We are pleased to note that the House of Representatives has already granted us a significant increase in our budget for 2020, although a stable increase in our budget and staffing framework remains strictly necessary for the proper implementation of our strategic plan.  
While at the national level we were laying the groundwork for the new functioning of the Authority, at the European level, the evaluation and potential adaptation of the GDPR were initiated. The vast majority of Member States do not seem to favor an update of the regulation at this stage, although all acknowledge that additional efforts are needed (e.g., in terms of cooperation and coordination) to enable better harmonized and effective enforcement of the GDPR.  
Finally, on behalf of the Management Committee, I would like to thank all the collaborators of the Authority. First of all, our external members of the Litigation Chamber and the Knowledge Center. For their perseverance and seemingly inexhaustible motivation, even during times of great change, I would also like to warmly thank all the staff of the Authority. The Authority is fortunate to have so many talented and motivated collaborators.  
For the Management Committee,  
David Stevens

1.  
About the Authority  
The Data Protection Authority (DPA) is the independent supervisory body regarding the processing of personal data. It is a federal entity with legal personality, established within the House of Representatives.  
The DPA consists of five departments (the General Secretariat, the Frontline Service, the Knowledge Center, the Inspection Service, and the Litigation Chamber) and a Management Committee (art. 12 of the DPA law).  
2019 was our first full year of operation and proved to be a year rich in many changes for the new Data Protection Authority, created by the law of December 3, 2017, as the successor to the former Privacy Commission.  
1.1  
NEW MANAGEMENT COMMITTEE AND NEW MEMBERS  
After a transitional period during which the members of the former Privacy Commission performed the tasks and responsibilities of the DPA, the members of the new Management Committee took their oath on April 24, 2019, before the President of the House of Representatives. The external members of the Knowledge Center and the Litigation Chamber were appointed on April 4, 2019, and April 25, 2019, respectively, by the House of Representatives. Together, they face the legal, economic, ethical, and technological challenges of the transition to a digital society. The members of the Management Committee, the Knowledge Center, and the Litigation Chamber are appointed for a renewable six-year term.  
The presidency is alternately held by the director of the General Secretariat during the first three years of the term and by the director of the Knowledge Center for the second half of the term.

1.3 STRATEGIC PLAN  
During the last quarter of 2019, the Management Committee of the DPA drafted its Strategic Plan 2020-2025 and submitted it for public consultation.  
The text defines the priorities for the next five years and lists the necessary means to carry out an ambitious mission: to guide organizations and citizens towards a digital world in which privacy would be a reality for all.

1.4 COLLABORATORS AND BUDGET  
However, an organization is nothing without human and financial resources. In its Strategic Plan, the Management Committee clearly indicated that it envisioned a significant growth trajectory for the DPA. It is not only about living up to its name of "Authority" in the field (for example, by taking on new tasks such as conducting proactive inspections or imposing sanctions). The DPA must also grow in terms of human and financial resources. Initial measures in this regard were taken in 2019.  
At the end of 2019, 62 collaborators were employed within the DPA; there were 59 at the beginning of the year. New collaborators were hired to better address the new tasks and competencies.  
The DPA intends to continue the phased expansion of its staff framework over the next 4 years. To this end, it has introduced a staggered recruitment plan to the Chamber's Accounting Commission. This plan provides for the minimum additional personnel resources necessary to firmly embed the additional competencies conferred upon it, such as certification, data protection impact assessments, inspections, sanctions, and awareness-raising on the one hand, and the obligation for increased collaboration at the European level on the other. Specifically, an additional 6 FTEs are planned for 2020 and 2021, 4 additional FTEs for 2022, and 2 additional FTEs for 2023 and 2024. The largest increase is expected in the first two years as the new tasks must be assumed in the short term. Subsequently, it is more about organic growth of the organization. As indicated, this is a minimalist growth scenario where the DPA observes, in comparison with other European controllers, namely in the surrounding Western European countries, that they have significantly larger human resources and generally experience a much stronger increase in personnel and budget.  
To finance its operations, the DPA was able to access an operating credit amounting to EUR 8,197,400.00 in 2019. The operating credits were financed by the actual allocation of EUR 6,345,000 (an amount included annually in the general expenditure budget of the Federal Authority), the carryover bonuses from previous years, and other revenues (e.g., the reimbursement of a disputed social security contribution).

3.  
The General Secretariat  
The General Secretariat has a dual role within the DPA. It not only undertakes a number of horizontal support tasks (such as, in particular, 1° managing requests concerning personnel policy, budget, and IT; 2° managing any legal issues related to the management and functioning of the DPA; and 3° managing internal and external communication), but also several important executive tasks, such as, among others:  

- monitoring societal, economic, and technological developments that impact the protection of personal data;  

- providing advice to a data controller in the context of a data protection impact assessment;  

- approving codes of conduct (including granting approval to an organization responsible for monitoring the codes of conduct);  

- promoting the introduction of certification mechanisms and approving certification criteria;  

- approving standard contractual clauses and binding corporate rules.  
In this broad range of competencies, we would like to specifically highlight the following cases for 2019.  
3.1 MONITORING SOCIETAL, ECONOMIC, AND TECHNOLOGICAL DEVELOPMENTS  
The General Secretariat monitors societal, economic, and technological developments that impact the protection of personal data. This is why, during the year 2019, the General Secretariat contacted several data controllers who were about to initiate a new processing activity using new technologies such as biometric recognition. The General Secretariat aims not only to proactively respond to current developments but also to advise data controllers and protect them against the hasty implementation of risky processing activities. If processing has already begun and the General Secretariat finds that necessary steps, such as conducting a data protection impact assessment, have not taken place, the DPA's Management Committee may refer the case to the Inspection Service. Throughout 2019, this competence was used to examine and/or refer to the Inspection, among others, the following cases:  

- A secondary school that wished to scan students' palms for meal and snack payments. After contacting the General Secretariat, the school abandoned the processing;  

- The National Office for Annual Leave (ONVA) which used Facebook to search for and contact beneficiaries. After contacting the General Secretariat, ONVA stopped this practice;  

- A pilot project by a large supermarket chain aimed at enabling payments using a fingerprint. After an initial contact with the General Secretariat, the Inspection Service was involved;  

- A platform for managing and updating customer data based on the electronic identity card. After an initial contact with the General Secretariat, the Inspection Service was involved;  

- Finally, the General Secretariat contacted several data controllers of websites that did not have a certificate for secure server transfer of personal data.

3.2 APPROVAL OF EXXONMOBIL CORPORATION'S BINDING CORPORATE RULES (BCR)  
On November 12, 2019, the European Data Protection Committee adopted a positive opinion regarding the binding corporate rules (BCR) of ExxonMobil Corporation. The DPA acted as the leading supervisory authority and reviewed the first version of these binding corporate rules before submitting this draft to the opinion of the European Data Protection Committee. This was the second time that the European Data Protection Committee issued a positive opinion on a draft of binding corporate rules since the GDPR came into effect on May 25, 2018. On December 4, 2019, the General Secretariat of the DPA adopted the decision to approve the binding corporate rules of ExxonMobil Corporation.  
The binding corporate rules of ExxonMobil Corporation are part of a comprehensive data protection policy that applies to the processing of all personal data within ExxonMobil Corporation. They apply to the personal data of workers, former workers, candidates, contractors, representatives, clients, and other business partners and consumers.  
3.3 ADOPTION OF PROCESSING CATEGORIES UNDER ARTICLE 35(4) OF THE GDPR BY THE DPA  
In order to determine whether a processing project requires the mandatory and prior completion of a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) before its launch, data controllers must take into account the list of processing activities in Article 35.3 of the GDPR and the list of criteria adopted by the Article 29 Working Party. Cumulatively, they must also consider the list of types of processing operations for which a DPIA is required, as adopted by the General Secretariat of the DPA on December 16, 2019, based on Article 35.4 of the GDPR.  
The DPA had submitted a draft list to the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) and took into account its opinion, issued on September 25, 2018, in order to retain a list of 8 categories of processing operations for which data controllers are required to conduct a DPIA. This list replaces the previously attached draft list to Recommendation 01/2018 and is subject to review every 6 months.

3.4 'I DECIDE' ACTIVITIES  
The world is not static; it is constantly evolving. This evolution brings about new opportunities and new challenges. In today's world, technological advancements concerning the processing of personal data hold significant importance, especially for children and young people. The 'I Decide' initiative is therefore based on the principle that the privacy of our children and young people can only be guaranteed if they are aware of it. All 'I Decide' activities aim to show young people how to adopt a respectful attitude towards privacy.  
The main information channel for 'I Decide' is its website www.jedecide.be. This site contains information on personal data protection, tailored to different target groups: young people, parents, and educators. With Gustave the hedgehog, young people learn how to better protect their personal data.  
The year 2019 provided the 'I Decide' team with the opportunity to observe a constant increase in visits to its website as well as inquiries from children and referring adults to our dedicated contact address for 'I Decide'. The topics addressed reveal a continuous need and demand from young citizens, as well as their parents or teachers, to better understand the rules of personal data protection and the tools or tips available to protect their privacy. 'I Decide' thus focused on the necessity of updating certain topics of interest to young people in light of the new rules arising from the GDPR, such as the rules for using mobile applications or configuring websites to navigate while respecting privacy. Our team also continued to disseminate information on this subject to raise awareness among young people and their surroundings about this rich and varied matter that is an integral part of their daily lives.

3.5 SMALL AND MEDIUM ENTERPRISE INITIATIVES  
In 2019, the DPA launched an awareness campaign on the GDPR for Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) to better assist and support them in the implementation of the GDPR. To carry out this campaign, the DPA collaborated with two types of key stakeholders: professional organizations representing SMEs and professional networks of Data Protection Officers ("DPOs"). - Conducting a survey among professional organizations to understand the GDPR-related challenges their SME members face;

2. the data protection impact assessment ("DPIA"); and  
3. the concepts of "data controller" and "processor".  
Having obtained funding, the project will be executed by the DPA in 2020, continuing the SME campaign.

4. The Frontline Service  
The Frontline Service receives requests addressed to the DPA. The Service processes information requests, paying particular attention to the exercise of rights by the data subjects. It mediates, striving to reconcile the parties in pursuit of a solution compliant with data protection law. It receives complaints, examines their admissibility, and informs the complainant if they are not admissible. It promotes data protection to the public, paying specific attention to minors, as well as raising awareness among data controllers and processors of their obligations. When a serious violation is identified, the Frontline Service may propose to the Management Committee to assess the situation so that binding measures can be considered.  
An important second task of the Frontline Service is the promotion of data protection and raising awareness of this issue among the general public and data controllers.  
In 2019, the DPA received funding from the European Commission to raise awareness among citizens about data protection. The aim of the campaign maitrisermesdonnees.be was to inform citizens about their privacy rights and to encourage them to question the information they share about themselves.  
The campaign was introduced by an experience organized with the participation of Ozark Henry. To win one of the 200 spots at an exclusive 3D showcase of Ozark Henry that took place on November 5, interested internet users had to participate in a contest by completing a participation form. Each participant was required to provide some personal data not necessary for the contest: mobile number, preferred music genre, or the last concert they attended. It was only on the evening of the concert that the artist revealed the campaign to the winners present at the showcase. Flyers to inform participants about their privacy rights were also distributed on the evening of the showcase. Other participants (non-winners) were informed about the experience and redirected to our campaign site maitrisermesdonnees.be via a mailing. Thus, all contest participants were prompted to question why they had shared their data.  
The experience with Ozark Henry, as well as our campaign site, allowed us to directly reach thousands of citizens. The campaign also garnered significant attention in the print and audiovisual media throughout the country, and even internationally. Several municipalities also ordered flyers and posters made available on our site maitrisermesdonnees.be.

5.  
The Knowledge Center  
The role of the Knowledge Center, for each legislative or regulatory text that organizes or modifies the processing of personal data, is to ensure:  

- that these processes are organized at the appropriate level of authority (law vs. decree);  

- that the text clearly specifies the essential elements of each processing (the purposes, the identity of the data controller, the categories of data, the data subjects and recipients, the retention period, etc.); and  

- that these processes are necessary and proportionate to the intended objective.  
5.1 CENTRAL MISSION: OPINION  
The central mission of the Knowledge Center is to issue opinions on regulatory projects leading to the collection of personal data, to grant access to personal data in someone's possession, or to communicate this data to another person (= processing of personal data).  
The predictability of a processing for the citizen is essential and must be reflected in a formal legal norm (law, decree, or ordinance) that is subject to debate in Parliament. If the proposed processing has significant consequences for the rights and freedoms of the citizen, the essential elements of the processing, such as the purpose for which the personal data will be processed, the identity of the data controller, the categories of personal data that will be processed, the categories of persons whose data will be processed, and the duration for which this data will be retained, must be established in the formal law. The details can be further developed in an implementing decree. The Knowledge Center ensures that the provisions of the regulation guarantee that personal data is collected, made accessible, or communicated only when necessary to achieve the purpose set by this regulation. The extent to which the opinions of the Knowledge Center influence the final drafting of texts varies, as illustrated by the opinions mentioned below. This is only a selection of the 196 opinions issued in 2019, which can be consulted in their entirety on the website of the DPA.  
5.1.1 Opinion No. 16/2019 of February 6, 2019, regarding a draft royal decree establishing the conditions, procedure, and consequences of the approval of service providers for the electronic exchange of messages via the eBox  
This draft royal decree aimed to implement several articles of the draft law concerning the electronic exchange of messages that was then under review in the House of Representatives. The DPA took advantage of this request for an opinion regarding this draft royal decree to conduct a new critical review of Article 11, § 5 of the draft law. This had already been done previously by the Privacy Protection Commission (see point 36 of opinion No. 47/2018 of May 23, 2018).  
The eBox is a system used for the electronic exchange of messages between public authorities, on the one hand, and businesses, citizens, or other public authorities, on the other hand. Article 11, § 5 of the draft law provided that approved private partners providing the eBox may not access the content of the messages unless this is necessary to enable them to offer an additional service (for example, generating automatic payments) and provided that the service requester has given their explicit and prior consent.

The Knowledge Center has determined that accessing the full content of messages in the eBox to provide an additional service is unacceptable. If compared to traditional mail, this would mean that letters could be opened and read, subject to some sort of general agreement from the individuals concerned, so that bpost could, for example, offer additional services. This goes against the confidentiality of correspondence enshrined in the Constitution and is clearly contrary to the principle of data minimization. Furthermore, there is a real risk that for many citizens, this consent is a formality whose consequences they do not fully grasp.  
The Knowledge Center has proposed an alternative that allows accredited private partners to offer additional services without requiring access to the content of messages. After obtaining explicit and prior consent from the recipient, access could be granted to metadata, that is, information that describes the data without disclosing its content. In this regard, it is important to precisely define the data (by type of message, by public service) so that the citizen understands what information the accredited private partner accesses following their consent.  

5.1.2 Opinion No. 72/2020 of March 20, 2020, regarding a draft decree from Wallonia concerning the accreditation of an organization responsible for implementing the citizen service in Wallonia  
It follows from Article 8, 21° of the draft that the non-profit organizations (ASBL) where a young person performs a citizen service must mandatorily communicate information regarding the activities of the young person in the context of their citizen service to support or assistance organizations (such as FOREM, which grants unemployment benefits, and CPAS, which provides integration income).  
Why must these ASBL provide this data? What information is provided about the young person performing a citizen service? To whom is this information provided? The draft is completely silent on this matter. The Knowledge Center could only form an idea of the answers to these questions based on the additional information it requested.  
It emerged that the information is provided to FOREM and CPAS so that the young person receiving unemployment benefits or integration income does not have to prove their availability for the labor market during their civil service. It is important that this is mentioned in the regulation as it allows for the evaluation of which young individuals need to have information provided (this cannot apply to young people who do not receive unemployment benefits or integration income) and what information can be provided.  
The objective is for the ASBL to provide the following information: the identification data of the young person; the start and end dates of the citizen service; the tasks performed and the skills acquired during the citizen service. Given the purpose, namely not having to prove availability in the labor market, all identification data as well as the start and end dates of the civil service are communicated and must be explicitly mentioned in the regulation. The tasks performed and the skills acquired during the civil service are not relevant and cannot be requested or provided. The text of the draft must reflect this.  
The ASBL should also provide the data of young individuals performing civil service to the General Directorate for Persons with Disabilities. It is not demonstrated in any way how this would be necessary. Based on the available information, the Knowledge Center has deemed this data flow to be superfluous.

5.1.3  Opinion No. 133/2019 regarding a draft law on the municipal administrative approach and establishing a Directorate for Integrity Assessment for Public Authorities  
One of the central themes of this draft law is the improvement of municipalities' capabilities to prevent serious and organized crime. What does this imply? In order to prevent serious and organized crime, municipalities will now be able, through police regulations, to subject the operation of public-access establishments in a specific area to an integrity investigation. Specifically, this means that operators of public-access establishments may be subjected to such an investigation. As part of an integrity investigation, a very intrusive measure, the municipality may seek the opinion of the Directorate for Integrity Assessment for Public Authorities, to be established within the Ministry of the Interior, which may, for this purpose, request data from the police, intelligence and security services, the Financial Information Processing Unit, judicial authorities, and various inspection services.  
This draft law contains various provisions that jeopardize the fundamental freedoms and rights of citizens - potentially resulting in the operator being unable to launch their business or being forced to cease operations - without sufficient guarantees being provided to preserve these rights and freedoms. This initiative has therefore generated a long list of questions and concerns from the perspective of data protection. Here is a selection of the identified issues that can only be resolved by thoroughly revising the draft law.  

- The purpose for which personal data is collected must be determined and explicit. This is not the case. The prevention of serious and organized crime is too general a purpose and leaves room for subjective interpretation, so that the individual does not know precisely why information is being collected about them. At least a reference to the punishable acts targeted should be added. Moreover, when the purpose is not precise enough, it is impossible to define the data that can be collected for this purpose (proportionality).  

- Certain categories of data are formulated in such vague terms and general language that they constitute a blank check on the basis of which any information can be collected, which is unacceptable in light of the principle of proportionality.  

- The designation of individuals about whom information is collected in the context of the integrity investigation is formulated very broadly, allowing not only for the collection of information about an operator of a public-access location but also about many other individuals with whom they are in contact, without those individuals ever being informed.  

- A very broad possibility is provided, both for the Directorate for Integrity Assessment and for municipalities, to share with third parties the information they collect respectively during the advisory mission and the integrity investigation. The text remains silent on the purpose for which this communication may be made and to whom it may be addressed.  

- Any person subject to an integrity investigation is recorded in a municipal integrity investigation file for up to 3 years after the conclusion of the investigation. When an integrity investigation is closed without follow-up, the individual who was the subject of it must be immediately removed from this file. Retention would be disproportionate in light of the purpose.  

- The text introduces an exception to the rights guaranteed by Articles 12 to 22 of the GDPR (right to information, right of access, ...) that is contrary to Article 23.2 of the GDPR.

5.2.1 Recommendation No. 01/2019 of February 6, 2019, regarding the obligation to create a user account with Microsoft to access public service applications

6.  
The Inspection Service  
The law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter the "DPA Law") describes the Inspection Service rather succinctly as "the investigative body of the DPA." In practical terms, this means that the Inspection Service is responsible for examining complaints regarding violations of European and Belgian data protection legislation, including the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter "GDPR") as well as serious indications of such violations.  
The Inspection Service is led by the Chief Inspector, consists of inspectors, and is supported by a secretariat. The inspectors have various profiles (auditors, information security experts, and legal professionals) to enable a multidisciplinary approach. This allows for the analysis of technical, organizational, and legal aspects. An inspector currently manages an average of 35 cases.  
The Inspection Service conducts control activities in a methodical, professional, efficient, and discreet manner through periodic and non-periodic monitoring and thematic inspection missions concerning (inter)national processing in both the public and private sectors, based on a risk-based approach specific to the field of GDPR and related legislation and regulations (for example, camera legislation).  
The Inspection Service has an extensive arsenal of investigative possibilities. To analyze a case, the Chief Inspector and the inspectors may, if deemed necessary, in accordance with the DPA Law:  

- identify individuals;  
- interview individuals;  
- conduct a written inquiry;  
- carry out on-site examinations;  
- consult computer systems and copy the data they contain;  
- access information electronically;  
- seize or seal goods or computer systems;  
- require the identification of the subscriber or usual user of an electronic communication service or the electronic means of communication used.  

The DPA Law provides for various ways to open a case by the Inspection Service:  

- at the initiative of the Management Committee when there are serious indications of an infringement, if cooperation with a foreign data protection authority is necessary, or at the request of a judicial authority or an administrative control body;  
- at the initiative of the Contentious Chamber if a complaint requires an investigation or if further investigation is necessary;  
- at the initiative of the Inspection Service itself if there are serious indications of an infringement.  

In addition to the GDPR and the DPA Law, the Inspection Service exercises additional oversight based on the provisions of Regulation 767/2008 concerning the Visa Information System (hereinafter "VIS"), Regulation No. 1987/2006 on the establishment, functioning, and use of the second-generation Schengen Information System (SIS II) (hereinafter "SIS II"), and Decision 2007/533/JAI on the establishment, functioning, and use of the second-generation Schengen Information System (SIS II).  
To carry out this specific oversight, it is possible to cooperate with other national data protection authorities (for example, the Police Information Control Body, hereinafter "PICB") as well as with international data protection authorities (for example, the EDPS).

6.1 THE MULTIDISCIPLINARY APPROACH ABOVE ALL

From the launch of the Inspection Service, the DPA opted for a multidisciplinary approach to inspection cases. The Inspection Service is therefore composed of inspectors with varied profiles, such as lawyers, auditors, and information security experts. The objective is not only to address cases from a legal perspective but also to go further and include technical, organizational, and other aspects in the analysis of the specific themes that arise.

Several inspectors also have prior experience as DPOs, which undeniably constitutes an advantage for the concrete handling of cases. This exercise is inspired by the Strategic Plan, which specifies, among other things: "The development as the Inspection Service of a comprehensive and objective investigation methodology that takes into account both the legal reality and the technical reality, and does not limit itself to a purely theoretical approach." (and is also reflected in what is developed in point 6.2).

6.2 ATTEMPTING TO ABANDON THE CLASSIC HANDLING OF CASES

The Inspection Service has broad investigative possibilities ranging from classic written inquiries to on-site examinations and interviews with individuals. It can also seize property (for example, information carriers) or place them under seal and impose provisional measures.

The transformation of a commission of predominantly advisory nature into an authority that imposes sanctions requires a significant cultural change, both internally (for the staff) and externally (for example, for the data controller). In 2019, the Inspection Service was a new directorate that still had to discover and clarify these investigative possibilities.

A primary priority was to attempt to abandon the classic way of handling cases by conducting written inquiries. Although sending letters and emails to ask questions as part of an investigation has advantages, it also has disadvantages (for example, the duration of the procedure due to response times). The Inspection Service thus wished to partially abandon this classic approach from the outset and invest more (initially) in on-site examinations and interviews with the individuals concerned. To do this, however, it was necessary to start by developing an internal procedure while giving due attention to the rights of the parties involved.

6.3 INSPECTION VERSUS AUDIT  
National legislation suffers from a gap in its current form as it only addresses the execution of inspections. However, the missions of the Inspection Service are much broader, and audits constitute a significant workload because the DPA has a monitoring/control mission as a supervisory authority within the framework of specific European legislation (which is situated within the "law enforcement" ecosystem).

However, this mission greatly differs from the "national" inspection tasks of the Inspection Service. The audit should be considered both as a control and as coaching/awareness-raising that highlights problematic points in a process, in consultation with the data controller. During a finding in the context of an audit, there is always a negotiation or assertion-response procedure, and a draft report only becomes a final report after an agreement with the data controller and the addition of points or remarks from the data controller, after which the latter receives an action plan with deadlines to address the issues. Only after a potential aggravation can an (audit) report become an (inspection) report to compel the data controller to comply, through the Litigation Chamber.  
To adopt a coordinated approach in this regard, the Inspection Service had to launch an audit program in 2019, which is not limited to 2019 but spans several years, given the considerable workload that these audits represent. Specifically, this launch involved, among other things, participation in an audit mission of a European Member State and the audit of several Belgian embassies in Europe. 7.  
The Contentious Chamber  
The Contentious Chamber is an administrative litigation body of the DPA, composed of external members and its own secretariat.  
The central mission of the Contentious Chamber is to evaluate individual cases. This includes admissible complaints from a citizen, matters submitted to it by the Inspection Service, in certain cases after an investigation initiated by the DPA, as well as matters submitted by authorities from other EEA member states within the framework of the GDPR's one-stop-shop mechanism, for which the DPA is the lead authority or the concerned authority. In this regard, the Contentious Chamber has several instruments at its disposal: corrective measures and administrative fines.  
Although it is an integral part of the DPA and is considered by the judge as an administrative authority, the Contentious Chamber presents certain aspects of a judicial instance. The resolution of disputes and the imposition of sanctions in individual cases diverges from the more administrative tasks of the DPA. External members play an important role, and the procedure is accompanied by guarantees. The procedure before the Contentious Chamber is conducted in accordance with principles of procedural law. The Contentious Chamber is a quasi-judicial body that must contribute to promoting the protection of fundamental rights regarding privacy and data protection. Furthermore, it must ensure that the application of fundamental rights is carried out in compliance with the high standards required by the rule of law.  
As an organ of the DPA, the Contentious Chamber is particularly interested in concrete and relevant technological innovation from a societal perspective, which must obviously occur in respect of citizens' privacy rights. In carrying out its mission, it naturally also takes into account the protection of other (fundamental) rights.  
In 2019, the Contentious Chamber, in its new composition, undertook the task and developed a number of basic principles. First, the accessibility of the complaint procedure, where the complainant is not subjected to excessively high procedural requirements; in this context, the Contentious Chamber decided to dismiss certain cases for reasons of opportunity. Second, efficiency, involving a swift handling of cases, certainly while respecting procedural guarantees, such as the right to contradiction. Third, consistency, involving that the Contentious Chamber, and its president in particular, ensure that decisions are consistent with each other, as well as with the positions of the DPA as a whole. Fourth, transparency, involving that the Contentious Chamber publishes all decisions, even if for many cases, they are anonymized. All these elements will be realized in 2020, notably in execution of the 2020 Management Plan.  
The Contentious Chamber also contributes in another way to the development of the law, by ensuring the defense of appeals against its decisions before the Market Court, which ruled in 2019 in three cases of appeals against such decisions. Furthermore, the Contentious Chamber has also been involved in preliminary questions posed to the Court of Justice of the European Union in an ongoing dispute between Facebook and the DPA.  
Finally, it must be noted that for the Contentious Chamber, 2019 was a year of transition, during which it made its debut, with entirely new competencies for the DPA and very limited resources. The main instrument of the Contentious Chamber to contribute to the development of the law in the field of data protection is the substantive decision. This instrument was fully developed from the first half of 2020.

7.4 FINE FOR CREATING A LOYALTY CARD THROUGH THE READING OF THE ELECTRONIC IDENTITY CARD  
On September 17, 2019, the Litigation Chamber ruled on a complaint regarding the creation of a loyalty card through the reading of the electronic identity card and the use of its data. The defendant refuses to issue the loyalty card if the customer does not wish to use their electronic identity card but only their written data. The Litigation Chamber finds a violation of the data minimization principle (art. 5.1.c of the GDPR), the lawfulness of processing (art. 6 of the GDPR), as well as the requirement to inform the concerned individual (art. 13 of the GDPR). Firstly, irrelevant data is used for the creation of the loyalty card, including the national registry number. It then appears that there is no legal basis for the processing since the complainant, and by extension all customers, can only benefit from discounts through their electronic identity card and that the defendant offers no alternative for the creation of a loyalty card to enjoy this advantage, so there is no question of free consent. Finally, the defendant admits that there are shortcomings in terms of information regarding the GDPR. Following these violations, the Litigation Chamber orders compliance with the respective provisions of the GDPR (art. 100, § 1, 9° of the LCA) and imposes a fine of 10,000 euros (art. 101 of the LCA).  
The defendant appealed this decision to the Market Court, which annulled this decision on February 19, 2020.

9.2 THE ONE-STOP-SHOP MECHANISM  
The one-stop-shop mechanism is at the heart of the GDPR. Any complaint filed with the DPA will not necessarily be handled by the DPA. Depending on the location of the main establishment of the data controller in question, the complaint may be transferred to another European authority with which the DPA, through its Litigation Chamber, will collaborate. Another scenario: a complaint filed against a company that processes data in the context of several establishments in different countries of the Union or whose processing impacts European citizens well beyond just the national citizens of the territory where it is established, also triggers this mechanism. In this second case, the Litigation Chamber also participates in the decision-making process. How? By being informed of the progress of the examination of the complaint, receiving the draft decision, and being able to oppose it for specific reasons. Facebook, Microsoft, Spotify, Sony are not established in Belgium? No matter, their practices denounced by complainants from Spain, Italy, or Slovakia can also be examined by the Litigation Chamber. In other cases, it is the DPA that is the "lead" authority, and its counterparts are invited to comment on its draft decisions. This work, while unfortunately sometimes prolonging the duration of procedures, is rich in insights into the practices and jurisprudence of our European colleagues who, for some, have been applying various corrective measures, including administrative fines, since Directive 95/46/EC. It ultimately contributes, and above all, to the harmonized application of the GDPR.  
In 2019, the Litigation Chamber gained experience in this area and also initiated a major case within the one-stop-shop where the DPA is the lead authority, which resulted in the substantive decision 25/2020.  
The Litigation Chamber also handled a large number of complaints – filed with the DPA – against major online platforms, in which another authority is the lead (often the Irish authority).

9.4	 GLOBAL PRIVACY ASSEMBLY  
The Global Privacy Assembly (GPA) brings together all data protection authorities from around the world each year to exchange views on privacy and data protection. In 2019, the International Conference of Data Protection and Privacy Commissioners (ICDPPC) celebrated its 40th anniversary and was renamed for the occasion "Global Privacy Assembly" to clearly reflect its ambition as a primary forum for privacy and data protection on the international stage.  
This year, the GPA met in Tirana, the capital of Albania. The Director of the Knowledge Center and the President of the Litigation Chamber participated in this meeting, with the former presenting an introduction in a panel on challenges for data protection authorities and delegates, and the latter explaining, during the closed session of all authorities worldwide, how the DPA model can contribute to balanced and independent data protection.  
During the meeting, the DPA supported, as a co-sponsor, the renewal of a resolution on cooperation between competition authorities, consumer protection, and data protection in order to protect consumers in the digital economy. Echoing its strategic objective of "strategic cooperation with partners," it was crucial for the DPA to support this resolution, which highlights the synergy between competition law, consumer rights, and data protection.  
Furthermore, the GPA in Tirana marked the starting point for the DPA's engagement in several thematic working groups. Two working groups deserve mention here:  

- The working group that was created on the occasion of the resolution on the fundamental right to privacy as a lever for other fundamental rights. Within this working group, the Authority contributes to developing a clear discourse on the importance of privacy in strengthening other fundamental rights and on the impact of privacy on democratic processes such as elections. The Litigation Chamber of the DPA has already made some decisions regarding violations of the GDPR in the electoral context.  

- The working group on international cooperation and law enforcement (link). Within this working group, the DPA aims to contribute to the development of a framework to enforce respect for privacy and data protection rights among large multinational organizations. Participating authorities share methods and investigative strategies and also attempt to identify procedural obstacles to international cooperation. During its meeting in Tirana, this working group exchanged best practices regarding the enforcement of rules on some major Internet platforms.  
In the spirit of its Strategic Plan, the DPA thus contributes to the necessary international cooperation to address global issues or disputes with an international dimension.

2.  
Policy for Monitoring and Sanctioning Non-Compliance with Regulations, Information and Assistance in Exercising Rights and Obligations  
The information mission of the DPA is directed at both data controllers and citizens. The main information mission consists of informing the public. This information mission can be both individual (client-oriented responses) and targeted at a specific group (conference) or the general public (website).  
The support regarding client-oriented information encompasses two categories of cases: information cases (where a question is answered) and mediation and complaint cases (where the DPA acts as a mediator between the requester and a third party). In 2019, the DPA opened 5,168 information cases and 331 mediation cases.  
In addition to its information and mediation missions, the DPA also handled 2,131 phone calls and other brief inquiries, for which the response was based on a known position of the DPA.  
The activities carried out in this area are directed not only at the competent public authorities but also at all data controllers. The DPA is also tasked with monitoring and inspecting data controllers, which it carries out by developing recommendations and evaluating the security measures taken.  
In 2019, the DPA opened 77 monitoring cases. Furthermore, since May 25, 2018, it has also been mandatory to notify data breaches to the DPA. Previously, this obligation only applied to telecommunications operators. In 2019, the DPA received 869 notifications of data breaches.  
In these notifications of data breaches, it systematically carried out the required verifications and contacted, if necessary, the parties involved.  
Additionally, the first cases regarding data protection impact assessments (DPIAs) were also opened. In 2019, there were 2 cases of this type.  
Finally, since May 25, 2018, the DPA has also managed a non-public database of declared DPOs.

2.7 DPIA  
In 2019, two cases concerning Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIA) were opened. Both cases concerned:  

- The installation of cameras in the buses and subways of a public transport company to assist drivers in their maneuvers to avoid accidents and to collect evidence in case of an accident. In 2020, the DPA determined that the processing for these two purposes was permitted, provided that the cameras only filmed what was necessary and that technical security measures (such as encryption) were taken to protect the images.  

- The relocation of a significant database that was used for CRM (customer relationship management) purposes to a cloud environment. After consultation with the DPA and a reassessment of risks, the applicant decided to terminate the prior consultation procedure.  

2.8 Q&A  
Question and Answer (Q&A) files are primarily files where the response is based on a known viewpoint of the DPA and where contacts are limited in number. They mainly take the form of phone calls or brief email exchanges. The DPA has no concrete reason to retain the information.  
In 2019, 2,131 files of this kind were processed.
