Think Before Acting: The Impact Analysis Regarding Data Protection  
Like the daily life of most of us, 2021 was marked for the Authority by the fight against COVID or at least by the protection of our privacy within this framework. While at the beginning of the year, the Litigation Chamber announced its first decisions (still concerning at that time, notably, video surveillance at the coast), we intervened on our own initiative at the end of the year regarding potential data leaks within the Bruvax platform and concerning the validation and reading of Covid Safe Tickets via the CovidScan application. Through this proactive approach, the APD aims not only to better protect citizens but also to encourage data controllers to conduct a thorough analysis of their projects before undertaking risky data processing. In many cases, conducting an impact analysis regarding data protection (DPIA) is, in fact, mandatory. This is one of the documents that the APD almost systematically requests during its inspections to have a better understanding of the problematic nature or not of certain data processing activities. It is better to think before acting!  
In 2021, the APD produced well-respected substantive decisions. The quality of the work of the Inspection Service (concerning a hospital network that requested the vaccination status of candidates) was confirmed on appeal by the Litigation Chamber. The Court of Justice also ruled in our favor (at least partially) regarding the fact that exceptional circumstances may justify that, as a supervisory authority, we intervene against a data controller for which we are not, however, the lead authority in a European context.  
2021 was not only marked by COVID or repression. As announced in its Strategic Plan 2020-2025 and in the related management plans, the APD also continued to develop and create new control instruments.  
Thus, shortly before the third anniversary of the entry into force of the GDPR (on May 20, 2021), the APD unanimously approved, along with all 26 European supervisory counterparts, the very first transnational code of conduct. The "EU Cloud CoC" brings together best practices in data protection for cloud service providers to contribute to better protection for all European citizens.  
In addition to repression and new control instruments, our Knowledge Center also issued 249 opinions on legislations and regulations during the year 2021, and the Frontline Service handled 3,735 information requests, 116 mediation requests, and 1,928 complaints from citizens or data controllers.  
Despite sometimes difficult circumstances in 2021, all employees continued to give their best to provide the best possible service to all stakeholders. On behalf of the entire Management Committee, I would like to once again warmly thank them.  
The achievements of the APD reported in this Annual Report partly reflect the contributions of directors who have left the APD, and to whom credit is also due.  
Cédrine Morlière

The Authority  
The Data Protection Authority (DPA) is the independent supervisory body regarding the processing of personal data. It was established by the law of December 3, 2017 (hereinafter: "LCA"). It has a much broader range of actions than its predecessor, the Privacy Protection Commission (the "Privacy Commission"). The DPA is a federal body with legal personality, established with the House of Representatives.  

Powers of the DPA  
The general mission of the DPA is defined by Article 4 of the LCA as "the supervision of compliance with the fundamental principles of personal data protection, within the framework of this law and laws containing provisions relating to the protection of personal data processing."  
The DPA is therefore competent not only for monitoring compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter: "GDPR") or general legislation related to data protection (such as the law of July 30, 2018, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data, hereinafter: "LTD"), but it can also oversee compliance with other more specific legislations and regulations, such as legislation regarding cameras.  
However, the DPA's oversight does not cover processing carried out by courts and tribunals as well as by the public prosecutor in the exercise of their judicial functions. The DPA is also not competent for the processing of personal data carried out by police services.  
The DPA is competent regarding actors and bodies in both the public and private sectors and has the ability to impose administrative sanctions autonomously.  

Composition of the DPA  
According to Article 7 of the LCA, the DPA is composed of a Management Committee, a General Secretariat, a Frontline Service, a Knowledge Center, an Inspection Service, and a Litigation Chamber. Both the Litigation Chamber and the Knowledge Center also include six external members who contribute their expertise to the work and deliberations of these bodies.

The Management Committee  
The powers and activities of the Management Committee are outlined in Article 9 of the LCA and include, in addition to the approval of the annual accounts, decisions regarding the annual budget, internal organization and composition, as well as the annual management plans of the DPA.  
The Management Committee also has significant operational responsibility in the area of citizens' privacy, as in accordance with Article 63, 1° of the LCA, it can decide to refer to the Inspection Service if it finds serious indications of the existence of a practice that may lead to a violation of the fundamental principles of personal data protection. In 2021, the Management Committee exercised this power 8 times.  
In 2021, the Management Committee met 25 times.

Enforcement of Legal Provisions  
In 2021, the Contentious Chamber paid significant attention to the consistent execution of its missions and continued to develop its methodology. The mission of the Contentious Chamber comprises three elements.  
First, the Contentious Chamber offers, as an organ of the supervisory authority, an efficient and accessible procedure. The Contentious Chamber has developed procedures to handle complaints in order to remain as accessible as possible, while still respecting basic procedural guarantees such as the right to defense and the right to contradiction.  
Second, the Contentious Chamber contributes to providing a coherent explanation of the GDPR through its substantive decisions, in which the Contentious Chamber addresses not only a question submitted in a complaint or an "initiative" report from the Inspection Service, but also provides its interpretation of the GDPR and other legislations. The Contentious Chamber has been able to develop a coherent "jurisprudence" and render several important decisions, among which is case 04/2021 (X v. SA S.N.P.M.E., concerning marketing, loyalty and transparency, the data protection officer, in which the appeal filed was declared admissible but unfounded), which has generated significant interest among commentators.  
Third, the Contentious Chamber collaborates in the oversight and sanctioning of non-compliance with regulations in cross-border cases within the Union. The intervention of the APD as a lead authority in cross-border cases allows the Contentious Chamber to develop its working methods in collaboration with other authorities. The contribution of the Contentious Chamber to the efficient application of European regulations is realized through the handling of individual cases, initiated either by the APD itself or by its counterparts within the European cooperation mechanism. The Contentious Chamber also actively contributes to the development of European guidelines within the framework of the EDPB.  
The development of new methodologies occurs against a backdrop of an enormous number of complaints, forcing the Contentious Chamber to establish priorities and adjust them continuously. Furthermore, in the context of handling complaints, it must apply national legislation that is incomplete and/or imprecise on certain points.  
In 2021, in addition to its ongoing activities, the Contentious Chamber also took the first steps in developing a methodology for handling new types of cases, such as "mass complaints" (thematic cases with a large number of data controllers) and cases...

Headline  
A New Year Under the Sign of Covid-19  
Just like in 2020, the year 2021 was particularly marked by the fight against Covid-19. In terms of privacy, vaccination and the related data processing constituted the main challenge of the year. Once again, the Data Protection Authority (DPA) was called upon to express its opinion on the implementation of data processing that would have been difficult to imagine before the pandemic, such as the establishment of the Covid Safe Ticket (CST).  
Finding a way to preserve public health and the hospital system while ensuring compliance with the protection of personal data was not always an easy exercise. In this regard, the DPA particularly emphasized the requirements of necessity and proportionality of the measures imposed to combat the coronavirus. Indeed, the data processed in the context of Covid-19 measures primarily consisted of health data, which are very sensitive, and the DPA was particularly vigilant to ensure that the necessary safeguards for their protection were provided. In fact, it sent a letter in 2021 to all Belgian governments and parliaments requesting that the measures taken in the fight against the coronavirus fully respect the fundamental principles of data protection.  
The year 2021 also marked the beginning of decisions developed in enforcement cases related to Covid-19, the very first concerning the establishment of a camera system at the coast to measure attendance.  
The DPA also provided numerous opinions to parliamentarians and governments regarding normative texts establishing data processing by the State in the context of the health crisis (such as the establishment of the CST, the introduction of short-time work for vaccination, or the creation of a vaccination obligation for healthcare staff). Finally, it increased its vigilance, particularly reacting to potential security breaches in systems processing health data such as the CovidScan application and the Bruvax platform.  
Several control cases related to the coronavirus have been opened and are currently under review by the Inspection Service and/or the Litigation Chamber of the DPA.  
Finally, the DPA also continued to raise awareness among citizens and data controllers by updating its Covid-19 thematic file and creating a dedicated page for vaccination.  

Opinions  
Since the beginning of the coronavirus pandemic, numerous normative texts have been drafted and implemented to frame various types of measures, generally temporary, aimed at limiting the spread of the virus, relieving the hospital system, or assisting citizens and organizations impacted by the coronavirus.  
In accordance with Article 36.4 of the GDPR, the Authority must be consulted prior to the implementation of any legislative and regulatory standard of general scope that creates, modifies, or conditions the processing of personal data. The DPA

reminded this obligation in a letter sent to all parliaments and the government of Belgium on February 3, 2021.  
The APD issued a series of opinions in 2021 regarding such draft normative texts. The complete list of these opinions can be consulted on the APD website (see the "Opinions related to Covid-19" page).  
In particular, it issued 9 opinions regarding the introduction and use of the Covid Safe Ticket (CST): opinions 124/2021, 163/2021, 164/2021, 170/2021, 180/2021, 232/2021, 244/2021, 245/2021, and 246/2021.  
In these opinions, the APD systematically reminded that the requirement for any person to prove, through the presentation of the CST, either that they have been vaccinated, or that they have just undergone a test that turned out negative, or that they have recovered from Covid-19 in order to access places and activities, including places and activities related to daily life, constitutes a particularly significant interference with the right to respect for private life and the right to the protection of personal data.  
Certainly, rights and freedoms are not absolute and can be limited if necessary in a democratic society to achieve a public interest objective, such as the preservation of the hospital system. However, it must be demonstrated (1) that the CST effectively allows for the creation of safer places with a lower risk of virus transmission, in order to avoid overwhelming the hospital system, while preventing further closures of specific sectors, (2) that there are no less intrusive means to achieve this objective, and (3) that the benefits provided by the CST outweigh the disadvantages and risks (including the risk of habituation and social normalization of behaviors that infringe fundamental rights as well as the risk of "slipping" into a surveillance society) that it introduces. The APD also reminded that it is not sufficient to assume the effectiveness, necessity, and proportionality of the CST, but that it must be shown, with sufficiently established factual and concrete elements, that the measure is very likely effective, necessary, and proportionate. Furthermore, effectiveness, necessity, and proportionality must be regularly reassessed taking into account the evolution of the health situation and related scientific knowledge. In its opinions, the APD repeatedly invited public authorities to justify, with sufficiently established factual and concrete elements, the effectiveness, necessity, and proportionality of resorting to the CST.  
Moreover, beyond the substantive remark concerning the very principle of resorting to the CST, the APD also made more specific remarks regarding the normative framework for the use of the CST. For example, it emphasized, on several occasions, that a legislative norm had determined the specific circumstances and conditions under which the use of the CST was imposed (no delegation to the executive power regarding the determination of essential elements of the treatment, such as the notion of "mass event" or "private gathering"). The APD also insisted that the decision to impose the CST must be made by political authorities and cannot therefore be left to the discretion of private individuals (organizers and operators of certain places and events).

Control of Compliance with Regulations  
In addition to the ex ante approach to new measures, the Data Protection Authority (DPA) also has a task of oversight regarding the specific processing activities that take place in the context of the fight against Covid-19.  
At the beginning of 2021, the DPA published its very first decision in a case related to COVID-19. This case concerned the installation of a camera system on the Belgian coast to measure crowd density. It had been initiated in 2020, at which time the measure was announced by the autonomous provincial company "Westtoer." In February 2021, the DPA therefore issued a reprimand to Westtoer and concluded that cameras measuring crowd density in the context of the fight against Covid-19 could be installed, but only under very strict conditions and provided that the strict minimum of personal data is processed.  
During the year, it was also called upon to rule on a series of complaints regarding requests sent to the Agency for a Quality Life (AVIQ), demanding the deletion of identification and contact data used for sending vaccination invitations against Covid-19. In its decision, the DPA reminded that the right to data erasure is not absolute and only applies in certain cases, for example, if the data has been subject to unlawful processing. It found that in this case, the processing by AVIQ was based on the cooperation agreement of March 12, 2021, in the context of organizing the vaccination campaign, and that AVIQ was therefore entitled not to respond favorably to the erasure requests from the complainants.  
2021 was also the year in which the Litigation Chamber ruled for the very first time on an appeal against a provisional measure imposed by the Inspection Service, in the context of a case related to vaccination against Covid-19. The Litigation Chamber indeed confirmed a measure aimed at temporarily suspending the verification of the vaccination status of candidates for recruitment in a hospital network. In this case, the DPA indicated that, although it understood the motivation of the hospital network, health data, due to their sensitive nature, are subject to a strict legal framework, and it was its duty to enforce it.  
In 2021, the Inspection Service and the Litigation Chamber of the DPA examined the temperature checks implemented at the airports of Zaventem and Charleroi to combat the coronavirus.  
These cases had been initiated on the DPA's own initiative, following reports in the press. It had also expressed its concern regarding the use of this type of technology.  
During its analysis, the DPA found that the airports lacked a valid legal basis for processing data related to the temperature of travelers, particularly since these are health data. Furthermore, it observed shortcomings in terms of information provided to travelers and the quality of impact assessments. In addition to data processing related to thermal cameras, processing of special categories of data (health data) collected, in particular, through questionnaires filled out by passengers subjected to a secondary check is also covered by the decision in the case related to Zaventem airport. Minor shortcomings were also noted regarding the register of processing activities of Charleroi airport.

Themes of the Year  
Direct Marketing  
Direct marketing represents one of the sectors identified as a priority in the strategic plan of the Data Protection Authority (DPA). The significance of the challenge it poses is proportional to the number of actors who resort to the processing of personal data in order to carry out direct marketing communications daily to millions of people in Belgium, who, in turn, wish to be able to understand and effectively exercise their rights.  
The DPA's frontline service was heavily solicited during the year 2021 for requests for information, mediations, and complaints regarding the processing of personal data related to direct marketing activities.  
In 2021, the DPA sanctioned a "data broker" for the first time. It imposed a fine of 50,000 euros on the company Family Service. This company distributes the well-known "pink boxes" to young parents, which include samples, special offers, and information sheets for expectant mothers.  
In addition to failing to meet its transparency obligations by not clearly and understandably informing its clients that their data was being sold/rented to third parties, the company shared, without valid consent, data concerning more than 1 million clients and their children with commercial partners. Given the number of individuals affected (approximately 21.10% of the Belgian population), the severity of the violation, and the nature of the data processed (notably data concerning children), the DPA's Litigation Chamber decided that a relatively high sanction was warranted. In this decision, the DPA emphasized that it is essential for data brokers, due to their invasive business model, to operate with complete transparency, so that citizens can maintain control over the data concerning them.  
The Litigation Chamber also ruled on several cases concerning requests to oppose direct marketing communications to which companies did not respond, or at least not satisfactorily. This is the case, for example, in its decisions 19/2021, 59/2021, 80/2021, 81/2021, 91/2021, 137/2021. It also expressed itself in cases specifically regarding requests about the source of the data used in direct marketing (decisions 32/2021 and 53/2021).  
In 2021, the DPA also exercised its monitoring powers by contacting companies that processed data to send direct marketing communications on two occasions. Sensitive Data  
Citizens are increasingly confronted with the processing of biometric data. Such data (for example, fingerprints or iris scans) are particularly sensitive (they fall under the "special categories of data" as defined in Art. 9.1 GDPR): they are indeed unique, and their processing can pose significant risks to individuals. This is why the DPA has made biometric data a priority in its Strategic Plan 2020-2025 and has decided to draft this recommendation.  
In this recommendation, the DPA focuses on:  
- the principles related to biometrics;  
- the relevant principles regarding data protection; and  
- how these principles apply to such processing.  
The DPA's analysis reveals that, as there is no legal standard in Belgian law allowing for the processing of biometric data in the context of person authentication, in all cases where explicit consent cannot be obtained, such processing currently takes place without a legal basis.

Children and Youth: www.jedecide.be  
The world is not static; it is in constant evolution. This evolution brings about new possibilities and new challenges. In today's world, technological advancements regarding the processing of personal data hold significant importance, especially for children and young people. The 'I Decide' initiative is therefore based on the idea that the privacy of our children and young people can only be guaranteed if they are aware of this issue. All 'I Decide' activities aim to show young people how to adopt a respectful attitude towards privacy. The main information channel of 'I Decide' is its website www.jedecide.be. This site contains information on personal data protection, tailored to different target groups: young people, parents, and educators. With Gustave the hedgehog, young people learn how to better protect their personal data.  
Unfortunately, the year 2021 did not signify a regained freedom. Due to the coronavirus crisis, 'I Decide' delved deeper into a theme about which teachers, as well as parents and students themselves, had many questions: distance learning. Distance learning is often organized using e-learning platforms, and many students are monitored through software to ensure they do not cheat during an online quiz or exam.  
E-learning is a generic term that refers to any software or electronic technological means used to support teaching activities or to facilitate communication between teachers and students or among teachers. There are various tools, ranging from platforms designed to access educational resources and conduct tests (electronic learning environments such as Smartschool, Moodle, etc.) to software that allows for interactive communication (for example, Microsoft Teams, Google Edu, but also PollEverywhere, Kahoot!, etc.). The use of tablets or computers in class, on which students can complete exercises or play educational games, is also part of what is called "e-learning." E-learning platforms often allow for the creation of a "virtual classroom" where teachers can share educational content, organize quizzes, and communicate with their students. Alongside the many advantages of e-learning, the use of these tools also carries risks. To mitigate these risks, 'I Decide' has formulated points of attention for schools in the form of a thematic text on its website regarding the use of such e-learning platforms. For the target group of young people (students), 'I Decide' has also created a thematic page on its website that explains to young people how their school must guarantee their privacy when e-learning platforms are used.  
The monitoring of online quizzes and exams is sometimes referred to as "proctoring." In the context of online exam monitoring, the student takes an exam at home. During this time, software checks whether the student is cheating during the exam. There are several ways to organize the monitoring of online exams. For instance, the teacher can perform a visual check via the student's webcam during the exam. This can be done during the actual exam or afterward by reviewing a recording. Another way to conduct online exam monitoring is to simultaneously observe what is happening on the student's computer screen. Opinion 46/2021 on the draft decree of the Wallonia-Brussels Federation regarding the implementation and monitoring of electronic surveillance  
This draft aims to organize the management of the placement of electronic surveillance mandates by the competent service. This project was developed following the community assignment of this competence and organizes the entry into force of the provisions (of the law of May 17, 2006) related to the new regime of electronic surveillance of offenders sentenced to one or more custodial sentences of less than three years. This project follows the disappearance of the (federal) mechanisms that previously allowed for the management of placements under electronic surveillance based on the actual placement possibilities.  
The project lists the purposes, the persons concerned, and the data but fails to link them to the envisaged data processing. As a result, it becomes impossible to understand what will be done concretely with this data, which theoretically leaves the door open to abuses such as, for example, the processing of data related to the ethnic origin or sexual orientation of the social service workers involved.  
The Authority therefore reminds that it is essential to distinguish, for each purpose, which processing can be carried out on which category of data and by which data controller, in order to (i) allow the citizen to understand the data processing that is envisaged and thus ensure the predictability of these processes.

Opinion 184/2021 regarding a draft law amending the law of July 10, 2006, on threat analysis  
The draft submitted for opinion to the Knowledge Center concerned the rules governing the exchange of data (including requests for joint threat assessments) between the Coordination Body for Threat Analysis (OCAM) and, in particular, its support services (the Treasury, the Crisis Center, the Secularism and Cults Service of the SPF Justice, etc.), in the context of the fight against extremism and terrorism.  
Within the limits of its competence, the Authority considered that the data processing provided for by the draft entailed a particularly significant interference with the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned: particular categories of data are processed long-term, in a largely secret context where the rights of the individuals concerned are significantly limited, with a view to establishing an assessment - a profile - of the threat that individuals may represent. This assessment will then be communicated to public authorities other than intelligence and security services. If poorly conducted and/or misused, this same assessment is likely to cause irreparable harm to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned.  
In substance, particularly considering the proposed extension of OCAM's mission, the Authority considered that the draft provided for a possibility, at times disproportionate and at times insufficiently predictable, of information exchanges between the concerned institutions. A draft law in this matter must clearly identify the essential elements of the data processing that may be involved in the collaboration between OCAM and its support services, and the appropriate safeguards required, particularly for the processing of personal data revealing political opinions and religious or philosophical beliefs, in a secret context.

Inspection Service  
The law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter the "LCA") describes the Inspection Service rather summarily as "the investigative body of the Data Protection Authority." In practical terms, this means that the Inspection Service is responsible for examining complaints regarding violations of European and Belgian data protection legislation, including the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter the "GDPR"), as well as serious indications of such violations.  
The Inspection Service is led by General Inspector Peter Van den Eynde and is composed of inspectors. The inspectors have different profiles (auditors, information security experts, and legal professionals) to ensure a multidisciplinary approach. This allows for the analysis of technical, organizational, and legal aspects. On average, an inspector had about 20 cases under their responsibility in 2021. Compared to the previous year, this figure remains more or less stable.  
The Inspection Service carries out its control activities independently, in a planned, efficient, and discreet manner. To this end, it conducts (non) periodic monitoring as well as inspection missions regarding (inter)national processing in the public and private sectors, taking into account relevant risks, under the GDPR and related legislation and regulations concerning data protection (for example, legislation related to cameras).  
The Inspection Service has an extensive arsenal of investigative possibilities. To analyze a case, the General Inspector and the inspectors may, if deemed necessary and in accordance with the LCA:  
- identify individuals;  
- interview individuals;  
- conduct a written inquiry;  
- carry out on-site examinations;  
- consult computer systems and copy the data they contain;  
- access information electronically;  
- seize or seal goods or computer systems;  
- require the identification of the subscriber or the usual user of an electronic communication service or the electronic means of communication used.  
The LCA provides for various ways to open a case by the Inspection Service. A case may be opened:  
- at the initiative of the Management Committee when there are serious indications of an infringement, if cooperation with a foreign data protection authority is necessary, or at the request of a judicial authority or an administrative control body;  
- at the initiative of the Litigation Chamber if a complaint requires an investigation or if a supplementary investigation is necessary;  
- at the initiative of the Inspection Service itself if there are serious indications of an infringement.

Contentious Chamber  
The Contentious Chamber in Outline  
The Contentious Chamber is the administrative contentious body of the APD (Article 32 of the LCA) and is tasked with intervening in a repressive manner in cases submitted to it, based on a complaint from a citizen or following an inspection initiated by the APD. The Contentious Chamber also deals with cases submitted to the APD by the authorities of European member states within the framework of the single contact point mechanism established by the GDPR, where the APD acts as the lead authority or concerned authority. In this regard, the Contentious Chamber has several instruments at its disposal: corrective measures and administrative fines. The procedure before the Contentious Chamber is conducted in accordance with the principles of procedural law.  
The Contentious Chamber is composed, in addition to its president, of six external members who are appointed by the Chamber based on their particular expertise. The Contentious Chamber has a unique structure that does not exist among market regulators in Belgium or other EU supervisory authorities. This structure has a hybrid character. The Contentious Chamber is a supervisory body, but the procedure is almost judicial, particularly with decision-making occurring after the exchange of conclusions between the parties and often also after a hearing. This structure has proven effective, especially due to the decisions that the Contentious Chamber has made in the meantime, which are available on the APD's website. The Contentious Chamber thus made 34 substantive decisions in 2020. The procedure allows for impartial rulings and respects legal guarantees.  

Language Policy of the Contentious Chamber  
The procedure of the Contentious Chamber, as an administrative authority, is defined by the general principles of good governance. This means that the law of June 15, 1935, concerning the use of languages in judicial matters does not apply since the Contentious Chamber is not an official court.  
Article 57 of the law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter "the LCA") states that the APD uses the language in which the procedure is conducted according to the specific needs of the case. Due to the applicability of this specific provision adapted to the APD, the Contentious Chamber has always assumed, since its creation, that it is also not subject to the laws of July 18, 1966, on the use of languages in administrative matters (hereinafter "the language laws").  
The Contentious Chamber regularly examines cases in which the complainant and the defendant are established in different language regions. However, given that the LCA formulates a general rule regarding the use of languages and considering the increasing number of 'multilingual' cases, the Contentious Chamber has decided to develop its own note, which it has been using since January 2021 (hereinafter "the Language Policy Note").

Accessible Complaint Procedure  
In accordance with the intention of the European and national legislator, a complaint procedure must be accessible. To avoid any potential language barrier for the complainant and assuming that the complainant is proficient in the language of their linguistic region, the Contentious Chamber therefore generally uses the language of the linguistic region where the complainant is established or resides. If the complainant is located in the bilingual region of Brussels-Capital, the language is chosen by the complainant (the language in which the complaint is filed).  
However, if the defendant is a public authority, the procedure will be conducted in the language of the linguistic region where it is established. Belgian administrative law indeed stipulates that the language of the region is the official language of a public authority, in which a citizen must communicate with it.  

Determination of the Language of the Procedure and Options for the Defendant  
The Note on Language Policy provides that the language of the procedure is established and communicated to the parties as part of the exchanges concerning the procedure. If a defendant opposes the established language of the procedure, it is possible, under certain circumstances, to deviate from the principle of the complainant's linguistic region. The possible criteria that may play a role in the Contentious Chamber's assessment based on such a motivated objection are as follows:  
- the position of the defendant (individual, small business or large enterprise, public authority);  
- the position of the complainant (does the complainant have a direct interest in the measure to be imposed?);  
- the abuse of the possibility of objection in order to complicate the procedure;  
- other specific circumstances in the case.  
In its ruling 2021-AR-320 of July 7, 2021, the Market Court, however, adopted the view that the APD, as an autonomous public service with legal personality whose responsibilities extend throughout the country, was subject to Articles 40 to 45 of the language laws. In principle, the parties therefore have the right to express themselves in the national language of their choice. The Market Court particularly specifies that "depriving the defendant of their language may constitute a violation of the rights of the defense" [Translator's Note: freely translated by the translation service of the General Secretariat of the APD in the absence of an official translation].  
Furthermore, the Market Court questions the principle of the complainant's language, given that it would not constitute prima facie a sufficiently objective criterion for determining the language of the investigation and the language of the draft decision.  

Issues  
Despite the fact that the viewpoint adopted by the Market Court is contrary to the opinion of the Permanent Commission for Language Control (CPCL), which has already concluded in its Annual Report 2018 that the APD is not subject to the language laws, the Contentious Chamber believes that one cannot also renounce the linguistic unity of the procedure.
Annual Report 2021  
45  
Indeed, there can only be one official language of the procedure per case, not only to ensure the proper functioning of the Contentious Chamber but also to guarantee the principle of procedural economy. Translations often involve high costs and inevitably lead to longer processing times for cases.  
Moreover, language laws do not require administrative authorities to ensure the translation of documents exchanged by the parties during the procedure. According to the reasoning of the Market Court, the parties will eventually have to defend themselves against arguments submitted in another national language. One may rightly wonder whether such a procedure will necessarily benefit the respect for the right to defense - which applies to both defendants and complainants.  

Status Quo  
In the absence of further explanations or a more appropriate solution from the legislator or the Market Court, the Contentious Chamber generally uses the language of the complaint as the starting point for its procedure.  
However, depending on the circumstances of the case, the Contentious Chamber may allow the parties to conclude in their own language if they provide a sufficiently motivated objection against the language of the procedure, as indicated in the invitation to submit conclusions.  
On the other hand, documents submitted by the parties are translated exclusively at the request of the opposing party, subject to the prior agreement of the Contentious Chamber.  

The Policy of Dismissal  
The Contentious Chamber decides on the follow-up it gives to the case and has the power to dismiss a complaint (Article 95, § 1, 4° of the law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority). The Contentious Chamber has made its criteria for dismissal transparent through the dismissal policy published for the year 2021-2022. The president of the Contentious Chamber defines the criteria for prioritizing complaints based on the effectiveness of the intervention of the Contentious Chamber. Dismissal is a necessary management tool given the large volume of complaints received by the Contentious Chamber. Once dismissed, complaints can contribute to determining future initiative investigations by the Inspection Service or future priorities of the DPA within its strategic plan or management plan.

DPA (2021/AR/1044 - available only in Dutch), in which the Court (re)evaluated the compliance with the information obligation under Articles 12.3 and 12.4 of the GDPR by the first named party, is an example. In this same judgment, the Court also ruled that in accordance with the precautionary principle, the Contentious Chamber must verify for each complaint filed what the real motive of the complainant is and whether it does not constitute an abuse of the GDPR complaint procedure by the complainant. The DPA filed a cassation appeal against the aforementioned judgment in 2021.  
Other decisions of the Contentious Chamber were confirmed in their entirety by the Market Court in 2021. This was notably the case for the substantive decision 04/2021 of January 27, 2021. The aforementioned decision concerned the processing of personal data by the National Service for the Promotion of Children's Products (Family Service) in the context of the well-known "pink boxes" - gift boxes with promotional items - that are offered to young mothers and which allow, via a card to be completed, the collection of personal data from the data subject (notably for transmission to business partners). Based on Article 83 of the GDPR, the Contentious Chamber imposed an administrative fine of 50,000 euros on the data controller and ordered compliance with the processing.  
The aforementioned sanction and corrective measure were imposed due to the lack of transparent information regarding the disputed processing and the transfer of personal data to business partners (Art. 5.1.a) and 13 of the GDPR), the excessively long retention period of the collected personal data (Art. 5.1.c) in conjunction with Art. 25 of the GDPR), and the absence of free, specific, informed, and unequivocal consent, and thus the legality of the processing (Art. 6.1.a) of the GDPR). By judgment of July 7, 2021, the Market Court declared the appeal filed admissible but unfounded and confirmed the decision of the Contentious Chamber.  
The DPA filed a cassation appeal against one of the annulment judgments issued by the Market Court in 2021.  
In 2021, the Court of Cassation also ruled in the context of an appeal previously filed by the DPA. On October 7, 2021, the Court specifically overturned the judgment of the Market Court that annulled decision 06/2019 of the Contentious Chamber. The aforementioned case concerned a complaint filed by an individual against a company because the latter required customers to have their electronic identity card read in order to obtain a loyalty card and to be able to

The Court of Cassation ruled that the statement by the Market Court that "the complainant had not presented an electronic identity card in this case and therefore no processing of her data had taken place and that the applicants had thus demonstrated no actual violation of personal data" could not convincingly justify the annulment ruling in law. The One-Stop-Shop Mechanism  
In cases involving cross-border data processing, the Contentious Chamber cooperates with other European data protection authorities, as provided for by the one-stop-shop system. Cross-border processing is involved when a data controller (or a processor) with establishments in several EU member states processes data of individuals residing in different member states, or when the data controller is established in only one member state but the processing in question is likely to have consequences for individuals residing in several member states.  
The system allows every citizen to file a complaint in their language with the data protection authority of their country, and that authority remains their point of contact throughout the procedure. This mechanism ensures that each data controller has only one data protection authority as an interlocutor, for the purpose of effective monitoring of compliance with the GDPR.

In any matter falling under the single window system, a European authority will be designated as the "lead authority" according to the criteria defined in the GDPR, in order to examine a complaint and make a decision. The authority to which the complaint has been submitted, as well as other interested authorities, retain a right of oversight and may raise objections or comments on the draft decision of the lead authority that will be submitted to them. If the authorities fail to reach an agreement, the discussion will be submitted to the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), which will resolve the contentious points.  
The Litigation Chamber has made several decisions within this cooperation system based on complaints that have been forwarded to it by its European counterparts. Most of the activities related to the following decisions took place during the year 2021, but led to decisions that were finalized and pronounced in 2022. The Litigation Chamber notably made the following decisions:  

- Decision 21/2022 regarding the Transparency & Consent Framework of IAB Europe (on advertising based on browsing habits), a process in which 21 other European data protection authorities participated, given the implications of the relevant processing for citizens in their respective Member States.  
- Decision 11/2022 (regarding cookies) in which a common position was reached after objections were raised by a concerned authority.  
- Decision 13/2022 (regarding online political profiling) which was also made after consultation based on the European cooperation mechanism.  
The Litigation Chamber also forwarded complaints submitted to the DPA but which fell under the jurisdiction of another data protection authority, based on the criteria defined in the GDPR.
