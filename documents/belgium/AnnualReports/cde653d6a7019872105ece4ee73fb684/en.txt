I am pleased to reflect below on a significant sanction decision made in collaboration with our European counterparts from the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) which ultimately strengthens the protection of citizens, particularly minors. These European actions gained momentum in 2023 with tangible results such as a binding decision made on August 2, 2023, against TikTok, accompanied by a fine of 345 million euros imposed by the Irish Data Protection Authority on this platform. The focus was on a deceptive design technique used to the detriment of privacy protection and the overall protection of the youngest among us. The aim was to compel TikTok to stop encouraging minors to publish their personal data on the public part of the site.  
At the same time, as a preventive action, the DPA reminded of the rules regarding information transparency, including the deceptive design techniques that must be prohibited, linking the EDPB guidelines on this matter with the DPA's enforcement actions.  
As illustrated by this example, in addition to these necessary enforcement actions, it is important for us to promote - as much as possible - a good balance between our coercive and preventive actions. We committed to this in our 2023 management plan, and we have continued to pursue this ideal vision in 2025. The trend is set with, for example, the publication at the end of 2023 of a checklist on "cookies and other tracking techniques" in addition to the decisions made in this area, as well as our coordinated clarifications regarding electoral advertising at the end of 2023/beginning of 2024.  
After the initial years of implementing the GDPR marked by a natural emphasis on the new sanctioning tasks of the DPA, the new cross-cutting ambition of the DPA Management Committee, which has been composed of three members since July 2022 and expanded to five in 2023, has been:  
1. To quickly identify and prioritize recurring themes in terms of complaints to enable the services responsible for preventive actions to anticipate these issues and provide timely reminders of the rules;  
2. To offer citizens and all stakeholders a coordinated view of the applicable rules in the areas identified as priorities in our 2023 management plan ("cookies," "elections");  
3. After committing sufficient human resources to carry out the DPA's core missions, to include citizens and stakeholders as much as possible in discussions related to data protection at the stage of constructive dialogue with the DPA on the cross-cutting priorities defined by the DPA (preparation in 2023 and organization in March 2024 of a "smart cities" conference);  
4. To reserve for the Litigation Chamber the disputes that deserve the most attention: in addition to the work carried out in this regard within the Litigation Chamber itself, I want to emphasize the strengthening of the role of the Inspection Service and the Frontline Service prior to any contentious decision.  
This last point of action will, as expected and in any case, be facilitated by the vote at the end of December 2023 by the federal parliament of a new law amending the law organizing the DPA, which is the focus of all our attention and energy in view of its entry into force on June 1, 2024. This includes enabling the DPA to define even more effective and coordinated organizational modes. A key instrument for this purpose, the new Internal Rules of Procedure of the DPA was published at the beginning of June 2024.

The Authority
The Data Protection Authority (APD) is the independent supervisory body in matters of personal data processing. It was established by the law of December 3, 2017 (hereinafter: "LCA"). It has a much broader range of action than its predecessor, the Privacy Protection Commission (the "Privacy Commission"). The APD is a federal body with legal personality, established with the Chamber of Representatives.

Competencies of the APD
The general mission of the APD is defined by Article 4 of the LCA as "the monitoring of compliance with the fundamental principles of personal data protection, within the framework of this law and laws containing provisions relating to the protection of personal data processing." The APD is therefore, in principle, competent not only for monitoring compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter: "GDPR") or general legislation relating to data protection (such as the law of July 30, 2018, relating to the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data, hereinafter: "LTD"), but it can also exercise control over compliance with other more specific legislations and regulations, such as legislation relating to cameras. However, the APD's oversight does not extend to processing carried out by courts and tribunals as well as by the public prosecutor in the exercise of their judicial function. The APD is also not competent for personal data processing carried out by police services. The APD is competent with respect to actors and bodies in the public and private sectors and has the ability to impose administrative sanctions autonomously.

Composition of the APD
According to Article 7 of the LCA, the APD is composed of a Management Committee, a General Secretariat, a Frontline Service, a Knowledge Center, an Inspection Service, and a Litigation Chamber. Both the Litigation Chamber and the Knowledge Center also include six external members who contribute their expertise to the work and deliberations of these bodies. The Management Committee of the APD has been fully constituted again since June 26, 2023, with the appointment of Anne-Charlotte Recker, Director of the Frontline Service, and Koen Gorissen, Director of the General Secretariat. Management Committee  
The competencies and activities of the Management Committee are outlined in Article 9 of the LCA and include, in addition to the approval of the annual accounts, decisions regarding the annual budget, internal organization and composition, as well as the annual management plans of the APD.  
The Management Committee also has significant operational responsibility in the area of citizens' privacy protection, as in accordance with Article 63, 1° of the LCA, it can decide to refer to the Inspection Service if it finds serious indications of the existence of a practice that may lead to a violation of the fundamental principles of personal data protection. In 2023, the Management Committee exercised this power on 3 occasions. The manner in which the Management Committee identifies and addresses the existence of indications of violations as per Article 63 § 1, 1° LCA has been reviewed by the renewed Management Committee in 2022 and will be profoundly modified following the entry into force of the new LCA.  
In 2023, the Management Committee met 29 times.

Headline: Even More Clarity on Cookies in 2023  
Do you ever feel like your phone is listening to you? We leave daily "traces" behind us that, when aggregated, can provide a sometimes very precise idea of our interests and can thus give the impression that we are being monitored.  
Among these traces are cookies, a type of "mini-file" that can be placed on a device connected to the internet.  
In light of, on the one hand, the growing concern of citizens regarding this issue, and, on the other hand, the willingness of data controllers to be supported in their compliance regarding cookies, the Data Protection Authority (DPA) announced that cookies (and other trackers) would be a cross-cutting priority for the year 2023.  
Thus, the DPA set to work in 2023 to create tools to raise awareness about the use of cookies and participated through the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) in harmonizing viewpoints on the subject at the European level.  

Cookie Banner Taskforce  
In February, the EDPB published a report on the work carried out by its "Cookie Banner Taskforce" regarding cookie banners. This report, resulting from cooperation among various European data protection authorities, is the outcome of a collaboration established to address complaints concerning cookie banners received by the organization NOYB.  
The report, available in full on the EDPB website, examines among other things the practices of pre-checked boxes, misleading design, and the absence of a "reject all" button at the same level as the "accept all" button.  
The DPA seized this opportunity to publish and disseminate a visual that allows different target audiences to grasp at a glance the best practices regarding cookie banner buttons.

It also published a summary of the report for broader dissemination to the Belgian public.  

Theme "Cookies" and "Cookie Checklist"  
To facilitate the compliance of cookie controllers or other trackers, the DPA published in October 2023 a synthetic tool that allows for a step-by-step overview of some "do's and don'ts" related to cookies and other similar tracking mechanisms, the "Cookie Checklist."  
It notably reminds that only strictly necessary cookies are exempt from consent, and that all other categories of cookies can only be placed and read if the user has consented beforehand and in a free, specific, informed, unambiguous, and active manner.  
The DPA is also updating the thematic page dedicated to cookies on its website, both in the "Citizens" section and in the "Professionals" section.  
These contents are the result of collaboration among all DPA services, crystallizing various expertise and taking into account the multiple facets of compliance regarding cookies and trackers.  

Guidelines on the Scope of Application of Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive  
In November, the EDPB published guidelines regarding the scope of application of Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, which will allow data controllers to better understand which tracking techniques, beyond cookies, are covered by the directive. These guidelines provide greater clarity regarding cookies and other trackers, contribute to better protection for users of internet services, and offer increased legal certainty for data controllers.  

In Practice  
In addition to these awareness-raising and support efforts arising from internal collaboration within the DPA (among services) or external collaboration (with other European data protection authorities), the DPA also concretely applied the rules regarding cookies and other trackers in 2023, notably in its compliance monitoring tasks for data controllers (see chapter "Transactions of the Contentious Chamber – The Transaction Policy and the 'Cookie Checklist'"), as well as by updating the cookie banner on its own websites.

Themes of the Year  
Theme 1: DPO  
In its role as an advisor on data protection within an organization, the Data Protection Officer (or DPO) is, in a sense, the guardian of citizens' personal data on the ground. This role is therefore essential for moving towards a society where privacy is a reality for all. The DPO is also an ally of the Data Protection Authority (DPA). For the DPA, it is extremely important to support the DPO in their mission, particularly by providing practical tools.  
In 2023, the DPA engaged in dialogue with stakeholders to implement its priority "Data Protection Officers" with a view to identifying their needs and proposing concrete solutions. For example, a meeting between the President and professional associations of DPOs identified the need for a common skills framework. This finding subsequently motivated a legislative recommendation in 2024 from the Knowledge Center encouraging the legislator to establish a certification mechanism for DPO training similar to that of French law.  
At the same time, the Inspection Service and the Litigation Chamber continued to carry out their questioning actions aimed at promoting the consideration of the DPO's independence and the necessary allocation of resources, including the time that the DPO can dedicate to data protection issues and training requirements.  

DPODay – May 25, 2023  
On May 25, 2023, the 5th anniversary of the GDPR provided the DPA with an opportunity to pay tribute to the data protection officers of the country during DPODay organized by the professional associations DPO PRO, DP Institute, and Isaca. The President of the DPA, Cédrine Morlière, was able to discuss issues such as the need for official recognition of a skills framework for DPO training or the difficulty of publishing a register of DPOs registered with the DPA. This latter observation motivated a legislative recommendation in 2024 from the Knowledge Center encouraging the legislator to establish a certification mechanism for DPO training similar to that of French law (Opinion 41/2024 of April 26, 2024). The Authority believes that the federal legislator should consider an appropriate legal framework to enable the establishment of certified data protection training in the context of receiving and processing breaches of integrity, similar to the legal framework provided in France, subject to any constraints related to the specific Belgian legal framework on certification. The Authority seizes the opportunity of this annual report to highlight this recommendation in order to lay the groundwork for a debate on this subject. At the same time, the Inspection Service continued to carry out its questioning actions aimed at promoting the consideration of the DPO's independence and the necessary allocation of resources, including the time that the DPO can dedicate to data protection issues and training requirements. The Litigation Chamber has also rendered several decisions in this regard.

Decisions  
The Data Protection Authority has identified DPOs as one of its priorities for 2023, and the decisions of the Litigation Chamber addressing this issue are listed below.  

Decision on the merits 110/2023  
In this decision, the Litigation Chamber provided an in-depth commentary on the obligation to appoint a DPO, in accordance with Article 37.1.a) of the GDPR. This provision requires that a public authority or public body appoints a DPO. It also assessed whether the tasks of the DPO could be adequately performed. In this case, it was found that the DPO did not have sufficient resources to advise the data controller on implementing the necessary safeguards provided by data protection legislation to assist and support data subjects in exercising their rights on one hand, and to prevent violations of data protection legislation on the other hand. The number of hours allocated on an annual basis was insufficient for a structural cooperation to be established between the data controller and the DPO.

Data Breach Processing  
In 2023, it appeared that ransomware attacks particularly affected Belgian cities and municipalities. In the 2022 annual report, we somewhat nuanced this fact by highlighting the global nature of this type of data breach, both in the private and public sectors.

This primarily emphasized a programmatic and structural approach. The DPA thus advises developing an incident response plan and/or a disaster recovery plan, monitoring compliance with them, and updating them. The Authority recalled that smart electricity meters are particularly intrusive as they allow for the inference of potentially sensitive data regarding the behaviors of residents in buildings where such meters are installed, specifically if they communicate electricity consumption data related to short periods.

Management of Priorities – Standard Opinions  
The priority task of the Knowledge Center consists of issuing prior and mandatory opinions regarding all legislative or regulatory texts of general scope that create, adapt, or implement personal data processing.  
The Knowledge Center sorts submissions based on the level of interference of the opinion projects submitted, as well as, where applicable, when relevant, by the cross-cutting priorities of the Data Protection Authority (DPA).  
The assessment of the level of interference is carried out using the information provided by the applicant in the opinion request form and a ‘quick screening’ of the draft legislation where applicable. The Knowledge Center has two options: a substantive opinion on all elements of the draft or a "standard opinion."  
The Knowledge Center generally issues a "standard opinion" summarizing the general principles of personal data protection to be considered when drafting legislation, when the applicant does not indicate in their opinion request form that the submitted project could pose high risks to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned, and when the information provided by the applicant in the form does not allow for the conclusion that such risks would exist.  
On the other hand, to the extent of its human resources, the Knowledge Center attempts to provide substantive opinions when legislative projects indicating a significant level of interference are submitted to it and/or when it corresponds to one of the cross-cutting priorities defined by the DPA for 2023 (see introduction to the DPA's budget requests for 2022). Thus, in 2023, the Knowledge Center prioritized opinions on bills related to the implementation of "smart city" projects, such as smart meters.  
In addition to the effort expended for sorting the submitted documents after a prima facie legal review, the provision of standard opinions also involves substantial administrative work as the related documents must be submitted for approval by the Knowledge Center as a collegial body concerned with validating the sorting carried out. Inspection Service  
The law of December 3, 2017 establishing the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter the "LCA")1 describes the Inspection Service rather succinctly as "the investigative body of the Data Protection Authority." In practical terms, this means that the Inspection Service is responsible for examining complaints concerning violations of European and Belgian data protection legislation, including the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter the "GDPR"), as well as serious indications of such violations.2  
The Inspection Service is headed by the Chief Inspector, Peter Van den Eynde, and consists of inspectors. The inspectors have various profiles (auditors, information security experts, and legal professionals) to ensure a multidisciplinary approach. This allows for the analysis of technical, organizational, and legal aspects.  
The Inspection Service carries out its monitoring activities independently, in a planned, professional, efficient, and discreet manner. To this end, it conducts (non) periodic monitoring as well as inspection missions regarding (inter)national processing in the public and private sectors, based on relevant risks, under the GDPR and related data protection legislation and regulations (for example, legislation concerning cameras).  
The Inspection Service has an extensive arsenal of investigative options. To analyze a case, the Chief Inspector and the inspectors may, if deemed necessary and in accordance with the LCA:  
- identify individuals;  
- interview individuals;  
- conduct a written inquiry;  
- carry out on-site examinations;  
- consult computer systems and copy the data they contain;  
- access information electronically;  
- seize or seal property or computer systems;  
- require the identification of the subscriber or usual user of an electronic communication service or the electronic communication means used.  
The LCA provides various ways for the Inspection Service to open a case. A case may primarily be opened:  
- at the initiative of the Management Committee when there are serious indications of an infringement, if it is necessary to cooperate with a foreign data protection authority, or at the request of a judicial authority or an administrative control body;  
- at the initiative of the Litigation Chamber if a complaint requires an investigation or if a supplementary investigation is necessary;

(Initial) experiences following an increased use of dismissals without further action  
In previous annual reports, the significant workload within the APD and the Inspection Service has already been highlighted. Although the GDPR aims for more accessible processing of files related to data protection and the APD is not opposed to this idea, it is nonetheless necessary to find mechanisms to keep the workload under control and not leave files pending for too long. This applies not only to the individuals concerned but also to the APD in general and to the Inspection Service in particular.  
One of the (legal) options available to the Inspection Service is indeed to dismiss a file without further action, as provided for in the LCA. The Inspection Service has already resorted to this option in the past, for example, if it did not obtain additional detailed information from the complainant to properly process the file, if the complainant themselves stated during the investigation that the complaint could be closed (if, for example, the complainant had already obtained a satisfactory solution from the opposing party), or if it turned out (after further investigation or not) that the APD was not competent.  
In the meantime, this tool has also been implemented as a sort of strategic tool.  
The purpose of such an intervention is indeed primarily to ensure the application of the GDPR, without the fine or any other sanction necessarily being an end in itself. The APD still aims to "be a guide to a digital world where privacy is a reality for everyone."  
The Inspection Service indeed examines various elements related to privacy "within the framework" of the file but, where appropriate, also "outside of its framework." In many of these cases, it specifically concerns means employed by the data controller aimed at increasing transparency towards citizens in general and the individual concerned in particular.

In its investigations, the Inspection Service regularly draws attention to these transparency-promoting instruments, given that they are beneficial for all citizens.

Contentious Chamber  
The Contentious Chamber in Outline  
The Contentious Chamber is the administrative litigation body of the APD (Article 32 of the LCA) and is tasked with intervening in a repressive manner in cases submitted to it, based on a complaint from a citizen or following an inspection initiated by the APD. The Contentious Chamber also deals with cases submitted to the APD by the authorities of European member states within the framework of the single contact point mechanism established by the GDPR, where the APD acts as the lead authority or concerned authority. In this regard, the Contentious Chamber has several instruments at its disposal: corrective measures and administrative fines. The procedure before the Contentious Chamber is conducted in accordance with the principles of procedural law.  
The Contentious Chamber is composed, in addition to its president, of six external members who are appointed by the Chamber based on their particular expertise.  
The Contentious Chamber has a unique structure that does not exist among market regulators in Belgium or among other supervisory authorities in the EU. This structure is hybrid in nature. The Contentious Chamber is a regulatory body, but the procedure is nearly judicial, particularly with a decision being made after the exchange of conclusions between the parties and often also after a hearing.  

Online Research Platform for Care Providers  
In 2021, the Contentious Chamber received several complaints against platforms that allow users to find nearby healthcare and support service providers or professionals with specific specializations, and then to make appointments. Several care providers filed a complaint with the APD against one of these platforms. They claimed that their personal data had been subject to unlawful processing and that the data controller had not appropriately addressed their right to erasure.  
At the conclusion of its analysis, the Contentious Chamber found that the data controller could not invoke any valid legal basis for processing the personal data of care and support providers on its platform. The Contentious Chamber determined that the data controller could not rely on legitimate interest as a legal basis for the disputed processing. Indeed, the processing did not meet two of the three conditions required for legitimate interest to be validly invoked as a legal basis. The Contentious Chamber acknowledged that the data controller does pursue a legitimate interest and that the personal data processed (name, first name, and specialty) are necessary to allow users of the website to contact the relevant care provider. However, the conditions related to the balancing of interests were not met. The Contentious Chamber recognized that the personal data in question are public data, but their public nature does not negate the need for appropriate safeguards in their processing. The fact that personal data is publicly accessible is a factor that can be considered during the assessment, particularly if their publication was accompanied by a reasonable expectation of subsequent use of the data for certain purposes. In this regard, the Contentious Chamber observes that the disputed processing does not fall within the reasonable expectations of the individuals concerned. Complaint for refusal of access to audio recordings  
In 2023, the Contentious Chamber delved into the right of access.  
The complainant is a client of the data controller and has entered into two contracts under which the data controller is tasked with developing a website and producing corporate videos for the complainant. In the context of these contracts, telephone conversations took place regarding the functional development and design of this website and these videos. These telephone conversations were recorded by the data controller for the proper execution of the complainant's wishes under the contracts. However, the complainant claims that he was not aware of these recordings. Since 2021, a dispute has arisen between the complainant and the data controller regarding the execution of the contracts. In this context, the complainant exercised his right of access concerning the telephone recordings. The data controller refused to provide a copy of the telephone recordings but offered the complainant the opportunity to listen to the recordings in its offices, after which the complainant filed a complaint with the DPA. In the complaint, the complainant challenges the lawfulness of the recordings of the telephone conversations on the one hand and the lack of an appropriate response to his access request on the other hand.  
The Contentious Chamber first considered that the disputed processing, the recording of the telephone conversations, was lawful given its necessity for the execution of the aforementioned two contracts.  
Regarding the right of access, the Contentious Chamber recalled that the GDPR requires the data controller to provide the data subject with a faithful and comprehensible reproduction of the personal data being processed.

The Contentious Chamber held that the data controller cannot invoke the rights and freedoms of its employees to refuse to provide a copy to the complainant. A violation of Article 14.1-2 combined with Article 12.1 of the GDPR is also noted in that the Ministry of Finance did not sufficiently inform the individuals concerned by the data processing carried out in execution of the "FATCA" agreement. The Litigation Chamber also finds a violation of Article 35.1 of the GDPR for lack of DPIA as well as a violation of Articles 5.2 and 24 of the GDPR (accountability). For these breaches, the Litigation Chamber issues a reprimand to the Ministry of Finance accompanied by orders for compliance. It should be noted that an appeal for suspension and annulment has been filed against this decision before the Market Court (CdM - Court of Appeal of Brussels), the appellate body for decisions of the Litigation Chamber. The Market Court granted the request for suspension of the decision (provisionally enforceable by default) considering the serious grounds invoked by the Ministry of Finance as well as the risk of serious harm since the absence of annual transfer by the Ministry of Finance would place it in breach of its international obligations.29

Right to Erasure from a Baptism Register  
A baptized person has requested to be removed from all files of the Roman Catholic Church, including the baptism register, from the Diocese of Ghent. However, the Church does not delete data from the baptism registers but rather adds a note in the margin of the register reflecting the person's wish to leave the Church.  
The right to erasure of data (Article 17 of the GDPR) is not absolute and can only be exercised under certain conditions. The Church believes that it has a legitimate interest in retaining the data recorded in the baptism register, as this retention is necessary for the purpose of data processing, and that the conditions applicable to a request for erasure are therefore not met in such cases. The Church  

29 Market Court, interlocutory ruling of June 28, 2023, 2023/AR/801.
Annual Report 2023

57  
invokes as the legal basis for the processing of baptism data its legitimate interest in preventing potential fraud (identity fraud) in the sense that, according to Catholic doctrine, a baptism can only take place once. This would necessitate the maintenance of a register of this data.  
The Contentious Chamber confirmed that there is indeed a legitimate interest for the Church. However, this legitimate interest can only serve as a basis for data processing if the processing is necessary to achieve this objective, and if the interest of the data subject (here: the complainant) does not outweigh the interest of the organization processing the data (here: the Diocese of Ghent).  
In this regard, the Contentious Chamber concluded that neither the requirement of necessity nor the balancing of interests was met. On the one hand, given that the register is only maintained in paper form within a single parish (the one where the baptism took place), it is not always possible to verify whether the baptism occurred or not. The data processing as it is currently performed does not, in fact, prevent a person from receiving the same sacrament twice, and it is therefore not appropriate a priori to achieve the intended purpose. On the other hand, the lifelong retention of the complainant's data—including data that is not strictly necessary to establish whether a person is already baptized—is disproportionate from the moment the complainant explicitly indicates a desire to distance themselves from the Church.  
Consequently, the processing is deemed unlawful, which implies that the complainant can successfully exercise their right to erasure of data. Furthermore, the data must also be erased when a legitimate objection to data processing is raised, which is indeed the case here.  
The Contentious Chamber ordered the Diocese of Ghent to comply with the complainant's request to exercise their right to object to the processing of their data, and to the request to erase their data.  
This decision is currently subject to appeal before the Market Court (Court of Appeal of Brussels).

The settlement policy defines the legal framework, procedural and intrinsic benefits, as well as the procedural framework of the settlement procedure.  
An example of a benefit of resorting to a settlement is that the less burdensome settlement procedure can potentially satisfy a larger number of complainants and reduce the total number of cases dismissed by the Litigation Chamber for reasons of opportunity. This is obviously the case only to the extent that most parties adopt a constructive attitude during the procedure.  
Regarding the procedural framework of the settlement procedure, it should be emphasized that - unlike other regulators - the complainant is assigned a formal and substantial role in the settlement between the regulator (in this case, the APD's Litigation Chamber) and the defendant party. This arises from the expanded role assigned to the complainant under the GDPR by the European legislator, for example in light of Article 77 of the GDPR.  

Dismissal of Certain Old Cases  
Given that the Litigation Chamber exercises its powers with a limited number of staff, the backlog of cases has increased over the years. The cases handled by the Litigation Chamber are often complex (in terms of technical and procedural aspects). Each decision of the Litigation Chamber also requires precise reasoning, in accordance with the legislation and the requirements set by the Market Court.  
In 2022, the Litigation Chamber adopted 189 decisions. The juxtaposition of this figure with the number of complaints (604 in 2022) received each year by the APD's Frontline Service explains why a significant backlog of cases has accumulated over the years. During the course of 2023, the Litigation Chamber decided to respond to this backlog.  
In the spring of 2023, the decision was made to simultaneously dismiss a large number of cases. As part of a project related to monitoring the backlog of cases, the Litigation Chamber individually found in 389 cases that it was no longer appropriate to continue processing them, notably because none of these cases had been processed for more than a year and because the circumstances of the case were not particularly priority or socially relevant.

In each of these cases, the Litigation Chamber has established transparency as a central element: thus, it has clearly informed each concerned complainant of their rights. The decision mentioned the possibility of appealing against the decision to dismiss the case as well as the possibility of filing a new complaint, especially if the complaint was still relevant and if there were new indications of a practice contrary to the GDPR.  
The dismissal of a large number of cases was justified by public interest. Ensuring the individual interests of the complainant whose rights are violated is indeed only possible when a smooth and qualitative flow of complaints is generally guaranteed.  
The DPA is indeed not a court. The reason why the complaint mechanism has been placed in the hands of an authority is precisely to allow for certain strategic choices to be made in a specialized manner and to be able to intervene effectively in a complex and digitized social context. Everyone has the right to effective data protection, and this method allows the Litigation Chamber, in particular, to handle cases impacting a larger number of people in society, as well as to intervene more quickly and firmly in new cases (of complaint).  
It is important to emphasize the exceptional nature of this project. The Litigation Chamber is working on complementary measures to avoid being faced in the future with an excessively large backlog of cases that have been pending for a long time. Thus, the Litigation Chamber continues to develop the legally mandated settlement (with the conditions and modalities possible in this context), just as the DPA continues to work on general prevention and to encourage dispute resolution through mediation whenever possible. The Litigation Chamber is also committed to sharpening the assessment of the appropriateness of its follow-up decisions early in the procedure in order to minimize future processing times. In this context, the priorities generally set by the DPA will serve as a guide. The decision to dismiss a case will also not be avoided in this context. The implementation of these measures, if necessary with the help of other services of the DPA, must ensure that the best possible service is offered to the citizen within a framework of limited resources.

Administrative fines  
On March 24, 2023, the EDPB adopted guidelines intended to complement the previously established guidelines for the application and imposition of administrative fines by DPAs.  
The GDPR prescribes that the amount of a fine must, in each case, be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive (Article 83, paragraph 1 of the GDPR). The EDPB has developed a five-step method to complement Article 83 of the GDPR. The objective is to achieve a more harmonized approach, even though authorities have some discretion in applying the steps.  
Step 1  
Identify the processing activities in the case in question and assess the application of Article 83.3 GDPR.  
Step 2  
Determine the starting amount for the subsequent calculation based on an assessment of the following elements:
Annual Report 2023

60
the distribution referred to in Articles 83.4 to 83.6 of the GDPR;  
the severity of the violation under Article 83.2, points a), b) and g) of the 
GDPR;  
the turnover of the company as a relevant element to be considered for 
imposing an effective, dissuasive, and proportionate fine under Article 83.1 of the GDPR.  
Step 3  
Examine whether there are aggravating and mitigating circumstances related 
to the past or current behavior of the data controller/processor and increase or 
decrease the fine accordingly.  
Step 4  
Establish the relevant maximum legal amounts for the different processing 
activities. This maximum amount cannot be exceeded as a result of increases 
applied in the previous or subsequent steps.  
Step 5  
Assess whether the final calculated amount of the fine is effective, dissuasive, 
and proportionate, as required by Article 83.1 of the GDPR, and increase or 
decrease the fine accordingly.  

The guidelines further elaborate on the existence of a possible concurrence of 
violations. When should behavior be considered a single violation because it 
involves the same processing activities or related activities, and when are the 
alleged acts multiple?  
This step also deepens the assessment of the severity of a violation. Article 
83.2 of the GDPR already includes factors that must also be taken into account, 
such as the nature, severity, and duration of the violation, whether the violation 
was committed intentionally or negligently, and the categories of personal data 
involved in the violation. The assessment of these elements influences the 
appropriate starting amount, which can be low, medium, or high. Generally, the 
more severe the violation in the relevant category, the higher the starting amount 
is likely to be.  
Throughout the guidelines, it is emphasized that the effective quantification 
of the fine depends on all elements collected during the investigation as well as 
other considerations related to the prior experience of the supervisory authorities 
in terms of fines.  
The Contentious Chamber has already relied on the adapted guidelines in 
several decisions in 2023. It thus applies a more structured and effective method 
for setting fines in its decisions. By 2024, we are exploring how this can be 
applied in a standardized manner (the recent decision 07/2024 is already an 
example).

Fine Policy  
Finally, it should be noted that the Market Court is adopting an evolving 
attitude regarding the sanctioning competence of the Contentious Chamber. It 
has notably criticized the reasoning used by the Contentious Chamber to impose 
administrative fines. In this regard, two important judgments were rendered in 
2023.  
In the SNCB judgment (2022/AR/723) of the Market Court on June 14, 2023, 
the amount of the fine was reduced to a symbolic euro. The APD appealed to 
the Supreme Court against this decision in order to obtain clarification on the 
applicable rules.  
In the Proximus judgment (2020/AR/1160) of September 6, 2023, the Market 
Court converted the fine imposed by the Contentious Chamber into a reprimand.  
Given these developments, it is essential to maintain a structured and 
coherent fine policy. The aforementioned EDPB guidelines should contribute to 
this.  
The Contentious Chamber is currently examining how to better meet the 
requirements of the Court. The judgment in the Proximus case already seems to 
be a step in the right direction.
