Introduction from the President  
The year 2022 concluded with the granting at the end of December to the Data Protection Authority (DPA) of a budget and a strengthened framework, based on a rigorous budget defense exercise, outlining the challenges faced by the DPA in a rapidly changing legal environment, both nationally and internationally.  
The DPA must prepare to play a regulatory role within the forthcoming multifaceted legal framework at the European level, the "Digital Package."  
For instance, the European regulation Data Governance Act (DGA), adopted in May 2022 and applicable from the end of September 2023, aims to facilitate data exchanges through new sharing mechanisms.  
The Digital Services Act (DSA), adopted in October 2022 and applicable from February 2024, includes measures aimed at protecting citizens against misinformation, as well as provisions related to targeted advertising and the transparency of algorithmic content recommendation systems used by digital platforms.  
Additionally, complementing the "Digital Package," a future European regulation on artificial intelligence (AI Act) is anticipated, with the ambition of regulating the design of "AI" products or services and enabling oversight of the various risks posed by these technologies.  
These forthcoming European legislations will impact the competencies of the DPA as well as those of various regulators (competition authorities, telecom regulators, etc.). Increased cooperation will need to be organized between these authorities and the DPA, particularly in the context of managing complaints that citizens may file based on these new rules.  
In this regard, the DPA wishes to emphasize that in 2022, the Court of Justice of the European Union clearly indicated, through the conclusions of its Advocate General (conclusions Rantos, C-252/21), that the competent data protection authorities are essential, especially when it comes to enforcing coercive actions related to the processing of personal data, even when such legal actions are initiated by other authorities.  
This cooperation will inevitably need to occur at the international level when complaints, products, or services have a cross-border aspect. It will be necessary to integrate into this approach transparent information for citizens regarding the progress and outcome of their potential complaints or inquiries. In this regard, the DPA is enthusiastic about sharing its experience in authority cooperation, gained within the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), with its Belgian partners.  
For example, even before the entry into force of the Digital Services Act, the Litigation Chamber collaborated with its French counterpart, the CNIL, to handle over 200 complaints, both Belgian and French, in a dispute related to information transparency (decision of January 27, 2022, in the so-called 'Benalla' case), following an investigation by the Inspection Service. On this occasion, the Litigation Chamber clarified the rules regarding the processing of personal data for statistical or scientific purposes in the fight against alleged "fake news." The Authority  
The Data Protection Authority (DPA) is the independent supervisory body regarding the processing of personal data. It was established by the law of December 3, 2017 (hereinafter: "LCA"). It has a much broader range of action than its predecessor, the Privacy Protection Commission (the "Privacy Commission"). The DPA is a federal body with legal personality, established with the House of Representatives.  

Powers of the DPA  
The general mission of the DPA is defined by Article 4 of the LCA as "the supervision of compliance with the fundamental principles of personal data protection, within the framework of this law and laws containing provisions related to the protection of personal data processing."  
The DPA is therefore competent not only for monitoring compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter: "GDPR") or general legislation related to data protection (such as the law of July 30, 2018, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data, hereinafter: "LTD"), but it can also exercise oversight of compliance with other more specific legislations and regulations, such as legislation related to cameras.  
However, the DPA's oversight does not cover processing carried out by courts and tribunals as well as by the public prosecutor in the exercise of their judicial functions. The DPA is also not competent for the processing of personal data carried out by police services.  
The DPA is competent regarding actors and entities in both the public and private sectors and has the ability to impose administrative sanctions autonomously.  

Composition of the DPA  
According to Article 7 of the LCA, the DPA is composed of a Management Committee, a General Secretariat, a Frontline Service, a Knowledge Center, an Inspection Service, and a Litigation Chamber. Both the Litigation Chamber and the Knowledge Center also include six external members who contribute their expertise to the work and deliberations of these bodies.  
The Management Committee of the DPA was partially renewed in July 2022 following the resignation of the previous director of the Knowledge Center and the termination of the mandates of the directors of the Frontline Service and General Secretariat. In July 2020, Cédrine Morlière was appointed as the new director of the Knowledge Center, and the Presidency of the DPA was entrusted to her following the previous President of the General Secretariat, in accordance with the rotation provided for in the organic law of December 3, 2017, establishing the DPA. The Management Committee collectively ensures the management of the two departments for which a procedure for appointing two new representatives was initiated on September 27, 2022.

Headline  
The DPA Prepares for the Digital Package  
Regardless of the future distribution of competencies anticipated by the Belgian legislator regarding the implementation of current and future rules outlined at the European level within the framework of the "Digital Package" among the various potentially concerned Belgian regulators (notably the DPA, the Belgian Institute of Post and Telecommunications, the Federal Public Service Economy, the Cybersecurity Centre Belgium, Belgian Competition Authority), the DPA will remain an essential independent interlocutor whenever the processing of personal data is at stake.  
The evolution of case law indicates an increased need for cooperation between the DPA and various regulators, particularly competition authorities. Thus, according to the conclusions of Advocate General Rantos submitted on September 22, 2022, to the Court of Justice of the European Union in case C-252/21 (Meta Platforms et al. (General Terms of Use of a Social Network)), a competition authority may, in the exercise of its powers, take into account the compatibility of a business practice with the General Data Protection Regulation. However, it must consider the decision-making or investigative powers of the competent supervisory authority under the GDPR. It also follows from these developments that the DPA will likely be solicited for collaboration by other national and European authorities in the context of complex "enforcement" cases involving the assessment of cutting-edge technologies.  
As of now, the DPA is de facto involved in national or international "enforcement" actions related to matters concerning certain aspects of the "Digital Package," notably through the work of its Litigation Chamber and its Inspection Service.  
Anticipation of the Digital Services Act:  
The Digital Services Act (DSA), published in the Official Journal of the European Union on October 27, 2022, includes measures aimed at protecting citizens against fake news and ensuring greater transparency in the use of algorithms by digital platforms. In some cases, the algorithmic systems used by platforms can amplify misinformation campaigns or discriminate against certain groups of people.  
The Litigation Chamber of the DPA has intervened as the leading authority in collaboration with the CNIL in response to more than 200 complaints from both Belgian and French parties in a dispute regarding information transparency.  
Through this decision, the DPA applied the rules of the GDPR to the publication of a study aimed at identifying the potential political origin of tweets circulating on a particularly heated controversy in France, "the Benalla affair," concerning revelations by the newspaper Le Monde about the impersonation of a police officer by a close associate of the President of the French Republic. The DPA clarified the rules regarding the processing of personal data for statistical or scientific purposes in the fight against alleged "fake news." In this regard, the DPA refers to the press releases published by its French counterpart, the CNIL, and itself.  
This unappealable decision, based on a thorough inspection report, has been praised in a specialized online review such as GDPR expert.

Themes  
Direct Marketing  
In 2022, the APD received numerous complaints regarding unsolicited direct marketing. The Litigation Chamber made several decisions in this matter. Here is an example: the subject of the complaint concerned phone calls made to the complainant's mobile phone offering energy supply contracts (telemarketing activity). Following these calls, the complainant exercised their rights with the data controller to request access to their data and to request its deletion.  
Based on an investigation report, the Litigation Chamber first noted that the data controller was unable to demonstrate that the complainant's consent had been validly obtained for this purpose. The Litigation Chamber reminded that for such consent to be valid, it must be informed and specific. This implies that at the time of obtaining consent from the data subject by a third party, the data subject must be informed of the partners to whom their data will be transmitted as well as the purposes of the processing. The Litigation Chamber also considered that, although the data controller responded quickly to the complainant's access request, the response was incomplete.  
Furthermore, the Litigation Chamber decided that when a data subject exercises their right to deletion, the data controller is entitled, under Articles 17.3 and 24 of the GDPR, to keep a record of this deletion request. However, they must inform the data subject and comply with the principles of data minimization, transparency, and purpose.  
Finally, the Litigation Chamber found a violation of the principles of information and transparency in that the data controller did not have any privacy policy at the time of the complaint. Given the data controller's prompt response to the complainant's requests and the compliance work carried out before the decision was adopted, the Litigation Chamber decided to impose a reprimand.

Awareness  
Children and Youth: www.jedecide.be  
The world is not static; it is constantly evolving. This evolution brings about new possibilities and new challenges. In today's world, technological advancements concerning the processing of personal data hold significant importance, particularly for children and youth. The 'I Decide' initiative is therefore based on the idea that the privacy of our children and youth can only be guaranteed if they are aware of it. All 'I Decide' activities aim to show young people how to adopt a respectful attitude towards privacy.  
The main information channel of 'I Decide' is its website www.jedecide.be. This site contains information on personal data protection, tailored to different target groups: young people, parents, and educators.  
With Gustave the hedgehog, young people discover how to better protect their personal data.  
Although informing young people about data protection is an important mission of the DPA, as it is explicitly included in all its tasks outlined in the legislation (art. 22, § 1, 3° of the LCA and art. 57.1.b) of the GDPR), the DPA has found that over the years, it has become increasingly difficult to develop new themes, information campaigns, educational materials, etc., through 'I Decide' due to a lack of operational resources.  
Due to this lack of resources, the operation of 'I Decide' actually relies entirely on "volunteers," in the sense that the 'I Decide' working group is composed of DPA staff who carry out tasks related to 'I Decide' in addition to their usual duties within the DPA. In December 2022, the DPA was able to obtain a framework extension to dedicate an officer to the "I Decide" projects, which is very positive.  
Thanks to new educational materials, the 'I Decide' team aims to encourage students to engage in a critical debate about privacy and wishes to provide them with several lessons that will allow them to form their own opinion on the importance of protecting their personal data.  
Citizens, especially the young among them, do not always understand the reasons why privacy and data protection are important, as the consequences of negligence in privacy and data protection are not always immediately visible, whereas those of, for example, a road accident are directly evident. It is important to be aware of the long-term consequences, often still invisible in the present, of privacy restrictions (what about access to employment in the case of released photographs from adolescence). Through this material, we aim to provide support that allows young people to discover for themselves what the often subtle and not directly perceptible consequences of data protection are.

Activities by Directorate  
General Secretariat  
The General Secretariat has a dual role within the DPA.  
On one hand, it performs several horizontal tasks, such as:  
- managing the support services of the DPA (IT, accounting, HR, DPO, reception, and translation service);  
- managing internal and external communication;  
- supporting the president and the Management Committee in carrying out their tasks.  
On the other hand, the General Secretariat also has several substantive tasks, such as:  
- monitoring societal, economic, and technological developments that impact personal data protection;  
- approving codes of conduct;  
- approving standard contractual clauses and binding corporate rules;  
- handling data breaches (according to a methodology reviewed in 2023 by the Management Committee).

International Transfers  
The General Secretariat actively participates in the work of the EDPB on the theme of international transfers of personal data.  
Several important guidelines and recommendations related to data transfers were adopted by the EDPB in 2022. Opinion 247/2022 regarding the planning, implementation, and monitoring of the mobility and road safety policy  
The Knowledge Center issued an opinion on a draft ordinance establishing a framework for the planning, implementation, and monitoring of the mobility and road safety policy, requested by the Brussels Minister of Mobility. - the number of concurrent opinions to be issued;  
- the necessary preparation of most opinion requests (exchange of questions and answers with the authors of the text);

Inspection Service  
The law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter the "LCA") describes the Inspection Service rather summarily as "the investigative body of the Data Protection Authority." Specifically, this means that the Inspection Service is responsible for examining complaints regarding violations of European and Belgian data protection legislation, including the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter the "GDPR"), as well as serious indications of such violations.  
The Inspection Service is led by Chief Inspector Peter Van den Eynde and is composed of inspectors. The inspectors have different profiles (auditors, information security experts, and legal professionals) to ensure a multidisciplinary approach. This allows for the analysis of technical, organizational, and legal aspects.  
The Inspection Service carries out its monitoring activities independently, in a planned, efficient, and discreet manner. To this end, it conducts periodic and non-periodic monitoring as well as inspection missions regarding (inter)national processing in the public and private sectors, taking into account relevant risks, under the GDPR and related legislation and regulations concerning data protection (for example, legislation related to cameras).  
The Inspection Service has an extensive arsenal of investigative options. To analyze a case, the Chief Inspector and inspectors may, if deemed necessary and in accordance with the LCA:  
- identify individuals;  
- interview individuals;  
- conduct a written inquiry;  
- carry out on-site examinations;  
- consult computer systems and copy the data they contain;  
- access information electronically;  
- seize or seal goods or computer systems;  
- require the identification of the subscriber or regular user of an electronic communication service or the electronic communication means used.  
The LCA provides various ways for the Inspection Service to open a case. A case may be opened:  
- at the initiative of the Management Committee when there are serious indications of an offense, if cooperation with a foreign data protection authority is necessary, or at the request of a judicial authority or an administrative control body;  
- at the initiative of the Contentious Chamber if a complaint requires an investigation or if a supplementary investigation is necessary;  
- at the initiative of the Inspection Service itself if there are serious indications of an offense.  
A Charter of the Inspection Service has also been developed and is subject to periodic updates based on new practices and knowledge. This charter aims to inform the general public as well as anyone dealing with the Inspection Service about the concrete process of an inspection, the various possible investigative actions of the Inspection Service, and their subsequent consequences.

In general, the Inspection Service notes (see also the numerical data further on) that cases are becoming more complex, not only in substance but also procedurally. After a few years of practice, the Inspection Service occasionally receives feedback that the number of questions asked does not always seem proportionate to the case or that the questions are very general.  
Regardless of the general investigation technique that begins with the request for access to documents that are supposed to be present in the context of ordinary "data protection management" in light of the principle of accountability (e.g., the processing register, the incident register...), the other questions are of a more general or professional nature.  
Some general questions primarily aim to understand the context of the processing and to know the resources available to a data controller (for example, questions about turnover, the annual report, ...). Each case is different and therefore requires a unique assessment and estimation based on the risks associated with the nature and scope of the processing, the "main" activities of a data controller, and the legal or ethical rules to which they are or are not subject (for example, a local or regional authority, a medical practice or a hospital, a "data broker," a telecom operator, ...).  
The content of the questions can also be viewed from a more positive angle. Often, the underlying reason for certain questions is educational in nature (for example, questions about the meaning of certain words in a privacy statement or about the technical installation of cookies or tracking on a website, ...). These questions thus facilitate awareness of certain issues and allow the person being questioned to already make adjustments to the processing during the course of the investigation. Accountability, one of the key elements of the GDPR, also requires the data controller to think in detail about each specific processing operation, to document this process internally, and to verify whether it is indeed compliant with the GDPR.  
In other words, the questions serve not only to examine a "burden" case but can also have the effect of raising awareness for the same data controller, enabling them to make some adjustments during the investigation. The data controller is thus invited to reflect deeply on all sorts of aspects of their processing activities and can more accurately assess whether their processing is compliant with the GDPR and adapt it accordingly, if necessary.  
The inspection phase can therefore also be seen as an adjustment tool, with the aim of some of the questions being to ensure that the GDPR is respected without the imposition of a fine being the sole objective, nor that a fine is in itself a cure-all. In several cases, this method of general, stratified, and diversified questioning has already led to concrete results on multiple occasions, either during the inspection phase, subsequently during the proceedings before the Litigation Chamber, or ultimately, after a decision on the merits.

The Resumption of Schengen Audits  
As part of the evaluation and monitoring mechanism for the enforcement of the Schengen acquis, the DPA, and in particular the Inspection Service, conducts "audits."  
These audits consist of a national component and a European component. The national component focuses on auditing a number of actors at the data protection level. Thus, the Inspection Service is currently auditing the Federal Public Service Foreign Affairs and the Office of Foreigners. This is primarily done through an audit program. In this context, it conducts audits on Belgian territory, but also in Belgian embassies abroad.  
This has already been mentioned several times. Although this audit mission is not found in the DPA law, it generates a considerable workload for a service of 9 inspectors. Given that this mission requires access to classified documents, only 3 inspectors (as well as the Inspector General) are currently tasked with these audits.  
An audit involves a whole process. First, the preparation and sending of a detailed questionnaire to the data controller, followed by reviewing the responses in preparation for an on-site visit. Then comes the actual on-site visit (if it is an embassy, this immediately involves a trip abroad lasting several days) and finally the sending of an (interim) report and the processing of responses to this report. If this data controller works with a subcontractor, an audit is also required for the latter in order to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the processing as a whole.  
In these audits, on-site visits are indeed important - a questionnaire already provides some insight, but the on-site visit allows the Inspection Service to gain an even more detailed overview of the various processes as well as to better contextualize and complement the responses to the questionnaire.  
During the Covid period, these audits were put on hold as on-site visits were no longer possible, whereas they constitute an essential aspect. In 2022, on-site visits were able to resume, and the ICT aspect of the specific processes of the Federal Public Service Foreign Affairs was also subject to thorough examination. The embassy in London (as well as its subcontractor - the so-called ESP for "external service provider") was also audited.  
The audit mission is complex in itself: an expert must master both the GDPR and the European regulations SIS II and VIS. They must have sufficient proficiency in English and possess legal and technical knowledge, as well as auditing skills to successfully carry out the on-site evaluation while adhering to a strictly defined methodology and timeline. So far, no specific training is planned (thus 'on the ground').  
The workload related to audit work will only increase, given the new European IT systems within this Schengen acquis (e.g., ETIAS, EES, ...) and what is called the "interoperability" regulation.

The phenomenon of "web scraping" (online data capture)  
of information from public websites  
In general, there has been a clear trend in recent years towards a more data-driven world. In many areas, the collection and possession (the “processing”) of data (“personal data”) for the purpose of analyzing and coupling it with other data or algorithms has become increasingly important.  
One of the techniques behind this growth, which is seeing increasing use, is what is known as "web scraping" of information from publicly accessible websites.  
"Web scraping" is, in summary, the extraction (= "scraping") of data from the aforementioned websites. The technique uses specific software to extract this information (often personal data) and then, where applicable, to store and analyze it. The Internet often contains a wealth of data that may be of interest to one party or another. Manually processing this information is too time-consuming, and moreover, it changes regularly.  
Thus, "scraping" allows for the relatively automated processing of personal data on a large scale.  
However, the belief that public information can be freely used (subsequently) remains a widespread misunderstanding (sometimes deliberately perpetuated). The GDPR indeed contains several rules that rightly restrict "scraping" indiscriminately.  
A primary issue often lies in finding a legitimate basis for the subsequent processing of the relevant personal data. In practice, consent is often very problematic, and without conducting a risk analysis, even invoking what is called "legitimate interest" is not an obvious option, nor is it sufficient to merely invoke this "legitimate interest" to consider the processing justified and legitimate in light of the GDPR.  
Another issue is also the lack of transparency and the fact, for example, that it is not always clear what the source of the obtained information is, who is precisely behind the "scraping," and what the exact purpose of the scraping is.

However, any potential concerns regarding the investigative method can be communicated to the Litigation Chamber during the adversarial phase. The Litigation Chamber remains completely independent of the investigation conducted, which provides sufficient assurance to the data controller who may have objections regarding the modalities of the investigation.

Contentious Chamber  
The Contentious Chamber in Overview  
The Contentious Chamber is the administrative contentious body of the Data Protection Authority (DPA) (Article 32 of the LCA) and is tasked with intervening in a repressive manner in cases submitted to it, based on a complaint from a citizen or following an inspection initiated by the DPA. The Contentious Chamber also deals with cases submitted to the DPA by the authorities of European member states within the framework of the single-window mechanism established by the GDPR, where the DPA acts as the lead authority or concerned authority. In this regard, the Contentious Chamber has several instruments at its disposal: corrective measures and administrative fines. The procedure before the Contentious Chamber is conducted in accordance with the principles of procedural law.  
The Contentious Chamber is composed, in addition to its president, of six external members who are appointed by the Chamber based on their specific expertise. The Contentious Chamber has a unique structure that does not exist among market regulators in Belgium or other EU supervisory authorities. This structure has a hybrid character. The Contentious Chamber is a supervisory body, but the procedure is almost judicial, particularly with a decision-making process following the exchange of conclusions between the parties and often also after a hearing. This structure has proven effective, especially due to the decisions that the Contentious Chamber has made in the meantime, which are available on the DPA's website. The procedure allows for impartial rulings and respects legal guarantees.  

Transaction Mechanism  
Under the General Data Protection Regulation, the Data Protection Authority is obliged to handle complaints and, after reviewing their content, to allocate the appropriate follow-up to these complaints. As the contentious body of the DPA, the Contentious Chamber processes a very large number of complaint files in this context. Furthermore, a supervisory authority must be able to firmly ensure the effective compliance with regulations, both for complaint files and for files in which an authority body itself submits a case.  
This is why the Belgian legislator has provided, in addition to European legislation, instruments with competencies for the Data Protection Authority and its bodies. These instruments are intended to enable the Authority to carry out its missions as effectively as possible. Thus, the Belgian organic law provides in both Article 95 and Article 100 for a competence for the Contentious Chamber to propose a transaction. As an instrument, the transaction plays a useful role in ensuring compliance with data protection legislation and can be used in various cases and for different strategic reasons.  
In 2022, the Contentious Chamber made use of its transaction competence for the first time. In accordance with its consistent policy of communicating in the most transparent and comprehensive manner possible about its decision-making process and the content of its decisions, the Contentious Chamber also published the transaction decisions on the website of the Data Protection Authority.

IAB Europe  
In 2019, the APD received a series of complaints from other supervisory authorities regarding the organization Interactive Advertising Bureau Europe (IAB Europe), concerning the compliance of the Transparency & Consent Framework (TCF) with the GDPR. Since the defendant is based in Brussels, the APD intervened as the supervisory authority.  
The TCF, developed by IAB Europe, aims to contribute to compliance with the GDPR by organizations that use the OpenRTB protocol, one of the most widespread protocols for "Real Time Bidding," which is the instant and automated online auction of user profiles for the sale and purchase of advertising space on the Internet.  
Through OpenRTB, technology companies, representing thousands of advertisers, can bid instantly ("in real time") behind the scenes for this advertising space via an automated auction system using algorithms, in order to display targeted advertisements specifically tailored to the users' profiles.  
When users first visit a website or application that uses the TCF, an interface provided by a Consent Management Platform (CMP) appears, allowing them to give their consent for the collection and sharing of their personal data or to object to various types of processing based on the legitimate interests of AdTech providers.  
The TCF thus facilitates the recording of user preferences via the CMP. These preferences are coded and recorded in a Transparency & Consent (TC) String, which is then shared with the participating organizations that have...

affiliated with the TCF, so that they can know what the specific user has consented to or opposed. In order to retain the TC String, the CMP also places a cookie euconsent-v2 on the user's device. In this context, the TC String and the cookie euconsent-v2 are also associated with the IP address of the data subject whose preferences have been captured, making the user identifiable. The TCF thus plays a key role in the architecture of the OpenRTB system, relaying user preferences regarding potential AdTech providers and for various processing purposes, including the offering of tailored advertisements. For this reason, the Contentious Chamber found that IAB Europe acted as a data controller concerning the capture of individual user preferences in the form of a TC String, which is associated with an identifiable user.

- a lack of clear and specific information provided to users via the CMP interface regarding data processing, such that they are unable to understand the nature, scope, and consequences of the processing;  
- a lack of appropriate technical and organizational measures, in accordance with the principles of data protection by design and data protection by default, in order to ensure the effective exercise of the rights of data subjects and to control the validity and integrity of user choices;  
- the omission to maintain a record of processing activities, to appoint a DPO, and to carry out a "DPIA."

Airports of Brussels Airport Zaventem and Brussels South Charleroi  
The APD imposed fines on Brussels Airport Zaventem and Brussels South Charleroi for passenger temperature checks conducted as part of the fight against COVID-19. According to the APD, these airports did not have a valid legal basis for processing these health data of travelers.  
At both airports, thermal cameras were used to filter individuals with a temperature exceeding 38°C. At Zaventem, these individuals were subsequently subjected to a questionnaire regarding possible coronavirus-related symptoms. The objective was to prevent individuals with a confirmed temperature above 38°C from boarding.  
These temperature checks took place from June 2020 to March 2021 at Charleroi Airport, and from June 2020 to January 2021 at Zaventem Airport.  
Upon concluding its analysis, the Litigation Chamber found, supported by the Inspection Service's report, that the airports lacked a valid legal basis for processing data related to travelers' temperatures, particularly since these are health data. Such data being sensitive, they cannot generally be processed, except in a very limited number of exceptions (listed in Article 9.2 of the GDPR). Processing for public health reasons or significant public interest is, for example, part of these exceptions, provided it is based on a clear, precise legal standard whose application is foreseeable for the individuals concerned; however, the temperature checks here primarily relied on a protocol that does not meet these requirements.  
Furthermore, the APD observed shortcomings in terms of information provided to travelers and the quality of impact assessments (i.e., the analysis of risks related to data processing).  
While acknowledging the difficult situation in which the airports found themselves due to the pandemic, the APD reminded that privacy and data protection rules entail essential protection for the rights and freedoms of individuals, particularly in situations of public health emergencies. It hopes that these two decisions can serve as a guide for any similar data processing, whether or not in the context of the health crisis.  
The APD imposed a fine on each of the parties involved. This fine was reduced or annulled by the appellate court, which nevertheless upheld the findings of the APD.

Jurisprudence  
Market Court  
In accordance with Article 108, § 2 of the law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority (LCA), the decisions of the Litigation Chamber of the DPA can be appealed to the Brussels Court of Appeal, Market Court section. For the sake of transparency, the DPA publishes on its website all rulings issued by the Market Court.  
In 2022, 16 new appeals were filed against decisions of the Litigation Chamber. The Market Court also ruled in 2022 in 13 appeal procedures, in which 8 final rulings and 5 interim rulings were issued, and 6 decisions were - either totally or partially - annulled.  
In its jurisprudence, the Market Court emphasizes the procedural aspects of the contentious procedure of the Litigation Chamber. Annulment rulings were also issued in 2022 for procedural reasons. The Market Court thus highlighted the obligation of motivation and partially annulled a number of decisions of the Litigation Chamber on the grounds that the reasoning of the decisions in question did not or at least did not sufficiently take into account various mitigating circumstances of Article 83 of the GDPR in its assessment of whether an administrative fine was required (2022/AR/560 & 2022/AR/564 and 2022/AR/556). References in a decision of the Litigation Chamber to a decision already annulled by the Market Court also constituted a violation of the obligation of motivation (2022/AR/483 & 2022/AR/484). The Market Court also drew attention to the guarantee of the rights of the defense. It held that during the procedure before the Litigation Chamber, the defendant must be informed of the violations alleged against them and what those violations consist of (2022/AR/42 and 2022/AR/457).  
Furthermore, the Market Court specified that it only had jurisdiction to rule on decisions of the Litigation Chamber and not on press releases issued by members of the DPA, even though of course subjective and objective impartiality must be guaranteed (2022/AR/560 & 2022/AR/564 and 2022/AR/556). The Market Court also confirmed that the autonomy and independence of the Litigation Chamber could not be limited to what is mentioned in the inspection report regarding the identification of possible violations and the imposition of possible sanctions (2022/AR/560 & 2022/AR/564 and 2022/AR/556).  
The Market Court also ruled on the composition of the Litigation Chamber. The Market Court stated that the Litigation Chamber sits collegially so that any irregularity in the appointment or even any bias on the part of a member cannot influence the legality of the decision (2022/AR/560 & 2022/AR/564). The applicant must demonstrate any violation of independence or any potential bias of one of the members of the Litigation Chamber (2022/AR/556). Subsequently, the Litigation Chamber emphasized that the president could not decide at their discretion to "de-collegialize" the handling of a case, but must do so taking into account two criteria: a) the nature of the complaint and b) the violation of the fundamental principles of protection.

Court of Justice of the European Union  
The Court of Justice of the European Union ruled in its judgment of October 27, 2022, on questions posed by the Market Court in a case concerning Proximus, which led to a decision by the Contentious Chamber regarding the modalities for withdrawing consent to be listed in telephone directories.  
The Court's ruling stated that "when different data controllers rely on the single consent of the data subject, it is sufficient for the data subject to address any of the controllers to withdraw their consent."

The One-Stop-Shop Mechanism  
The One-Stop-Shop mechanism has been established by the GDPR, with the aim of harmonizing, for cross-border processing, the decisions of the various national European data protection authorities. There is cross-border processing when a data controller (or a processor) with establishments in several EU member states processes data of individuals residing in different member states, or when the data controller is established in only one member state but the contested processing may have consequences for individuals living in several member states.  
For the citizen, this mechanism has the advantage of allowing them to file a complaint in their language and with the data protection authority of their country of residence, and for that authority to be their point of contact during the procedure (and not the data protection authority of the country where the data controller is based).  
At the level of the data controller, this one-stop-shop system allows for one and only one data protection authority to be their interlocutor, for effective monitoring of compliance with the GDPR.  
Specifically, one authority will be designated as the "lead" authority for the examination of a complaint and will draft a decision proposal, which will be submitted to the other interested authorities. These authorities may issue comments or oppose the proposal. In the absence of an agreement among the authorities, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) must resolve the disputed points based on Article 65 of the GDPR.  
For example, on December 5, 2022, the EDPB adopted binding decisions regarding a draft decision from the Irish data protection authority against Meta (specifically its Facebook and Instagram services) following objections raised by several authorities.  
In its decisions, the EDPB stated that Meta IE improperly invoked the contract as a legal basis for processing personal data in the context of the terms of use of Facebook and Instagram for behavioral advertising purposes. Consequently, the EDPB concluded that Meta had no legal basis for this processing and requested the Irish authority to order Meta to bring its processing into compliance with Art. 6(1) GDPR. Furthermore, the EDPB noted that serious failures by Meta to meet transparency obligations had impacted the reasonableness of user expectations, that Meta had presented its services to users in a misleading manner, and that the relationship between Meta and users of Facebook and Instagram services was unbalanced. The EDPB ordered the Irish authority to amend its decision, notably to impose significantly higher fines. The APD actively participated in the discussions surrounding the adoption of this binding decision.  
In 2022, the Litigation Chamber of the APD published the following decisions under the one-stop-shop mechanism:  
Decision 21/2022 concerning the Transparency & Consent Framework of IAB Europe (on advertising based on browsing habits), a process in which 21 other European data protection authorities participated, given the implications of the relevant processing for citizens in their respective member states.  
Decision 11/2022 (regarding cookies) in which a common position was reached after objections were raised by a concerned authority.  
Decision 13/2022 (regarding online political profiling) which was also taken after consultation based on the European cooperation mechanism.  
The Litigation Chamber also forwarded complaints submitted to the APD but which fell under the jurisdiction of another data protection authority, based on the criteria defined in the GDPR.
