I.  
INTRODUCTION  

Preamble  

1. Adopted on April 27, 2016, Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (hereinafter "GDPR") has been in effect since May 25, 2018. Repealing the Directive of the European Parliament and of the Council of October 24, 1995, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (hereinafter "Directive 95/46/EC"), it confirms and consolidates the rules as applied by the Court of Justice of the European Union and by the Article 29 Working Party through its official positions and Guidelines. By moving from a directive to a regulation, the European legislator intended to make the protection of personal data, elevated to the status of a fundamental right by Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, directly and uniformly applicable within the Member States.  

2. One of the main objectives of the GDPR is to strengthen the rights of data subjects. The GDPR thus endows the Supervisory Authorities with significant powers, allowing them, moreover, to impose sanctions in cases of violations of the rules it establishes. The Eurobarometer of May 2019 on the GDPR demonstrates that the awareness of data subjects regarding the applicable rules on data protection and their rights is significantly increasing. Furthermore, they are exercising their rights more than before. This is particularly true for the withdrawal of their consent or the objection to the processing of their data for commercial communications.  

3. In light of these strengthened rights, many consumer and civil rights associations agree that the GDPR significantly contributes to a fair digital society, built on mutual trust between data subjects and actors involved in the processing of their data.

In order to achieve this objective, the GDPR emphasizes the accountability of the various actors involved in the processing of personal data, whether they are individuals or professionals, legal entities or public administrations, at every stage of the processing, both at the national and European or international levels.

5. As a result, the supervisory authorities do not have the sole role of intervening downstream regarding these actors in the event of a violation of the GDPR rules by them. Given the significant sanctions that can be imposed and the fact that personal data has become essential for the continuation of the majority of socio-economic activities, the support of data controllers by data protection authorities constitutes one of their most important competencies.

Context and Scope of the Recommendation

6. Numerous actors engage in direct marketing communications daily, targeting millions of affected individuals. These communications involve the processing of personal data. The regularity, sophistication, and proliferation of these data processing activities, as well as the active operators in this area, create a fertile ground for the repetition of certain practices that may sometimes contradict the rules of the GDPR. To the marketing adage "send the right message to the right person at the right time," you must add "in the right way," because the GDPR is now an integral part of your marketing campaigns.

7. While it may appear as a framework of binding rules for certain data controllers in the context of direct marketing, the GDPR is also an indispensable and useful ally in your relationship with the affected individuals, whether they are customers, prospects, members, subscribers, or voters. By communicating with them transparently regarding your direct marketing, you represent one of the sectors identified in the strategic plan of the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter "DPA" or "Authority") as a priority in the various actions it intends to undertake. Providing, through this Recommendation, an interpretation of the rules applicable to the processing of personal data carried out for direct marketing purposes and a framework of good practices constitutes one of these actions. In order to ensure a harmonized approach to this interpretation and these good practices, the DPA relies in particular on all relevant guidelines adopted by the European Data Protection Board (hereinafter "EDPB").

However, the EDPB has not adopted general guidelines regarding direct marketing as such. Should this be the case, and taking into account any future European position related to this recommendation, the DPA will consider it and adapt the content of this recommendation if necessary.

8. In order to best address the questions raised in this area, the Data Protection Authority (APD) launched a public consultation on July 12, 2019, aimed at data controllers active in direct marketing to inquire about the difficulties encountered since the entry into force of the GDPR.

It primarily emerged that Recommendation No. 02/2013 of January 30, 2013, regarding direct marketing and data protection, did not provide answers to all the questions arising from the adoption of the GDPR and/or the sophistication of direct marketing techniques, which are mainly related to the possibility of finding a legal basis to legitimize the intended processing of personal data, the exercise of the rights of the data subjects, or the understanding of the very notion of direct marketing, which is not defined by the GDPR.

This recommendation aims to assist data controllers using direct marketing techniques (or participating in them) in acquiring the right reflexes to act in compliance with the applicable GDPR rules. It therefore examines frequently asked questions related to the protection of personal data in the context of direct marketing. The Recommendation also takes into account, where applicable, the multitude of actors that may interact with data controllers but is primarily addressed to the latter.

The Recommendation is not limited to communications made in the context of direct marketing but also examines all processing of personal data carried out for this purpose and the rules that apply to it.

The rules, concepts, and principles developed in this Recommendation are based on the GDPR and do not take into account other applicable legislation. However, the Recommendation is not intended to be an exhaustive study of the GDPR. This does not mean that data controllers carrying out data processing for direct marketing purposes are exempt from complying with all applicable rules, including those established by the GDPR that are not covered by this recommendation, such as the respect for all rights granted to data subjects as provided in Articles 12 to 22 of the GDPR or in matters of international transfers as framed by Chapter V of the GDPR.

The points examined are illustrated by situations specific to direct marketing. Each example does not imply a thorough and exhaustive examination of the various questions or compliance issues with the GDPR.

The Recommendation also reports on certain sanctions adopted by Data Protection Authorities in this area. Some of these decisions, however, may be subject to appeal or other remedies at the time of the adoption of the Recommendation. These decisions are therefore subject to revision, if applicable.

II.  
Direct Marketing: What Are We Talking About?  

Direct marketing is  
1.  
Definition  

13. The APD retains the notion of "direct marketing" as soon as the term "prospecting" mentioned in consideration 47, or in Article 21.2 of the GDPR under the term "prospecting," is generally understood as solely targeting the action of soliciting new clients, thus distinguishing between prospects and existing clients. However, the GDPR rules applicable to direct marketing apply to communications addressed to both "prospects" and those addressed to former and current clients, members, or subscribers. Furthermore, these rules are not limited to the realm of commercial activity but extend, more broadly, to any form of promotion, in its commercial acceptance (including promotion for sales purposes, advertising, electoral purposes, or increasing visibility but excluding non-profit promotion of public health or other forms of behaviors and practices encouraged by public authorities in the exercise of their missions). Finally, they do not solely target communication as such but also encompass all processing carried out in the context of marketing operations. The processing of personal data carried out to (automatically) adjust the price of a product or service based on a customer profile constitutes direct marketing operations.  

14. The GDPR does not define what is meant by "direct marketing." To date, there is no legal, official, or commonly accepted definition at the European level of this notion. Drawing on Recommendation No. 02/2013 of January 30, 2013, from our predecessor regarding direct marketing and data protection on one hand, and the Proposal for a regulation from the European Parliament and Council of September 18, 2019, concerning the respect for privacy and the protection of personal data in electronic communications and repealing Directive 2002/58/EC, the Authority proposes to define what is meant by direct marketing as follows:

Any communication, solicited or unsolicited, aimed at promoting an organization or a person, services, products, whether paid or free, as well as brands or ideas, addressed by an organization or a person acting in a commercial or non-commercial context, directly to one or more natural persons in a private or professional setting, by any means, involving the processing of personal data.

2.  
Key Concepts  

 Any communication, solicited or unsolicited  

15. This definition covers all types of communication, whether they aim to promote products or services, promote ideas proposed or supported by a person or organization, but also the promotion of that person or organization itself, including its brand image or the brands it owns or operates, excluding promotion carried out at the initiative of public authorities acting strictly within the framework of their legal obligations or public service missions for the services for which they are solely responsible.  

Example 1  
A company that sells cleaning products contacts the clients listed in its database to inform them that its products are eco-friendly.  

Example 2  
An NGO sends a postal mobilization letter for a new campaign to its list of members and donors.  

Example 3  
A political party sends an invitation to its contacts to invite them to participate in a day of meetings and activities to celebrate the start of the new season.  

16. It is therefore important to keep in mind that the notion of "marketing" should not necessarily be understood as communication pursuing a commercial or profit-making purpose.  

17. Furthermore, the notion of direct marketing communication targets both solicited and unsolicited communications.  

Example 4  
Ms. Verdura contacts the collective "Raconte-moi des salades," which provides various gardening information for free and sells natural products on its online platform to help plants grow. Ms. Verdura fills out the simplified online contact form to ask them which product to use to naturally get rid of the slugs in her garden. To receive a response to her request, she must provide her email address.  

Ms. Verdura quickly receives a "technical" response to her question (the generic name of the substance generally used to combat slugs) along with an offer related to their miracle product to deal with these unwanted guests. A few days later, she receives the newsletter from "Raconte-moi des salades."
10

Example 4 (continued)
 The technical response would not have constituted "direct marketing" without the addition of a message praising the merits of the seller's miracle product.
 The sending of the newsletter involves several data processing activities for direct marketing purposes.

18. Finally, the concept of direct marketing does not refer to advertisements appearing randomly on the Internet, such as banner ads that appear to any visitor of the site in question, without any link to the collection/use of personal data. If a banner ad appears on the screen in a targeted manner, for example due to the visitor's browsing history, it is considered direct marketing. The same applies to flyers distributed in all mailboxes of the Kingdom, a Region, or a municipality. The "door-to-door" approach is not, in principle, a tool of direct marketing. However, if, for example, advertising flyers are specifically distributed in the mailboxes of individuals who are not yet customers of an established brand in their neighborhood, inviting them to come and try its products, it will be considered direct marketing.

19. Any marketing communication that does not involve any processing of personal data is excluded from the concept of direct marketing and thus from the scope of the rules arising from the GDPR.

 Aiming at the promotion of an organization or an individual, services, products, whether paid or free, as well as brands or ideas

20. Direct marketing communications aim to promote something, without necessarily having to focus on goods or services.

Example 5
Mr. Engaged regularly receives emails from an anti-pollution association. This association informs its members about various actions taken around the world. It neither requests financial support nor offers services or goods.
 The emails sent are, however, subject to the rules of direct marketing as they promote the actions taken by the NGO and enhance its image to the public.

21. On the other hand, the rules regarding direct marketing do not apply when contact is made with individuals, such as consumers, to conduct market research, a survey, or a satisfaction inquiry, provided that the communication is carried out solely for this purpose.

22. This exception does not apply in cases where the market research or survey also aims to sell or promote goods or services, or if it allows for the collection of personal data that will later be used for marketing purposes. If this is the intention, it must be clear, and the individuals to whom the study, survey, or poll is sent must be clearly informed of it. Consequently, the rules governing direct marketing will apply.

23. Finally, communications sent by public administrations promoting certain campaigns (for example, vaccination) or services (for example, helplines for people in difficulty) for which they are legally responsible or mandated by public service do not qualify as direct marketing communications unless they simultaneously promote specific services or products offered by private providers.

Example 6
A public administration responsible for health-related tasks directly sends awareness messages about certain diseases, such as the papillomavirus for females of a certain age group, or mumps in case of an outbreak to warn parents of young children, or flu for the most vulnerable individuals due to their advanced age. This type of communication is not considered direct marketing.

However, if this communication mentions the name of vaccine XX developed by pharmaceutical company XY, this communication will be considered direct marketing and must therefore comply with the rules governing it.

 By whom?

24. Communications covered by the direct marketing rules can be sent by any type of organization, whether or not pursuing a commercial purpose. Thus, they can be communications made by companies pursuing profit-making goals but also by non-profit organizations, foundations, and public authorities. They can also be made by individuals not pursuing any profit motive, as long as their communications aim to promote something such as actions taken by them (for example, the public administration promoting the vaccine from company XY), ideas, or positions, unless these are conducted within the framework of purely and strictly private and domestic activities.

If a communication is sent to the concerned individuals under the guise of a survey or market study without revealing that the real purposes, or at least one of them, is a direct marketing purpose, there is a diversion of purpose and thus a violation of GDPR rules. This could also constitute a violation of the ePrivacy Directive if the communication is made without the consent of the individual when it was necessary to request it.
12

Example 7  
"Natura First," an NGO for nature protection, contacts individuals who have agreed to make a donation to confirm their bank details. If the contact is limited to this verification, the rules regarding direct marketing do not apply, as it does not promote ideas, services, or products.

If, on this occasion, individuals are encouraged to make further donations or if any information regarding ongoing campaigns is mentioned, it will be considered direct marketing, and the relevant rules will apply.

Example 8  
A political representative sends New Year’s wishes to several of his contacts from his private social media account, via private message from his private account. Limited to the wishes, the message sent is not a direct marketing communication.  
  
If he adds to his message information regarding the various actions and campaigns conducted over the past year and those to come, it will be considered direct marketing.  
  
If he uses his contact list from his public social media account or any other source that he does not possess purely privately, to send private messages of good wishes, it will be considered direct marketing.

25. The reasoning in the above example is also applicable to non-profit organizations, such as those active in charitable activities.

26. On the other hand, direct marketing does not generally include communications made by public authorities acting within the scope of their legal obligations or exercising their public service missions. However, if they directly send communications promoting a specific private service or organization to certain citizens, the rules of direct marketing will apply.

27. Finally, communications of direct marketing do not target communications sent by individuals in the context of purely domestic activities, as defined in Article 2 and Recital 18 of the GDPR, which excludes from its scope the processing of personal data carried out by an individual in the context of strictly personal or domestic activities (for example, sending a wedding invitation to attend the event and maintaining a database of responses), and thus without any link to a professional or commercial activity.

Example 9  
Lucie is going to celebrate her 18th birthday. For the occasion, her parents invite all her friends to come celebrate and go karting. As long as the birthday is not sponsored by the establishment managing the karting and the parents do not give away T-shirts and free passes offered by this establishment to all those who come to the birthday, the invitation obviously does not constitute a direct marketing action.

13

 To whom?  

28. The communication must be directed towards one or more identified or identifiable natural persons.

29. The notion of "identified or identifiable natural person" is intrinsically linked to the definition of personal data, which conditions the application of the GDPR.

30. As soon as the communication made is addressed to a person, either by name or based on information relating to that person (such as their IP address, for example), allowing for communication with them, it is referred to as direct marketing, provided that the other criteria of the definition are met.

Example 10  
While visiting the website "Cherry on the Cake," a kitchenware brand, Mr. Sosweet spends a few minutes looking at the various models of cake molds offered for sale. Not decided to buy, he leaves the website and connects to his email inbox. He then sees, on the right side of his screen, a pop-up window showing the different models of cake molds he viewed, with the message "Craving to cook? Click here!"  
  
Cherry on the Cake has installed cookies on Mr. Sosweet's computer, allowing the collection of information such as his IP address and the products he clicked on. Based on this data, Cherry on the Cake was able to track Mr. Sosweet's browsing and display targeted direct marketing communications in a pop-up that appears in the display area provided on Mr. Sosweet's email account host page.  
  
The message appearing in the pop-up window is framed by the rules of direct marketing.  
  
The use of cookies by Cherry on the Cake for direct marketing purposes must comply with the rules of the GDPR but also with the e-Privacy Directive, which we do not examine in this Recommendation.

31. Furthermore, whether the communication is made to a person in the context of their "private" life, for example via their personal email address, or in the context of their professional activities, via their professional email address, it remains direct marketing as long as the other criteria of the definition are met.
14

Example 11  
The company "Hi Tech" sends one of its employees to visit a trade show organized by "Technologia" dedicated to new technologies. Several brands are present with exhibition stands, and conferences and workshops are also organized. After participating in one of these workshops, the employee fills out a satisfaction form regarding the workshop but also, more generally, concerning the overall organization of the trade show. He is invited to provide his contact details as well as the name of his company since he attended in a professional capacity. The form specifies, "you authorize us to use your data to communicate about our upcoming events and trade shows." To expedite the process, participants are also invited to leave their business cards instead of filling out the form. They must deposit their cards in the designated box for this purpose. The employee is in a hurry and leaves a business card that includes his name, surname, and email address, as well as all the contact details of the company Hi Tech.  
 If the organizers of the trade show later send communications about their upcoming events to the company Hi Tech, based on the general contact data provided (without indicating the name and surname of the employee or his specific role), this does not constitute direct marketing as there is no processing of personal data;  
 If these same communications are sent to the Hi Tech employee based on the professional contact details provided on his business card left at the workshop stand, the data protection rules applicable to the processing of data for direct marketing purposes apply, even if these are his professional contact details. This implies, in particular, that the employee's contact details cannot be used for purposes other than those indicated in the form, and therefore, it is not permissible to send him direct marketing communications about anything other than the events and trade shows organized by Technologia itself.  

Example 11bis  
Let us imagine that the Hi Tech employee spontaneously gave his business card to certain participants or individuals he met during this event, whether to stay in touch if necessary or simply with the idea of meeting again on another occasion.  

One of the individuals to whom he gives his card decides, upon returning to his own organization, to enter his contact details, as well as all other details collected in this manner, into his database in order to send communications regarding his company's products and services.  
 By distributing his business card, the employee did not consent to his data being processed for direct marketing purposes;  
 Such processing of personal data exceeds the purpose for which the business card was originally given.  

32. Finally, without prejudging the necessary conditions to ensure the legality of the communications addressed to these individuals, communications sent to a prospect, an interested person, or a client/affiliate/subscriber/member, are also considered direct marketing communications, as long as they aim to promote your products, services, brand image, company, or ideas.
15

33. A prospect or an interested person (for example, a potential client who has requested extensive information about your products or services, or your organization) is distinguished from your clients, subscribers, affiliates, and existing members in that the former have not yet purchased a product from your organization, agreed to the provision of one of your services, or committed firmly to what you offer. In contrast, unlike an interested person, a "pure" prospect has no relationship with you.

 By what means?

34. Direct marketing communications can take several forms. The most obvious distinction is to separate electronic communications from non-electronic communications.

35. Non-electronic communications encompass postal mailings or human interactions without the use of electronic technical means, such as door-to-door sales, for example, when it aims to conclude the provision of a service or a sale and involves the processing of personal data intended to be included in a file. The concept of a file is important as it frames the material scope of the GDPR.

Example 12
The scouts' association makes cookies each year that they will sell to raise funds for preparing summer camps, buying equipment, and improving their facilities. They send some scouts to sell them, whose mission is to go door-to-door with boxes of cookies. They ring the doors of as many houses and apartments as possible in hopes of raising as much money as possible to help them.
 If the scouts ring the doors randomly, merely selling the cookies, there is no direct marketing because no personal data is contained or intended to be included in an organized file (such as a list of specific houses or a named list of people who have purchased a box of cookies);
 If, on the other hand, the scouts are sent by the association to specific houses or apartments to sell boxes to identified individuals based, for example, on a list established in previous years to identify those who have already purchased, it will be considered direct marketing because the approach involves the processing of personal data contained in a file.

36. Electronic communications, for their part, refer to textual, video, photo, image, or sound communications made using technology-based means, such as telephone calls, SMS, MMS, emails, chat boxes, pop-ups, or other advertising inserts whose content is directly related to the processing of personal data. These processes can be carried out using various techniques and/or technologies such as targeting (including microtargeting) or real-time targeting.

**III.**  
Direct marketing and personal data protection: how to act in compliance?  

**Actors in direct marketing and roles defined by the GDPR**  

37. The individuals, whether natural or legal persons, involved in the processing of personal data necessary for your marketing activities can be numerous, and the relationships established with them can sometimes be complex. As a data controller, you likely rely on the services of various partners who may act either as pure processors or as joint controllers with you. It is important that you determine the roles of each party in order to properly understand your obligations and theirs.  

1. **Data controller and joint controllers**  

38. You are a "data controller" when, alone or jointly, you determine the purposes and means of processing personal data.  

39. It is important to remember that an organization is not a data controller or processor "by nature." It all depends on how it behaves in practice. You must ask yourself, for each operation you perform with personal data, who determines the purposes of processing and how the processing of the data in question is carried out.  

40. To clarify your role, and that of other stakeholders (such as your technical service providers or third parties who provide you with data), ask yourself:  
 who decides to collect personal data in the first place and the type(s) of personal data to be collected;  
 who determines the individuals or categories of individuals targeted;  
 who decides on the data or categories of data to be collected;  
 who determines the purpose(s) for which the data must be used;  
 who determines and ensures the legal basis for doing so (consent, legal obligation, legitimate interest, etc.);  
 who determines whether to communicate the data and, if so, to whom;  
 who determines the content of the information to be provided to the data subjects regarding the processing or processing operations applied to their data;  
 who determines the retention period for the data; and  
 who determines how to respond to data subjects in the exercise of their rights.  

41. All these decisions can only be made by the data controller within the framework of its overall control of data processing. If you make any of these decisions, you are most likely a data controller.  

42. Article 26 of the GDPR also provides for the situation in which two or more controllers coexist, referred to as "joint controllers" of the processing. This is the case when several operators/organizations jointly determine the purposes and means of processing. Article 26 of the GDPR stipulates that in this case, the joint controllers must define their respective obligations transparently through an agreement between them that duly reflects their respective roles towards the data subjects.

CJUE, judgment C-40/17 "Fashion ID" of July 29, 2019

The CJUE decided, in its judgment "Fashion ID GmbH & Co. KG v. Verbraucherzentrale NRW eV," that the operator of a website equipped with the Facebook "like" button can be jointly responsible with Facebook for the collection and transmission to Facebook of the personal data of visitors to its site.

It had already determined that a legal or natural person who influences, for its own purposes, the processing of personal data and thus participates in determining the purposes and means of that processing can be considered as responsible for the processing (judgment of July 10, 2018, Jehova todistajat, C-25/17, paragraph 68). It had also decided that the joint liability of several actors for the same processing does not presuppose that each of them has access to the personal data concerned (judgment of June 5, 2018, Wirtschaftsakademue Schleswig-Holstein, C-201/16, paragraph 38), nor that each of these data controllers has equivalent responsibility, but rather that they may be involved at different stages of this processing and to varying degrees, such that the level of responsibility of each must be assessed taking into account all relevant circumstances of the case.

By inserting a Facebook "like" button on its website, Fashion ID consciously offers Facebook Ireland the opportunity to obtain personal data from visitors to its website at the moment they visit said website, regardless of whether these visitors have a Facebook account or even clicked on the "like" button, and without them being informed of this (paragraphs 75 and 77 of the judgment). By inserting such a button, Fashion ID thus significantly influences the collection and transmission of the personal data of visitors to its site for the benefit of the provider of this button, namely Facebook.

Regarding the purposes, the CJUE specifies that the insertion by Fashion ID of the "like" button on its website allows it to optimize advertising for its products by making them more visible on the social network Facebook when a visitor to its website clicks on said button. Fashion ID has, at the very least implicitly, consented to the collection and transmission of the personal data of visitors to its website in order to benefit from a commercial advantage consisting of increased advertising for its products. The processing operations thus carried out are therefore in the economic interest of both Facebook and Fashion ID. In such circumstances, these two organizations jointly determine the purposes of the operations of collection and communication of personal data (paragraphs 80 and 81 of the judgment).

It is indeed your responsibility, as a data controller, even jointly, to provide transparent information to the individuals whose personal data you are processing.

2. Subcontractor

46. When a public or private entity, or a natural person processes personal data on your behalf, based on your instructions, solely for the purpose of enabling you to achieve your objectives, this constitutes a subcontracting situation.

47. This subcontracting situation requires compliance with the provisions of Article 28 of the GDPR.

48. First, note that in any situation and with any subcontractor, as long as you act as the data controller, you remain bound by the obligations imposed on you by the GDPR and you must be accountable, where applicable, for any breaches of these obligations. This is particularly why Article 28.1 of the GDPR requires you to engage only subcontractors that provide sufficient guarantees regarding the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures. This also stems from your obligation under Article 24 of the GDPR, which requires you to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure that your data processing is carried out in accordance with the GDPR and to be able to demonstrate it. Engaging a certified subcontractor or one that has adhered to an approved code of conduct can serve as evidence of the existence of the sufficient guarantees required by Articles 28.1 and 4 of the GDPR.

49. In the event of a breach of the obligations of the GDPR, Article 83 of the GDPR provides that the data controller and its subcontractor may be subject to various sanctions by the Authority. A good
20

Collaboration between you and your subcontractor is therefore essential. For example, in the event of a data breach, Article 33.2 of the GDPR specifies that the subcontractor must notify the data controller of any personal data breach as soon as possible after becoming aware of it. Given that you, as the data controller, have only 72 hours to notify the Authority of any breaches that may pose a risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals, it is advisable to ensure that your subcontractor will comply with their notification obligation.

50. Your relationship with your subcontractor must be governed by a contract or another legal act, and this act or contract must be in writing, even in electronic format, in order to comply with your documentation obligation and Article 30 of the GDPR. This contract or legal act must specify the purpose and duration of the processing or processes, as well as the purposes and nature of the processing entrusted to the subcontractor. It must also define the type of personal data processed, the categories of data subjects, and remind you of your rights and obligations as the data controller. Finally, this act must at a minimum contain the details listed in Article 28.3, a) to h) of the GDPR, including the fact that the subcontractor may only process personal data on documented instructions from the data controller.

51. If you are unsure about the content of this act that must be concluded with your subcontractor, you can refer to the model of standard contractual clauses adopted by the Danish supervisory authority in accordance with Article 28 of the GDPR and which was the subject of Opinion 14/2019 of the European Data Protection Board. Even if this model of clauses has been adopted by another national authority, the GDPR does not prevent data controllers and subcontractors from other Member States from drawing inspiration from it (see Recital 81 of the GDPR).

52. While the ideal situation is for the data controller to provide complete instructions regarding the processing entrusted to the subcontractor, the reality is sometimes less clear-cut, and certain elements may be determined not by the data controller but by the subcontractor, considering the expertise that the latter has, particularly regarding the technologies applied to the processing and/or the most appropriate data security measures. The fact that a subcontractor has more expertise than you regarding the technical means to be used in the context of data processing does not, in itself, lead to a reclassification of their position from subcontractor to data controller. Some subcontractors offer "turnkey" solutions without this interfering with your obligation as the data controller to make the necessary decisions regarding the processed data, the pursued purposes, and/or the means to be implemented to achieve them.

Determine Your Processing Purposes

1. 
Initial Purpose(s)

75. One of the primary obligations of a data controller is to determine the purpose(s) it pursues that require the processing of personal data. In other words, the objectives it intends to achieve through the use of this personal data.

Article 5.1, b) of the GDPR:  
"Personal data must be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; (...)"
28

76. The correct determination of your processing purposes is essential for the proportionality assessment of the data and therefore of your data processing (a mandatory exercise aimed at ensuring that the data processed and their processing are proportionate to the purposes pursued). You must first clearly define and anticipate your processing purposes in order to subsequently define the processing activities that will be necessary to achieve them.

77. Here are some examples of direct marketing purposes:
 to inform your customers about your new products or services;
 to establish the profile of your customers;
 to allow third parties to use your customers' data to establish voter profiles;
 to offer personalized promotions for your customers' birthdays;
 to keep your customers informed about your various actions;
 to promote your brand image to the general public;
 to invite your customers or prospects to events (for the promotion of your organization);
 to communicate targeted offers to your customers that may meet their interests;
 to solicit new customers, subscribers, or affiliates.

78. It is then your responsibility to indicate them as precisely as possible in your record of processing activities (See Recitals 88, 89, and 90 of this recommendation) as well as in the document you use to provide the required information to the data subjects (since the precision of your processing purposes is also essential to ensure compliance with the transparency obligation set forth in Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR, which expressly state that data subjects must be informed of the processing purposes).

In most cases, stating that "we process your data for direct marketing purposes" is not sufficient to provide precise information in the sense of the GDPR. The level of detail expected depends, in particular, on the type of marketing communications (SMS, email, phone, mail, etc.), their frequency (monthly, biannual, etc.), their content (information about the brand, a product, a service, newsletter, discount coupons), or the complexity of the processing in question (notably based on profiling and the accuracy of that profiling, for example).

Transparency regarding your processing purposes also implies being fair. Stating that "we process your data to improve our services" when the actual goal of your marketing communications is to promote your services and encourage your customers to use them does not adequately inform the data subject that you intend to process their data for direct marketing purposes.

2. Profiling

93. Just like other processing activities that are regularly used for direct marketing purposes, profiling is a data processing activity about which you must clearly inform the data subjects. Profiling is not a purpose but rather a processing activity carried out to achieve certain purposes such as, for example, selling data or learning to better target your clientele.

Example 16

94. Article 4.4 of the GDPR defines profiling as "any form of automated processing of personal data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, in particular to analyze or predict aspects concerning work performance, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behavior, location, or movements of that natural person."

95. Profiling is given special attention as its process is often invisible to the data subjects. It leads to the creation of new personal data inferred or derived from data previously provided directly by these individuals, transactional data, or information and traces left by them during their navigation on website pages.

96. 1.  
Notion of "personal data"24  

103.  
The terms "identified" and "identifiable" are central to understanding what constitutes personal data. While data that directly identifies a person is often obvious (for example, the combination of name, surname, address, or date of birth, or even a unique identification number, such as a customer number), it can sometimes be more difficult to understand what is meant by "data that indirectly identifies a person" (for example, pseudonymized data).  

104.  
Any information that is linked to a person, no matter how minimal it may seem when considered in isolation (for example, an age, a municipality of residence, eye color, or gender, or even a registration number), is considered personal data as long as it, when combined with one or more other pieces of information, allows for the identification or individualization of a natural person. Thus, the GDPR applies to personal data that has been subject to pseudonymization. A pseudonymized data point cannot, in principle, be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such information is kept separately and subjected to technical and organizational measures to ensure that the data cannot be attributed to an identified or identifiable person. Pseudonymized data is therefore covered by the definition of personal data and thus by the GDPR, to the extent that it can allow for the identification of a person.  

105.  
Conversely, so-called "anonymized" data is not considered personal data in that it should not allow for the identification of a person, even when using additional information. While the GDPR still excludes so-called "anonymous" data from its scope for this reason, the boundary separating them is tending to diminish considerably.

**Check that you have a legal basis**

1. **Why a legal basis?**

129. The processing of personal data is only permitted if it is based on one of the six legal bases provided for in Article 6 of the GDPR30. You cannot process data without a legal basis, so you must ensure that you have one before starting your processing.

130. It is also essential to question your legal basis since the conditions for each of the bases provided for in Article 6 of the GDPR are different.

131. The legal basis has implications for the rights of the data subjects, whose specificities you must master in order to not only inform individuals correctly but also to ensure the effective exercise of their rights.

132. Finally, be aware that certain specific legislations require you to rely on one or another legal basis to frame a certain type of processing. In this regard, concerning electronic direct marketing, the e-Privacy Directive generally requires obtaining the consent of the data subjects, while allowing, as an exception and subject to certain conditions, the use of what is known as "soft opt-in," which is a lighter application of the legal basis of legitimate interests.

133. Apart from very specific cases provided for by these potential laws, no legal basis is automatic. You must take into account the context of the processing you wish to implement.

2. **Is it possible to change the legal basis?**

134. The legal basis cannot be changed during the processing. This means that if you indicate that you have an inappropriate legal basis or that it "falls away" because its conditions are not or are no longer met, the processing can no longer continue.

135. For example, in the case of processing based on consent, as soon as the individual withdraws their consent, you must cease all data processing based on that basis, unless you continue to process the same data for another purpose for which you have another valid legal basis.

136. It is also not possible to have two legal bases for the same processing purpose, nor to alternate between one and the other. You must make a choice and stick to it.

**Exception: consent obtained under Directive 95/46/EC**

When the GDPR came into effect, many data controllers active in direct marketing who based their data processing on consent faced a particular situation. The GDPR having strengthened the conditions for the validity of consent, it was no longer possible to process personal data for direct marketing purposes based on previously obtained consent unless that consent was obtained in accordance with the GDPR requirements.

If not, the WP259 Guidelines on consent under Regulation 2016/679, adopted by the G29 on November 28, 2017, as revised on April 10, 2018, and confirmed by the EDPB at its first plenary session, opened the possibility for data controllers to change, if necessary, their legal basis during processing. (See in this regard page 36 of the aforementioned guidelines)

Data controllers could therefore either renew the previously obtained consent by requesting new consent in accordance with GDPR requirements, or base this processing on another legal basis while ensuring that the processing carried out complies with the principles of fairness and accountability.

The majority of data controllers active in direct marketing chose to ask for consent again from the data subjects so that the new consent would comply with the requirements of Article 7 of the GDPR. Others opted to continue their processing activities based on legitimate interests and consequently provided their clients with all the required information regarding the processing of their data as well as an immediate option to object to such processing.

In this regard, some then resorted to the "soft opt-in" exception, derived from the ePrivacy Directive. This exception is only valid for communications to customers and solely within the framework of electronic communications for the promotion of products or services similar to those already acquired by these customers.

We will see below how to approach these legal bases.

3. 
What legal basis for direct marketing processing?

137. 
As indicated above in recital 132, certain specific laws specify the legal basis on which data controllers must necessarily rely in order to be authorized to process data. It is therefore your responsibility to verify that you are not required by a specific law to use a specific legal basis. Furthermore, when processing personal data falling under Article 9 of the GDPR, you must be able to demonstrate that your data processing is based on one of the legal bases provided for in that same provision. Also, be mindful that data falling under Article 10 of the GDPR can only be processed subject to the conditions set out in that article.

138. 
Moreover, and in any case, do not forget to inform the data subjects, in accordance with the requirements of Article 13 or Article 14 of the GDPR, about the legal basis for your processing activities. Regardless of the legal basis you rely on, it must be communicated to the data subjects.

139. 
There is no hierarchy among the legal bases provided by the GDPR. It is your responsibility, however, to be able to demonstrate that your processing is validly based on one of the legal bases set forth in Article 6 of the GDPR (and 9.2 of the GDPR, where applicable). Note, however, that some legal bases are more suitable than others for the reality of personal data processing carried out for direct marketing purposes. Others may be difficult to apply validly in light of the conditions associated with them. This is particularly the case for the legal basis of "contract" provided for in Article 6.1, b) of the GDPR. For it to be applicable, this legal basis implies that the data processing is necessary for the performance of a contract concluded with the data subject or for the performance of pre-contractual measures taken at the request of the data subject.

140. 
The fundamental notion to consider in examining a possible reliance on this legal basis is that of "necessity." For processing to rely on a contract, it must be strictly necessary for the fulfillment of that contract or for pre-contractual measures requested by the data subject. Relying on the legal basis of the contract implies specifically defining the object of this contract, its substance, and its fundamental purpose. It also implies that any data processing that is not directly necessary for the realization of this object cannot rely on this legal basis.

141. 
As the EDPB reminds us in its opinion on the legal basis of the contract, a contract cannot artificially extend the categories of data or types of processing operations necessary for the realization

of the contract to which the data subject is a party31, and what a contract encompasses does not depend solely on the perspective of the data controller but also on the reasonable expectations of that person32. Given the extreme precision of this legal basis, its application is therefore very limited.

a. Evaluation of Legitimate Interest  
Article 6.1, f) of the GDPR  
Data processing may be carried out if it is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, unless the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject requiring protection of personal data prevail, particularly when the data subject is a child.

As a reminder, when you send unsolicited direct marketing communications electronically, including via automated calling systems and communication without human intervention (automated calling machines), faxes, or emails for commercial purposes, you must have the prior consent of the subscribers or users to do so (Article 13.1 of the e-Privacy Directive).

a.3. Balancing Interests

148. 149. To carry out this balancing act, Recital 47 of the GDPR provides an important parameter as it specifies that the reasonable expectations of the data subjects based on their relationship with the data controller must be taken into account. These "reasonable expectations" are those that the data subject may have regarding the processing that will or could be carried out on their data, regarding the data about them that could be subject to such processing, and regarding the reason(s) (purpose(s)) for which it will or could be processed and by whom.

150. Note that under no circumstances can you invoke any interest of the individuals you aim to communicate with (or more generally to process their data for direct marketing purposes). The interests to be balanced against the interests, freedoms, and fundamental rights of the data subjects are solely your own, namely, in general terms, promoting your products, services, or brand image. The interest that consumers, clients, members, or other individuals you are targeting may have in receiving direct marketing communications does not come into play.

You must particularly consider the target audience whose data you are processing and to whom you direct your direct marketing communications. If these concern or may concern minors, you must be extremely cautious before considering that your interests can prevail over their freedoms and fundamental rights as well as their own interests. b. Examination of the validity conditions of consent

b.1. Informed consent

177. The person giving consent must fully understand why and to what they are consenting. This condition is inextricably linked to the information that must be provided by the data controller to the data subject at the time of data collection if it is obtained directly from the data subject or within a reasonable time if it is not obtained from that person. This information must be certain (and therefore not merely "accessible" but presented upfront to the person so that they cannot overlook it), clear, formulated in understandable language, and complete (and must also cover, for example, any profiling operations that would be carried out in addition to other data processing that may be more visible to the data subject).

The CNIL, the French Data Protection Authority, imposed a fine of 50 million euros on GOOGLE LLC on January 21, 2019, in a restricted formation, under the GDPR for lack of transparency, unsatisfactory information, and absence of valid consent for the personalization of advertising.

It found that the information provided by GOOGLE is not easily accessible, clear, and understandable for users and does not allow them to clearly understand that the legal basis on which data processing for advertising personalization is based is consent.

The restricted formation concluded that the consent obtained is neither "specific" nor "unambiguous." Although GOOGLE allows users to configure the usage options of its services when creating an account, the GDPR is not respected since the user must take the step of clicking on "more options" to access the settings, where, moreover, the display of personalized ads is pre-checked by default.

Consent is "unambiguous," as required by the GDPR, only if the user performs a positive act (for example, checking an unchecked box). Finally, before creating their account, the user is prompted to check the boxes "I accept the terms of use of Google" and "I accept that my information is used as described above and detailed in the privacy policy" in order to create their account. Such a process leads the user to consent in bulk for all purposes pursued by GOOGLE based on this agreement (advertising personalization, voice recognition, etc.). However, consent is "specific," as required by the GDPR, only if it is given distinctly for each purpose.

You must therefore clearly and specifically enumerate each of the pursued purposes; otherwise, the consent given is not valid as it is not specific.  

On December 17, 2019, our Authority imposed a fine of 15,000 euros on a company due to its non-compliance with GDPR rules regarding its technique for obtaining consent for the placement of cookies.  

The APD was thus able to note a repeated lack of vigilance by this company regarding its obligation of transparency as provided by Articles 12 and 13 of the GDPR and for not having obtained valid consent (opt-in) for the placement of cookies.

b.4. Unambiguous consent  

191.  
The GDPR stipulates that consent requires a clear affirmative act, which must be given by means of a statement, written or oral. This condition primarily aims to avoid any ambiguity regarding the will of the concerned individual to provide their consent.  

192.  
This requirement puts an end to the method of pre-checked boxes, boxes to check to express refusal of consent (opt-out), or the technique of stating that continuing to browse a website equates to consent (to data collection via cookies, for example).  

When the legal basis chosen for your data processing is consent, the criterion of a clear affirmative act established by the GDPR no longer allows you to consider the silence or inactivity of the concerned individual as an indication of their choice, nor does it allow you to assume that simply continuing to use a service or not unchecking a pre-checked box constitutes consent.

Furthermore, as we will see below, the GDPR adds an obligation for the data controller to be able to prove consent if they intend to use it as a legal basis for their data processing. A single button allowing the person to check all the boxes requesting consent is therefore not valid.  

Do not lose sight of the fact that the use of the so-called opt-out mechanism does not result in the collection of valid consent!  
The requirement to obtain a positive act of consent cannot be read in isolation from the other requirements applicable to consent. As a reminder, consent must be given in an informed manner. This implies, in particular, that the person must understand not only what they are consenting to but also that they are in the process of consenting.

b.5 Explicit Consent

201. In cases where your processing of personal data related to your direct marketing purposes involves the processing of data covered by Article 9 of the GDPR, you must take into account the additional condition for the validity of consent for the processing of such data: it must be "explicit."

202. You must therefore implement a system that allows the individual concerned to expressly formulate their consent, for example through a written statement from the individual, which may include their signature. You may also ask the individual concerned to expressly confirm their consent by sending an email for this purpose or by having them fill out an online form. Alternatively, you could ask the individual concerned to verbally confirm their consent while recording it, provided that they have been given sufficiently clear and complete information regarding this matter.

e.2. Withdrawal of Consent

219. The GDPR allows individuals who have given their consent to the processing of their data to withdraw it at any time.

220. This also implies that this withdrawal can be done free of charge and without detriment, such as a reduction in the level of service provided until then.

Example 32 A music festival sells tickets through an online platform. For each ticket sold, it requests the buyer's consent to use their contact details for commercial purposes. To confirm – or not – their consent regarding the use of their data for this purpose, customers can select either "no" or "yes." The data controller informs customers that they will have the option to withdraw their consent. To do this, they can contact a call center free of charge on weekdays between 8 AM and 5 PM. By proceeding in this way, the data controller does not comply with Article 7.3 of the GDPR. Although it is free, it is more difficult to withdraw consent here than to give it.

221. Although the GDPR places fundamental importance on the withdrawal of consent, it does not specify the form that this withdrawal must or can take. The EDPB indicates in this regard that when "consent has been given electronically solely by a click, a tap, or by swiping the screen, the individuals concerned must, in practice, be able to withdraw this consent by the same means."45 Requiring individuals to follow a complicated path marked by links to subsequent electronic documents or forcing them to provide a password does not meet this requirement for equivalent simplicity. In the case of third-party cookies, redirecting the user to the third party's informational pages and requiring them to search there, for each of these third parties, for the means to express their withdrawal/refusal of consent also does not satisfy this requirement.

222. When a person withdraws their consent, all processing operations of their data based on that person's consent must cease. However, this does not render unlawful the operations carried out on the basis of consent, validly given, prior to the withdrawal.

223. Furthermore, in the event of withdrawal of consent, you must assess whether the retention of the data used for your direct marketing purposes is justified or not, even in the absence of a deletion request from the individual concerned, in accordance with Article 5.1, e) of the GDPR, which requires you to limit the retention of personal data to the purpose pursued.

224. **IV. Conclusion**

236. In light of the number and variety of stakeholders involved, the number and categories of data processed, as well as the types of processing operations carried out, sometimes very intrusive, within the framework of direct marketing purposes, the data controllers active in this area are a priority for the supervisory authorities, both in terms of their support and their oversight. It is due to the number of actors, affected individuals, and data processed in this sector that direct marketing constitutes one of the priority actions of the 2020-2025 strategic plan of the Data Protection Authority.

237. The GDPR provides numerous clarifications for operators engaging in direct marketing. This Regulation also introduces a new paradigm by requiring that the protection of personal data be considered by data controllers from the design stage of their personal data processing and at all stages thereof. In this regard, it specifically requires data controllers to ensure that affected individuals can control their data. Individuals are concerned by and associated, as a key and indispensable actor, with the management of their personal data.

238. You must therefore precisely determine your processing purposes, ensure that you have a valid legal basis for pursuing them, be able to comply with and respond to your transparency obligations by being perfectly clear and honest with affected individuals about what you do with their personal data, and ensure and respect the exercise of their rights. You must also implement appropriate security measures in light of the risks that your processing may pose to the personal data for which you are responsible. You must also be able to demonstrate at any time what you have put in place to comply with the GDPR, in accordance with the principle of accountability.

239. Your compliance with the GDPR should not be dictated solely by the risk of sanctions but, above all, by the desire to establish a genuine relationship of trust with the affected individuals who are essential to your business through adherence to data protection rules. The GDPR should become a common language for all stakeholders to whom it applies, and it is important to master the codes and vocabulary so that the different parties involved can understand each other and ensure respect for what it aims to preserve: the protection of personal data and the individuals to whom it pertains.

240. Finally, acting in accordance with the GDPR is not only an obligation regarding the personal data you process. It is also an ethical behavior in the market, both towards affected individuals and among partners. To ensure uniformity and consistency in direct marketing practices among data controllers active in this area and to communicate these practices clearly and transparently to affected individuals, the Data Protection Authority recommends the establishment of Codes of Conduct for the relevant sectors in this matter, as provided for by Article 41 of the GDPR.
