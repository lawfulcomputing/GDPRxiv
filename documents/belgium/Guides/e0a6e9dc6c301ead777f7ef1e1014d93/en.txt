Summary  
The Data Protection Authority (DPA) has many missions, including informing citizens, businesses, and public actors about certain issues related to data protection. Among these issues, those related to a 'secure' elimination of data or data media are certainly recurring.  
Regardless of their motivations, data controllers wish to carry out this operation but sometimes lack a clear vision of what constitutes a satisfactory outcome (particularly in terms of personal data protection) and how to achieve such an outcome.  
The scarcity, at the international level, of reference documents on the subject, or their absence at the European and national levels, combined with the DPA's desire to provide stakeholders with a useful guide in the form of clear, up-to-date, and comprehensive guidelines, is the basis for this recommendation.  
This document presents the various existing "cleaning" techniques (rewriting, cryptographic erasure, demagnetization, etc.) for different types of media (HDD, SSD, paper, etc.) that either make access to data impossible on a preserved medium (erasure without the possibility of reconstruction and encryption) or result in the destruction of the medium (without the possibility of reconstruction).  
The recommendation also addresses this treatment (cleaning and destruction) in a broader manner by detailing its various aspects, both legal (notably related to the GDPR) and technical or organizational, and examines the treatment from before the purchase of the media to the stages of verification and recording of results.  
Finally, a summary table presents to the reader, depending on the type of media, the recommended cleaning and destruction techniques that allow achieving the desired level of confidentiality.  
While the principles and concepts mentioned in this document are inherently relatively enduring, there are certain tools, methods, or examples presented that, in light of the evolution of knowledge and techniques in the field, may need to be updated more quickly. The paragraphs or parts of text potentially concerned are preceded by the characters ‘\\’ (double backslashes).

1. Introduction  
01. In the context of its activities, the data controller1 encounters numerous situations in which it wishes to ensure that the transfer of information media to another environment does not lead to unauthorized disclosure of the data contained on these media.  
These situations in which the data controller must make a decision regarding the "cleaning2" of data are often related to the end of their life cycle or that of their media, or to their reuse in a different security context3.  
02. For example:  
 The disposal of decommissioned IT equipment (in the broad sense4);  
 The sending of a photocopier for repair;  
 The removal of paper archives;  
 The sorting of HR department files;  
 The transfer of computers to a charitable organization;  
 The return of PCs under a leasing agreement;  
 The end of the lease contract for a multifunction printer;  
 Or the sale, after depreciation, of the company's desktops and laptops to staff members.  
03. The motivations of the data controller can be varied, such as the need to protect data that has particular value in their eyes or is classified

2. Principles and Preliminary Concepts  
15. The ‘cleaning’ or destruction of a data medium18 will be:  
 Authorized (according to an internal procedure and/or applicable law);  

 Appropriate (irreversible, in accordance with risk analysis and the resulting security/confidentiality requirements);  

 Supervised by the data controller (in case of subcontracting, see section 3.1.3. for additional measures to be taken);  

 Documented (proof of destruction, see section 6);  

 And executed in a timely manner (taking into account legal deadlines, issues related to waiting storage).  

2.1. Inventory and Classification of Information  
16. In order to determine which method will be used to best mitigate the risks of unauthorized data disclosure, the data controller must know:  
2.1.1. The nature and categories of data present on the medium  
17. They must, at the very least, know whether personal data is present or not, and if so, they may also want to:  
 Identify, among this data, those that are referred to as “sensitive” (belonging to a particular category19) or related to criminal convictions or offenses (Article 10 of the GDPR);  

 Distinguish data that is encrypted20, and/or pseudonymized21;

2.2. Processing Steps  
26. In summary, the main steps of the ‘cleaning’ and  
destruction operations may be as follows:  
A. Policy (security and confidentiality)  
27. The first step is to draft a document, validated by management,  
encompassing the entire issue of ‘data sanitization’ and including all the data  
media existing in the organization. This document must notably describe the  
context, the objectives to be achieved, the authorization procedure for ‘cleaning’ or  
destruction (including for backups) and the various steps of the processing. It  
will also detail the responsibilities of the stakeholders (and management) regarding  
the execution as well as the control of the different steps of the processing  
(chain of responsibility). It is important that each step, without  
exception, is placed under the responsibility of a duly designated person (see  
e.g. par.240).  

28. The responsibility of the stakeholders extends beyond the actual  
cleaning/destruction procedure. It may be useful to determine who would be  
responsible for reputational damage, and any potential sanctions if, at a later stage,  
it turns out that certain data media were not processed according to the validated  
procedure.  

29. The authors of the document will ensure top-down, full, and complete  
support from the hierarchy. In binding areas, such as security and  
confidentiality, management support is essential, otherwise these  
policies may only be paper with content that is not applied. It must be ensured that  
the responsibility for the ‘cleaning’ of the media is entrusted to a member of  
the organization with an appropriate level of authority.

C. Risk Analysis  
36. The main objective is to determine what risk is incurred if an unauthorized person accesses personal data contained on the information media, which constitutes a data breach and an infringement of Articles 5.1.f and 32.2 of the GDPR (see Annex B). - “personal data” (and not all the data of the organization),  
- on the data subject (i.e., the person to whom the data relates) and not on the organization.  

38. If the risk analysis is an essential step, the data controller, assisted by their Data Protection Officer if applicable, may also usefully carry out a “data protection impact assessment” (DPIA, Article 35 of the GDPR28), whether it is mandatory or not.  
The DPIA will help the data controller to ask the right questions;  
- It will contain useful information for filling out the record of processing activities (Article 30 of the GDPR);  
- It will help to comply with the obligation of data protection by design (Article 25 of the GDPR). Given that the DPIA also serves, even before processing, to identify measures to address risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects, the DPIA can provide valuable assistance in this regard.

D. Security Measures  
40. The next step is to implement the technical and organizational measures that allow for the reduction of any identified risks to an acceptable level (for the organization and for the individuals concerned).  

41. This step also includes identifying actions that could be taken quickly and effectively to respond to a potential data breach. If personal data were to be compromised during the 'cleaning' of the media or even after leaving your organization, you could still be held responsible for the breach (you remain responsible for the processing until the end of the data lifecycle).  

E. Evaluation  
42. It is then necessary to evaluate to what extent the actions taken have achieved the set objective (preventing the loss of confidentiality). If necessary, choose another technique.  

F. Documentation

3. The Different Methods and Techniques  
3.1. Introduction  
3.1.1. Important Clarifications  
50. Let us start with an important remark, clarifying that simply deleting (by pressing the ‘delete’ key on your keyboard, for example) files or directories via your device's interface only removes the pointers to these files and not the data itself. By deleting the pointers, the device makes the area where the files were located available again for writing other data. By analogy, it is as if to erase a chapter of a book, you removed all references to that chapter from the table of contents. Thus, by browsing through the book, you could still find the content of the chapter.  

51. This is why this action, which does not result in any real erasure, is not discussed in this document.  

52. It should also be noted that formatting does not erase the data either, whether it is quick or standard.  
3.1.2. Three Levels of Confidentiality  
53. In specialized literature, different techniques are often classified according to the desired level of confidentiality (security) or, in other words, the probability of recovering the original data. Three levels of confidentiality are distinguished, associated with three classes of techniques: clear, purge, and destroy.  
The "clear" level techniques aim to prevent data recovery using software. They offer moderate confidentiality (some data may be recoverable if one has the time, knowledge, and necessary skills). These are purely logical techniques.  

Examples: partial rewriting using standard commands (read and write) and resetting the device or media (factory reset - often recommended for mobile devices and routers/switches).  

The "purge" level techniques aim to prevent data recovery using advanced laboratory techniques. They offer a higher level of confidentiality and are appropriate when the media is intended to be reused in a security/confidentiality context different from the initial context. These techniques are both logical and physical.  

Examples: rewriting using dedicated commands, degaussing, and cryptographic erasure (see section 3.2.4.).  

32 The difference between the two mainly lies in the fact that a full format will check all 'bad sectors', which explains the length of the operation compared to a quick format.  
33 The term 'logical' refers to a technique whose mechanisms are executed via software.

They rely on physical destruction and are therefore incompatible with the reuse of the media. It should be noted that a technique rendering the media unusable will not achieve the destroy level if some data remains recoverable.  

Examples: incineration, shredding, and grinding.

68. What differentiates these various protocols includes, on one hand, the number of overwrites, that is to say, the number of successive rewriting passes that the surface of the disk will undergo, and on the other hand, the final step of the protocol, namely the verification of the effect of the rewriting passes.  

69. For example, the DoD 5220.22-M protocol, which is very commonly used and present in all leading software on the market, recommends writing to all addressable spaces of the medium, a binary character (in this case 0), then its complement (1), and finally a random binary character (0/1). The verification of the result constitutes the last step of the protocol.  

70. The version of this "erasure" protocol, still perceived as a true standard and which is delivered in most third-party software, corresponds to an obsolete version of a standard from the U.S. Department of Defense (DoD). The triple rewriting of the disk imposed by this old version of the protocol is more than sufficient to prevent data recovery by commercially available software (level 'clear'). While the effectiveness of erasure protocols seems logically and a priori related to the number of rewriting passes that all areas of the disk will have undergone, this logic is, however, outdated.  

71. Indeed, for several years now, a consensus has emerged (NIST, HMG British Standard, BSI-GS, CMRR) asserting that, following the technological evolution of media (notably related to the increase in their density and thus their capacity), the number of rewriting passes can be reduced to 1, without increasing the possibility of recovering data from the disk using logical solutions. However, a verification pass must be executed.  

72. If one writing pass and one verification pass are sufficient (except for older hardware dating from before 2000 or of unknown age), one can therefore conclude that a protocol proposing 3 rewriting passes and one final verification or a verification after each writing pass (such as the old version of the very popular DoD 5220.22-M) is also sufficient.  

73. On the other hand, although they are not strictly discouraged, protocols proposing a number of passes greater than one writing pass and one verification pass can be qualified, based on our current knowledge and the techniques used, as unnecessary.

91. We have so far discussed the ‘Secure Erase’ command. This is the term most frequently used in the literature, but it is often used imprecisely.  
92. Indeed, among the commands of the ATA standard, it is more accurate to refer to the ‘Security Erase Unit’ command, which is available in two modes: the standard mode ‘Secure Erase’ or ‘Normal Erase’ and the mode called ‘Enhanced Secure Erase’ or ‘Enhanced Erase’.  
93. The ‘enhanced’ mode, which targets "sectors that are no longer used due to reassignment, is not supported by all ATA media.  
94. Although their names are similar, these two modes present differences. When the normal erase mode is selected, the ‘Security Erase Unit’ command writes zeros (in binary) to all areas where data has been written by the user.  
95. When the enhanced erase mode is selected, the ‘Security Erase Unit’ command writes data according to predefined patterns and also rewrites the sectors of the disk that are no longer used or marked inaccessible to the user. The implementation of this mode is optional and is not supported by all manufacturers. However, if available, it will be preferred over the standard mode.

3.2.2. Anonymization  
111. \\ An anonymization, making it impossible to re-identify the individuals concerned, is becoming increasingly difficult to ensure as access to larger and larger online databases intensifies.  

112. \\ For this reason, this technique will not be considered to provide a sufficient level of confidentiality/security. This is regardless of the resources (time and personnel) required for its execution, which further diminishes its appeal compared to other techniques.  

113. If anonymization has already been performed, it will be necessary, before any transfer of an information medium, to examine the validity of the method used and preferably to conduct a re-identification test carried out by personnel independent of those who performed the anonymization (which will be justified all the more as the amounts of data on the medium are large).  

114. Finally, let us not forget that modifying the data contained on a medium (values in a database, for example) does not necessarily remove them from the medium (no data overwriting).  
3.2.3. Demagnetization - degaussing  
115. Demagnetization involves applying a magnetic force of sufficient strength to erase all data from a particular magnetic medium. The effectiveness of this technique is related to the relative intensity of the magnetic force provided by the degaussing device and the magnetic properties of the data medium.  

116. Although it is an important technique for cleaning magnetic media, the reader can deduce from the above that demagnetization is not effective, given their nature, on most flash memory devices, including SSDs. Indeed, they use integrated circuits to store data instead of storing it magnetically. It will also not be used on mixed information media consisting of at least one non-magnetic non-volatile medium.  

117. This reminds us of the necessity for proper inventorying of media, with mention of their type and the associated cleaning method, because if care is not taken to distinguish SSDs from hard drives during demagnetization, the data stored on the SSDs will remain intact.  

118. Let us not forget that some devices may integrate both types of media (electronic and magnetic). If demagnetization is considered for these media,

3.2.4.2. SEDs  
130. Many information media contain built-in "self-encryption" mechanisms. They are collectively referred to as "hardware-based full disk encryption" (FDE) and more specifically as "self-encrypting devices" (SEDs75) when it comes to hard drives or solid state drives (SSD). Self-encryption implies that all data written to the media is encrypted by the media before writing and decrypted by the media at the time of reading76. The encryption key is known only to the media but can nonetheless be changed by an authorized user. If the key is modified, any data previously written with the initial key becomes undecipherable. Changing the key can therefore serve to 'destroy' the data by making it unrecoverable (undecipherable).  

131. The technique of cryptographic erasure is therefore easy and especially quick to execute on SEDs since the encryption phase has already been performed.  

132. SEDs that comply with the OPAL77 standard of the Trusted Computing Group78 use the AES79 (Advanced Encryption Standard) encryption algorithm with 128 or 256-bit keys. For these media, cryptographic erasure is referred to as "PSID Revert" because it requires, before launching the actual command and erasing the keys, the introduction of a unique identifier specific to each media: the PSID80 or Physical Security ID.

136. This technique (encryption followed by cryptographic erasure) can be used on other media (that are not SEDs or do not support built-in commands), by employing third-party encryption software and permanently deleting the keys once encryption is performed. However, the preliminary encryption phase of the media can be a very time-consuming process (several hours, depending on the capacity of the media, its write/read speed, and the computing power allocated to the operation).  

137. In contrast, on-the-fly encryption of SEDs makes the technique of cryptographic erasure very fast and almost immediately prevents access to the data contained on the media.  

138. In the case of cryptographic erasure, it is also necessary to ensure that no personal data has been written before on-the-fly encryption, as this data will not be protected by cryptographic erasure.  

139. In the risk analysis prior to the choice of this technique, the responsible party must take into account future technological developments that may render current encryption methods less secure.  

140. The encryption operation, performed to prevent access to the data contained on the media, must be carried out according to a procedure validated by the data controller.  
3.2.4.5. Risks  
141. Once encrypted, the data, although recorded in another form, is still present on the media. The use of this technique implies that the encryption algorithm must be sufficiently robust to withstand decryption without knowledge of the key and, on the other hand, that the initial key (i.e., before its modification/destruction) must, in no way, be recoverable, either on the media itself or elsewhere (also consider potential backups). These requirements are common to techniques using encryption.  

142. This procedure will provide that:

3.3. The data carrier is destroyed  
147. It should be noted from the outset that there are a number of cases where the physical destruction of the information carrier should be preferred to its 'cleaning':  
- If the carrier is defective;  
- If the reader is defective;  
- If the equipment necessary to access the data is no longer available;  
- If the type of carrier makes 'cleaning' impossible, such as WORM media (write once, read many - for example: non-rewritable CD-ROM);  
- If the verification step that concludes the 'purge' or 'clear' methods does not yield reliable results or fails (for known or unknown reasons).  

148. Regardless of environmental concerns, it may be more economical to proceed with the destruction of the carriers than to 'clean' them for reuse.  

149. Finally, it should be noted that chemical destruction will not be considered in this document. Even though certain chemical agents can attack and destroy data carriers, this rarely used technique is also dangerous to health and harmful to the environment.  
3.3.1. Segmentation of Techniques  
150. A) Some destruction techniques only partially damage the carrier.  
Consequently, the data stored on the intact parts may remain accessible. This is the case for the deformation techniques discussed in the following section 3.3.2.  

151. B) Other techniques, such as shredding, grinding, or disintegration, break the carrier into pieces (see section 3.3.3.).  
It is important to realize that in this case as well, the data is still present on the targeted carrier. It is simply divided into smaller parts. If one knows that a hard drive can contain terabytes of data, it will be easily understood that a fragment of a hard drive platter, measuring barely 1 cm², could still contain several gigabytes of data.  

The level of security/confidentiality provided by fragmenting the carrier will be linked to the size of the fragments obtained. The smaller the fragments, the more resources and time will be required to reconstruct the data. This link (fragment size - security/confidentiality) is at the heart of the DIN 66399 standard, discussed in section 3.3.6.  

Guide on the Erasure and Destruction of Information Carriers | Version 1.0 - 07/01/2021  
39  
152. C) Finally, a third group of techniques allows for the complete destruction of the carrier and, by extension, the data it contains.  
The result is achieved by changing the state of the carrier, that is, by transitioning from a solid state to a gaseous state (sublimation) or to a liquid state (melting).  

3.3.2. Physical Deformation  
153. A large number of different techniques are covered by the term 'physical deformation techniques'. They can be implemented by both large industrial machines and common tools such as a hammer, a pneumatic nailer, a drill, or a press.  

154. Among these techniques, one will find in particular:  
- Folding;  
- Cutting;  
- Drilling / puncturing / punching / piercing.  

155. Benders use a metal wedge to bend a carrier (essentially hard drives) along its length at a 90-degree angle. The metal wedge, pressed with great force, damages the platters, read heads, electric motor, and electronics of the hard drive so that it can no longer be accessed via its interface.  

156. As for perforation, while the image of a technician using a drill to make holes in a hard drive immediately comes to mind, this is not the method recommended by the ITAD (IT Asset Disposition) sector. Dedicated machines for this method do exist. A perforator uses a hardened steel pin to drill the carriers. When drilling a hard drive, the platters, read heads, electric motor, and electronics of the hard drive are damaged so that it is no longer accessible via its interface.  

157. Some devices optionally offer a module that can also destroy SSDs (Solid State Drives) by perforation. Depending on the model, the SSD is pierced in several places with metal pins or cracked in a wave-like pattern.  

158. These techniques have in common that they only partially damage the carrier and leave the data stored on the unaffected parts accessible.  

159. Consequently, these techniques do not achieve the level of confidentiality "destroy," even if they may render the data impossible to recover via the carriers' interface and that they can no longer be used for further storage. The carrier is indeed not considered 'destroyed' until...

3.3.3. Shredding, Crushing, and Disintegration88  
161. Although these techniques are different, they all result in the disaggregation of the media, transforming it into smaller components. The size of the debris will depend on the technique, the materials composing the media, and the technical characteristics of the device used for this purpose.  

162. Shredders, for example, come in a wide range of sizes and depending on the model, can reduce almost anything from tires to hard drives or SSDs, as well as paper or a sofa. Thus, for a hard drive, the plastic pieces of the casing will likely be larger than the pieces of the platters.

182. The data controller must keep in mind that when choosing to engage an external provider to eliminate their media using the techniques discussed in chapter 3.3, once the destruction is completed, this provider will likely recycle them or dispose of them in a landfill.  

183. This means that the data, if the media have not been securely destroyed, will potentially be accessible again to third parties. The remnants of the media could even end up in different parts of the world if they are sold to waste management or recycling companies. Incineration eliminates this risk.  
3.3.4. Incineration  
184. Incineration, although less commonly used and having a significant environmental impact, is an effective technique because, if carried out properly in suitable incinerators, it guarantees the total and irreversible destruction of data and media. These can consist of large waste incinerators, as well as smaller mobile and compact incinerators that specialized companies can bring to the site of the data controller upon request. Some mobile models are dedicated to the disposal of paper, while others are capable of melting metal.  

185. As part of their digital transformation, many organizations digitize documents to store them online or archive them, and then find themselves with the originals to dispose of. When the quantities of paper to be disposed of are large, incineration can be an alternative to shredding.  

186. Other types of media can also be destroyed by this technique. In its manual on media cleaning, the NSA cites magnetic tapes, floppy disks, optical media, electronic media, and paper as being able to be securely destroyed by incineration, provided that the material has been reduced to ash. Regarding hard drives, it specifies that the coating of the internal platters must be reduced to ash and/or the internal platters must be physically deformed by the action of heat.

3.3.5. Demagnetization - degaussing  
188. Demagnetization, already presented as a 'cleaning' technique (with preservation of the media - see section 3.2.3.), also allows for the destruction (rendering unusable) of magnetic media if they are subjected to a sufficient magnetic force, regardless of their operating system and interface, even if they are damaged.  

189. It should be emphasized again that demagnetization is, however, not effective on flash memory devices such as SSDs. Similarly, this technique is not suitable for paper and optical media.  

190. Demagnetization subjects magnetic media to a powerful magnetic field that can be created either by strong magnets or by an electromagnetic discharge.  

191. It is recommended to follow demagnetization with another destruction technique; this will achieve the highest level of confidentiality/security, address a failure of the degausser or an oversight by the technician, and provide a visual verification that the media has been destroyed and is ready for disposal. Under these conditions and using an approved device, the NSA validates the technique at the 'purge' level.

Comparison DIN - NSA - NIST  
208. Generally speaking, the DIN 66399 standard is not as demanding as the guidelines and standards of NIST or NSA.  

209. Thus, unlike the DIN standard, the NSA recommends that the destruction of hard disks (magnetic and electronic) be preceded by demagnetization. Furthermore, destruction must be carried out with devices approved by the NSA.  

210. Moreover, demagnetization, like any technique that does not result in fragmentation of the carrier, is not taken into account by the DIN standard, whereas the NSA incorporates it and recommends that it be followed by a deformation or destruction technique, and the NIST incorporates it at the "purge" level (and indirectly at the "destroy" level, given the irreparable damage that the technique can cause).  

211. Regarding the security levels themselves, the NIST requires, for example for paper carriers, shredding that produces residues not exceeding 1 mm x 5 mm in size, or pulverization/disintegration using a device equipped with a safety screen102 of 2.4 mm. Only the last security level of DIN (P-7) meets this requirement (maximum residue size of 5 mm²).  

212. Another feature, associated with each security level of the DIN standard and absent from the guidelines and standards of the NIST and NSA, also lowers the level of requirement. This is the allowable deviation or tolerance, which is the permitted deviation from the recommended particle size for each security level of the DIN standard.  

213. This in itself would tend to disqualify H5 as acceptable for confidential data of fundamental importance to an organization or the individual concerned.

216. It is not always possible for the data controller to proceed with the erasure or destruction of information media.

5. Verification  
222. The last step of the procedure, before issuing a document certifying the cleaning or destruction (see section 6 "Recording"), consists of verifying the destruction of the data, which should ensure that they have been properly cleaned or destroyed. This is essential because the sources of possible failure are numerous. Think of human errors (e.g., an untreated disk placed in the stack of treated disks, lack of training, desire to hurry), hardware errors (e.g., a damaged shredder knife or failure of one of the components located between the erasure software and the data on the disks), and software errors (lack of updates, quality of the software).  

223. When the data controller engages a subcontractor for cleaning or destruction operations, this will require discussing with them the verification modalities and possibly defining them contractually.  

224. Verification is ideally performed by an independent person who has not participated in the actual destruction or cleaning of the data media. Following the same logic, when software is used for data cleaning, the verification part should be ensured by software different from that used for cleaning.  

225. This quality control process is documented just like the other steps of the destruction/cleaning procedure. For example, the number of samples to be tested will be specified in the documentation.  

226. In parallel to this documentation, the data controller must have an information system that allows, upon request, to produce proof of compliance (i.e., confirm the successful erasure of the data) for each media.  

227. We will conclude this section 5 with some information on verification specific to different "cleaning" techniques.  
Erasure - Rewriting  
228. Depending on its extent, the result of the verification may be more or less reliable, with the best assurance of effective data "cleaning" generally obtained by a complete read of all accessible areas of the media, to verify that the expected values (binary numbers 0 or 1) are indeed found, that is to say, those determined in the configuration of the rewriting pass.  

229. This verification read is obviously only possible when the media is not destroyed.  

230. Even though verification is a time-consuming process, the percentage of the media surface to be verified should, depending on the time available, be as large as possible, and in any case should not be less than 10% (which is often the percentage proposed by default by third-party software).

6. Record Keeping  
237. Proof of destruction is an essential element of the traceability chain. In accordance with the principle of accountability under the GDPR (art. 5.2), it will allow the data controller to demonstrate compliance with the principles regarding the processing of personal data, including those related to data retention limitation and integrity and confidentiality (art. 5.1.e and f - see Annex B).  

238. It is therefore important to record and retain information regarding the proper execution of the cleaning and/or destruction and the technique (and thus the level of confidentiality/security selected), whether the procedure is carried out internally or with the assistance of a subcontractor. Proof of destruction/cleaning is generally issued by the person in charge of the operation (under the authority of the subcontractor or the data controller) and validated by a person designated by the data controller.  

239. Although this proof is often referred to as a 'certificate' of destruction by various industry players, we prefer the terms attestation or declaration, for their less official connotation104.  
Subcontracting  
240. When a subcontractor is engaged for the cleaning and/or destruction of information media, these may be gathered and stored by the data controller in a location that is not secure until the subcontractor retrieves them. The temporary storage of these media does not exclude the possibility of loss or theft. Therefore, it may be useful to compare the list of media that have been stored with the list of media that are actually taken over by the external service provider. We will again emphasize the necessity of designating one or more responsible persons for each step of the processing, including those for the collection of the media and their storage.  

241. The actual cleaning process can take place on the premises of the data controller or off-site (depending on the technical capabilities of the subcontractor or the request of the data controller). In the case of off-site processing, it would ideally require that an agent of the data controller be physically present throughout the destruction process to ensure that the media have indeed been properly destroyed. Without this, the "proof of destruction" provided by the subcontractor may not correspond to reality and may not constitute a valid document. The data controller may also engage bailiffs to oversee and record all operations.

 Overwriting (scrubbing) the media using software approved by the organization, then validating (verification).  

For ATA & SCSI Solid State Drives, USB Removable Media, and Memory Cards:  
 Overwriting (scrubbing) the media using software approved by the organization, then validating (verification). The Clear level must involve at least one write pass with a fixed data value (e.g., all zeros).  
Optional: multiple write passes or more complex values may be used.  

For ATA Solid State Drives (only):  
 Secure Erase Command: If supported, use the Secure Erase Unit command in ‘enhanced’ mode.  
Purge  
A) ATA Solid State Drives  
 1. Sanitize Device Command: If supported, use one of the commands from the ATA Sanitize Device feature set (preferable to the Secure Erase command). One or both of the following options may be available:  
1.a) Block Erase Command  
Optional: once the command has been successfully applied, write binary 1s in the user-addressable area of the media, then perform a second block erase.  
1.b) Cryptographic Erasure (crypto scramble command).  
Optional: Once the cryptographic erasure has been successfully applied, use the block erase command. If this command is not supported, the Secure Erase or Clear procedure may also be applied after the cryptographic erasure.  

 2. Cryptographic Erasure via the Opal Security Subsystem Class (see par.132), if built-in commands are not available.  
Optional: Once the cryptographic erasure has been successfully applied, use the block erase command. If this command is not supported, the Secure Erase or Clear procedure may also be applied after the cryptographic erasure.  

B) SCSI Solid State Drives  
 1. Sanitize Command: If supported, use one of the commands from the SCSI Sanitize feature set. One or both of the following options may be available:  
1.a) Block Erase Command  
1.b) Cryptographic Erasure (cryptographic erase command).  
Optional: Once the cryptographic erasure has been successfully applied, use the block erase command. If this command is not supported, the Secure Erase or Clear procedure may also be applied after the cryptographic erasure.  

 2. Cryptographic Erasure via the Opal Security Subsystem Class (see par.132), if built-in commands are not available.  
Optional: Once the cryptographic erasure has been successfully applied, use the block erase command. If this command is not supported, the Secure Erase or Clear procedure may also be applied after the cryptographic erasure.  

C) USB Removable Media and Memory Cards - Not available  
Most of these media do not support built-in commands, or if they are supported, the interfaces are not standardized.  
Destroy  
 Incineration of the media: the media must be reduced to ashes.  

 Shredding - Disintegration  
The NSA cites a maximum debris size of 2mm on each side and recommends batch destruction with other storage devices (possibly refer to the list of NSA-approved devices).  
The DIN standard recommends for levels E3 a maximum debris size of 160 mm², E4 max. 30 mm², E5 max. 10 mm², E6 1 mm², and P7 max. 0.5 mm².

Article 5: Principles relating to the processing of personal data  
1. Personal data must be:  

a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (lawfulness, fairness, transparency);  

b) collected for specified, legitimate, and explicit purposes and not further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; further processing for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research, or for statistical purposes is not considered incompatible with the initial purposes, in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1 (purpose limitation);  

c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (data minimization);  

d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable steps must be taken to ensure that personal data that is inaccurate, in relation to the purposes for which it is processed, is erased or rectified without delay (accuracy); 4.5.2016 L 119/35 Official Journal of the European Union FR.  

e) kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed; personal data may be stored for longer periods insofar as they will be processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research, or for statistical purposes in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, provided that the appropriate technical and organizational measures required by this Regulation are implemented to safeguard the rights and freedoms of the data subject (storage limitation);  

f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, using appropriate technical or organizational measures (integrity and confidentiality);  

2. The data controller is responsible for compliance with paragraph 1 and is able to demonstrate that compliance (accountability).  

Article 32: Security of processing  
1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of processing as well as the risks, varying in probability and severity, to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, including, among others, as appropriate:  

a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  

b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  

c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data in a timely manner in the event of a physical or technical incident;  

d) a process for regularly testing, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of technical and organizational measures for ensuring the security of processing.  

2. In assessing the appropriate level of security, account shall be taken in particular of the risks presented by processing, which may result from the destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of, or access to personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, whether accidental or unlawful.  

3. The application of an approved code of conduct as referred to in Article 40 or an approved certification mechanism as referred to in Article 42 may be used as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this Article.  

4. The data controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the data controller or of the processor, who has access to personal data, does not process them except on instructions from the data controller, unless required to do so by Union law or the law of a Member State.  

Article 33: Notification to the supervisory authority of a personal data breach  
1. In the event of a personal data breach, the data controller shall notify the breach to the competent supervisory authority in accordance with Article 55, without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after becoming aware of it, unless the personal data breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. When the notification to the supervisory authority is not made within 72 hours, it shall be accompanied by the reasons for the delay.  

2. The processor shall notify the data controller of any personal data breach without undue delay after becoming aware of it.

3. The notification referred to in paragraph 1 must, at a minimum:  

a) describe the nature of the personal data breach including, if possible, the categories and approximate number of individuals concerned by the breach and the categories and approximate number of personal data records affected;  

b) communicate the name and contact details of the data protection officer or another point of contact from whom further information can be obtained;  

c) describe the likely consequences of the personal data breach;  

d) describe the measures taken or that the data controller proposes to take to remedy the personal data breach, including, where appropriate, measures to mitigate any possible negative consequences.  

4. If, and to the extent that, it is not possible to provide all the information at the same time, the information may be communicated in phases without any further undue delay.  

5. The data controller documents any personal data breach, indicating the facts relating to the personal data breach, its effects, and the measures taken to remedy it. The documentation thus created enables the supervisory authority to verify compliance with this article.  

Article 34: Communication to the Data Subject of a Personal Data Breach  
1. When a personal data breach is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of a natural person, the data controller shall communicate the personal data breach to the data subject without undue delay.  

2. The communication to the data subject referred to in paragraph 1 of this article shall describe, in clear and plain language, the nature of the personal data breach and shall at least contain the information and measures referred to in Article 33, paragraph 3, points b), c), and d).  

3. The communication to the data subject referred to in paragraph 1 is not necessary if one of the following conditions is met:  

a) the data controller has implemented appropriate technical and organizational protection measures and those measures have been applied to the personal data affected by the breach, in particular measures that render the personal data incomprehensible to any person not authorized to access it, such as encryption;  

b) the data controller has taken subsequent measures that ensure that the high risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects referred to in paragraph 1 is no longer likely to materialize;  

c) it would involve disproportionate effort. In this case, a public communication or a similar measure is taken to inform the data subjects in an equally effective manner.  

4. If the data controller has not already communicated the personal data breach concerning the data subject, the supervisory authority may, after assessing whether this personal data breach is likely to result in a high risk, require the data controller to make that communication or decide that one of the conditions referred to in paragraph 3 is met.
