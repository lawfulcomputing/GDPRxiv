EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Biometric data are personal data derived from the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of an individual that allow for the identification or authentication of that person. Citizens are increasingly confronted with the processing of biometric data on their smartphones or tablets, as well as by public authorities and private companies. In accordance with Article 9.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (hereinafter the "GDPR"), biometric data constitute a particular category of personal data, in contrast to the situation prior to the entry into force of the GDPR. These are personal data that, by their nature, are particularly sensitive because their processing may pose significant risks to the freedoms and fundamental rights of individuals. Under Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the processing of biometric data is therefore prohibited unless the data controller can legitimately invoke one of the exceptions listed exhaustively in Article 9.2 of the GDPR.

It is this change in the legal qualification of the concept of biometric data and the considerable increase in biometric processing processes in daily life that have prompted the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter "the Authority") to express itself on this subject.

The recommendation primarily aims to assist data controllers and processors in interpreting and correctly applying the GDPR rules regarding the processing of biometric data. It should be noted in this context that the recommendation does not apply to the processing of biometric data carried out by competent authorities within the meaning of Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of preventing and detecting criminal offenses, investigations, and prosecutions in this regard, or the execution of criminal sanctions, and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Framework Decision 2008/977/JHA of the Council (hereinafter the Police-Justice Directive), or to any other processing of data that is not covered by the GDPR. Furthermore, this recommendation seeks to invite the legislator to provide a legal basis for the processing of biometric data.

Initially, the Authority explains in detail in Chapter II the legal framework for the processing in question. Particular attention is paid to the concepts of 'personal data', 'processing of personal data', 'data controller', and 'processor'.

4

The recommendation then provides explanations on the concrete scope of the notion of 'processing of biometric data'. A good understanding of the terminology used is indeed fundamental before being able to apply the principles of data protection to the processing of biometric data.

Chapter III then addresses the relevant principles of data protection in the context of the processing of biometric data. In this context, particular attention is devoted to the choice of a correct legal basis (exceptional ground), the correct definition of the purposes of processing, the requirement of proportionality, the security of processing, the principle of data retention limitation, the obligation of transparency, and the (potential) obligation to conduct a data protection impact assessment.

This analysis, particularly regarding the use of a legal basis or an exceptional ground that justifies the processing of biometric data, reveals that currently, there is a gap in Belgian law such that any processing of biometric data in the context of the authentication of individuals, to the extent that explicit consent cannot be relied upon and the exception for the processing of biometric data in the context of the eID (electronic identity card) and passport is not applicable, occurs without a legal basis. This concretely means that the Belgian legislator will need to define in law the modalities for the processing of biometric data to the extent that it wishes to (continue to) authorize the use of biometric data in a specific context. However, the Authority acknowledges that this requirement deviates significantly from the regime prior to the entry into force of the GDPR. Therefore, considering the principles of good governance, from the publication of this recommendation, a transitional period of one year is provided during which the processing of biometric data will be tolerated in accordance with the old standard, and the Authority will not intervene proactively. This one-year period is intended to allow data controllers and the legislator to take the necessary measures to bring the processing in question into compliance with the provisions of the GDPR, as explained in this recommendation.

Introduction  
1. Foreword  
The GDPR came into effect on May 25, 2018. It repeals the Directive of the European Parliament and of the Council of October 24, 1995, on the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (hereinafter "Directive 95/46/EC") and confirms and consolidates the case law as applied by the Court of Justice of the European Union and by the Article 29 Working Party (hereinafter "Group 29") through official opinions and guidelines. By moving from a directive to a regulation, the European legislator aimed to make the protection of personal data, defined as a fundamental right in Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, directly applicable and uniformly enforceable in the Member States.

One of the main objectives of the GDPR is to strengthen the rights of data subjects. The GDPR indeed grants supervisory authorities significant powers so that they can also impose sanctions in case of non-compliance with the rules defined therein. The Eurobarometer of May 2019 on the GDPR reveals that the awareness of data subjects regarding applicable data protection rules and their rights is significantly increasing. Thus, they are exercising their rights more than before. This is particularly true for the withdrawal of their consent or the objection to the processing of their data for commercial purposes.

In light of these strengthened rights, many consumer and citizen rights advocacy groups agree that the GDPR significantly contributes to a fair digital society, based on mutual trust between data subjects and the actors involved in the processing of their data.

To achieve this objective, the GDPR emphasizes the accountability of various actors who process personal data, whether they are individuals, professionals, legal entities, or public authorities, at every stage of processing, both at the national and European or international level.

2. Context and Scope of the Recommendation
In accordance with Article 9.1 of the GDPR, biometric data constitutes a particular category of personal data, in contrast to the situation prior to the entry into force of the GDPR. These are personal data that, by their nature, are particularly sensitive because their processing may involve significant risks to the freedoms and fundamental rights of individuals. Biometric physical characteristics can be unique or nearly so and can therefore almost always be linked to a single individual. Furthermore, they often contain more information than is strictly necessary for the purposes pursued, may contain sensitive data regarding health status, and are permanent or evolve slowly, which implies that a data breach has serious long-term consequences. Under Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the processing of biometric data is therefore prohibited unless the data controller can legitimately invoke one of the exceptions listed exhaustively in Article 9.2 of the GDPR. However, this change in qualification implies that the guidelines of the Privacy Protection Commission, the predecessor in law of the Authority, regarding the processing of biometric data in the context of the authentication of individuals, in accordance with Opinion No. 17/2008, are no longer relevant.

Moreover, due to the increasingly reliable and cost-effective nature of biometric authentication methods, the Authority has noted that individuals, both in their interactions with public authorities and with private companies, are increasingly confronted with the processing of their biometric data.

It is particularly this change in the legal qualification of the notion of biometric data and the considerable increase in biometric processing processes in daily life that have prompted the Authority to express itself again on this subject.

The recommendation primarily aims to assist data controllers and processors in interpreting and correctly applying the GDPR rules regarding the processing of biometric data. A GDPR-compliant method is indeed an indispensable and useful ally in their relationships with the data subjects. II.  
Processing of Biometric Data: What Is It?  
1. Legal Framework  
1.1  
Personal Data  
Article 4.1) of the GDPR defines ‘personal data’ as follows: "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (hereinafter referred to as 'data subject'); an 'identifiable natural person' is a natural person who can be identified, directly or indirectly, particularly by reference to an identifier, such as a name, identification number, location data, online identifier, or to one or more specific elements characteristic of their physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity."

The terms ‘identified’ and ‘identifiable’ are fundamental to understanding what constitutes personal data. While data that directly identifies a person is often obvious (for example, a combination of surname, first name, and address or date of birth or a unique identification number, such as a customer number), it is sometimes more difficult to determine what is meant by ‘data that can identify a person indirectly’ (for example, pseudonymized data).

In accordance with Article 4.5) of the GDPR, pseudonymized data are personal data processed in such a way that they can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such additional information is kept separately and subject to technical and organizational measures to ensure that the personal data are not attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person. In other words, any piece of information that has been attributed to a person, no matter how insignificant it may seem when considered in isolation (for example, an age, a residence, eye color, or sex, or even a registration number), constitutes personal data as soon as, combined with one or more other pieces of data, it allows for the identification of a natural person. As long as such additional information is available, and regardless of the quality of the technical and organizational measures implemented, pseudonymized data fall within the scope of the GDPR.

‘Anonymized’ data, on the other hand, are not classified as personal data because the identification of a natural person using such data is no longer possible, not even with additional information. Although for this reason, the GDPR excludes such data from its scope, practice shows that the distinction between pseudonymized data and anonymized data is becoming increasingly difficult to establish. In this context, it is worth noting that the EDPB is currently working on drafting guidelines regarding data anonymization.

Finally, keep in mind that - in principle - only data of living natural persons are considered personal data.

1.2  
Processing of Personal Data  

Article 4.2) of the GDPR defines ‘processing’ as follows: "any operation or set of operations performed or not by automated means and applied to personal data, such as collection, recording, organization, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment or combination, as well as restriction, erasure or destruction of personal data."

Just as for the purposes of processing, each data controller must also be transparent regarding the processing they carry out with the data of a data subject. The level of detail depends, in particular, on the type of data subjects (children, professionals, experts, etc.), the manner in which their personal data are processed, and the extent to which such processing involves interference with their right to privacy. Defining the processing activities carried out for each distinct purpose is also a fundamental element when assessing the proportionality (i.e., admissibility) of the processing in question. In this sense, it is unlikely that the installation of a fingerprint sensor to access a cafeteria would pass the proportionality test.

In order to have an overview of processing activities, it is also mandatory, under Article 30 of the GDPR, to maintain a record of processing activities when a company or organization employs more than 250 people or when the processing it carries out is likely to pose a risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects, if it is not occasional or if it relates to particular categories of data. Therefore, regarding the processing of biometric data, it will always be mandatory to maintain such a record.
10

In addition to a list of processing activities carried out, this register also contains other information such as personal data or categories of personal data that are processed. This register must be established in writing or electronically and must be clear and understandable.

The register thus also contributes to the accurate inventory of processed data and therefore constitutes an indispensable tool for understanding the ecosystem of data processing for which a specific data controller is responsible. A properly maintained register saves time in complying with your obligations under the GDPR and also allows all individuals working within your organization and involved in the processing of data relevant to your organization to be consulted and/or informed, as necessary, regarding the processing carried out within the organization, which also contributes, at all levels, to greater awareness of data protection. The register will also assist you in drafting a data protection impact assessment (see below section III.7) and in collaborating with the Authority if it has questions regarding any of your processing activities.

1.3
Data Controller

You are a 'data controller' when you determine, alone or jointly with others, the purposes and means of processing personal data.

It is important to note that an organization is not "by nature" a data controller or a processor. It all depends on how the organization behaves in practice. For each operation you carry out with biometric data, you must ask yourself who determined the purposes of the processing as well as the manner of processing this data.

To clarify your role and that of other parties (such as technical service providers or third parties providing you with data), ask yourself the following questions:
- Who decides in the first instance to proceed with the collection of (biometric) data?
- Who defines the data subjects or categories of data subjects?
- Who determines the categories of data that need to be collected?
- Who determines the purpose(s) for which the data is used?
- Who determines the legal basis for the processing?
- Who determines whether the data should be transmitted and if so, to whom?

- Who determines the content of the information provided to data subjects regarding the processing or processing activities applied to their data?
- Who determines the retention period for the data? and
- Who determines how to respond when data subjects exercise their rights?
All these decisions can only be made by the data controller in the context of their overall control of data processing. If you make any of these decisions, you are most likely a data controller.

Article 26 of the GDPR also provides for the situation where two or more data controllers coexist, known as 'joint controllers'. 1.4
Processor

When a public authority or a private entity or a natural person processes personal data on your behalf, based on your instructions, solely for the purpose of enabling you to achieve your purposes, this constitutes a processing relationship.

The use of a processor implies that the requirements of Article 28 of the GDPR must be respected.

First, keep in mind that regardless of the situation and regardless of the processor, as soon as you act as a data controller, you are bound by the obligations imposed on you by the GDPR and that, where applicable, you must be accountable for violations of these obligations. This is why Article 28.1 of the GDPR states that you may only engage processors who provide sufficient guarantees regarding the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures. This also stems from Article 24 of the GDPR, which requires you to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that your data processing is carried out in accordance with the GDPR. Engaging a certified processor or a processor that has adhered to an approved code of conduct constitutes a factor that can demonstrate the existence of sufficient guarantees as required by Articles 28.1 and 28.4 of the GDPR.

For a complete overview regarding the relationship between data controller and processor, see: EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on the concepts of controller and processor in the GDPR, see footnote 8.
12

While in an ideal situation, the data controller provides complete instructions regarding the processing entrusted to the processor, in reality, this is often less clear, and certain elements may not be determined by the data controller but by the processor based on its expertise regarding the technologies applied during processing and/or the most appropriate data security measures. The fact that a processor has more expertise than you regarding the technical means to be used during data processing does not, in itself, lead to a reclassification of its position from processor to data controller. Some processors offer ready-to-use solutions without this affecting your obligation, as the data controller, to make the required decisions concerning the processed data, the purposes pursued, and/or the means to achieve them.

Furthermore, it should also be noted that the same entity can simultaneously fulfill the role of both processor and data controller, but not for the same processing of personal data. A processor acting in this manner must ensure that its systems and procedures distinguish between the personal data it processes in its capacity as data controller and the personal data it processes in its capacity as processor. If certain data are identical, these systems must be able to establish a distinction between these two situations, so that different processes and different measures can be applied to each situation.

However, if an organization simultaneously acts as a data controller and as a processor for different processing activities based on the same personal data, the data subjects must be properly informed by the organization, both in its capacity as data controller and in its capacity as processor, and this obviously to the extent that the processing activities are lawful. This particularly applies to designers of facial recognition software who, on the one hand, act as processors towards an entity that uses this software for specific purposes (the data controller) but, on the other hand, will also process the collected data for personal purposes. The information to be provided must in this case mention the different processing activities as well as the collected data, the recipients of the data, and their specific purposes.

Example
In a Chinese restaurant of the KFC chain, facial recognition software (designed by Baidu, the Chinese counterpart of Google) is used to predict customer preferences. In this relationship, KFC is the data controller (as this company determines the purposes of the processing) and Baidu is the processor, given that its software ensures the actual processing. However, if Baidu

uses the personal data in question to establish biometric profiles of clients and communicates them to third-party companies, it acts in its capacity as data controller.

Finally, the Authority wishes to emphasize that your employees are not your subcontractors. As long as these employees act under your authority within a subordinate relationship, they are an integral part of your organization.

2. Definition of biometric data
2.1
Context
Article 4.14) of the GDPR defines ‘biometric data’ as "personal data resulting from a specific technical processing, related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person, which allow or confirm their unique identification, such as facial images or fingerprint data." When the data is not processed for the unique identification of individuals but such identification is still possible, given the nature of the data, it will also be considered biometric data processing under the GDPR.

To avoid any confusion in this regard and to promote legal certainty, it is important to note that the concept of biometric data has a different meaning in the scientific context than in the data protection (European) context. The scientific definition is found in the International Standard ISO/IEC 2382-37 and is worded as follows: "biometric sample or aggregation of biometric samples at any stage of processing, e.g., biometric reference, biometric probe, biometric feature or biometric property." The ISO/IEC standard thus considers the following as biometric data: (1) the recording of data (‘biometric sample’), (2) the extraction of data from samples (‘biometric feature’), (3) the assignment of biometric samples to specific individuals (‘biometric reference’), and (4) the comparison between different samples (‘biometric probe’). It is immediately noticeable that the notion in the scientific context does not necessarily concern the link between an individual and their biometric data, whereas the identification or rather the "identifiability" of an individual is fundamental to the notion of ‘biometric data’ in the context of data protection. Therefore, the processing of biometric data in the scientific sense without the possibility of identifying individuals does not fall within the scope of the GDPR. Biometric data can indeed only be classified as personal data when processed in a specific technical manner that allows for the unique identification or authentication of a natural person (even when this is not the primary purpose).

Such a conclusion is consistent with Article 4.1) of the GDPR, from which it follows that the notion of personal data relates only to an identified or identifiable natural person.

Nevertheless, it should be noted that the notion of biometric data has been defined in fairly general terms, which implies that the GDPR recognizes that biometric technology is still under development and will continue to evolve.

Finally, under Article 9.1 of the GDPR, biometric data is a particular category of personal data. The processing of such special categories is generally prohibited unless the conditions of one of the exceptions to the prohibition of processing listed in Article 9.2 of the GDPR are met. The implications of this qualification on processing are explained in detail in Chapter III of this recommendation.

2.2
Concrete interpretation of the notion of biometric data

The GDPR distinguishes two categories of information that may be considered biometric data. The first category concerns physical properties - namely the physical or physiological characteristics of a person. The content of this category is quite simple and corresponds to what most people understand by biometric data, such as information related to the face, fingerprints, and iris scans.

The second category, behavioral information, is considerably broader. Any behavioral characteristic that allows for the unique identification of a person is logically supposed to be biometric data. However, the possibilities for processing behavioral information are evolving rapidly. Therefore, it is not possible to establish an exhaustive list of such processes. It will always be necessary to verify with concrete elements whether or not it is a processing of behavioral data allowing for the unique identification of individuals.

Examples of behavioral biometric data
The use of keyboard, touchscreen, and mouse patterns to authenticate individuals (which is of great interest in the banking sector).

2.3  
Biometric Templates  

The operation of a biometric system is divided into two phases of information collection and two methods of comparing the collected information (the two functions of biometric systems).  

PHASES OF COLLECTION  

The first phase of collection, known as enrollment (or registration), is the moment when a biometric characteristic of the individual concerned is collected and recorded on a medium to store information (either an individual medium such as a badge or a token, or in a database). This reference information will either be the raw biometric data (such as the image of the face, hand, iris, or fingerprint), or a set of coded information, obtained from the individual and unique characteristics of the raw data for the purpose of verifying or establishing an individual's identity (a template). Although these templates are distinct from the raw biometric data, they undoubtedly fall within the scope of the GDPR. Furthermore, the Authority notes that in accordance with Article 5.1.f) in conjunction with Article 32 of the GDPR and considering the principle of ‘privacy by design15’, it will no longer be possible to lawfully use a system that records reference information in its raw form (i.e., the raw biometric data). During the first phase of collection, raw biometric data must always be converted into templates. After that, the raw data must be immediately deleted.  

In the second phase of collection, the individual presents their biometric characteristics to the system that must authenticate them. At this moment, a second biometric sample is taken (for example, a person holds their finger in front of the sensor) and this information (the raw data or the template) is then compared to the reference information to verify if they match. If the information collected during the second collection matches the reference information (positive association), the system considers that the person presenting themselves is the one who was previously registered during the enrollment phase.

Depending on how the template is stored, strict conditions apply. The recommendation distinguishes three possibilities:  
-  
(Type 1) Control of the template by the individual concerned themselves: the only durable storage medium of the template is exclusively kept by the individual concerned or, if applicable, in the device in which the biometric sensor is installed without the possibility of association with other computer systems. One can think of a badge, a token, or a local recording in the sensor at the entrance of the building. This method should be used in principle, and deviations can only occur in exceptional cases;  
-  
(Type 2) Shared control: There is a central database of templates under the control of the data controller, but the latter cannot use it without the consent of the individual concerned. For example, access to a specific template is granted only upon entering a password chosen by the individual concerned.  
-  
(Type 3) Exclusive control by the data controller: the template is recorded in a usable form in a database of templates under the exclusive control of the data controller. In this case, the strictest conditions must be met, and a prior data protection impact assessment will always be necessary.
17

The Authority emphasizes that the registration of templates according to types 2 and 3 will only be possible under exceptional circumstances. One can think, for example, of authentication in critical environments where the loss of a token or badge (exclusively controlled by the individual concerned) would have particularly serious consequences (for example: access to an operational center of a nuclear power plant).

Finally, the Authority wishes to emphasize in this context that this recommendation does not primarily concern the purely local use by the user of authentication using biometric systems. Such processing of biometric data indeed falls under the domestic exception in accordance with Article 2.2.c) of the GDPR. One can think in this context of personal authentication using smartphones or other electronic devices in which facial recognition software and fingerprint sensors are increasingly offered as an alternative to the traditional PIN code. The conditions and modalities of the domestic exception are explained in detail in section III.1.3.3 below.

COMPARATIVE PHASE

There are two ways to compare the information obtained during the collection phases, and they constitute the two main functions of biometrics: the identification function and the verification function. Although these two functions can be used in the context of authentication, the verification function is undeniably preferable (given that biometric reference information does not necessarily need to be stored in a central database), and the identification function can only be used in exceptional and justified cases.

The identification function consists of comparing the information from the second phase with all the biometric information available in the biometric system and recorded by definition in a database (‘one-to-many comparison’). This function will first allow for the identification of the user among all registered individuals and can serve to authenticate them in a subsequent phase.

The verification function consists of comparing the information from the second phase with the previously recorded information belonging to a single individual (‘one-to-one comparison’). This function is particularly suited to situations where the individual wishes to authenticate themselves and is therefore willing to voluntarily disclose an element that allows for their identification based on the comparison between the previously defined reference information and the sample from the new collection.

III. Application of data protection principles to the processing of biometric data

1. Legal basis

1.1 Why a legal basis?
The processing of personal data is generally only permitted if it is based on one of the six legal bases provided for in Article 6 of the GDPR. When it comes to the processing of special categories of personal data, it will also be necessary to rely on one of the exception grounds provided for in Article 9.2 of the GDPR. The presence of an exception ground serves as the legal basis for processing in this case. You cannot process data without a legal basis; therefore, you must ensure that you have such a basis before commencing your processing.

It is also essential to ask yourself what your legal basis is, given that the conditions for each of the legal bases differ. The choice of your legal basis has implications for the rights of the individuals concerned, whose specificities you must be aware of, not only to inform them correctly but also to ensure the effective exercise of their rights.

The qualification of the notion of ‘biometric data’ as a special category of personal data within the meaning of Article 9.1 of the GDPR has significant consequences for determining the legal basis and implies that the guidelines regarding the processing of biometric data in the context of the authentication of individuals from the Privacy Protection Commission are no longer applicable. This aspect is explained in detail in section III.1.3. below.

1.2 Can the legal basis be changed?

The legal basis cannot be changed during processing. This means that if the data controller invokes an inappropriate legal basis or if it "is no longer valid" because the conditions are not or are no longer met, the processing cannot continue.
19

If a processing is based, for example, on consent (explicit), as soon as the person withdraws their consent, you must cease all data processing based on this legal basis, unless you continue to process the same data for another purpose for which you have another valid legal basis.

1.3 What legal basis to use for the processing of biometric data?

There is no hierarchy among the legal bases provided by the GDPR. It is your responsibility to demonstrate that your processing is validly based on one of the exceptions in accordance with Article 9.2 of the GDPR, given that the processing referred to in this recommendation always concerns specific categories of personal data, namely biometric data. In this regard, it should be noted that the guidelines from Article 29 Working Party and the EDPB indicate that a dual legal basis is only required if the exception mentioned in Article 9.2 of the GDPR offers less protection than the legal bases mentioned in Article 6 of the GDPR. This will be the case, for example, when the data controller invokes Article 9.2.e) of the GDPR: for sensitive data made public by the data subject themselves, a legal basis from Article 6 of the GDPR must be used. However, when the processing is based on Article 9.2.a) of the GDPR (explicit consent) or Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR (a significant public interest), this is not necessary.

Furthermore, as mentioned in Recital 132 of the GDPR, certain specific laws specify the legal basis on which data controllers must rely to process data. It is therefore your responsibility to verify whether, based on a specific law, you are required to use a specific legal basis.

Do not forget, in accordance with the requirements of Article 13 or Article 14 of the GDPR, to inform the data subjects of the legal basis for your processing in any case. Regardless of the legal basis on which you rely, you must communicate it to the data subjects.

Finally, despite the absence of any hierarchy, some legal bases are more suitable than others for the reality of biometric data processing under the GDPR. In other words, given the conditions associated with it, it will be difficult to rely on certain legal bases in this context. Regarding the processing referred to in this recommendation, two legal bases deserve further clarification due to their practical relevance. These are particularly explicit consent (Art. 9.2.a) of the GDPR) and significant public interest (Art. 9.2.g) of the GDPR).

Article 9.4 of the GDPR allows Member States to maintain or introduce additional conditions, including limitations, regarding the processing of genetic data,

biometric data or health-related data. Such provisions may then serve as a legal basis for processing. This will be particularly necessary when the data controller wants to rely on a significant public interest. This is explained in detail in section III.1.3.2. below.

1.3.1. Explicit Consent

Explicit consent must be divided into two elements. First, there must be valid consent for the processing, and then this consent must be explicit.

VALID CONSENT

Article 4.11) of the GDPR specifies that there can only be talk of consent from the data subject if it is a manifestation of will that is (1) free, (2) specific, (3) informed, and (4) unambiguous, by which the data subject accepts, through a statement or a clear affirmative action, that personal data concerning them be subject to processing.

The following sections will briefly clarify how these elements should be assessed.

First, the element of 'free' implies that there must be a genuine choice for the data subject. Generally, if the data subject does not have a genuine choice, feels compelled to give consent, or risks suffering harm in the absence of consent, it cannot be considered valid consent under the GDPR. Such consent is not regarded as having been given freely if the data subject is unable to refuse or withdraw their consent without suffering harm. The GDPR also pays attention to the concept—important in this context—of an imbalance between the data controller and the data subject.

An imbalance occurs particularly within the framework of the employment relationship. Given the subordination resulting from the relationship between employer and employee, it is unlikely that the data subject could refuse their consent to data processing without fear or real threat of negative consequences arising from that refusal. It is improbable that the employee could respond freely to a request for consent from their employer, for example, for the activation of authentication systems with

A fourth and final condition is that consent must be unambiguous. The GDPR explicitly states that for consent, a clear statement or positive action from the data subject is required. In other words, there can be no reasonable doubt about the intention of the data subject to give consent for the intended processing of their personal data. Silence, the use of pre-checked boxes, or inactivity cannot therefore be considered as consent. The consent given must apply to all processing activities having the same purpose or purposes. When processing has multiple purposes, consent must be given for each of them.

The unambiguity of consent also implies that data subjects must be able to clearly understand the options available to them and, if multiple options are available, the choice to consent to the processing of their biometric data must be clearly distinct from any other choice.

EXPLICIT CONSENT

In accordance with Article 9.2.a) of the GDPR, explicit consent is required in certain situations where there is a serious risk to data protection and therefore where a high level of individual control over personal data is deemed appropriate.

The term ‘explicit’ refers to the manner in which consent is expressed by the data subject. This means that the data subject must provide an explicit statement of consent. An obvious way to ensure that consent is explicit is to confirm it in a written statement. If applicable, the data controller could ensure that the written statement is signed by the data subject to dispel any potential doubt and to exclude any future lack of evidence.

However, such a signed statement is not the only way to obtain explicit consent, and the GDPR does not specify that in all circumstances requiring valid explicit consent, a written and signed statement is required. In the digital or online context, for example, a data subject may provide the required statement by completing an electronic form, sending an email, uploading a scanned document bearing their signature, or through an electronic signature. In theory, the use of verbal statements may also suffice to obtain valid explicit consent, but it may be difficult for a data controller to prove that when recording the statement, all conditions for valid explicit consent were met.

VALID CONSENT UNDER ARTICLE 7 OF THE GDPR

Even when it comes to free, specific, informed, unambiguous, and explicit consent, the data controller must also comply with the conditions of Article 7.1 and Article 7.3 of the GDPR.

Article 7.1 of the GDPR states that the data controller must be able to demonstrate that the data subject has given consent to the processing of personal data concerning them. To meet this requirement, the data controller is free to choose the most appropriate method. Thus, in the event of a complaint from the data subject or a check by the supervisory authority, the data controller can simply demonstrate that they have acted in accordance with the provisions of the GDPR.

The obligation to be able to demonstrate consent applies as long as the processing takes place. At the end of this processing, proof of consent cannot be retained beyond the time necessary to comply with this legal obligation or to establish, exercise, or defend legal rights, as provided for in Article 17.3.b) and e) of the GDPR.

Furthermore, in accordance with Article 7.3 of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to withdraw their consent at any time. Before the data subject gives their consent, they must be informed that they can withdraw their consent freely and without detriment (such as a reduction in the level of service previously provided or even the refusal of that service).

Although the GDPR places significant emphasis on the withdrawal of consent, it does not prescribe the form in which this withdrawal must or can be made. The EDPB states in this regard: "However, when consent is obtained electronically solely by a click, a tap, or by swiping the screen, data subjects must, in practice, be able to withdraw that consent in the same manner."26. Requiring data subjects to follow a complex process via links to electronic documents or forcing them to enter a password does not comply with the requirement to withdraw consent as easily as it was given. Furthermore, the data controller must ensure that the consent of another user cannot be withdrawn without their knowledge or consent.

When consent is withdrawn, all processing activities concerning that person must cease. However, this does not affect the lawfulness of the processing (based on that consent) prior to the withdrawal of consent. The data controller must also verify whether the retention of the data used for the processing in question is justified or not, even if the data subject has not made a deletion request. Indeed, in accordance with Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR, the retention of personal data must be limited to the purpose pursued (see below Section III.5).

It is only when the data of the data subject is necessary for the execution of processing for other purposes for which there is also a valid legal basis that the data may potentially be retained. If this is not the case, it must be deleted.

1.3.2. Significant Public Interest

In accordance with Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, biometric data may only be processed when "such processing is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest, based on Union law or the law of a Member State, which must be proportionate to the aim pursued, respect the essence of the right to data protection, and provide for appropriate and specific measures to safeguard the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject." This exception plays an important role, particularly when— for example, due to the existence of a power imbalance between the data controller and the data subject—explicit consent cannot be invoked, in accordance with Article 9.2.a) of the GDPR. This indeed implies that explicit consent should always be preferred and that a data controller may only invoke significant public interest as a legal basis for the processing of biometric data in specific cases defined by law.

Example
The only law that currently explicitly provides for the processing of biometric data is the law of July 19, 1991, concerning population registers, identity cards, foreigner cards, and residence documents, executed by the royal decree of March 25, 2003, concerning identity cards.

Article 29 of the UAVG, the Dutch law implementing the GDPR, states that due to Article 9.2(g) of the GDPR, the prohibition on processing biometric data for the unique identification of a person does not apply if the processing is necessary for authentication or security purposes.

However, this implies that to the extent that explicit consent cannot be invoked and except for the processing of biometric data in the context of the eID (electronic identity card) and the passport, any processing of biometric data for the authentication of individuals currently takes place without a legal basis. Relying on public interest grounds, without any provision of Union law or that of a Member State providing for it, indeed seems incompatible with the spirit of Article 9.2(g) of the GDPR.

Therefore, the Authority believes that a generally formulated obligation on the part of the data controller to 'provide adequate security measures' cannot be considered sufficient to justify the use of biometric data. Although the Authority acknowledges that the processing of biometric data for the identification or authentication of individuals may be justified in certain cases, there must always be a legal provision (general or sectoral) that allows for the processing of biometric data in specific cases, given Article 9.2(g) of the GDPR. In this sense, the Authority wishes to emphasize that the mere existence of a legal provision is not a blanket permission for the processing of biometric data and does not exempt the data controller from the obligation to substantiate the necessity and proportionality of the data processing. In other words, the data controller must verify whether the purposes they pursue are such that the use of biometrics is unavoidable.

Example  
On August 12, 2019, the shoe store Manfield was convicted by the Amsterdam court for using a cash register system based on fingerprint scanning. Manfield argued that this was permitted under Article 24 of the UAVG in conjunction with Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, given that the use of a fingerprint scanning authorization system was necessary to secure sensitive information, namely financial information and personal data of both employees and customers. Furthermore, such a system was supposed to prevent fraud at the cash registers. The judge rejected these arguments and stated that the use of biometrics for authentication or security purposes only passes the proportionality test in exceptional cases. In this instance, Manfield did not sufficiently demonstrate that there were no less radical alternatives to achieve the same purposes. 

It will always be necessary to weigh the (important) interests pursued against the risks to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned. For this purpose, one can, for example, check how the intended processing affects society, both 'in depth' (the extent of the benefit or harm experienced due to the processing) and 'in breadth' (the number of people who perceive a benefit or harm). Thus, in the aforementioned example, there is a relatively large harm (the mandatory use of fingerprints) for a relatively large group (proportionally) of individuals concerned (all employees of the shoe store) that is not proportional to the benefit perceived by a single person (the store owner). Let us compare this to the use of biometric authentication to grant access to the premises of a nuclear power plant. The harm perceived by the employees (proportionally a relatively small group of individuals concerned) does not counterbalance the benefit enjoyed by the entire population (the security of a critical infrastructure). 

All this concretely means that given Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, the Belgian legislator must explicitly regulate the modalities of processing biometric data by law to the extent that it wants to (continue to) allow such use of biometric data. For this purpose, the relevant sectors, organizations, or professional bodies may submit a motivated request demonstrating that the processing is proportionate and necessary for the intended purposes, that the intrinsic content of the right to the protection of personal data is respected, and that appropriate and specific measures are taken to protect the fundamental rights and interests of the individual concerned. The Authority emphasizes that under Article 23 of the law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority, legislative initiatives in this framework must always be submitted for opinion to the Authority's Knowledge Center. In this context, it will be verified whether the legal provision complies with the GDPR, and more specifically whether the intended processing is indeed necessary for reasons of significant public interest. 

These new requirements create a break with the regime prior to the entry into force of the GDPR. Therefore, considering the principles of good governance, from the publication of this recommendation, a transitional period of one year is provided during which the processing of biometric data will be tolerated in accordance with the old standard, and the Authority will not intervene proactively. This one-year period should allow data controllers and the legislator to take the necessary measures to bring the intended processing into compliance with the provisions of the GDPR, as explained in this recommendation.

1.3.3. The Domestic Exception

There exists a very specific, albeit omnipresent, application of the use of biometric data in the context of personal authentication via smartphones and other electronic devices, where facial recognition software and fingerprint sensors are increasingly offered as an alternative to the traditional PIN code. Given the importance of such applications and the ambiguity that often surrounds them, this subject deserves some explanations.

The Authority points out that a distinction must be made between, on the one hand, biometric data stored on the device itself and, on the other hand, biometric data stored elsewhere. The impact of this distinction is considerable, as for the first category, the domestic exception of Article 2.2.c) of the GDPR may apply. However, when biometric data is not (only) stored on the device or when it is also communicated to third parties, this exception no longer applies, and the data controller must demonstrate that one of the exceptions listed in Article 9.2 of the GDPR exists.

Article 2.2.c) of the GDPR states that the regulation does not apply "to the processing of personal data carried out by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or household activity." 2. Limitation of Purposes31  
The principle of purpose limitation is defined in Article 5.1(b) of the GDPR and establishes that personal data must be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and cannot be processed further in a manner incompatible with those purposes.

Two elements must be distinguished: (1) specifying a determined, explicit, and legitimate purpose(s) for the intended processing and (2) an element of compatibility which implies that further processing is only permitted to the extent that it is not incompatible with the purpose(s) for which the data was initially collected. Given that the chosen purpose(s) will largely determine the legal basis on which the processing must be founded, it is clear that the purpose(s) must be defined before processing can begin32.

Personal data may only be processed for actual or existing purposes or for purposes that, in light of the actual activities of the data controller, are achievable in the near future.

2.1 Initial Purpose(s)

For each data processing that it contemplates, the data controller must define the purpose(s). This means that it must specify what it concretely aims to achieve by using certain personal data.

This definition of the purposes of processing is essential for the mandatory proportionality test concerning the processing (to ensure that the data processed and the specific processing of that data are proportionate to the purposes pursued). These purposes of processing must be clearly delineated and allow the data controller to choose the most appropriate processing activities. In other words, the mere definition of a purpose, just as identifying a significant interest, does not automatically imply that the intended processing of biometric data is legitimate.

Below are some examples of purposes for the processing of biometric data:
- Authentication of individuals for security purposes;
- Authentication of individuals in the context of payments or for access to private devices or applications;
- Time recording in a professional context;
- Direct marketing33;
- Screening (using facial recognition software or individualization) of public places in the context of crime prevention34;
- DNA analysis in the medical sector;
- Commercial DNA analysis for the purpose of establishing a person's ethnic background and/or genetic specificity35.

Once defined, the purposes must be accurately recorded in the register of processing activities (see above section II.1.2) as well as in the document used to provide the required information to the concerned individuals (the precise communication of the purposes of processing is indeed essential to meet the transparency obligation in accordance with Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR, which state that the concerned individuals must be informed of the purposes of processing).

2.2 Subsequent Purpose(s)

The second obligation arising from Article 5.1(b) of the GDPR implies that personal data that has been collected and processed for a specified and explicit purpose cannot be further processed in a manner incompatible with that purpose. This means that for any

Proportionality indirectly arises from Article 5.1.a) (lawfulness) and c) (data minimization) of the GDPR and involves proportionality when balancing the respective interests of the data controller on one hand and the data subjects on the other. To this end, the data controller must always ask whether the processing activities they are considering are (1) appropriate (is the measure relevant for achieving the purposes?), (2) necessary (is the measure necessary for achieving the purposes?), and (3) not excessive (does the measure go beyond what is necessary to achieve the purposes?).

More concretely, the mandatory proportionality test is part of complying with the obligations imposed by the GDPR. It is only when the data controller can effectively demonstrate that all principles of data protection have been respected that one can speak of lawful and thus proportional data processing. For example, consider the identification of an appropriate legal basis, the clear definition of purposes, ensuring that only data necessary for those purposes is processed, the selection of specific processing activities, compliance with the obligation of transparency, ensuring that data is not processed and retained longer than necessary, guaranteeing the integrity of data processing, and adherence to the principles of 'privacy by design' and 'privacy by default', etc.

Regarding the processing of biometric data in particular, the notion of proportionality plays an important role. Indeed, according to Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, personal data must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed. Therefore, the processing of personal data can only take place to the extent that the purposes of the processing cannot reasonably be achieved by other means. In the context of biometric data processing, this means that even if a legal basis has been identified and the purposes have been clearly defined, processing cannot take place unconditionally. It must indeed be verified whether there are less radical solutions (the processing of biometric data will always be the last resort).

**Example**  
In August 2019, the Ghent school Saint Bavo announced that students and staff would soon be able to make various payments (cafeteria, photocopiers, etc.) with a scan of their palm. To do this, the school relied on the explicit consent of parents and students and aimed to eliminate paper meal vouchers and cash (to avoid children having to carry too much money). Those who refused could use alternative payment methods. However, following a negative opinion, the idea was abandoned given that there was a clear imbalance between the risky processing of biometric data and the payment of small amounts in a school context. In this case, it can be estimated that although the measure is appropriate for achieving the intended purposes, it has not...

If the data controller can demonstrate that the processing of biometric data is the most appropriate means to ensure security, they must also document and justify the use of a specific biometric characteristic. In particular, regarding the biometric authentication of individuals, the Authority emphasizes that the data controller must limit authentication to characteristics based on the morphological traits of the individuals concerned (for example, facial recognition, palm or fingerprint scanning, iris recognition), taking into account, however, that the use of morphological characteristics that do not leave a trace (for example, facial or iris recognition) carries less risk than the use of, for example, fingerprint or palm scans. As for the processing of biological samples (for example, saliva, urine, or blood), the strictest conditions will always apply.37

The method of recording the biometric template in the context of the authentication of individuals (see above section II.2.3) also plays an important role in the proportionality test. Indeed, as explained above, the recording of templates under shared control or under the exclusive control of the data controller is only possible in exceptional cases. The recording of the template under the exclusive control of the individual concerned (for example: in a token or badge) remains applicable as a general rule.

Finally, if authentication must absolutely be carried out using a biometric system, its use must always be limited to the spaces/services that justify such specific measures. For example, regarding access control, a site may include several areas that are freely accessible and other areas that justify the use of biometrics. Access using biometric systems must, as such, be limited to these areas, and the biometric data processed must remain limited to individuals authorized to enter these spaces. Furthermore, in order to restrict access to a space to a certain group of individuals, it is not always necessary to process data that allows for direct identification (such as names) of the individuals who have access rights. Therefore, as long as a person has access rights and biometrics can control this aspect, it is unnecessary to associate biometric information with additional identification means.

4. Processing Security  
In accordance with Article 5.1(f) in conjunction with Article 32 of the GDPR, taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks, the likelihood and severity of which vary, to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller implements appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk. Such measures may include:  
a. Measures related to biometric data:  
- encrypting biometric data, including templates, using a cryptographic algorithm in accordance with the state of the art;  
- associating an integrity code with biometric data (for example, with an electronic signature);  
- integrating fraud detection measures;  
- prohibiting external access to biometric data;  
- ensuring that copies of data collected during the collection phase are not retained longer than necessary for comparing the collected data with reference information;

- clearly delineating and training individuals within a company who have access to biometric systems/data;

5. Limitation of Retention  
In accordance with Article 5.1(e) of the GDPR, "personal data shall be kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed." In practical terms, this means that once the purpose of processing has been fulfilled, or when the legal basis is no longer valid (for example, due to the withdrawal of consent by the data subjects or the disappearance of a significant public interest), the biometric data in question must be deleted. However, this does not preclude the data from being retained for a longer period under a legal obligation or when such data are necessary in the context of a judicial procedure. 

When the processing of biometric data occurs under a legal obligation on the part of the data controller, the retention period specified in the law must be respected. 

Regarding biometric authentication, as already explained above in section II.2.3, the Authority wishes to clarify that the raw biometric data collected during the first phase of collection of a biometric system (registration phase) must be immediately deleted as soon as the biometric template has been created. Furthermore, the data collected during the second phase of collection may not be retained longer than necessary to compare the collected data with reference information.

7. Data Protection Impact Assessment
In accordance with Article 35.1 of the GDPR, the data controller must carry out, prior to processing, an assessment of the impact of the proposed processing operations on the protection of personal data when the processing, particularly by means of new technologies, and taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals.

Article 35.4 of the GDPR further provides that each supervisory authority is required to establish and publish a list of processing activities for which a data protection impact assessment is required. Pending the establishment of the Authority, the Privacy Protection Commission adopted Recommendation No. 01/2018 on the modalities for conducting a data protection impact assessment, taking into account the provisions of Article 35 of the GDPR and the guidelines of Article 29 Working Party. This recommendation was subsequently supplemented by Decision No. 01/2019 of the General Secretariat of the Authority, which includes a list of processing activities for which a data protection impact assessment is required.

As indicated in point 6 of Decision No. 01/2019, a data protection impact assessment must always be conducted when the processing uses biometric data for the unique identification of individuals located in a public place or in a private place accessible to the public. However, the Authority wishes to emphasize that the processing of biometric data for purposes other than those explicitly stated in the decision is also subject to the obligation to conduct a data protection impact assessment. Moreover, given the high inherent risk to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned that the processing of biometric data entails, not conducting a data protection impact assessment will only be justified in exceptional cases.

In this regard, concerning the modalities for conducting a data protection impact assessment, the Authority refers to Recommendation No. 01/2018, Decision No. 01/2019, and the DPIA Guide.
