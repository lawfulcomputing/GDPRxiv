1. Introduction  

1. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) establishes several new obligations for data controllers. One of the new obligations included in the GDPR concerns the requirement to conduct a "data protection impact assessment," abbreviated as "DPIA," under certain circumstances.  

2. A DPIA is a process aimed at describing the processing of personal data, assessing its necessity and proportionality, and helping to manage the risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals associated with it by evaluating and determining the necessary measures to address those risks.  

3. The Police and Justice Directive (Directive 2016/680) also provides for an obligation to conduct a DPIA under certain circumstances. The guidelines set forth in this recommendation, which are inspired by the provisions of the GDPR, apply mutatis mutandis to the interpretation of the relevant provisions of Directive 2016/680.  

4. The purpose of this recommendation is to provide more detailed explanations regarding:  

(1) the circumstances under which a DPIA is mandatory (section 3);  

(2) the essential elements of a DPIA (section 5);  
(3) the circumstances under which prior consultation is mandatory (section 6);  
(4) the actors that must be involved in a DPIA (section 7); and  
(5) several specific provisions (section 8).  

5. A previous draft recommendation was submitted for public consultation from December 20, 2016, to February 28, 2017. This recommendation takes into account the comments and suggestions made by businesses, sectoral federations, and academic institutions, as well as the final version of the guidelines from the Article 29 Data Protection Working Party issued in October 2017.  

6. This recommendation does not contain any established template or manual for conducting a DPIA. Although there are several templates and manuals, the Commission encourages sectoral federations to develop codes of conduct tailored to data processing within their sector, particularly suited to the needs of small, medium, and micro-enterprises. However, it is not excluded that in the future, the Commission may provide a form and/or additional manual that could serve as a starting point for conducting a DPIA or in the context of prior consultation.

2. Why a DPIA?  

8. The obligation to carry out a DPIA - in certain circumstances - must be examined in light of two central principles of the GDPR, namely the principle of accountability and the principle of a risk-based approach.  

9. The principle of accountability implies that the data controller is not only required to comply with the principles and obligations of the GDPR but must also be able to demonstrate that they are in compliance. The DPIA serves as an important tool in this regard, as it can contribute to both compliance with the principles and obligations of the GDPR and the demonstration of such compliance.  

10. The principle of accountability goes hand in hand with a risk-based approach. The GDPR requires that data controllers take appropriate measures to ensure compliance with the GDPR, taking into account "the risks, the likelihood and severity of which vary, to the rights and freedoms of natural persons." The obligation for data controllers to carry out a DPIA in certain situations must be understood in the context of their general obligation to appropriately manage the risks presented by the processing of personal data. However, the mere fact that the conditions triggering the obligation to carry out a DPIA are not met does not limit the general requirement for data controllers to implement measures to appropriately manage the risks to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  

11. The risk-based approach of the GDPR aims to promote a "scalable and proportionate approach" without undermining the principles of data protection or the rights of data subjects. This means that more protective measures will need to be taken for processing activities that present a high risk than for those that present a low risk.

12. The obligation to conduct a DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment) was developed in the context of Directive 95/46/EC, which provided a general obligation to notify each processing of personal data to the supervisory authorities. This obligation generated an administrative and financial burden, without necessarily improving the level of protection for personal data. The new system therefore emphasizes the obligation of the data controller to carry out a prior DPIA for processing "likely to result in a high risk" and on the measures that can be taken to mitigate these risks.

13. Finally, conducting a DPIA can help the data controller comply with the obligation of data protection by design. Article 25(1) of the GDPR obliges the data controller to take appropriate technical and organizational measures, both at the time of determining the means of processing and at the time of the processing itself. Since a DPIA is specifically intended, prior to processing, to identify measures to address risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the DPIA can play an important supporting and/or guiding role in this regard.

3. When is a DPIA required?

14. The GDPR does not require the data controller to conduct a DPIA for every processing of personal data. Generally, a DPIA is only required when the processing of data, considering its nature, scope, context, and purposes, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Furthermore, Article 35(3) of the GDPR lists a number of cases where a DPIA is always required (for which the European legislator has therefore established that these are processing operations that are by nature "likely to result in a high risk"). Finally, Article 35(4) and Article 35(5) of the GDPR provide that each national supervisory authority establishes lists of the types of processing operations for which a DPIA is required or not.

A) When the processing is "likely to result in a high risk" to the rights and freedoms of natural persons  
15. Article 35(1) of the GDPR states that:  
"When a type of processing, in particular by using new technologies, and taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall carry out, prior to the processing, an assessment of the impact of the envisaged processing operations on the protection of personal data."

-  
What is a "risk"?

16. A "risk" is a scenario that describes an event and its effects, estimated in terms of severity and probability. In other words, a risk is the possibility ("probability") that a specific event or threat occurs, resulting in a determined impact ("severity"). Article 35(1) of the GDPR refers to a particular category of risks, namely risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. In the context of a DPIA, a "risk" is therefore a possibility that a negative consequence for the rights and freedoms of natural persons may occur, resulting from the processing of personal data. The way to interpret this notion in practice is explained below in section 520.

-  
What is a "high risk"?

17. The notion of "high risk" is not defined in more detail in the GDPR. The Commission is aware that different organizations use different scales and methods when conducting risk assessments. It is therefore possible that the interpretation of these values may differ, depending on the risk scale and the method used. However, the notion of "high risk" in the sense of the GDPR does not necessarily correspond to the notion of "high risk" as found in other risk management models.

18. The Commission believes that the notion of "high risk" refers to data processing operations that are or may be likely to have significant negative impacts on the fundamental freedoms and rights of natural persons. The expression "likely to" does not mean that there is a remote possibility of significant impact. The significant impact must be more probable than improbable. However, this also means that it is not necessary for individuals to be actually affected: the probability that they may be significantly affected is sufficient to meet this criterion. 

19. Given that a DPIA is a risk management tool for the rights of data subjects, the perspective of the data subject is central. This situation is different from risk management in other areas (such as information security), which is generally focused on the interests and purposes of the organization itself. (See also Group 29, DPIA Guidelines, p. 21.)

When is there a question of a probable high risk?  

19. To determine whether or not it is probable that a proposed processing operation could give rise to a high risk, the guidelines developed by Group 29 are particularly important. Group 29 has identified nine criteria that data controllers must take into account in their analysis to determine whether a proposed processing operation is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. These criteria are as follows:  
1. Assessment or scoring, including profiling and prediction activities, particularly concerning "aspects related to the performance at work of the individual concerned, their economic situation, health, personal preferences or interests, reliability or behavior, or their location and movements."  
2. Automated decision-making with legal effect or similarly significant effect: this criterion includes processing focused on making decisions concerning the individuals that produce legal effects regarding a natural person or significantly affect them in a similar manner.  
3. Systematic monitoring: this criterion involves processing used to observe, monitor, or control individuals, including the collection of data via networks and through the systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area. This is a criterion given that the collection of personal data is likely to occur in circumstances where the individuals concerned do not know who is collecting their data and how.

22. Article 35(3) of the GDPR lists three situations in which a DPIA is always required:  
a) in the case of systematic and comprehensive evaluation of personal aspects concerning natural persons which is based on automated processing, including profiling, and on the basis of which decisions are made that produce legal effects concerning a natural person or similarly significantly affect them;

4. When should a DPIA be conducted?  

32. When conducting a DPIA is mandatory, it must be carried out before the processing53. This approach is in line with the principle of data protection by design, which requires the data controller, both when determining the means of processing and during the processing itself, to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures, taking into account the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons54. The DPIA should be regarded as an aid to decision-making regarding the processing.  

33. A DPIA should be initiated as early as possible during the design of the processing (preferably when the "idea of creating" a new processing arises), even if certain processing activities are not yet known. Updating the DPIA throughout the project lifecycle allows for consideration of data protection and privacy and encourages the creation of solutions that generally promote compliance with the GDPR55, and in particular the obligation of data protection by design.

5. What are the essential elements of a DPIA?  
A) Overview  
35. Article 35(7) of the GDPR states that a DPIA must at least contain the following elements:  
"a) a systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes of the processing, including, where applicable, the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller;  
b) an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations in relation to the purposes;  
c) an assessment of the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects in accordance with paragraph 1; and  
d) the measures envisaged to address the risks, including safeguards, security measures, and mechanisms to ensure the protection of personal data and to demonstrate compliance with this regulation, taking into account the rights and legitimate interests of the data subjects and other affected persons."  
B) Description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes of the processing  
36. Article 35(7) of the GDPR requires that the DPIA first contain a systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes of the processing. It is important to take into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing and the sources of the risks. The description must include at least the following elements:  
- a clear description of the processing, including any business processes and system requirements;

D) Risk Assessment  
-  
What is a "risk assessment"?  
 
42. The notion of "risk assessment" refers to the entire process of (1) identifying risks, (2) analyzing risks, and (3) evaluating risks71. Risk identification refers to the process of examining, recognizing, and describing risks72. Risk analysis refers to the process implemented to understand the nature of a risk and to determine the level of risk73. Risk evaluation is the process of comparing the results of the risk analysis with pre-established risk criteria to determine whether the risk (and/or its significance) is (are) acceptable or tolerable74.  
 
43. In risk management, a general distinction can be made between "inherent" risk and "residual" risk. "Inherent" risk refers to the probability that a negative impact will occur when no protective measures are taken75. "Residual" risk, on the other hand, refers to the probability that a negative impact will occur despite the measures taken to influence (limit) the inherent risk76.  
 
44. At the time of executing the DPIA, the data controller conducting it will have already taken several measures to comply with the obligations of the GDPR. These existing measures may influence the risk assessment for the rights and freedoms of natural persons. It is therefore important that these measures are documented so that they can also be taken into account during the evaluation and determination of the final residual risks77.

Need for Contextual Analysis  

47. Not all processing of personal data presents the same risks. Furthermore, the severity and/or likelihood of a risk can vary significantly depending on each processing operation. Risk analysis must always be conducted based on the specific circumstances of each processing operation (or group of similar processing operations). Thus, Recital (76) of the GDPR states the following:  
"It is necessary to determine the likelihood and severity of the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subject based on the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing."  
Therefore, it is based on the specific circumstances of each processing operation that the data controller must assess the risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals and must take appropriate measures to ensure compliance with the provisions of the GDPR.

48. Risk assessment involves identifying the likelihood and severity of the risk. When assessing the risk, the data controller must ask the following questions: what is the extent of the potential impact on the individuals concerned, and what is the likelihood that this impact will occur? It is not always possible to provide clear-cut answers to these questions; in practice, it will often involve a weighting that will determine the level of risk.

49. When assessing risks, it is important to consider the origin, nature, specificity, and severity of the risks in question. This particularly involves establishing, for each risk, at least the following elements:
- the sources of the risks;
- the potential impacts on the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned, especially in the event of occurrences such as unauthorized access to data, unwanted modification, or their disappearance;
- the threats that could lead to illegitimate access to the data, unwanted modification of it, or its disappearance; and
- the likelihood and severity of the risk.

50. Recitals (84) and (90) of the GDPR specify that the DPIA primarily targets "high" risks. For example, if during a specific processing operation there is a high risk of reputational harm but only a very low risk of discrimination, the latter risk does not necessarily need to be included as such in the risk assessment of a DPIA. Nevertheless, the Commission recommends that, as part of a DPIA, all non-negligible risks be expressly mapped and effective protective measures identified, given that even medium risks can be an important factor when assessing the proportionality of the intended data processing. Furthermore, conducting a DPIA does not exempt the data controller from its general obligation to take measures to appropriately manage all risks to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned. Finally, a DPIA must, in any case, include a record of all measures taken to provide evidence of compliance with the GDPR, taking into account the rights and legitimate interests of the individuals concerned and other affected persons. In this regard, it is also important that all relevant risks are taken into account.

- What method should be applied when assessing and managing risks?

51. The data controller is free to choose the method it wishes to apply, provided that it leads to an objective risk assessment and takes into account the minimum elements prescribed by the GDPR. It is the responsibility of the data controller to use a method that allows it to comply with the requirements of the GDPR. This also means that the choice of one method or another must be justifiable, considering the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing.

52. Nevertheless, the Commission believes that good risk management has several minimum characteristics that are listed in Annex 1 of this recommendation.

53. The Commission considers it important that each data controller undertaking a DPIA uses a method that is suitable for the needs and context of its own business. The necessity of a contextual analysis does not prevent a data controller from using standardized procedures or templates that have been developed by (or in collaboration with) other entities (for example, at the level of a specific sector or industry) for conducting a risk assessment.

54. Furthermore, the Commission strongly recommends that the data controller rely on existing methods for risk management. The use of international standards, such as those developed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), as well as codes of conduct developed or approved at the European level, is particularly important in this context.

55. Regardless of the method ultimately chosen by the data controller, the Commission considers it essential that the data controller explicitly indicates which method has been chosen and that it is applied consistently throughout the DPIA process.

E) Measures in question  
56. A Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) not only includes a risk assessment but also a record of the measures in question to address the risks, including safeguards, security measures, and mechanisms to ensure the protection of personal data and to demonstrate compliance with the GDPR93.  

57. The relevant measures may be of a technical, organizational, or legal nature, such as:  
- Organizational measures: increasing awareness, training, policy measures, separation of functions (commonly referred to as "Chinese Wall"), reporting, periodic checks, additional options for choice, participation, or objection for the data subjects, etc.  
- Technical measures: technical limitations on the collection and/or communication of personal data (for example, using specific cryptographic techniques for data minimization), anonymization, pseudonymization, and/or encryption of personal data after collection, technical limitations on the reuse of personal data (purpose), multi-factor authentication, logging and monitoring, data splitting, additional backups, etc.  
- Legal measures: contractual guarantees, binding corporate rules, etc94.  

58. When the envisaged data processing involves profiling or what is known as "big data" analytics, the risk of processing inaccurate data and/or indirect discrimination may increase. In such cases, the data controller must employ appropriate mathematical and statistical procedures and take technical and organizational measures that allow for the correction of factors leading to inaccuracies in personal data and minimize the risk of errors95. Furthermore, the data controller must avoid processing that has discriminatory consequences based on racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religion or beliefs, trade union membership, genetic status, health status, or sexual orientation, or that results in measures producing such effects96. To avoid such consequences, it is also advisable to verify if there is a possibility that a specific risk is highly likely to occur among certain minorities or protected groups.  

59. In the context of evaluating the measures in question to address the risks, the data controller must ensure that the rights and legitimate interests of the data subjects and other individuals are duly respected97. In this regard, it is essential to always keep an overview in mind: the nature, scope, context, and purpose of the processing, the risks, the state of technology, and the costs of implementation98. The higher the risks, the greater the expectations regarding the data controller concerning the measures to be taken. Therefore, the data controller does not always have to take all possible measures. The GDPR only requires that the measures bring the risks to an acceptable level and that there is an adequate balance between the measures envisaged and the risks involved99.  

60. The cost of the envisaged measures cannot in itself constitute a reason for carrying out processing without sufficient safeguards. If the data controller is unable to foresee sufficient safeguards and bring the risk to an acceptable level, given the available technology and the costs of implementation, they must either refrain from processing or carry out a prior consultation with the supervisory authority100.  

61. When identifying the envisaged measures, the data controller must explicitly indicate which measures serve to limit which risks. In this regard, it is recommended that the data controller also expressly specifies:  
- what means will be necessary to implement the measures;  
- which person(s) will be responsible for the implementation of these measures;  
- the duration during which the measures will be executed;  
- how the relevance of the measures will be monitored and evaluated101.

The extent of the consultation (which individuals and how many) should preferably be determined based on the risk and the scope of the processing. If the proposed processing only poses risks to a limited number of affected individuals (for example, workers in a small organization), the consultation may be limited to a small number of these workers and/or their representatives. If the proposed processing involves risks for a large number of affected individuals (for example, all residents), a broader consultation should be organized124.  

84. The data controller generally decides freely how to consult affected individuals or their representatives. Their opinions can be gathered in various ways, depending on the context (for example, a generic study regarding the purposes and means of processing, a question addressed to employee representatives, or regular surveys sent to future clients of the data controller)125. If the data controller has their contact information, they can reach out directly to ask for their opinions regarding the proposed data processing (for example, via email). If the identities of the affected individuals are not known in advance, the data controller could, for example, organize a public consultation. If necessary, the data controller may also arrange a joint consultation session. Although Article 35(9) of the GDPR only mentions "affected individuals or their representatives," there may also be added value in seeking the views of organizations that more generally advocate for the interests of affected individuals or consumers.  

85. In the context of the consultation, the data controller must ensure that the questions are posed in a way that generates reliable results (for example, through a validated questionnaire).  

86. If the final decision of the data controller differs from the opinions of the affected individuals, they should document the reasons for their decision to proceed or not126.  
E) The supervisory authority  
87. As previously mentioned, prior consultation is only mandatory if it turns out that the residual risk of the proposed processing is high. If the risk can be effectively mitigated through appropriate technical and organizational measures, no prior consultation should take place.

D) Management and Control

96. Article 35(11) of the GDPR states that the data controller must, if necessary, conduct a review to assess whether the processing is carried out in accordance with the DPIA. Such a review must at least take place when there is a change in the risk presented by the processing operations.

97. Article 35(11) of the GDPR contains two elements: on the one hand, an obligation to verify if the data processing is indeed carried out in accordance with the DPIA (including the indicated protective measures) and, on the other hand, the obligation to review the DPIA when there is a change in the risk.

98. A change in the risk presented by the processing operation may be due to various factors, such as a change in the means of processing employed or an evolution in the state of knowledge (for example, when new data minimization techniques become available) or the discovery of a new vulnerability in security justifying the implementation of additional or new security measures. The review of a DPIA may also be necessary if there is a change in the organizational or social context of the processing operation (for example, if it turns out that the effects of certain automated decisions have increased or that new categories of data subjects appear vulnerable to discrimination). In each of these examples, the factor in question may lead to an evolution of the risks arising from the relevant processing activity.

99. Conversely, certain developments may also reduce risks. For example, consider a processing operation that has evolved such that decisions are no longer automated or an activity of monitoring that has lost its systematic nature. In this case, the reassessment of risks may show that a DPIA is no longer necessary.

100. Given that risks generally evolve over time, the Commission recommends explicitly providing for periodic review when conducting a DPIA. The frequency of the periodic review should be determined based on the risk presented by the processing operation. Furthermore, the conduct of a DPIA may itself reveal factors requiring particular monitoring (for example, technical and organizational measures whose effectiveness is not yet established). As part of good risk management, the Commission expects the data controller to conduct a review at least every 3 years. The Commission also recommends that the result of the review be officially submitted.

E) What about existing processing?  
101. The obligation to carry out a DPIA applies from May 25, 2018. Processing operations that may 
result in a high risk and that begin after May 25, 2018 must therefore be preceded by a DPIA. For 
existing processing, a DPIA is generally required only if the risks to the rights and freedoms of 
natural persons change after May 25, 2018, for example because new technology is used or because 
personal data is used for a different purpose144. 
 
102. The above elements do not mean that data controllers should only carry out a DPIA when the 
processing itself is adapted. As mentioned earlier, the risks posed by a processing operation can also 
evolve due to changes in the environment in which the data processing takes place (for example, the 
social context, the consequences of the processing), which may reveal new vulnerabilities145. As soon 
as there is a change in the risk presented by the processing operation (and the processing still has the 
potential to generate a high risk), the carrying out of a DPIA is mandatory.  
 
103. The Commission recommends, in any case, that the data controller, as a good practice, carry out 
a DPIA for all existing processing that is likely to generate a high risk to the rights and freedoms of 
natural persons. Even if a DPIA is not strictly required as of May 25, 2018, it is necessary for the data 
controller to carry out a DPIA at the appropriate time as part of their overall responsibility and risk 
management146. 
 
104. Processing that has already been authorized (general or specific) by one of the sectoral committees 
of the Commission should, in principle, not require a DPIA as long as the processing is carried out in 
accordance with the prior terms of the authorization. Recital (171) of the GDPR states that authorizations 
granted by the supervisory authorities based on Directive 95/46/EC remain in effect until they are 
amended, replaced, or repealed147. Conversely, this means that any data processing whose 
implementation conditions (scope, purposes, personal data collected, identity of the data controllers or 
data recipients, data retention period, technical and organizational measures, etc.) have changed since 
the authorization and are likely to generate a high risk must undergo a DPIA148.

9. Annex 1: Minimum Characteristics of Good Risk Management  

Any data controller chooses which procedure and method they wish to use to assess and manage risks, provided that they meet a number of minimum characteristics of reliability and objectivity. To avoid a situation of legal uncertainty arising, in the absence of additional elements allowing for the quality of a risk analysis to be controlled, the Commission sets out below several minimum characteristics. The Commission emphasizes that these are minimum characteristics which, in themselves, do not guarantee that the processing in question will take place in accordance with the GDPR.  

1.  
Methodologically Supported  
Risk management and risk assessment must be methodologically supported, preferably using existing methods in the field of risk management. The use of international standards, such as those developed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), as well as codes of conduct developed or approved at the European level, is particularly important in this context. However, the data controller may freely choose which method they wish to use, provided that it allows for an objective assessment of the risk and takes into account the minimum elements prescribed by the GDPR. The data controller must, however, explicitly indicate which method has been chosen and ensure that it is applied consistently.  

2.  
Structured  
Good risk management is carried out in a structured manner, where the following steps can generally be distinguished:  
• communication and consultation with internal and external stakeholders;  
• definition of the relevant context (including a description of the subject of the risk analysis, a definition of the criteria used to assess risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, and the definition of (un)acceptable risk values);  
• identification, analysis, and evaluation of risks (including the identification of vulnerabilities, threats, and the assignment of a risk value).  

It is important that the chosen method allows for the analysis of risks from the perspective of the data subject. Given that a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is a tool for managing risks to the rights of data subjects, the perspective of the data subject is central. This situation is different from risk management in other areas (such as information security), which is generally focused on the interests and purposes of the organization itself.  
See in particular ISO 31000 (Risk management) and ISO 27005 (Information security risk management). These standards have a general scope and are not specifically dedicated to conducting a DPIA. When applying them, it is important to take into account the particular interpretation that the GDPR gives to the notion of "risk," as well as the potential safeguards and mechanisms that can be activated to limit these risks.

identification of appropriate risk mitigation measures (i.e., the technical, organizational, and legal measures necessary to bring the risk to an acceptable level); and

3.  
Tailored  
A risk assessment is always a tailored job and is adapted to the context and risk profile of the company conducting the assessment153. A good risk appraisal does not simply involve reproducing previously conducted analyses but requires a concrete evaluation based on the specific context (i.e., with reference to the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing). However, nothing prevents a data controller from using procedures or templates developed by (or in collaboration with) other entities (for example, at the level of a specific sector or industry) to conduct a risk assessment.  
4.  
Understandable  
The result of a risk appraisal must be readable and accessible to as wide an audience as possible. The result cannot only be readable to experts (in risks), technicians, or specialized personnel. Brief summaries and visual representations (for example, color graphs, tables with figures) can enhance the accessibility of the risk analysis (both its process and its transcription)154.  
  
5.  
Sufficiently nuanced  
A risk analysis must include enough scales to allow for a nuanced assessment of the identified risks. Providing only three scales (low, medium, high) to assess risks is not always sufficient to yield a correct appraisal. A clear description of the criteria used to evaluate the risk is, in any case, essential.  
6.  
Communication and consultation  
A good risk management system involves those who are best positioned to contribute to the process of identifying, analyzing, evaluating, and managing risks. This group includes not only the data protection officer and/or the security advisor but also the designers of new applications, those making strategic decisions regarding project development, and the staff members (or their representatives) who will use the personal data in question in the course of their duties. Where appropriate, the data controller seeks the opinion of the data subjects or their representatives, without prejudice to the protection of general or commercial interests or the security of processing operations.  
7.  
Management and control  
A dated and written report of the risk appraisals conducted must be prepared. An internal mandated body that makes decisions (for example, the management committee, strategic committee, or security committee, mandated by the board of directors) must be periodically informed of the result (or status) of the risk appraisal process. This mandated body must formally approve the risk assessment as well as the measures aimed at mitigating the risks.  
However, the risk appraisal process cannot be reduced to a mere bureaucratic process. The data controller must take appropriate measures to ensure that good risk management is part of the "corporate culture" of the data controller. A risk appraisal that has been conducted must be periodically reviewed and at least in the event of changing circumstances that may have a significant impact on an appraisal that was conducted in the past. The frequency of the periodic review must be determined based on the risk presented by the processing operation. Furthermore, the Commission also recommends that the result of the review be officially submitted for approval to the highest authority within the organization of the data controller.
