SYNTHESIS  

Biometric data are personal data resulting from specific technical processing, related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of an individual, which allow or confirm their unique identification, such as facial images or fingerprint data. Citizens are increasingly confronted with the processing of biometric data on their smartphones or tablets, as well as by public authorities and private companies. In accordance with Article 9.1 of the GDPR, biometric data constitute a particular category of personal data, in contrast to the situation prior to the entry into force of the GDPR. These are personal data that, by their nature, are particularly sensitive because their processing may involve significant risks to the freedoms and fundamental rights of individuals. Under Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the processing of biometric data is therefore prohibited unless the data controller can legitimately invoke one of the exceptions listed exhaustively in Article 9.2 of the GDPR.  

It is notably this change in the legal qualification of the concept of biometric data and the considerable increase in biometric processing processes in daily life that have prompted the Knowledge Center of the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter: the Knowledge Center) to express itself on this subject.  

The recommendation primarily aims to assist data controllers and processors in interpreting and correctly applying the rules of the GDPR regarding the processing of biometric data. It should be noted in this context that the recommendation does not apply to the processing of biometric data carried out by competent authorities within the meaning of the Police-Justice Directive. Furthermore, this recommendation aims to invite the legislator to provide a legal basis for the processing of biometric data.  

Initially, this recommendation explains in detail in Chapter II the legal framework for the processing in question. Particular attention is paid to the concepts of ‘personal data’, ‘processing of personal data’, ‘data controller’, and ‘processor’. The recommendation then provides explanations on the concrete scope of the concept of ‘processing of biometric data’. A good understanding of the terminology used is indeed fundamental before being able to apply the principles of data protection to the processing of biometric data.

In this context, particular attention is devoted to the choice of a correct legal basis (exceptional reason), the correct definition of the purposes of processing, the requirement of proportionality, the security of processing, the principle of data retention limitation, the obligation of transparency, and the (potential) obligation to conduct a data protection impact assessment.  

This analysis, particularly regarding the use of a legal basis or an exceptional reason justifying the processing of biometric data, teaches us that currently, there is a gap in Belgian law such that any processing of biometric data in the context of the authentication of individuals, insofar as explicit consent cannot be relied upon and the exception for the processing of biometric data in the context of the eID (electronic identity card) and passport is not applicable, occurs without a legal basis. This concretely means that the Belgian legislator will need to define in law the modalities of processing biometric data to the extent that it wishes to (continue to) authorize the use of biometric data in a specific context.

Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (hereinafter "GDPR") came into force on May 25, 2018. It repeals Directive 95/46/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of October 24, 1995, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (hereinafter "Directive 95/46/EC") and confirms and consolidates the jurisprudence and viewpoints adopted by the Court of Justice of the European Union and by the Article 29 Working Party (hereinafter "Group 29"). By moving from a directive to a regulation, the European legislator aimed to make the protection of personal data, defined as a fundamental right in Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, applicable directly and uniformly across the Member States.  

One of the main objectives of the GDPR is to strengthen the rights of data subjects. The GDPR indeed grants supervisory authorities significant powers so that they can also impose sanctions in case of non-compliance with the rules defined therein. The Eurobarometer of May 2019 on the GDPR reveals that the awareness of data subjects regarding applicable data protection rules and their rights is significantly increasing. In light of these strengthened rights, many consumer and citizen rights advocacy organizations agree that the GDPR significantly contributes to a fair digital society, based on mutual trust between data subjects and the actors involved in the processing of their data.

II.  
Processing of Biometric Data: What Does It Involve?  
1. Legal Framework  
1.1  
Personal Data  
Article 4.1) of the GDPR defines ‘personal data’ as follows: "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (hereinafter referred to as 'data subject'); an 'identifiable natural person' is a natural person who can be identified, directly or indirectly, particularly by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more specific elements characteristic of their physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity."  

The notion of ‘any information’ must be interpreted broadly and includes both objective elements (such as a name or specific physical characteristics) and subjective elements (such as opinions or assessments), regardless of the form or medium of the information. Furthermore, the information must relate to a person. In other words, there must be a link between the person and the information. Consequently, information relating to an asset (belonging to a person) or an event may also be classified as personal data. Next, the data subjects must be identified or identifiable. Generally, a natural person can be considered 'identified' if, within a group, they can be distinguished from all other members of the group. Similarly, a natural person is 'identifiable' if that person has not yet been identified but can nonetheless be identified. To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means reasonably likely to be used by the data controller or by any other person to identify the natural person directly or indirectly should be taken into account. Finally, the information must concern living natural persons. This implies that data about companies, public authorities, and organizations (legal entities) and deceased persons do not fall within the scope of the GDPR.  

However, ‘anonymized’ data are not classified as personal data because the identification of a natural person using such data is not (no longer) possible, even with additional information. In this context, it should be noted that the EDPB is currently working on drafting guidelines regarding data anonymization.  

1.2  
Processing of Personal Data  
Article 4.2) of the GDPR defines ‘processing’ as follows: "any operation or set of operations performed or not by automated means and applied to personal data, such as collection, recording, organization, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of making available, alignment or combination, as well as restriction, erasure or destruction of personal data."  

Just as for the purposes of processing, each data controller must also be transparent regarding the processing they carry out with a data subject's data. The level of detail depends, in particular, on the type of data subjects (children, professionals, experts, etc.), how their personal data are processed, and the extent to which such processing involves interference with their right to privacy. Defining the processing that is carried out for each distinct purpose (of processing) is also a fundamental element when assessing the proportionality of the processing in question.  

1.3  
Data Controller  
Article 4.7) of the GDPR defines the data controller as follows: "the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing."  

The Knowledge Center reminds that the designation of the data controller or qualification as such must be adequate in light of the factual circumstances. In other words, for each processing of personal data, it must be verified who is actually pursuing the purposes and who is actually controlling the processing.  

For this purpose, the following considerations may be taken into account:  
▪ Who decides primarily to proceed with the collection of (biometric) data?  
▪ Who defines the data subjects or categories of data subjects?

1.4  
Processor  
A processing relationship exists when a natural or legal person, a public authority, a service, or another body processes personal data on behalf of the identified controller, without that entity being able to determine/modify the purposes and means of processing.  

The use of a processor implies that the requirements of Article 28 of the GDPR must be met. According to Article 28.1 of the GDPR, the controller may only engage processors that provide sufficient guarantees regarding the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures so that the processing meets the requirements of the GDPR and ensures the protection of the rights of the data subject.  

Although the controller generally provides clear instructions to its processor regarding the intended processing of personal data, in reality, this is not always possible and/or desired. Indeed, the designated processor often possesses significant expertise in the field of technologies and/or security measures concerning the processing for which it is responsible. The fact that a processor has more expertise than the controller regarding the technical means to be used during the processing of data does not, in itself, lead to a reclassification of its position from processor to controller. Some processors offer ready-to-use solutions without this affecting the obligation of the controller to make the necessary decisions regarding the processed data, the pursued purposes, and/or the means to achieve them.  

Furthermore, it should be noted that the same entity can simultaneously fulfill the role of both processor and controller, but not for the same processing of personal data. A processor acting in this manner must ensure that its systems and procedures distinguish between the personal data it processes in its capacity as a controller and the personal data it processes in its capacity as a processor. If certain data is identical, these systems must be able to establish a distinction between these two situations, so that different processes and different measures can be applied to each situation.  

However, if an organization simultaneously acts as a controller and as a processor for different processing activities based on the same personal data, the data subjects must be properly informed by the organization, both in its capacity as a controller and in its capacity as a processor, and this obviously to the extent that the processing activities are lawful. This particularly applies to developers of facial recognition software who, on the one hand, act as a processor for an entity that uses this software for specific purposes (the controller) but, on the other hand, will also process the collected data for their own purposes. The information to be provided in this case must mention the different processing activities as well as the collected data, the recipients of the data, and their own purposes.  

Example  
In a Chinese restaurant of the KFC chain, facial recognition software (designed by Baidu, the Chinese counterpart of Google) is used to predict customer preferences. In this relationship, KFC is the controller (as this company determines the purposes of the processing) and Baidu is the processor, as its software performs the actual processing. However, if Baidu uses the personal data in question to establish biometric profiles of customers and communicate them to third parties, it acts in its capacity as a controller.

2. Definition of Biometric Data  
2.1  
Context  
Article 4.14) of the GDPR defines ‘biometric data’ as "personal data resulting from a specific technical processing, related to the physical, physiological or behavioral characteristics of a natural person, which allow or confirm their unique identification, such as facial images or fingerprint data." When data is not processed for the purpose of the unique identification of individuals but such identification is still possible, given the nature of the data, it will also be considered biometric data in the sense of the GDPR.  

To avoid any confusion in this regard and to promote legal certainty, it is important to note that the concept of 'biometric data' has a different meaning in the scientific context than in the data protection (European) context. The scientific definition is found in the International Standard ISO/IEC 2382-3710 and is worded as follows: "biometric sample or aggregation of biometric samples at any stage of processing, e.g. biometric reference, biometric probe, biometric feature or biometric property." The ISO/IEC standard thus considers the following as biometric data: (1) the recording of data (‘biometric sample’), (2) the extraction of data from samples (‘biometric feature’), (3) the assignment of biometric samples to specific individuals (‘biometric reference’), and (4) the comparison between different samples (‘biometric probe’). It is immediately noticeable that the concept in the scientific context does not necessarily concern the link between an individual and their biometric data, whereas the identification or rather the "identifiability" of an individual is fundamental to the notion of ‘biometric data’ in the context of data protection. The processing of biometric data in the scientific sense without the possibility of identifying or re-identifying individuals therefore does not fall within the scope of the GDPR. Biometric data can indeed only be classified as personal data when processed in a specific technical manner that allows for the unique identification or authentication of a natural person (even when this is not the primary purpose). Such a conclusion is consistent with Article 4.1) of the GDPR, from which it follows that the notion of personal data relates solely to an identified or identifiable natural person.

2.2  
Concrete Interpretation of the Notion of Biometric Data  
The GDPR distinguishes two categories of information that can be considered biometric data. The first category concerns physical properties – namely the physical or physiological characteristics of a person. The content of this category is quite simple and corresponds to what most people understand by biometric data, such as information related to the face, fingerprints, and iris scans.  

The second category, behavioral information, is considerably broader. Any behavioral characteristic that allows for the unique identification of a person is logically considered to be biometric data. Furthermore, the possibilities for processing behavioral information are evolving rapidly. Therefore, it is not possible to establish an exhaustive list of such processing. It will always be necessary to verify with concrete elements whether or not it is a processing of behavioral data allowing for the unique identification of individuals.  

Examples of Behavioral Biometric Data  
The manner of using the keyboard, touchscreen, and mouse as well as browsing habits and work behavior to authenticate individuals.  
Identification using the unique gait of individuals (in English, gait recognition).  

2.3  
The Biometric Processing Process  
A biometric processing process is referred to whenever a specific system is used to identify or authenticate individuals using their unique (static and/or dynamic) characteristics.

The operation of a biometric system is divided into two phases of information collection (collection phases) and two ways of comparing the collected information (the two functions of biometric systems) (comparative phase).

**COLLECTION PHASES**

The first collection phase, known as enrollment, is the moment when a biometric characteristic of the individual concerned is collected and recorded on a medium to store information (either an individual medium such as a badge or token, or in a database). This reference information will either be the raw biometric data (such as an image of the face, hand, iris, or fingerprint), or a set of encoded information obtained from the individual and unique characteristics of the raw data for the purpose of verifying or establishing an individual's identity (a template). Although these templates are distinct from the raw biometric data, they undoubtedly fall within the scope of the GDPR. Furthermore, the Knowledge Center notes that in accordance with Article 5.1.f) in conjunction with Article 32 of the GDPR and considering the principle of 'data protection by design', it will only be possible to legitimately use a system that records reference information in its raw form (i.e., the raw biometric data) in extremely exceptional cases. In principle, during the first collection phase, raw biometric data must be converted into templates. After which, the raw data must be immediately deleted.

During the second collection phase, the individual again presents their biometric characteristics to the system that must authenticate them. At this moment, a second biometric sample is taken (for example, a person holds their finger in front of the sensor) and this information is then compared to the reference information (the raw data or the template) to verify if they match. If the information collected during the second collection matches the reference information (positive association), the system considers that the person presenting themselves is the one who was previously registered during the enrollment phase. In this context, it should be noted that a biometric match is generally never exact. Each biometric system operates using a predefined threshold (often referred to as 'threshold'). This threshold indicates the point at which the system deems that the information obtained during the second collection phase sufficiently corresponds to the reference information.

**COMPARATIVE PHASE**

There are two ways to compare the information obtained during the collection phases, and they constitute the two main functions of biometrics: the identification function and the verification function.

The identification function involves comparing the information from the second phase with all the biometric information available in the biometric system and recorded by definition in a database ('one-to-many comparison'). This function will first allow the identification of the user among all registered individuals and can serve to authenticate them in a later phase.

In contrast, the verification function consists of comparing the information from the second phase to the previously recorded information belonging to a single person ('one-to-one comparison'). This function is particularly suited to situations where the person wishes to authenticate themselves and is therefore willing to voluntarily disclose an element that allows for their identification based on the comparison between the previously defined reference information and the sample from the new collection.

Although both functions can be used in the context of authentication, the verification function is undoubtedly preferable (given that biometric reference information does not necessarily need to be stored in a central database), and the identification function can only be used in exceptional and justified cases.

One can think of a badge, a token, or a local recording in the sensor at the entrance of the building. This method should be used in principle, and deviations can only occur in exceptional cases;
- (type 2) Shared control: there is a central database of templates under the control of the data controller, but the latter cannot use it without the...

**2.5**  
Exception  
There is a very specific, albeit ubiquitous, application of the use of biometric data in the context of personal authentication via smartphones and other electronic devices in which facial recognition software and fingerprint sensors are increasingly offered as an alternative to the traditional PIN code. Given the importance of such applications and the ambiguity that often surrounds them, this topic deserves some explanations.  

Article 2.2.c) of the GDPR states that the regulation does not apply "to the processing of personal data carried out by a natural person in the course of a purely personal or domestic activity." When biometric data (meaning: the templates created that allow authentication by facial recognition or by means of a fingerprint) are exclusively stored on the device, this results in the biometric authentication process being able to occur locally and autonomously without external access. Data processing in this sense - initiated by the data subject and carried out under their control - may fall, under certain conditions, under the domestic exception, which implies that the rules of the GDPR do not apply.

**III. Application of Data Protection Principles to the Processing of Biometric Data**

1. **Legal Basis**

1.1 **Why a Legal Basis?**  
Any processing of personal data must be based on a legal basis as defined in Article 6.1 of the GDPR. However, when it comes to the processing of special categories of personal data, the data controller must also demonstrate that they can legitimately invoke one of the exceptions outlined in Article 9.2 of the GDPR. The processing of special categories of personal data, including biometric data, effectively requires a dual legal basis.

It is essential that the data controller has identified the relevant legal basis before commencing processing, given that the conditions applicable to the different legal bases differ significantly. The correct choice of legal basis is indeed crucial in order to duly inform the concerned individuals and to ensure the effective exercise of their rights.

1.2 **Can the Legal Basis be Changed?**  
In principle, the legal basis cannot be changed during processing. This means that when the data controller wrongly invokes a specific legal basis, for example, because the conditions for it are not or are no longer met, the processing cannot continue.

1.3 **Which Legal Basis to Use for the Processing of Biometric Data?**  
Although there is no hierarchy among the legal bases provided by the GDPR, the nature of the processing is often decisive in identifying an appropriate legal basis. Specifically, regarding the processing of biometric data referred to in this recommendation, two exceptions deserve further explanation due to their importance in practice: explicit consent (Article 9.2.a of the GDPR) and significant public interest (Article 9.2.g of the GDPR).

1.3.1 **Explicit Consent**  
Explicit consent must be divided into two elements. First, there must be valid consent for the processing, and second, this consent must be explicit.

**VALID CONSENT**  
Article 4.11 of the GDPR specifies that consent from the concerned individual can only be considered valid if it is a manifestation of will that is (1) free, (2) specific, (3) informed, and (4) unambiguous, by which the concerned individual accepts, through a declaration or a clear affirmative action, that personal data concerning them be processed.

The following sections will briefly clarify how these elements should be assessed.

First, the element of 'free' implies that there must be a genuine choice for the concerned individual. Generally, if the concerned individual does not have a genuine choice, feels compelled to give their consent, or risks suffering harm in the absence of consent, it cannot be considered valid consent under the GDPR. Such consent is not valid.

EXPLICIT CONSENT  

In accordance with Article 9.2(a) of the GDPR, explicit consent is required when a data controller wishes to process special categories of personal data, as is the case here.  

The term 'explicit' refers to how consent is expressed by the data subject. This means that the data subject must provide a clear statement of consent. An obvious way to ensure that consent is explicit would be to expressly confirm consent in a written statement. If applicable, the data controller could ensure that the written statement is signed by the data subject to dispel any potential doubts and to exclude any possible lack of evidence in the future.  

However, such a signed statement is not the only way to obtain explicit consent, and the GDPR does not specify that in all circumstances requiring valid explicit consent, a written and signed statement is required. In the digital or online context, for example, a data subject may provide the required statement by completing an electronic form, sending an email, uploading a scanned document bearing their signature, or using an electronic signature. In theory, the use of verbal statements may also suffice to obtain valid explicit consent, but it may be difficult for a data controller to prove that when recording the statement, all conditions for valid explicit consent were met.

Even when it comes to free, specific, informed, unequivocal, and explicit consent, the data controller must also comply with the conditions of Article 7.1 and Article 7.3 of the GDPR.

However, this does not affect the lawfulness of the processing (based on that consent) prior to the withdrawal of consent. The data controller must also verify whether the retention of the data used for the processing in question is justified or not, even if the data subject has not made a request for deletion. Indeed, in accordance with Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR, the retention of personal data must be limited to the purpose pursued (see below Section III.5. Limitation of Retention).  

Only when the data of the data subject is necessary for the execution of processing for other purposes for which there is also a valid legal basis can the data potentially be retained. If this is not the case, it must be deleted.  

1.3.2. Significant Public Interest  

In accordance with Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, biometric data may only be processed when such "processing is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest, based on Union law or the law of a Member State, which must be proportionate to the aim pursued, respect the essence of the right to data protection, and provide for appropriate and specific measures to safeguard the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject." In principle, this exception plays an important role when—for example, due to the existence of a power imbalance between the data controller and the data subject—the conditions for explicit consent under Article 9.2.a) of the GDPR cannot be met. However, in contrast to explicit consent, a data controller can rely on significant public interest grounds only to the extent that Union law or the law of a Member State explicitly recognizes these interests and permits the processing of biometric data within this framework.

2. Purpose Limitation30  
The principle of purpose limitation is defined in Article 5.1.b) of the GDPR and establishes that personal data must be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and may not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes.  

Two elements must be distinguished: (1) specifying a (set of) determined, explicit, and legitimate purpose(s) for the intended processing and (2) an element of compatibility which implies that further processing is only permitted to the extent that it is not incompatible with the purpose(s) for which the data was initially collected. Given that the chosen purpose will largely determine the legal basis on which the processing must be founded, it is evident that the purpose must be defined before processing can begin31.  

Personal data may only be processed for actual or existing purposes or for purposes that, in light of the actual activity of the data controller, are achievable in the near future.

2.2 Subsequent Purpose(s)

This means that for any new purpose that is not compatible with the initial purpose, a specific legal basis must be identified.

3. Proportionality  
The mandatory proportionality test is indirectly derived from Article 5.1.a) (lawfulness) and c) (data minimization) of the GDPR and requires proportionality when balancing the respective interests of the data controller on one hand and the data subjects on the other. To this end, the data controller must always ask whether the processing activities they are considering are (1) appropriate (is the measure relevant for achieving the purposes?), (2) necessary (is the measure necessary for achieving the purposes?), and (3) not excessive (does the measure go beyond what is necessary to achieve the purposes?).  

More concretely, the mandatory proportionality test is part of complying with the obligations imposed by the GDPR. Only when the data controller can effectively demonstrate that all principles of data protection have been respected can one speak of lawful and therefore proportional data processing. For example, consider the identification of an appropriate legal basis, the clear definition of purposes, ensuring that only the data necessary for these purposes are processed, the selection of specific processing activities, compliance with the obligation of transparency, ensuring that data is not processed and retained longer than necessary, guaranteeing the integrity of data processing, and adherence to the principles of 'data protection by design' and 'data protection by default', …  

Regarding the processing of biometric data in particular, the notion of proportionality plays an important role. Indeed, in accordance with Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, personal data must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed. Therefore, the processing of personal data can only take place to the extent that the purposes of processing cannot reasonably be achieved by other means.  

If the data controller can demonstrate that the processing of biometric data is the most appropriate means to ensure security, they must also document and justify the use of a specific biometric characteristic. In particular, regarding the biometric authentication of individuals, the Knowledge Center emphasizes that the data controller should, in principle, limit themselves to authentication based on behavioral characteristics (the way of writing or typing, mouse movements) or morphological characteristics (for example, facial recognition, palm scanning).

In this context, reference should also be made to Article 25 of the GDPR concerning data protection by design and data protection by default.  

Data protection by design defines the obligation, on the part of the data controller, to integrate data protection principles in accordance with Article 5 of the GDPR as fundamental principles from the design stage throughout the system design process, in order to minimize risks to the data subjects from the outset (before processing actually begins). Thus, it is advisable to encourage manufacturers of biometric systems to take into account the right to data protection when developing and designing such systems and, given the state of knowledge, to ensure that data controllers are able to fulfill their obligations regarding data protection.  

Data protection by default implies that the data controller implements appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure that only personal data which is necessary for each specific purpose of processing is processed. This obligation applies to the amount of personal data collected, the scope of its processing, its retention period, and its accessibility, and aims to prevent unlawful, unforeseen, or unjustified processing of data.  

In this context, it is essential that the data controller closely monitors technological developments in this area in order to adapt security measures to these developments. The Knowledge Centre therefore wishes to draw attention to the fact that under Article 5.2 of the GDPR, the data controller is responsible and can therefore be held liable for damages resulting from non-compliance with security measures.

5. Limitation of retention  
In accordance with Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR, "personal data must be kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed." In practical terms, this means that once the purpose of processing has been fulfilled, or when the legal basis is no longer valid (for example, due to the withdrawal of consent by the data subjects or the disappearance of a significant public interest), the biometric data in question must be deleted. However, this does not exclude the possibility that the data may be retained longer due to a legal obligation or when such data are necessary for a judicial procedure.  

When the processing of biometric data occurs under a legal obligation on the part of the data controller, the retention period specified in the law must be respected.  

Regarding biometric authentication, as already explained above in section II.2.3. The biometric processing process, the Knowledge Center wishes to clarify that the raw biometric data collected during the first phase of data collection of a biometric system (registration phase) must, in principle, be deleted immediately once the biometric template has been created. Furthermore, the data collected during the second phase of data collection may not be retained longer than necessary to compare the collected data with the reference information.

7. Data Protection Impact Assessment  
In accordance with Article 35.1 of the GDPR, the data controller must carry out, prior to processing, an assessment of the impact of the intended processing operations on the protection of personal data when the processing, particularly by using new technologies, and taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.  

Article 35.4 of the GDPR additionally states that each supervisory authority is required to establish and publish a list of processing activities for which a data protection impact assessment is required. Pending the establishment of the Authority, the Privacy Protection Commission adopted recommendation No. 01/2018 on the modalities for conducting a data protection impact assessment, taking into account the provisions of Article 35 of the GDPR and the guidelines of Article 29 Working Party. This recommendation was subsequently supplemented by decision No. 01/201944 of the General Secretariat, which includes a list of processing activities for which a data protection impact assessment is required.  

As indicated in point 6 of decision No. 01/2019, a data protection impact assessment must always be conducted when the processing uses biometric data for the unique identification of data subjects located in a public place or in a private place accessible to the public. However, the Knowledge Center wishes to emphasize that the processing of biometric data for purposes other than those explicitly mentioned in the decision is also subject to the obligation to conduct a data protection impact assessment. Moreover, given the inherent high risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects involved in the processing of biometric data, not conducting a data protection impact assessment will only be justified in exceptional cases.  

In this regard, concerning the modalities for conducting a data protection impact assessment, the Knowledge Center refers to recommendation No. 01/201845, decision No. 01/2019, and the DPIA Guide46.
