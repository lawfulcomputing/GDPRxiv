II. Summary Proceedings  
21. On March 6, 2025, Y filed a claim with the court by summons. She requests, primarily, a declaration that the publication of the Dispute Chamber's decision should be suspended until the moment the decision becomes final (or reformed), i.e., until the expiration of the appeal period or until the ruling of the Market Court, under penalty of a coercive fine. In a subordinate order, Y requests a declaration that the Dispute Chamber's decision should be anonymized prior to its publication, under penalty of a coercive fine. Y also requests the right to verify the anonymization of the decision and to make suggestions for (additional) anonymization. Furthermore, Y requests a declaration that the Data Protection Authority must refrain from any publication regarding the decision (via news reports or social media) to avoid the risk of Y's anonymity being breached.  
22. At the public hearing on March 19, 2025, the parties were heard, the documents of the parties were submitted, the debates were closed, and the case was taken under advisement.  
23. On March 26, 2025, the summary judge declared Y's claim admissible but unfounded, and ordered her to pay the costs of the proceedings and the court fees. According to the president of the court, “no material evidence or elements were presented to support the claims and doomsday scenarios that the plaintiff [Y] puts forward in the event of publication of the ruling of the defendant [the Data Protection Authority].” Firstly, the summary judge ruled that Y did not demonstrate urgency. The alleged reputational damage is, according to the judge, based on unilateral claims, and financial damage to a commercial enterprise can, unless proven otherwise, be assumed to be recoverable. Y does not credibly demonstrate that her existence would be jeopardized by the mere publication of the decision. Furthermore, the judge points out that the publication of the decisions of the Dispute Chamber is based on the law (art. 95, § 1, 8°, 100, § 1, 16° and 108, § 3 WOG) and is therefore fully to be expected. Thus, this publication cannot constitute a sufficient ground for alleged damage. Following Y's argument would, according to the judge, lead to a prohibition on publication for any decision until a ruling has been made on the appeal, while art. 108, § 3 WOG precisely prescribes publication to safeguard the rights of third parties in the context of the appeal procedure. Secondly, the summary judge ruled that Y did not demonstrate a sufficient appearance of right. The president of the court clarified that the decision-making authority regarding publication is exclusive and

III.1.1. Qualification of the data  
26. The respondent states in its synthesis conclusions that it processes personal data only to a very limited extent. The processing that gave rise to the present complaint cannot, according to it, be qualified as processing of personal data, which means that the GDPR does not apply. According to the respondent, the email address “[...]” is not a personal data but a piece of information about a legal entity. It refers to Recital 14 of the GDPR:  
“The protection provided by this regulation concerns natural persons, regardless of their nationality or residence, in relation to the processing of their personal data. This regulation does not apply to the processing of data concerning legal persons and in particular to legal persons that are established enterprises, such as the name and legal form of the legal person and the contact details of the legal person.”  
(emphasis of the respondent)  

According to the respondent, the email address “[...]” should be classified as a contact detail of a legal entity, and the GDPR is, in accordance with Recital 14, not applicable. In general, the respondent states that the data in its databases can only be classified as personal data in the case of sole proprietorships or the names of the directors of legal entities.  
27. In this regard, the Disputes Chamber points to Article 4.1 GDPR, where the concept of personal data is defined:  
“Personal data: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name…”  
28. The complainant is a natural person who can be directly identified by the email address “[...]”, which means that this email address must indeed be considered as personal data according to Article 4.1 GDPR. Although it concerns an email address that the complainant uses in the context of a professional activity, it remains information about a natural person. Moreover, the Court of Justice of the European Union has ruled that the fact that information belongs to a professional context does not detract from its qualification as personal data. The Disputes Chamber also reminds that this Court already ruled in 2010 that the name of a legal person is a

III.2. Legality of the processing (Article 5.1.a and 5.2, as well as Article 6.1)  
32. Article 5.1.a) GDPR stipulates that personal data must be processed in a manner that is lawful, fair, and transparent with respect to the data subjects. Furthermore, Article 6.1 GDPR states that the processing of personal data is only lawful if and to the extent that it is based on one of the legal grounds laid down in Article 6.1.a) – f) GDPR. The data controller must verify prior to processing whether the conditions of one of the possible legal grounds are met. Finally, the data controller must be able to demonstrate that the processing is lawful, considering the accountability obligation that rests on him under Article 5.2 GDPR.  
33. In this case, the respondent invokes the legal ground provided in Article 6.1.f) GDPR. This legal ground states that processing is lawful insofar as it is “necessary [for]

the safeguarding of the legitimate interests of the data controller or  
of a third party, except where the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject requiring the protection of personal data outweigh those interests, particularly when the data subject is a child.” The legal basis referred to in this provision must be interpreted restrictively according to the Court of Justice, as the processing of personal data may thereby be lawful without the data subject having given consent.  
34. The Court of Justice has ruled that Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR sets out three cumulative conditions that must be met for the processing of personal data referred to therein to be lawful, namely, first, the safeguarding of a legitimate interest of the data controller or of a third party, second, the necessity of processing personal data for the safeguarding of that legitimate interest, and, third, the condition that the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not outweigh the legitimate interest of the data controller or of a third party.  
35. In the present case, the Inspection Service finds that the respondent does not comply with the obligations imposed by Articles 5.1(a), 6.1, and 5.2 of the GDPR. The Inspection Service concludes that the respondent does not sufficiently take into account the three cumulative conditions that must be met in order to invoke the legal basis of a legitimate interest. According to the Inspection Service, the respondent in particular does not meet the condition that the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not outweigh the legitimate interest of the data controller or of a third party (the “balancing test”).  
36. This balancing of the conflicting rights and interests depends on the circumstances of the specific case. The data controller must take into account the interests, the fundamental rights, and the freedoms of the data subject, the impact of the processing on the data subject, and the reasonable expectations of the data subject. Recital 47 of the GDPR states that the interests and the fundamental rights of the data subject weigh more heavily than the interest of the data controller when personal data is processed in circumstances where the data subjects could not reasonably expect such processing.

III.2.1. Balancing of Interests Regarding the Interests, Fundamental Rights, and Freedoms of the Data Subject and the Impact of Processing on the Data Subject  
38. The defendant states in its synthesis conclusions that its own interests, taking into account the measures it has provided, outweigh the rights of the data subjects. It refers, just like the Inspection Service, to the ruling in TK, and more specifically to points 54 and 55 of this ruling in which the Court of Justice rules that it can be taken into account that the severity of the infringement of the fundamental rights of the data subject as a result of processing may vary depending on whether the relevant data is available in publicly accessible sources22. Furthermore, according to the defendant, it follows from that same ruling that this balancing must also take into account the nature of the relevant personal data and the processing, the specific manner of processing and access to the data, and the reasonable expectations of the data subject. The defendant asserts that the data it processes is not sensitive as it consists solely of "business data" that is always publicly available.  
39. Regarding the defendant's assertion that the relevant data is also publicly available in other ways, the Disputes Chamber is of the opinion that the defendant has not sufficiently demonstrated this. The complainant's data was publicly available in this case via the KBO, but the defendant claims that the KBO was not the source for its database. It is therefore up to the defendant to demonstrate that all personal data it holds is publicly available.  
40. Furthermore, the defendant states that the number of data subjects whose personal data it would process is limited because it is a small percentage of individuals in relation to the population23. The Disputes Chamber reminds that the defendant itself assessed in May 2021 that it processes personal data on a large scale24. As outlined in paragraph 26 of this decision, it cannot be ruled out that the defendant processes at least 68,165 email addresses that may be personal data and fall within the scope of the GDPR. The ratio between the number of data subjects and the general population does not diminish the fact that the defendant processes personal data on a large scale.  
41. The defendant further states that the data subjects have the opportunity to object to the processing. In this regard, the Disputes Chamber refers to Chapter III.3 of this decision, which shows that the defendant does not take sufficient measures to proactively inform data subjects that their data is being processed. In this case, the complainant was not informed by the defendant that his data would be processed, and he could not exercise his right to object until he obtained the necessary information from a customer of the defendant.  
42. The defendant then states that the risk of a data breach is limited because it has taken measures to secure it, and that, if a breach were to occur, its impact would always be limited because no sensitive data is processed and because the data is always publicly accessible. As mentioned above, the Disputes Chamber finds that the defendant has not demonstrated this last point.  
43. The Disputes Chamber notes that in the document "balancing of interests" that the defendant prepared in April 2021,25 no attention is paid to the interests, fundamental rights, and freedoms of the data subject, the impact of the processing on the data subject, or the reasonable expectations of the data subject. Thus, it does not demonstrate that it assessed, prior to processing, to what extent data subjects might experience negative consequences. Without these essential elements, there can be no balancing of interests as required by the Court of Justice.

44. The Dispute Chamber notes that the respondent demonstrates misconceptions regarding the required balancing of interests in its "balancing of interests." To begin with, section 2.2 of the "Balancing of Interests" reads as follows: “2.2 Balancing of Interests: is the processing necessary and essential to achieve one or more objectives of Y?”. The question that the respondent poses to itself concerns the necessity test, and not a balancing of the opposing rights and interests involved. Furthermore, the respondent writes in the same "balancing of interests" that “Y [always carefully weighs its interests] against the interest of providing customers with the necessary business information.” This is an incorrect test. The respondent must weigh its interests against the interests of the data subjects, and not against those of its customers.  
In summary, the Dispute Chamber finds that the respondent does not demonstrate having conducted a balancing of interests regarding the interests, fundamental rights, and freedoms of the data subject and the impact of the processing on the data subject.

Furthermore, the respondent has not confirmed whether the data was originally collected from the KBO. Therefore, it is impossible for her to invoke the reasonable expectations that the data subjects would have regarding the processing of their data by the KBO. The Disputes Chamber thus rules that the general possibility of commercial reuse of KBO data in no way implies that the data subjects in this case could reasonably have expected that the respondent would process the data for her own purposes.  
48. According to the Court of Justice, it follows from recital 47 of the GDPR that the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject weigh particularly heavily against the interests of the data controller when personal data is processed in circumstances where the data subject does not reasonably expect such processing.  
49. Furthermore, account must be taken of the relationship between the data subject and the data controller. Recital 47 of the GDPR states that a legitimate interest may be present when there is a “relevant and appropriate” relationship between the data subject and the data controller, for example in situations where the data subject is a customer or is employed by the data controller. In this case, there was clearly no relevant or appropriate relationship between the complainant and the respondent and her customers. The complainant was not aware that the respondent was processing his data until he exercised his right of access with a third party that had obtained the data from the respondent.  
50. From the preceding points, it follows that the respondent does not demonstrate that the data subjects, including the complainant, could reasonably have expected such processing.

94. As a first step, the Dispute Chamber establishes that there is a single infringing behavior. The processing of personal data without a valid legal basis, without proactively informing the data subjects, and without fully addressing the complainant's requests for access, constitutes a series of processing activities that are carried out by a single will and that are contextually, spatially, and temporally interconnected. They must be considered as “related” and as a single behavior.  

95. However, the Dispute Chamber finds that this behavior resulted in three different violations, and that these violations can be cumulatively attributed when calculating the fines. The provisions that were violated pursue independent objectives (the principle of lawfulness, the duty to inform, and the right of data subjects to obtain access to the processing of their personal data), whereby one provision is not excluded or encompassed by the applicability of the other, which justifies the imposition of separate fines. The Dispute Chamber refers, for example, to the Binding Decision 1/2021 of the EDPB: “Regarding the meaning of Article 83(3) GDPR, the Committee [EDPB] notes that taking into account the positions of the relevant supervisory authorities in the case of multiple violations, multiple amounts may be established. However, the total fine must not exceed a maximum amount that, in abstracto, is prescribed by the GDPR.”  
96. Furthermore, Article 83.3 GDPR stipulates that if a data controller intentionally or negligently violates multiple provisions of this regulation concerning the same or related processing activities, the total amount of the fine shall not exceed the maximum amount allowed for the most serious violation.  
97. In summary, the Dispute Chamber concludes in this case that it must impose three separate fines, and that the total fine cannot exceed the maximum amount for the most serious violation.

Effectiveness  
120. Recital 148 GDPR emphasizes that administrative fines must be imposed “[w]ith a view to a more effective enforcement of the rules of this regulation.” The imposed fine must therefore be high enough to achieve this objective.

Proportionality  
122. The principle of proportionality implies that the amounts of the fines must not be disproportionate to the objectives pursued and that the imposed fine must be proportionate to the infringement, viewed in its entirety, taking into account, in particular, its severity.  
123. In this case, the three relevant infringements were assessed as of moderate severity. According to paragraph 60 of the EDPB Guidelines, in cases of moderate severity, the Dispute Chamber must set the starting amount for further calculation at a point between 10% and 20% of the applicable statutory maximum amount. Deterrent effect  
125. In imposing a fine, the Dispute Chamber considers both specific and general deterrence. A fine is deterrent when it prevents an individual from violating the objectives and regulations enshrined in EU law.  
126. The deterrent nature of the fine must have two dimensions. It must prevent the person to whom the fine is imposed from repeating the infringement in the future, but it must also deter other individuals from repeating the infringing behavior of the first person.  
127. Various factors determine the deterrent effect of a fine: the nature and amount of the fine and the likelihood that the fine will be imposed are decisive in this respect. A fine must be high enough to have a significant financial impact on the business committing the infringement, while the fine must be proportionate to the severity of the infringement. In other words, the criterion of deterrence overlaps with that of effectiveness.  
128. In the present case, the total amount of the fine is reduced to 20,000 EUR. However, this amount remains sufficiently deterrent to prevent the defendant from repeating its infringement of the GDPR rules. Moreover, it is also intended to deter other businesses from committing similar infringements. This fine, which is proportionate to the severity of the infringement and takes into account the defendant's turnover, is intended to have both a specific and a general deterrent effect.

She specifically states that it appears that all data is categorized under the same label of personal data, while a large part of the data processed by Y should not be classified as such. In this way, a heavier infringement would be assumed than what is actually present. The Dispute Chamber finds that this concern is not pertinent as it has indeed conducted an analysis of the nature of the data that Y processes (see section III.1.1. Qualification of the data). Furthermore, Y argues that the fact that the data concerns businesses should be reflected in the calculation of the fine. The Dispute Chamber
Decision on the Merits 72/2025-44/48  
judges that the mere fact that personal data also concerns companies cannot be a mitigating circumstance.  
131. Furthermore, Y states that it has taken measures to ensure that data is processed correctly. In particular, it claims to have entered into contractual guarantees, limited the amount of data made available to its customers, and updated and deleted the data. The Dispute Chamber acknowledges these measures but rules that they are part of the legal obligations to which Y is bound, and thus do not constitute "special circumstances." Compliance with legal obligations is, as previously noted with reference to EDPB guidelines, a neutral circumstance.  
132. Moreover, Y believes that the total administrative fine would be disproportionate in light of Article 83.1 and 83.2 GDPR. It substantiates this point by referring to decision 07/2024 of January 16, 2024, of the Dispute Chamber, in which the Dispute Chamber imposed administrative fines on Z6. According to Y, the sanction imposed on Y would be significantly heavier in relation to the sanction imposed on Z6. The Dispute Chamber rules that these two separate cases are different and cannot be compared. This was confirmed by the summary proceedings judge in decision 2025/25/C, in which the president of the court ruled that the situation of other companies, specifically Z6, is not comparable to that of Y.  
133. Furthermore, Y asserts that the intended violations have a very minor factual impact on the rights of the data subjects. This data would concern companies and would be publicly available. Regarding the latter claim, that the data is publicly available, the Dispute Chamber concludes that this was not demonstrated by the defendant. Y expressly stated that the source of the data was not the KBO and provided no other evidence that the data would be publicly available at all times. Concerning the factual impact on the data subjects, the Dispute Chamber reminds that the processing involves a large number of data subjects. Furthermore, the Dispute Chamber recalls that these data subjects did not receive information from Y regarding the processing of their data. They thus find themselves in a situation where their personal data is unlawfully and without their knowledge repeatedly traded. This can create a feeling of losing control over their data and touches the core of the right to data protection.  
134. In conclusion, the Dispute Chamber rules that Y does not present any new elements in its response to the sanction form based on which the administrative fines should be adjusted.

V. Immediate Enforceability  
138. In its response to the sanction form, Y requested the suspension of the immediate enforceability of the sanctions.  
139. Regarding the order based on Article 100, §1, 10° of the WOG (the order to delete personal data for which Y cannot demonstrate that it has a valid legal basis for processing), the Dispute Chamber reminds that this is not immediately enforceable based on Art. 108, §1, WOG.  
140. Regarding the other decisions based on Article 100 WOG, the Dispute Chamber refuses the requests for suspension of immediate enforceability for the following reasons.  
141. Firstly, immediate enforceability is the standard situation for the national legislator. The European legislator has granted powers to the authority to take measures: it is thus the authority that decides which (corrective) measure is most appropriate to implement or impose on the defending party.

142. The possibility of legal recourse before a judicial authority after a decision has been made does not detract from the powers of the authority. In light of the separation of powers, the judiciary must assess a posteriori whether the supervisory authority has acted within the legal framework and within its discretionary powers. When the judge exercises their own authority to suspend enforceability, this is a decision that falls within their evaluative jurisdiction.  
143. It cannot be the standard situation—considering the credibility of the powers granted to the authority by European and national legislators—that the enforceability of the decisions and measures taken by an authority is suspended as soon as a party requests it. If this were indeed the standard situation, it would undermine the entire purpose of the legislator to act decisively and effectively in a digitalized society. This does not align with the teleological design of the powers granted to the authority under the GDPR.  
144. In this sense, it is indeed the intention, both of the European and the Belgian legislator, that a party against whom the Dispute Chamber takes measures complies without unnecessary delay with the provisions of the authority's decision. The Dispute Chamber again points out that this does not mean that a suspension cannot occur, but it must be based on significant grounds.  
145. Secondly, where the enforceability is not suspended and the decision is subsequently assessed as flawed, legal redress is in any case possible, as the rulings of the Market Court constitute the final substantive judgment in the relevant matters. There is no indication in this case that such legal redress would be difficult or impossible.
