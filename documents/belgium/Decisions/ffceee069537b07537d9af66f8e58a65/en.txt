I. Facts and procedure  
1.  
On September 19, 2022, the complainant filed a complaint with the Data Protection Authority against the defendant.  
2.  
The complaint concerns the following facts. In December 2021, the complainant received a credit offer from the defendant in the context of purchasing a property. The credit offer includes a conditional rate reduction, one of the conditions of which is to take out a life insurance policy linked to the credit for the amount of the credit. If no remaining balance insurance is taken out with the defendant, the rate reduction is not applied. The complainant asserts that when applying for the remaining balance insurance through a broker in March 2022, it became apparent that signing a consent for the processing of health data was necessary. According to the complainant, this consent would not only apply to the medical acceptance for the remaining balance insurance in question but also to all processing of health data, such as claims processing, pricing development, refinement of affiliation and coverage conditions, and fraud detection and prevention. The complainant adds that this is also coupled with consent for automated decision-making based on health data. On March 9, 2022, the complainant received a response from the insurance broker regarding the consent for the processing of health data. This response indicated that it was impossible for the defendant to rely on more specific consent. The complainant asserts that only a remaining balance insurance is requested. The complainant notes that the defendant's website prevents the completion of the questionnaire if no consent is given. Given the risk of not benefiting from the rate reduction, the complainant gives his consent through the consent document and completes the medical questionnaire on the website. Subsequently, a summary document that must be signed via the website is prepared. According to the complainant, this summary document does not contain the same information as what was available in the fields to be completed in the questionnaire. On July 18, 2022, the complainant exercised his rights by email by partially withdrawing his consent; he also requested a limitation of processing pending clarification of the legal basis, withdrew his consent for automated decision-making, and finally requested a rectification of the signed document and sufficient integrity protection of the signed document. The complainant received a copy of his personal data from the defendant on August 12, 2022. On the same date, the complainant received from the defendant the response to the other elements of the request.

II.2. Lawfulness of Processing  
II.2.1. Findings of the Inspection Service  
29.  
The Inspection Service notes in its inspection report that the defendant invokes explicit consent as the legal basis for processing health data in the context of subscribing to outstanding balance insurance. However, the Inspection Service finds that the lawfulness of the processing in question has not been respected as there is a violation of Article 4.11) of the GDPR, Articles 7.1 and 7.3 of the GDPR, and Article 9.2(a) of the GDPR.
Decision on the Merits 109/2024 — 7/24  
30. According to the Inspection Service, the consent that the defendant requests from the complainant via the "consent to the processing of health data" form is not free and specific, given that the form applies to various processing purposes and that the data subjects only have the option to give their consent for all these processing purposes as a whole. Therefore, the Inspection Service finds that the consent requested via the aforementioned form is not free and specific, rendering the consent invalid in law. Finally, the Inspection Service concludes based on its investigation that it is not as easy to withdraw consent as it is to give it, as this withdrawal requires the data subject to first read the privacy statement which indicates how to proceed with this withdrawal.  
II.2.2. Position of the Complainant  
31. In his reply conclusions, the complainant asserts that the consent given was not provided in a specific and free manner. Regarding the lack of specific consent, the complainant argues that the defendant lists various purposes, such as the development of correct pricing, effective cost management, and the refinement of affiliation and coverage conditions. The defendant claims that these purposes are inextricably linked to one another. The complainant contests this and asserts that these are vaguely formulated purposes that do not specifically concern the insurance contract in question. According to the complainant, these are general activities that an insurer may engage in while managing its business, but which are not necessary for the potential large-scale use of all the health data in its possession. The complainant states that the defendant could also rely on other elements such as a subset of personal data (i.e., individuals who have given specific consent), other sources of statistical data (such as public death statistics), or scientific research.  
32. The complainant adds that even if all these purposes were inextricably linked, which is denied, this link cannot justify the collection of consent for all insurance contracts. Therefore, the consent is not specific.  
33. The complainant asks why the defendant, before the medical questionnaire was filled out for the outstanding balance insurance, did not briefly request consent specifically for the outstanding balance insurance in question, making the necessary distinction between the different purposes. This method would also provide the defendant with the opportunity to inform the data subject that the health data already known could also be used in determining the risk.

34. Regarding the free nature of consent, the complainant emphasizes that there is a certain prejudice related to the refusal of consent, which goes beyond the mere inability to obtain insurance. The complainant also criticizes the fact that, by collecting consent for different purposes within a single consent, it is not obtained freely.  
35. Concerning the legal basis for the processing of health data, in the context of medical acceptance for the outstanding balance insurance, the complainant requests that the Dispute Chamber take a position regarding the legal basis, namely whether there is a sufficiently specific legal basis for the processing or if consent is the applicable legal basis. The complainant asserts that the defendant cannot be blamed for any imperfections in the legislation regarding the processing of health data in the context of medical acceptance for the outstanding balance insurance. Nevertheless, according to the complainant, the defendant must correctly define the legal basis, and if it still invokes explicit consent as the legal basis, consent must be validly requested.  
II.2.3. Position of the Defendant  
36. In its conclusions, the defendant argues that it has complied with the GDPR when processing health data based on explicit consent. It claims that explicit consent is free and specific and that it is as easy to withdraw as it is to give.  
37. Regarding the specificity of explicit consent, the defendant contends that the applicable legal framework related to (life) insurance implies that the processing activities or "sub-purposes" outlined by the defendant in the consent form, which the Inspection Service considers as distinct purposes, are intrinsically linked to a single more general purpose, namely the proper fulfillment of the insurer's role in offering and executing insurance contracts. The splitting of the purpose of processing would result in either the defendant being unable to apply or respect legal principles, or creating a false impression for the concerned individual that they have the choice to granularly indicate their agreement or disagreement by processing activity or 'sub-purpose', which would be contrary to Article 5.1.a) of the GDPR, which states that "personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject." Given that the processing of the complainant's health data occurred solely for a single general purpose, the explicit consent obtained by the defendant is indeed specific, in accordance with the conditions set by the GDPR for the processing of personal data based on consent.

38. Regarding the nature of free explicit consent, the defendant emphasizes that it follows from the legal framework concerning insurance that the processing of health data in question is not 'unnecessary' for properly fulfilling the role of insurer during the proposal and execution of insurance contracts, but must instead be considered an essential element. One cannot conclude from the finding that the defendant makes consent a condition for taking out a debt balance insurance that there is no question of a 'free' expression of will by the concerned individual and that consent is thus, by definition, invalid. The defendant highlights in this context the use of explicit consent as a ground for exception under Article 9.2.a) of the GDPR for the processing of special categories of personal data that are necessary for the performance of the contract within the meaning of Article 6.1.b) of the GDPR. The defendant points out that consent as the sole ground for exception under Article 9.2 of the GDPR can apply in the absence of a national legal framework. In this context, the defendant refers to legislative initiatives from the insurance sector aimed at obtaining such a legal framework, without success thus far.  
II.2.4. Evaluation by the Dispute Chamber  
39. The question arises as to whether the defendant can validly invoke explicit consent for the processing of personal data in the context of taking out a debt balance insurance.  
40. Article 5.1.a) of the GDPR requires that personal data be "processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject ('lawfulness, fairness, transparency')." The principle of lawfulness is one of the most important principles of the GDPR and serves as a condition for the application of the other principles of the GDPR relating to the processing of personal data.  
41. It is the responsibility of the data controller to determine what the appropriate legal basis is in relation to the purpose of the processing.1 Given that different consequences arise from the choice of legal basis, particularly in terms of rights for the data subjects, the data controller is not permitted to rely on one or another legal basis at their discretion. Once a certain legal basis is chosen, one cannot subsequently opt for another legal basis. When the basis

42.  
Under Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the processing of health data is generally prohibited. If there is processing of special categories of personal data in accordance with Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the data controller must designate a legal basis in accordance with Article 6 of the GDPR as well as a justification for the exception under Article 9.2 of the GDPR in order to speak of lawful processing. This combination of legal bases from Article 6 and Article 9.2 of the GDPR stems notably from the Meta judgment (C-252/21) of the Court of Justice3 in which the Court expressly states that the processing of sensitive personal data is only permitted if such processing can be qualified as lawful under Article 6.1 of the GDPR. Recital 51 of the GDPR clearly indicates that Article 6 of the GDPR must always be applied.  
43. In national legislation, one cannot find an applicable legal basis either. Neither the law of July 30, 2018, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data6, nor the law of April 4, 2014, on insurance7, nor any national law contains a basis for processing on which health data can be processed in the context of entering into insurance contracts. Therefore, the defendant must find a legal basis in the GDPR.

II.3.4. Assessment by the Contentious Chamber  
63. When personal data is used to arrive at a certain decision and that decision is based solely on automated processing of personal data, this constitutes automated individual decision-making. Based on Article 22.1 of the GDPR, data subjects have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing (including profiling) when that decision has legal effects for them or similarly significantly affects them.  
64. However, the above does not apply if the decision: a) is necessary for the conclusion or performance of a contract between the data subject and a data controller; b) is authorized by Union law or the law of the Member State to which the data controller is subject and which also provides for appropriate measures to safeguard the rights and freedoms and legitimate interests of the data subject; or c) is based on the explicit consent of the data subject (Article 22.2 of the GDPR).  
65. Under Article 22.4 of the GDPR, automated individual decisions cannot be based on special categories of personal data unless there is explicit consent from the data subject for that purpose, or if the use is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest, based on Union law or the law of a Member State. In both cases, appropriate measures must be taken to safeguard the legitimate interests of the data subject. In the first situation, the data controller takes these measures themselves; in the second, these measures are legally prescribed.  
66. As asserted by the defendant, automated individual decision-making is based on explicit consent as prescribed by Article 9.2.a) of the GDPR. In this case, the Contentious Chamber found that explicit consent had not been validly obtained, but that this infringement could not be attributed to the defendant (see section II.2). Therefore, the defendant can indeed invoke Article 22.2.c) of the GDPR in conjunction with Article 22.4 of the GDPR, provided it meets the applicable conditions.  
67. Given that the defendant invokes Article 22.2.c) of the GDPR in conjunction with Article 22.4 of the GDPR, it must take appropriate measures to protect the rights of the data subject. These measures must at least include the following: the right to obtain human intervention, the right for the data subject to express their viewpoint, and the right to contest the decision.
Decision on the Merits 109/2024 — 16/24  
68. Based on the defendant's statement and the privacy statement regarding decisions made in an automated manner as transmitted by the defendant, the Contentious Chamber finds that the defendant has taken various measures. Automated individual decision-making without human intervention occurs only in the case of a positive decision. If different warning signals are triggered and a negative decision must potentially be made, the file will nonetheless be submitted for review by a collaborator. The concerned individual may object to profiling so that the decision to grant credit or related debt insurance cannot be made automatically. If a concerned individual disagrees with the automated individual decision, they may, in accordance with the privacy statement, contact the defendant in several ways to express why they disagree with the decision and request a review of the decision made.  
69. In light of these elements, the Contentious Chamber finds, based on the statements of the defendant and the applicable privacy statement, that the defendant lawfully bases automated individual decision-making on health data on explicit consent and has taken the appropriate measures necessary to protect the rights of the concerned individual. Since neither in the inspection report nor in the complainant's conclusions does the Contentious Chamber find any evidence that would refute this finding, the Contentious Chamber concludes that there is no violation of Article 22 of the GDPR, Article 24 of the GDPR, and Article 25 of the GDPR.

The defendant contests the allegations made by the complainant and asserts that it has indeed taken appropriate technical and organizational measures "to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk," in accordance with the provisions of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR and Article 32 of the GDPR.
