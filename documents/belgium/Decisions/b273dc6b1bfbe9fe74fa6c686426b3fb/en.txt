The Facts  
1.  
On October 28, 2021, the complainant filed a complaint with the National Commission for Information Technology and Liberties (hereinafter "CNIL") against the defendant.  
2.  
The complaint filed by the complainant denounces the publication of the remuneration of her private professional activity on the defendant's website.  
3.  
For the proper understanding of this draft decision, the Contentious Chamber specifies that the law of May 2, 1995, imposes on a series of public officials and high-ranking civil servants the obligation to submit an annual declaration to the Court of Auditors of all their mandates, functions, or professions exercised during the previous year, as well as a declaration of assets where applicable. This obligation to declare mandates and remuneration applies to individuals who have held one of the mandates within an administration, a public institution, or an organization linked to public authorities listed in Article 1 of this law (hereinafter "mandate subject to declaration"). Individuals who have held one of the mandates subject to declaration are also required to declare all other mandates or functions they have exercised in the same year, regardless of whether they are remunerated or not, whether they fall within the public or private sector, and whether they were exercised in Belgium or abroad. The law of October 14, 2018, amended the law of May 2, 1995, to extend its scope to public administrators and incorporate the obligation to declare remuneration. The declaration must therefore indicate the designation of each activity, the name of the institution or organization, the related remuneration, and the start and end dates of the mandate. Regarding the obligation to declare remuneration, the law provides a distinction between mandates subject to declaration, for which the gross annual remuneration must be declared, and other mandates, functions, or professions, for which an estimate of the remuneration is only required, within ranges set by the law. These lists of mandates and declarations of assets are then published in the Belgian Official Gazette.

II. Motivation  
II.1. Regarding the territorial jurisdiction of the Contentious Chamber  
22.  
In light of Article 77 of the GDPR, the complainant – residing in France – has the right to lodge a complaint with the supervisory authority of her choice, even if this authority is located in a Member State where the complainant does not have her habitual residence or place of work.  
23.  
By application of Article 56.1 of the GDPR, the Data Protection Authority (DPA) is competent to act as the lead authority regarding cross-border data processing carried out by the data controller(s) whose main establishment or sole establishment is located on its territory. In this case, the territorial jurisdiction of the DPA, and thus of the Contentious Chamber, is not in dispute as the defendant, responsible for the processing of the Cumuleo website, has its sole establishment in Belgium.  

II.2. Regarding the merits of the request for erasure following the exercise of the right to object  
24.  
In view of the content of the complaint, it is for the Contentious Chamber to assess whether the defendant rightly refused to respond favorably to the request for erasure following the exercise of a right to object concerning the disputed content currently available on the defendant's website.  
25.  
The Contentious Chamber recalls that under Article 17.1(c) of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to obtain from the data controller the erasure, without undue delay, of personal data concerning him or her when (…) c) the data subject objects to the processing pursuant to Article 21, paragraph 1, and there are no overriding legitimate grounds for the processing, or the data subject objects to the processing pursuant to Article 21.2 of the GDPR. The data controller is obliged to erase such personal data without undue delay when this ground applies.  
26.  
However, the right to the protection of personal data is not an absolute right; it must be considered in relation to its function in society and balanced against other fundamental rights. 27.  
Article 17.3. a) of the GDPR provides that Article 17.1 does not apply insofar as the processing is necessary for the exercise of the right to freedom of expression and information, thereby establishing, in the very terms of Article 17 of the GDPR, an exception regime that involves a balancing of interests between the fundamental right to freedom of expression and information on the one hand and the fundamental right to the protection of personal data on the other. In its judgment "Google Spain" of May 13, 2014, the CJEU states that, as a general rule, the rights of the data subject enshrined in Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (namely, the right to privacy and the protection of personal data) prevail. Nevertheless, "this balance may, however, depend, in particular cases, on the nature of the information in question and its sensitivity for the privacy of the data subject as well as the public interest in having access to that information, which may vary, in particular, depending on the role played by that person in public life."  

28.  
For its part, the European Court of Human Rights (hereinafter "ECHR") has also developed over the course of its case law a series of relevant criteria that must be taken into consideration in order to balance these competing fundamental rights, namely the contribution to a public interest debate, the notoriety of the person concerned, the subject matter of the reporting, the prior behavior of the data subject, the content, form, and repercussions of the publication, the manner and circumstances in which the information was obtained, as well as its veracity. In its recent judgment "Buivids," the CJEU directly referred to these criteria established by the ECHR.  

29.  
Furthermore, the ECHR has consistently emphasized in its case law the indispensable role of the press and other "public watchdogs" in a rule of law state. Indeed, as the Court frequently reminds, the primary function of the press is to serve as a vehicle for the dissemination of public interest debates, but it also fulfills a secondary role, that of revealing and bringing to the public's attention information likely to generate interest and foster such debate within society. Consequently, the ECHR warns that the utmost caution should be exercised.

II.3. Additional Considerations  
47. The Contentious Chamber is aware that the defendant, in support of its refusal to respond to the exercise of the complainant's right to object, invoked Article 24.2 of the LTD implemented pursuant to Article 85.2 of the GDPR. The defendant indeed considers that the disputed processing benefits from the exception for processing for journalistic purposes provided by this provision of Belgian law and therefore does not need to justify any "overriding legitimate interest" to maintain the disputed processing carried out on the basis of Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR.  
48. Article 85 of the GDPR precisely offers Member States the possibility to reconcile, through their national law, different fundamental rights such as the right to the protection of personal data, on the one hand, and the right to freedom of expression and information, on the other hand. Member States are free to provide for a specific regime in their national law applicable to the processing of personal data for journalistic purposes or freedom of expression, in the form of exemptions or derogations from certain rules of the GDPR. For example, data controllers for journalistic purposes may be exempted from their obligation to provide prior information to the data subjects (Chapter III of the GDPR).  
49. Under Belgian law, Article 24 of the LTD was implemented pursuant to Article 85 of the GDPR. This provision defines in its first paragraph the notion of processing carried out for journalistic purposes in the following terms:  
"Processing of personal data for journalistic purposes means the preparation, collection, writing, production, dissemination, or archiving for the purpose of informing the public, using any media, and where the data controller is subject to the rules of journalistic ethics" (The Contentious Chamber emphasizes).  
50. This provision of Belgian law conditions the invocation of the journalistic exception on the consideration of ethical rules and would therefore, in principle, fall within a context of professional journalism. It proposes a definition of journalistic purpose that is certainly more restrictive than that envisaged by the GDPR and the case law of the CJEU and the European Court of Human Rights (points 31 to 35).  
51. The Contentious Chamber recalls that Article 24.2 of the LTD provides that in the case of processing for journalistic purposes, Articles 7 to 10, 11.2, 13 to 16, 18 to 20, and 21.1 of the GDPR do not apply. However, Article 85.2 of the GDPR stipulates that such derogations or exemptions are only possible to the extent that they are "necessary" to reconcile the right to the protection of personal data and the freedom of expression and information.  
52. In this context, the CJEU stated in the aforementioned "Buivids" judgment:  
"It should be noted that the exemptions and derogations provided for in Article 9 of Directive 95/46 (mutatis mutandis similar to Article 85.2 of the GDPR) must be applied only to the extent that they are necessary to reconcile two fundamental rights, namely the right to respect for private life and the right to freedom of expression" (The Contentious Chamber emphasizes).  
53. Following this CJEU ruling, a Belgian legislative proposal aimed to amend Article 24 of the LTD. This legislative proposal intended, in particular, to insert a new paragraph specifying that the exemptions provided by Article 24 of the LTD must meet the necessity criterion. The Knowledge Center of the APD issued an opinion in this regard stating:  
"The Authority shares, of course, the observation made by the developments of the legislative proposal that the automatic nature of the exemptions granted to the data controller who processes data for journalistic purposes is not compliant with Article 85 of the GDPR. It is indeed necessary to review Article 24 of the LTD to eliminate the automatic derogations and exemptions it contains. The legislator must ensure that the regulation grants, in compliance with the principle of foreseeability, only those derogations and exemptions that are actually necessary to achieve a fair balance between the right to data protection and freedom of expression."
Draft decision of June 20, 2024, pursuant to Article 60.3 of the GDPR – 19/22  
in  
particular  
when it  
is

the

of

54. Therefore, in line with previous decisions61, the Contentious Chamber questions the compliance of the current Article 24 of the LTD with the GDPR. While this article provides for automatic exemptions, Article 85 of the GDPR and the case law of the CJEU require a balancing exercise between the two fundamental rights of freedom of information and expression and the right to data protection, which must be carried out in concreto. In this regard, the Contentious Chamber could, in support of the case law of the CJEU, decide to set aside Article 24 of the LTD if it deemed it non-compliant with the GDPR62. As the CJEU has ruled repeatedly, the duty to disapply national legislation that is contrary to Union law falls not only on national courts but also on all state bodies, including administrative authorities, tasked with applying Union law within their respective competencies63.  
55. Notwithstanding the above, the Contentious Chamber notes that Article 17 of the GDPR is not among the rights of the data subject that the Belgian legislator purely and simply excludes from exercise in the case of processing for journalistic purposes under the said Article 24.2 of the LTD. Thus, Article 17.1.c) of the GDPR invoked by the complainant, even if it follows from the exercise of a right of opposition (Article 21.1 of the GDPR, which is indeed included in the list of excluded rights), would therefore fully apply, albeit within the limits of Article 17.3.a) of the GDPR. Since Article 17 of the GDPR is not mentioned in Article 24.2 of the LTD, it cannot be affected by the fact that Article 21.1 of the GDPR is, on the other hand, impacted.  
56. However, the considerations above remain superfluous since, in this case, the Contentious Chamber concluded that, pursuant to Article 17.3.a) of the GDPR, the right to freedom of expression and information must prevail and that the defendant rightly refused (and continues to refuse) to comply favorably with the complainant's request for erasure.
