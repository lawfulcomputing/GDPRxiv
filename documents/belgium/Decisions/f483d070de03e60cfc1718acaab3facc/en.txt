1.  
The subject of the complaint concerns the alleged unlawful processing and commercialization of personal data of the complainants by the former NV BISNODE BELGIUM, now known as “BLACK TIGER BELGIUM”1.  
2.  
BISNODE BELGIUM claims to be a direct marketing and big data specialist that has been active in the B2B market in Belgium for several decades. For several years, this company was active in the field of data broking (broker in personal data), whereby BISNODE BELGIUM purchased data from sources and processed this data on behalf of its clients, who themselves carried out direct marketing activities, either for businesses or for individuals and consumers2.  
3.  
On October 23, 2020, and on November 27, 2020, the two complainants, each separately, submitted a request to the respondent, exercising their right of access in accordance with Article 15 GDPR.  
4.  
On November 13, 2020, and December 23, 2020, they each received a response to their request. Both responses were sent by regular mail by the respondent and provided further explanation regarding the various files in which the personal data of the complainants may or may not be included, as well as a summary of the categories of personal data held by the respondent, the purposes of processing, and the legal basis (Article 6.1.f) GDPR). Furthermore, the respondent provided a list “of the (potentially) involved sectors” within which companies may be active that could potentially receive personal data of the complainants from the respondent. The respondent then clarified that the personal data of the complainants would be retained for 15 years from the last registration in its database, and mentioned the sources of the personal data in the consumer file [the company Z8] respectively the business file (Belgian Official Gazette and Crossroads Bank for Enterprises). The respondent also emphasized that it does not use automated decision-making, but assesses the so-called “marketing potential” of data subjects to create a marketing segmentation profile for each user. The respondent also stated that it, or “one of its clients,” may transfer the personal data of the complainants “in certain cases” to countries outside the EEA. Finally, the respondent reminded the complainants of the possibility to exercise their other rights and to consult more information regarding this on the website www.bisnodeenu.be as well as the possibility to file a complaint with the Data Protection Authority.

II. Justification  
II.1. Jurisdiction of the Belgian Data Protection Authority  
36. Between 2007 and 2020, BISNODE BELGIUM was part of [the company Z3], hereinafter referred to as ‘[Z3]’, an international holding consisting of entities primarily in Northern and Eastern Europe. On October 8, 2020, the Swedish private equity firm [Z4], also the majority shareholder (70%) of BISNODE AB, announced the sale to [the company Z5] of all its shares in the holding BISNODE AB, except for the operational activities of BISNODE BELGIUM.  
37.  
The Dispute Chamber notes that BISNODE BELGIUM was acquired on March 31, 2021, by the French company [Z1] — now [Z2]9 — operating under the commercial name BLACK TIGER GROUP. As a result of the acquisition, the name BISNODE BELGIUM was changed to BLACK TIGER BELGIUM on June 15, 2021.

38. The Dispute Chamber rules that this acquisition of BISNODE BELGIUM by the French group BLACK TIGER and the name change to BLACK TIGER BELGIUM do not impact the jurisdiction of the GBA regarding the alleged violations of the GDPR, for the following reasons.  
39. First, the Dispute Chamber points out that the original complaint was directed against BISNODE BELGIUM NV, established in Belgium, and that the website https://bisnodeandyou.be, which is no longer accessible, explicitly mentioned BISNODE BELGIUM as the data controller for the processing of personal data as of March 31, 2021.10  
 
40. Pursuant to Article 55 GDPR, each supervisory authority has the competence to carry out the tasks and exercise the powers assigned or conferred upon it by the GDPR within the territory of its Member State. It follows that the Belgian Data Protection Authority was competent at the time of the complaint regarding the processing activities carried out by BISNODE BELGIUM. The fact that BISNODE BELGIUM was still owned by the Swedish publicly traded group [Z4]11 until March 31, 2021, cannot lead to a lack of jurisdiction on the part of the GBA. The respondent's assertion that BISNODE BELGIUM had only “limited leeway to determine […] its own strategies and policies regarding personal data”12 does not convince the Dispute Chamber either. Indeed, after the transfer of the parent company BISNODE AB to [the company Z5] in October 2020, BISNODE BELGIUM could no longer be considered bound by the former policy choices imposed by BISNODE AB.  
41.  
That the respondent was taken over by a French group at the time of the investigation does not lead to the conclusion that the GBA would not be competent for the processing of personal data until the aforementioned date of acquisition. In this case, the Dispute Chamber finds that the processing activities of the respondent, including the various websites managed by it, are aimed at a Belgian target audience and

II.2. Description of the disputed processing activities by the respondent  
44. Based on the submitted documents, the Dispute Chamber understands that the  
respondent had three different databases containing personal data under its  
management at the time of the complaints:  
i.  
the consumer database Consu-Matrix (hereinafter, “CMX”), which contains  
personal data of consumers that the respondent has collected from various  
external sources (“source partners”). These sources provide their own  
customer databases to BISNODE BELGIUM for commercialization for direct  
marketing purposes. CMX is a B2C information database intended for  
marketing, analysis, profiling, statistics, verification and control  
purposes (data quality), as well as for reference and “other” purposes.  
ii.  
the business database Spectron, which contains company and contact  
information of Belgian companies that the respondent has obtained  
through public/government sources (Crossroads Bank for Enterprises,  
National Bank of Belgium) as well as through commercial data sources.  
Spectron is a B2B information database intended for  
marketing profiling, analysis, credit purposes, statistics,  
verification and control purposes (data quality), as well as for  
reference and “other” purposes.  
iii.  
the direct marketing database Permesso, which contains personal data of  
“Permesso members” that the respondent has collected via an online  
marketing platform. Personal data included in Permesso are intended for  
marketing and direct marketing purposes (marketing, analysis, profiling  
and statistics).  
45. Unlike Permesso, which contains personal data that has been directly collected  
from the data subjects for direct marketing purposes, based on the consent  
of the data subjects (Article 6.1.a) GDPR), CMX and Spectron are  
exclusively supplemented with personal data that has been indirectly  
collected, based on the legitimate interest of the respondent and its  
clients (Article 6.1.f) GDPR).  
II.2.1. Data Controller  
46. A data controller is defined as “a natural or legal person, a public authority,  
agency or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes  
and means of the processing of personal data” (Article 4.7) GDPR). It is an  
autonomous concept, specific to data protection legislation, which must be  
assessed based on the criteria established therein: the determination of the  
purposes of the relevant data processing and the means for that processing.

48. Given the information available, the Dispute Chamber considers it sufficiently proven that BISNODE BELGIUM acted as the data controller for the aforementioned processing activities at the time of the complaints.

101. In addition to the existence of a legitimate interest, the data controller must also demonstrate the necessity of the processing for that interest before being able to invoke Article 6.1.f) GDPR. Balancing Test  
118. In order to rely on Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR, the data controller must finally weigh the interests and demonstrate that its own interests—or those of third parties—outweigh the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  
119. The Dispute Chamber reminds that the balancing test does not aim to prevent any effect on the interests and rights of the data subjects, but to prevent disproportionate consequences as well as to assess the relative weight of these interests. The fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subjects referred to in Article 6.1.f) GDPR include not only the right to data protection and privacy but also other fundamental rights, such as the right to freedom and security, freedom of expression and information, freedom of thought, conscience and religion, freedom of assembly and association, the prohibition of discrimination, property rights, or the right to physical and mental integrity, which may be directly or indirectly affected by the processing.  
The wording of Article 6.1.f) GDPR also assumes that besides the fundamental rights of data subjects, other interests are taken into account, such as social, financial, or personal interests. In short, a data controller is expected to consider all relevant interests that may be affected by the data processing, including—but not limited to—

II.4.3. Opinion of the Dispute Chamber  
150. Regarding the legality of the disputed processing, it is clear that the reasonable expectations of the data subjects are indeed relevant — contrary to what the respondent argues in his defense — to assess the extent to which there may be lawful data processing based on the legitimate interests of the respondent or his clients. These reasonable expectations must also be taken into account when determining the timing of when information about the processing is communicated to the data subjects, considering the general principle that data subjects should not be surprised by the purpose of the processing of their personal data. The Dispute Chamber has repeatedly emphasized in previous decisions that transparency is crucial to give data subjects control over their personal data and to ensure effective protection of personal data. The transparency obligation in the GDPR requires that all information or communication regarding the data processing of data subjects must be easily accessible and understandable, but also must be provided to the data subjects in a timely manner.

152. Pursuant to Article 14.3 GDPR, which specifically relates to the modalities of information provision and thus constitutes an inherent supplement to the core obligations arising from the two preceding paragraphs of Article 14 GDPR, the aforementioned information must be communicated to the data subjects within certain timeframes. Generally, the rule is that the data controller must inform the data subjects within a reasonable time but no later than one month after obtaining their personal data, depending on the specific circumstances (14.3.a) GDPR). According to the guidelines on transparency under the GDPR, this timeframe may, however, be shortened to the extent that the collected personal data are intended for contacting the data subjects, in which case the information must be provided at the time of the first contact with the data subject (14.3.b) GDPR). Finally, the one-month period may also be shortened if the personal data are communicated to a recipient within the meaning of Article 4.9) GDPR. In such circumstances, the data subjects must be informed no later than at the time their personal data are provided (14.3.c) GDPR).  
153. In its defenses, BLACK TIGER BELGIUM asserts that the data subjects were informed based on the mandatory mentions of the name of the defendant in the advertising messages directed at the data subjects. This concretely means that the data subjects were informed of the existence of the defendant, as well as the fact that the defendant may have collected and processed their personal data, only for the first time through the — often unsolicited — advertising messages. The Disputes Chamber assumes that such an approach aims to comply with the timing provided under 14.3.b) GDPR, which is to inform the data subjects at the moment of effective contact with the data subject, without necessarily taking into account the time period between the initial data collection and the first contact. Nevertheless, the guidelines on transparency under the GDPR explicitly state that the one-month period provided under Article 14.3.a) GDPR is a maximum period, which cannot be extended but can only be shortened depending on the purposes of the processing.

195. The Dispute Chamber finds that the submitted register of processing activities is limited to the mention of the categories of data subjects, without further describing these categories. Since the requirement to actually describe the relevant categories is also present in the English and French translations of the GDPR, the Dispute Chamber concludes that there can be no doubt about the scope of this provision. As previously raised in this decision, the descriptions of the categories of personal data processed by the defendant vary depending on the policy document referenced. It is, however, of utmost importance that each data controller clearly defines for itself which personal data is precisely processed under its supervision and documents this in the register of processing activities, as required under the accountability obligation (Article 5.2 in conjunction with Article 24 GDPR). An appropriate level of granularity in the internal register of processing activities is all the more important as the information that data controllers must provide to data subjects under Articles 13 and 14 GDPR is limited to the relevant categories of personal data. However, as soon as a data subject exercises their right of access, the data controller must, in accordance with the guidelines of the EDPB, nonetheless provide a complete picture of the processed personal data to the requester, including the specific personal data concerning them that the data controller actually processes. Consequently, the Dispute Chamber finds the violation of Article 30.1.c) GDPR by the defendant to be proven.

i.  
Violation of Article 5 GDPR; Article 6 GDPR; Article 12 GDPR; Article 14 GDPR; Article 24 GDPR and Article 25 GDPR — The Dispute Chamber finds that the respondent indirectly collected personal data from the parties involved on a large scale and for a period of at least 15 years, without providing individual information to the parties from whom the respondent nonetheless had contact details, for both Data Delivery and Data Quality services.  
The processing activities associated with these services are, in fact, in violation of Article 5.1 GDPR, and more specifically, the principle of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency (5.1.a) GDPR), the principle of data minimization (5.1.c) GDPR), and the principle of storage limitation (5.1.e) GDPR). ii.

Pursuant to Article 58.2(d) GDPR as well as Article 100, § 1, 8° and 9° WOG, order the respondent to cease and refrain from the violation of Article 5.1 GDPR, Article 6.1 GDPR, Article 12.1 GDPR, as well as Article 14.1 and 14.2 GDPR, in the context of the B2B Data Quality services, until the processing is brought into compliance with the GDPR, by halting the processing of personal data in the Spectron database, before proactively and individually informing the data subjects whose contact details the respondent possesses about the processing of their personal data by the respondent. The respondent must also provide the data subjects with the opportunity to object to the processing of their personal data in a simple and effective manner for a period of three months from the provision of information, before resuming the processing. Regarding the other categories of data subjects, for whom the respondent does not possess contact details, the Dispute Chamber decides, due to the lack of a lawful basis for processing, to prohibit the processing of their personal data definitively.  
-  
Pursuant to Article 58.2(d) GDPR as well as Article 100, § 1, 9° WOG, order the respondent to remedy the violation of Article 5.1 and 5.2 GDPR, Article 24.1 GDPR as well as Article 25.1 and 25.2 GDPR by taking appropriate technical and organizational measures so that the retention period of the personal data — which the respondent may only further process on the condition that the previous order has been complied with — is proportionate to the purposes of processing, and so that the respondent, in the context of its current Data Quality services, only retains the most up-to-date personal data of data subjects, as required by the principle of data minimization. Additionally, the Dispute Chamber orders the respondent to supplement or adjust the current documentation regarding the processing of personal data and compliance with the GDPR, in order to take into account the actual circumstances under which the respondent processes personal data and thereby fulfill the accountability obligation that rests on the respondent.
