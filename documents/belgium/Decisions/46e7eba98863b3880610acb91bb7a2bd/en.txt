41. The promulgation of any regulatory provisions regarding personal data by the federal authority or a federated authority must therefore take place within the framework defined by the GDPR. In this regard, the Contentious Chamber refers to Article 22 of the Constitution and the consistent jurisprudence of the Constitutional Court in this matter, which specifies that the right to respect for private life, as guaranteed by Article 22 of the Constitution (as well as in treaties), has a broad scope and includes, in particular, the protection of personal data and personal information.17  
42. Regarding the right to respect for private life, Article 22 of the Constitution states the following:  
“Everyone has the right to respect for their private and family life, except in cases and conditions provided for by law.  
The law, decree, or regulation referred to in Article 134 guarantees the protection of this right."  
43. Given that Article 22 of the Constitution is subsequent to the state reform of 1980, the term "law" in this provision refers to a federal law. Limitations on the rights guaranteed by this constitutional provision cannot, in principle, be established by a decree or an order. This would mean that an interference in private life – including the processing of personal data – cannot result from decrees or orders.18  
44. Since such an interpretation would undermine the powers of the communities and regions, the Constitutional Court and the legislative section of the Council of State, in particular, have deemed that the establishment of general limitations is a matter reserved for the federal legislator. The federated entities retain, in this case, the possibility, within the framework of their competencies, to provide for specific limitations, provided that they respect the general federal framework.19

51. In light of the above, the Contentious Chamber concludes that for a supervisory authority of a federated entity to be competent, it is far from sufficient that the processing of data concerns a matter of a federated entity, in this case, the matter of supplementary traffic regulations. Furthermore, the federated entity in question must also have enacted specific rules for the processing of personal data within the framework of this matter, within the margin left by the GDPR to Member States. Only the oversight of compliance with these specific rules adopted by the federated entities can be entrusted to the supervisory authority of the federated entity.

52. The Contentious Chamber emphasizes that the notion of 'specific rules' cannot be subject to an overly broad interpretation. It follows from the cited opinion of the Council of State that the notion of 'specific rules' refers to specific limitations or particular guarantees that deviate from or go beyond the general provisions, guarantees, and limitations set forth in or arising from the GDPR or federal legislation. In other words, the mere fact that federated entities implement or confirm a general rule (by decree or order) does not mean that this rule is granted the status of a 'specific rule.' A specific rule is only present when federated entities establish additional guarantees or limitations using the margin left for this purpose by the GDPR.

53. Additionally, any potential limitations on the powers of a data protection authority under the GDPR would only be possible if a supervisory authority that meets all the requirements imposed on supervisory authorities under the European Treaties and has been entrusted with all the missions and powers of a supervisory authority were created at the level of a federated entity. In this regard, reference should primarily be made to Articles 51 to 59 inclusive of the GDPR.

In this sense, the federal chamber of the Information Security Committee also expressly informed Defendant 1 on August 28, 2020, that its adherence to deliberation AF No. 14/2016 did not exempt it from its obligations to comply with the GDPR.

b) Regarding Defendant 1 and Defendant 2  
97. A data controller is required to comply with data protection principles and must be able to demonstrate that these are respected (principle of accountability – Article 5.2 of the GDPR). Defendant 1 is a data controller regarding the personal data it requests and obtains from Defendant 2. Defendant 2 is also a data controller regarding the personal data it provides to Defendant 1. Although Defendant 2 denies being a party defendant because the complaint is directed solely against Defendant 1, there is no doubt that the complainant is targeting not only Defendant 1 but also Defendant 2, given the finding that the complainant expressly states that there has been a violation of his privacy because both Defendant 1 and Defendant 2 used an erroneous authorization as the basis for identifying him through his license plate via the DIV directory for which Defendant 2 is the data controller. There is no doubt that the complainant is targeting not only Defendant 1 but also Defendant 2, since the identification of the license plate holder is only possible if Defendant 2 provides the necessary personal data to Defendant 1. In other words: the identification of the complainant through his license plate is only possible if Defendant 1 requests the identification data from Defendant 2 and Defendant 2 subsequently also provides this identification data to Defendant 1. If Defendant 2 had not provided Defendant 1 with the complainant's identification data, it would simply not have been possible to identify the complainant based on his license plate. As evidenced by the documents in the file, the complainant has addressed the defendant 2 several times. The complaint particularly concerns the identification by the license plate of the vehicle registered in the name of the complainant and thus indisputably targets defendant 1 and defendant 2.

98. Both defendants base their conclusions on Article 6.1 e) of the GDPR for the processing of data carried out by each of them (defendant 1 regarding the processing of data requested and obtained from defendant 2; defendant 2 regarding the communication of data to defendant 1). Article 6.1 of the GDPR, which embodies the principle of lawfulness referred to in Article 5.1 a) of the GDPR, requires that all processing be based on a legal basis. This means that before commencing processing activities, the data controller must determine which of the six legal grounds applies and for what specific purpose. It does not appear from the file that the complainant was informed of the legal basis on which the defendants are now relying in the proceedings before the Litigation Chamber, namely that the processing is necessary for "the performance of a task carried out in the public interest" (Article 6.1 e) of the GDPR). This legal basis is only invoked after the facts and therefore after the processing of personal data has taken place. Consequently, the defendants processed the complainant's personal data against his expectations and, therefore, without any information being provided by the defendants prior to the processing of the data. In this regard, the Litigation Chamber notes that the communication of information concerns not only the legal basis (Article 14.1 c) of the GDPR) but all the information provided for in Article 14.1 of the GDPR in order to comply with the principle of transparency (Article 5.1 a) of the GDPR).

99. Both defendants simply relied on the deliberations granted by the Sectoral Committee for the Federal Authority without any examination of the requirements imposed by the GDPR since its entry into force.

100. Based on the factual elements of the file, it appears that neither defendant 1 nor defendant 2 has assumed its responsibility regarding the principles of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency, which leads the Litigation Chamber to conclude that at the time of the facts, a violation of Articles 5.2 and 24 of the GDPR was committed by both defendants.

101. Regarding the aforementioned violations of the legal basis, the obligation of transparency, and the principle of accountability, the Litigation Chamber takes into account the circumstances invoked by defendant 1, with the determining factor being that the current privacy statement of defendant 1 includes a complete list of the authorizations on the basis of which defendant 1 can consult personal data from defendant 2, particularly the adherence to deliberation AF No. 14/2016. The invoices for the collection of parking fees have since been completed and now also include information regarding the processing of personal data. Thus, defendant 1 has demonstrated that it pays the necessary attention to the communication of information, particularly regarding the legal basis and the identity of the data controller, making every effort to avoid such violations of the GDPR in the future. This element is therefore decisive regarding the sanction that the Litigation Chamber imposes in this decision.

102. Defendant 2 also refers to its privacy statement, which includes information regarding the legal basis and the identity of the data controller. Although defendant 2 had informed the complainant by email dated February 15, 2021, that at the time of the facts, there was no protocol in accordance with Article 20 of the law of July 30, 2018, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and that defendant 1 was also not a beneficiary of deliberation AF No. 14/2016, thus there was no legal basis, defendant 2 now rejects this point as "legally incorrect." Defendant 2 continues to argue that at the time of the facts, defendant 2, just like defendant 1, had automatically assumed all rights and obligations of RCA Parko. Given the Litigation Chamber's perspective on this matter, as outlined in this decision, this reasoning cannot be followed. However, in the current context where the situation has been clarified between defendant 1 and defendant 2 to avoid the repetition of similar facts, the Litigation Chamber also takes this into account when determining the sanction it must impose.
