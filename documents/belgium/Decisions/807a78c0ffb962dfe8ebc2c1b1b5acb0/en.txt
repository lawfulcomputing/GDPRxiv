I. Facts and procedure  
1.  
On February 10, 2020, the complainant filed a complaint with the Data Protection Authority against the defendant.  
2.  
The subject of the complaint concerns the use of the national register identification number in the defendant's online appointment scheduling system and the related technical and organizational (security) measures.  
3.  
On March 5, 2020, the complaint was declared admissible by the Frontline Service based on Articles 58 and 60 of the LCA, and the complaint was forwarded to the Contentious Chamber under Article 62, § 1 of the LCA.  
4.

5.

1. Violation of Article 30.2 of the GDPR due to an incomplete and inaccurate record of processing activities;  
2. Violation of Article 32.1 of the GDPR due to the absence of appropriate technical and organizational measures, and violation of Article 32 of the GDPR due to insufficient cooperation with the Inspection Service's investigation;  
3. No violation of Article 28.3 of the GDPR following the presentation of subcontracting contracts by the defendant;  
4. Violation of Article 37.1 of the GDPR due to the absence of the designation of a data protection officer;  
5. Processing of the national register number by the defendant on behalf of its clients.

b) Standing of the Defendant  
27.  
In order to determine the obligations incumbent upon the defendant under the GDPR, it is necessary to first ascertain in what capacity the defendant is involved in relation to the processing of data that is the subject of the complaint. The crucial point in this regard is to determine whether the defendant acts as a data controller (Article 4.7) of the GDPR4) or as a processor (Article 4.8) of the GDPR5).  
28.  
The inspection report specifies that the defendant was the subject of the investigation in its capacity as a processor concerning the personal data of clients for whom it processes personal data. The defendant also argues that it only acts as a processor, not as a data controller. The Contentious Chamber verifies whether the premise that the defendant should only be regarded as a processor corresponds to the (broad) interpretation of the notion of data controller by the Court of Justice6 and the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), particularly in the Guidelines 07/2020 regarding the notion of data controller.  
29.  
The GDPR defines a "data controller" as an entity that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data7. This definition must be understood in light of the legislator's purpose of attributing primary responsibility for the protection of personal data to the entity that actually exercises control over the processing.

33. The determining factors for establishing whether data processing occurs as a data controller or as a processor are the purposes and means, more specifically the extent to which there is decision-making power in this regard.  
34. In concrete terms, the data processing in this case is carried out by the defendant using software that consists of an appointment scheduling system based on Internet technology. To assess the defendant's impact on the purpose and means, it is first necessary to understand how this application works. It operates as follows:  
a. The patient browses the medical practice's website or uses the application;  
b. The patient identifies themselves in the online calendar system. The application user, in this case the healthcare provider, chooses the patient's connection method, namely via 1) a username and password; 2) name, surname, and date of birth; 3) e-ID; 4) national registry number; or 5) a social media account;  
c. The patient selects the doctor with whom they wish to make an appointment;  
d. The patient optionally selects a type of appointment;  
e. The patient chooses the desired time slot;  
f. The patient optionally adds additional remarks;  
g. The patient confirms their appointment.  
35. The defendant then checks whether the relevant personal data of the concerned patient is present in the healthcare provider's database. If so, the patient can make an appointment. If not, either the patient can register manually, or access is denied if the healthcare provider does not wish to accept new patients. The investigation conducted by the Inspection Service established that the defendant does not transfer any personal data of patients to the mentioned social media managers.  
36. Based on the elements of this case, the Contentious Chamber finds that the purpose pursued is to have an online scheduling system that allows for making appointments and receiving appointment reminders, exchanging data with other applications, and making appointments in a fully automated manner by phone. 2° Data Protection Officer  
49. The report of the Inspection Service indicates that, contrary to what the defendant claims, a data protection officer should have been appointed. According to the Inspection Service, the defendant processes personal data of patients on a large scale, including health data, and the online appointment system requires regular and systematic monitoring of the appointments that these patients make with their doctor, which obliges the defendant to appoint a data protection officer, in accordance with Article 37.1 of the GDPR.  
50. The defendant refutes the regular and systematic large-scale monitoring of the data subjects (Article 37.1.b) of the GDPR) by arguing that it is not established that appointments are regularly made through the defendant's software application, given that the patient is not required to use it. The patient is indeed free to attend the consultation without an appointment or to make an appointment by phone, without any intervention from the defendant. It is also possible, according to the defendant, that a patient consults a doctor a maximum of 1 or 2 times a year, so that regularity cannot be questioned either.  
51. This analysis is relevant, in light of Article 37.1.b) of the GDPR which requires regular and systematic monitoring. The concepts of "regular" and "systematic" are not defined in the GDPR, but an interpretation is provided in the Guidelines on Data Protection Officers19. The possible interpretation given to the notion of "regular" is:  
• continuous or occurring at regular intervals over a given period;  
• recurring or repeating at fixed times;  
• taking place consistently or periodically.  
The notion of "systematic" is defined as follows:

• occurring in accordance with a system;  
• pre-established, organized, or methodical;  
• taking place within the framework of a general data collection program;  
• carried out as part of a strategy.  
52. The Contentious Chamber deduces from the examples provided in the aforementioned Guidelines of processing that may constitute regular and systematic monitoring of the data subjects20 that behavioral monitoring is not isolated, but is always accompanied by a specific purpose. According to the Contentious Chamber, the recording of an appointment between a healthcare provider and a patient is distinguished in that no other purpose is pursued other than the mere scheduling of an appointment, which is completely unrelated to any monitoring of the behavior of the concerned patient. The data obtained is exclusively used to set an appointment. There is no indication that this same data is analyzed to associate it with any consequence or purpose, so it cannot be concluded that it aims to subject the concerned patients to any form of regular or systematic monitoring.  
53. Regarding the large-scale nature of the processing of specific categories of personal data within the meaning of Article 9 of the GDPR, the defendant emphasizes both in the reply conclusions and during the hearing that there is no management of health data files, but that the application merely enables the scheduling of an appointment, which at most allows for the deduction that the concerned person may be ill. The large-scale nature of the data processing is also refuted by the defendant, who points out that the data processing in question is distinguished from the data processing by a hospital or an insurance organization, which must indeed be qualified as being on a large scale. Viewed from this angle, the defendant believes that the appointment of a data protection officer is therefore not required.  
54. In order to determine whether the defendant must have a data protection officer or not, the Contentious Chamber examined the elements brought to its attention in the context of this case in light of the Guidelines on data protection officers21. Given that according to Article 37.1 b) and c) of the GDPR, data processing must be carried out "on a large scale," but that the interpretation

As stated in Recital 91 of the GDPR, the Guidelines regarding data protection officers also indicate that the processing of personal data cannot be considered large scale if it involves the processing of personal data of patients or clients by a doctor or another healthcare professional acting on an individual basis. According to Recital 91 of the GDPR, such processing should therefore not be regarded as large scale in the sense of processing aimed at handling a considerable volume of personal data at the regional, national, or supranational level, which may affect a significant number of data subjects and is likely to result in a high risk, for example, due to their sensitive nature, when, in accordance with the state of technological knowledge, a new technique is applied on a large scale, or in the sense of other processing operations that pose a high risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects, particularly when, due to these operations, it becomes more difficult for these individuals to exercise their rights. In the present case, the Contentious Chamber also takes into account the fact that, as a subcontractor, the defendant processes data on behalf of the healthcare provider. The fact that the healthcare provider entrusts data processing to a subcontractor does not affect the absence of "large scale" character of the processing. Furthermore, the defendant only processes the patient identification data that are relevant to schedule an appointment in the healthcare provider's agenda and that ensure unique identification without any associated management of health data records. The mere inference from scheduling an appointment with a healthcare provider that a patient may be ill is not sufficient to classify this processing, whose purpose is limited to making an appointment, as risky.  
56. Therefore, the Contentious Chamber considers that no violation of Article 37.1 of the GDPR has been committed.

d) Use of the National Register number and security measures  
57.  
Regarding the use of the National Register number by the defendant, the Inspection Service's report merely states that the use or non-use of the National Register number in the defendant's online appointment system depends on the choice made in this regard by the client, namely the healthcare provider or the defendant's healthcare provider office, since it is the client who chooses the patient's connection method to the system. The Inspection Service does not find any infringement concerning the use of the National Register number but limits itself to noting that the defendant processes the National Register number on behalf of its clients.  
58. The Disciplinary Chamber considers that the Inspection Service's finding that the client chooses the connection method for the patient, and that this choice does not fall to the defendant, confirms that the client, namely the healthcare provider, is the data controller (see above point 'b) Quality of the defendant'), and that the latter must have the authorization to use the National Register number if it decides that the patient can connect using their National Register number. The defendant can therefore apply this connection method and use this number only to the extent that the healthcare provider is itself authorized to use the National Register number in accordance with Article 8 of the law of August 8, 1983, organizing a national register of natural persons. This is possible based on legislation that provides for the use of the National Register number or based on an authorization to use this number. As necessary, the Disciplinary Chamber recalls that a deliberation granting authorization must be considered a more specific provision in national law within the meaning of Articles 6.2 and 6.3 of the GDPR, which thus has legal significance.  
59. Although this point is not the subject of the complaint and is therefore not at issue, it is indeed essential to determine to what extent and under what conditions the defendant can process the National Register number. The Disciplinary Chamber draws attention to the fact that healthcare providers with whom the patient has a therapeutic relationship are authorized to use the National Register number of the patient concerned to, among other things, access the overall medical record. The National Register number constitutes, in this regard, the key to unique identification, the importance of which must be emphasized, given the patient's health, which is at the heart of the process, and any risk of identity confusion must be excluded. Based on the above, the Disciplinary Chamber concludes that the defendant, who processes the National Register number in its database in the form of its hash, on behalf of the data controller, namely the healthcare provider, does so lawfully to the extent that it meets all obligations imposed by the GDPR on a processor, including the conclusion
