I. Facts and procedure  
1. On September 8, 2019, the complainant submitted a request for mediation, which was transformed into a complaint on December 24, 2019, to the Data Protection Authority against the defendant.  
2. The subject of the request concerns an unsatisfactory response to a request for access as well as a lack of sufficient information.  
3. The complainant is the mother of a student who attended the defendant's school.  
4. On June 1, 2019, the complainant exercised her right of access with the defendant and specifically requested: the data held by the defendant concerning the complainant and her daughter; the purposes pursued in the context of the processing of this data; and, finally, the retention period of this data. Furthermore, she requested – in the event that photos of her daughter taken during the school trip had been or would be published on Facebook – their deletion.  
5. On June 2, 2019, the defendant responded, regarding the access request, that it is subject to the control of an ad hoc person and that it must refer only to this person concerning the control of personal data processing. Regarding the request concerning the photos, it responded that at the beginning of the school year, a document related to the "right to image" was submitted to the parents, binding them for the entire school year.  
6. On August 26, 2019, the complainant wrote to the defendant that she was still waiting for a response. On the same day, the defendant replied that it had already responded on June 2, 2019. Still on the same day, the complainant indicated that she intended to contact the DPA regarding this matter.  
7. On September 8, 2019, the complainant submitted a request for mediation to the DPA.  
8. On October 3, 2019, the First Line Service declared the mediation request admissible.  
9. On December 24, 2019, the complainant decided to transform her mediation request into a complaint.  
10. On January 6, 2020, the complaint was declared admissible by the First Line Service based on Articles 58 and 60 of the LCA, and the complaint was forwarded to the Contentious Chamber under Article 62, § 1 of the LCA.  
11. On January 27, 2020, in accordance with Article 96, § 1 of the LCA, the request from the Contentious Chamber to conduct an investigation was transmitted to the Inspection Service, along with the complaint and the inventory of documents.

II. Motivation  
II.1. Regarding the breach of Articles 12.1, 15.1, and 15.3 of the GDPR  
22.  
It appears from the file that the complainant exercised her right of access in writing on June 1, 2019.  
23.  
The right of access has three components. First, under Article 15.1 of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to obtain from the data controller confirmation as to whether or not personal data concerning her are being processed. Second, when personal data is being processed, the data subject has the right to obtain access to such personal data as well as to a
Decision on the merits 86/2024 — 5/9  
series of information listed in Article 15.1. a) – h). Thirdly, under Article 15.3 of the GDPR, the data subject also has the right to obtain a copy of the personal data undergoing processing.  
24. Article 12.1 of the GDPR specifies that the information provided to the data subject pursuant to Article 15.1 of the same Regulation may be communicated either in writing or by other means such as electronically.  
25. The European Data Protection Board (hereinafter "EDPB") has clarified in its guidelines that the information – or the copy of the personal data – provided to the data subject within the scope of Article 15 of the GDPR must take a permanent form and thus be durable over time.  
26. Article 12.1 of the GDPR further provides that the data controller may respond to the exercise of a data subject's right of access orally, provided that this is done at the initiative of the data subject and that the identity of the latter is demonstrated by other means.  
27. Furthermore, Article 5.2 of the GDPR specifies that it is the responsibility of the data controller to demonstrate compliance with the fundamental principles of the GDPR set out in Article 6.1, which include the obligation of transparency and explicit purpose.  
28. In the case at hand, the defendant claims to have responded to the complainant's requests by phone. The defendant specifies that these voice and telephone exchanges with the complainant should therefore be taken into account alongside the written exchanges held in the same context.  
29. However, no element from the case file demonstrates that the complainant requested a response to the exercise of her right of access orally.  
30. Furthermore, it should be noted that the defendant provides for the first time in its summary conclusions information about the data still in its possession, their purpose, or their retention period. Finally, it confirms itself that it did not deem it necessary to provide the purposes of processing on the grounds that they seemed obvious to it.  
31. Therefore, the Contentious Chamber cannot consider that the alleged oral responses given by the defendant could compensate for the absence of a written response that emerges from the file.  
32. As raised by the SI (see point 12), the Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant did not provide a complete response to the complainant's request and also did not provide a copy of the processed personal data.

33. Furthermore, while the defendant repeatedly states that it has "responded" to the complainant's requests, it must be noted that this response must be relevant and include the information referred to in Article 15 of the GDPR, which it does not.  
34. Finally, the fact that, as the defendant communicated to the complainant, the teachers do not have access to this information and are subject to professional secrecy does not contradict this finding.  
35. Consequently, the Contentious Chamber finds that the defendant has violated Articles 12.1, 15.1, and 15.3 of the GDPR.  
II.2. Regarding the breach of Articles 12.1 and 13 of the GDPR  
36. The Contentious Chamber acknowledges that the defendant published, on its website, a "sheet of information regarding the collection and processing of personal data" before the start of the 2020-2021 school year.  
37. The Contentious Chamber nevertheless concludes that there has been a violation of Articles 12.1 and 13 of the GDPR, on which it aligns with the opinion of the SI (see point 12).  
38. Indeed, there is no debate in this case regarding the lack of prior information concerning the collection of the complainant's daughter's data – particularly regarding the retention period of health data – which is acknowledged by the defendant.  
39. In this regard, the defendant states that it has always responded to parents' questions regarding retention and archiving periods as well as the identity of the recipients of these same data.  
40. However, this cannot have any impact in this case. The obligation to inform is a positive obligation that falls on the data controller, and it must be fulfilled at the time of data collection when it is direct, or within one month after obtaining this data when it is indirect, unless there is communication made with the data subject based on the collected data or if the same data is communicated to another recipient, in which case the one-month period established by Article 14.3.a) may be reduced.  
41. The Contentious Chamber adds that transparency is a fundamental principle of the right to the protection of personal data. It allows data subjects not only to be aware of the processing of their personal data but also, thereby, to exercise control – which may lead to certain actions being taken by the data subject if necessary.  
42. It is precisely for this reason that the European legislator has sharpened the obligation to inform data subjects about the processing of their data in the GDPR.

personal data by adding additional qualities such as conciseness, transparency, comprehensibility, and easy accessibility (see Article 12.1 of the GDPR).  
43. This information is all the more crucial as it is a condition that allows the data subjects to have genuine freedom of choice in situations where they may be required to consent to the processing of their personal data2.  
44. Furthermore, the defendant does not invoke any of the exceptions referred to in Article 13.4 of the GDPR.  
45. Moreover, it cannot be accepted under any circumstances that the apparent obviousness of a processing purpose – at least for the data controller – would relieve the data controller of its obligation to inform the data subjects. Consequently, the Contentious Chamber finds that the defendant has violated Articles 12.1 and 13 of the GDPR.
