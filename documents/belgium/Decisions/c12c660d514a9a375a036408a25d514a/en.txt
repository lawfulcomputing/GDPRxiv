I. Facts and procedure  
1.  
The central registration system MeldJeAan (hereinafter: MeldJeAan) has been used for several years in various cities in the context of school allocation to achieve a greater social mix within schools.  
2.  
To this end, parents and, where applicable, guardians were required to provide the following personal data: identification data (name, address, date of birth, telephone number of both parents/guardians and children), electronic identification data (email addresses of both parents/guardians and children), personal characteristics (age, gender of children), education and training (mother), national number (the national register number of the child), whether there are already siblings of the child present in a particular school in that city, whether the parents are staff members of a school where they are registering, whether the family received a school allowance in the current or previous school year, the child's spoken language, and any report on special education.  
In addition, other personal data were processed within the application, namely indicators regarding students: home language, education level of the mother, neighborhood indicator, school allowance, and preferences for a particular school. Based on these personal preferences and the other data, children were assigned to a school. Subsequently, lists were drawn up per school of the children who had registered for that school. By logging into the back office of “MeldJeAan,” a staff member for their school can download a list with all personal data of the parents and their children who had registered at that school. Due to a possible flaw in the online application MeldJeAan as used for secondary education in Ghent, it appeared that the URL with the link to the download list was also directly accessible without logging into the back office. Each secondary school in the system receives a unique ID of 30 characters, which is included in the URL, allowing anyone with that unique ID to download the list with personal data of the parents and their child who had registered at the school linked to the unique ID without first logging in. At least one list was downloaded in this manner. The method of downloading was also reported to the press, which subsequently downloaded a list themselves and published it, albeit pseudonymized.  
3.  
This online application MeldJeAan was also used in, among others, Antwerp, concerning primary education. The GBA has not received any notification of a data breach related to the application due to the

II.2.3. Assessment by the Dispute Chamber  
35. Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR stipulates that “[personal data] shall be processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, by taking appropriate technical or organizational measures.”  
36. In further elaboration of Article 5.1.f) GDPR, Article 32.1 GDPR states that the respondent, as the data controller, must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk. This must take into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, as well as the nature, scope, context, processing purposes, and the likelihood and severity of the various risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals.  
37. Article 32.2 of the GDPR provides that, in assessing the appropriate level of security, account must be taken of the processing risks, particularly as a result of the destruction, loss, alteration, or unauthorized disclosure of or unauthorized access to transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed data, whether accidentally or unlawfully.  
38. The Dispute Chamber points out that the accountability obligation under Article 5.2 GDPR, Article 24.1, and Article 25.1 GDPR entails that the data controller has the obligation, on the one hand, to take proactive measures to ensure compliance with the provisions of the GDPR and, on the other hand, to be able to demonstrate that such measures have been taken.  
39. In short: the respondent has the obligation to take appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure an appropriate level of security and to be able to demonstrate this.  
40. Regarding the aforementioned accountability obligation, the Dispute Chamber notes that the Inspection Service requested the following from the respondent:  
“A copy of the documents from [respondent] regarding the measures and decisions taken for the security of the processing of personal data in the context of the online registration system ‘Sign Up’ and the accountability thereof in accordance with Article 1, paragraph 1, f) and paragraph 2 of the GDPR, Article 24, paragraph 1 of the GDPR, Article 25, paragraph 1 of the GDPR, and Article 32 of the GDPR. Please also provide a copy of the information and advice that the data protection officer of [respondent] has provided in this regard and give a documented explanation of their involvement in this context in accordance with Article 38, paragraph 1 read in conjunction with Article 39, paragraph 1 of the GDPR.”  
41. The Dispute Chamber finds that the respondent does not submit any documents or otherwise demonstrate how and when compliance with the processor agreement was monitored, neither at the start nor during the execution of the processor agreement. However, Article 28, paragraph 1 stipulates that when processing is carried out on behalf of a data controller, this data controller may only engage processors who provide sufficient guarantees regarding the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures so that the processing meets the requirements of the GDPR. The data controller can verify this by, for example, requesting a description of the security measures of the processor and by assessing the processor's working methods, with the involvement of the data protection officer. In the context of the aforementioned accountability obligation, it is important to document these assessments properly.  
42. The Dispute Chamber then finds that the document “Measures Taken Regarding the Data Breach MeldJeAan” provides a series of measures, along with a clarification of the purpose of each measure and the status of implementation. An additional document indicates when the meetings regarding these measures took place and who participated in them. The Dispute Chamber notes that this document is vague regarding the approval of these measures and the further timing of the implementation of certain measures and follow-up of the already implemented/to be implemented security measures. Consequently, the respondent does not demonstrate with these documents that account was taken of the state of the art, the costs of implementation, the nature, scope, and context of the processing, nor does it demonstrate that these measures are sufficiently aligned with the security risk and take into account the processing risks, as prescribed by Articles 32.1 and 32.2 GDPR.  
43. Regarding the Inspection Service's finding concerning the involvement of the data protection officer, the Dispute Chamber finds that the respondent does not provide documentation, such as advice, indicating that the data protection officer was consulted in the context of the security of MeldJeAan. The respondent argues that MeldJeAan was established before the GDPR came into effect. However, it is the responsibility of each data controller, after the GDPR comes into effect, to proactively verify whether the processing of personal data complies with the requirements of the GDPR and to make any necessary adjustments and document this accordingly.

In light of the above, the Dispute Chamber finds that there is a violation of Article 5.1.f), Article 32.1 and 32.2 in conjunction with Article 5.2, Article 24.1, and Article 25.1 of the GDPR, namely the accountability obligation regarding the security of the processing of personal data in the context of MeldJeAan.

II.3.3. Assessment by the Dispute Chamber  
54. The Dispute Chamber refers to section II.1.3 where the defendant was qualified as a joint data controller. Article 26.1 of the GDPR stipulates that joint data controllers shall transparently determine and agree upon their respective responsibilities for compliance with the obligations under the regulation. Joint data controllers must therefore determine "who does what" by mutually deciding who will carry out which tasks to ensure that the processing complies with the applicable regulations.

obligations under the GDPR concerning the involved joint processing. One of these obligations that must be included in this task allocation, if applicable, is the preparation of a DPIA (Article 35 GDPR).  
55. In accordance with the risk-based approach established in the GDPR, a DPIA is not mandatory for every processing activity. A DPIA is only required if the processing "is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons" (Article 35, paragraph 1 GDPR). When joint controllers are involved in the processing, they must precisely determine their respective obligations. The DPIA must describe which party is responsible for the various measures designed to address risks and protect the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. Each controller must outline what their needs are and must share useful information without disclosing secrets (for example, protection of trade secrets, intellectual property, confidential business information) or revealing vulnerabilities.  
56. Although a data protection impact assessment may be required in other circumstances, Article 35.3 GDPR provides some examples of when processing "is likely to result in a high risk":  
"(a) a systematic and comprehensive assessment of personal aspects of natural persons, based on automated processing, including profiling, and on which decisions are based that produce legal effects concerning the natural person or similarly significantly affect the natural person;  
(b) large-scale processing of special categories of personal data as referred to in Article 9, paragraph 1, or of data concerning criminal convictions and offenses as referred to in Article 10; or  
(c) systematic and large-scale monitoring of publicly accessible areas."  
57.  
When assessing whether a DPIA is required for processing based on their inherent high risk, nine criteria must be taken into account, namely: (1) evaluation or scoring, (2) automated decision-making with legal effects or similarly significant effects, (3) systematic monitoring, (4) sensitive data or data of a highly personal nature, (5) data processed on a large scale, (6) matching or combining datasets, (7) data concerning vulnerable data subjects, (8) innovative use or innovative application of new technological or organizational measures.

62. The respondent submitted the DPIA it prepared regarding the processing of personal data in the context of MeldJeAan on December 9, 2022. The Dispute Chamber notes that the DPIA designates the respondent as the processor, which does not align with the position set forth by the respondent during the hearing.  
63. The data controller must also seek the advice of the data protection officer in the DPIA, if one has been appointed (Article 35.2 GDPR). The respondent also submits the positive advice of the data protection officer regarding the DPIA.  
64. The Dispute Chamber notes that the registration system was already in place before the GDPR came into effect and that various (sometimes contradictory) opinions regarding the data processing responsibility were provided by, among others, VTC and AGODI. However, this does not mean that the obligations arising from the GDPR should not be complied with. As soon as the respondent decided to allocate funding to the LOP Antwerp to fulfill its statutory obligations, the respondent should have evaluated whether it should, in fact, be considered a (joint) data controller and whether it complied with all obligations arising from this qualification, such as preparing a DPIA.

II.4. Article 38.1 and Article 39 GDPR  
II.4.1. Findings of the Inspection Service  
66. During the investigation, the Inspection Service requested the respondent to provide copies of the information and advice given by the data protection officer in the context of (i) the security of the processing of personal data, (ii) the register of processing activities, and (iii) the data protection impact assessment.  
67. The respondent answered during the investigation that the application MeldJeAan has been in use since 2014. Since this predates the entry into force of the GDPR, no advice was requested from the data protection officer at that time. The processing was included in the register of processing activities, and the data protection officer was informed about the data breach in Ghent and the steps taken as a result. At the time of writing this response, a GEB was being created on which the data protection officer would provide advice.  
68. Based on the above response from the respondent, the Inspection Service finds that the respondent does not demonstrate that the data protection officer was effectively and timely involved in:  
- the security of the processing of personal data in the context of MeldJeAan;  
- the register of processing activities; and  
- the assessment of the necessity of and, where applicable, the execution of a data protection impact assessment.  

II.4.2. Position of the Respondent  
69. In its conclusions, the respondent argues that the data protection officer was initially not involved in MeldJeAan since the establishment of this platform predates the entry into force of the GDPR, and therefore there was no data protection officer and no obligation to involve one.

II.4.3. Assessment by the Dispute Chamber  
70. It is important to note that the MeldJeAan platform dates from before the entry into force of the GDPR. Since May 25, 2018, the GDPR has been applicable, and the data controller should therefore have proactively checked whether it complied with the requirements of the GDPR, rather than adopting a wait-and-see attitude. As soon as the respondent made the decision to grant funding to LOP Antwerp to fulfill its statutory obligations, the respondent should have evaluated whether it should, in fact, be considered a data controller and whether it complied with all obligations arising from this qualification.  
71. These obligations included, among others, the provisions regarding the position and tasks of the data protection officer as described in Articles 38 and 39.1 of the GDPR. The GDPR recognizes that the data protection officer is a key figure concerning the protection of personal data, whose appointment, position, and tasks are subject to rules. These rules help the data controller to comply with its obligations under the GDPR, but also assist the data protection officer in properly carrying out their tasks.  
72. The Dispute Chamber reminds that Article 38.1 GDPR prescribes that the data controller ensures that the data protection officer is involved in a timely and proper manner in all matters related to the protection of personal data.  
73. Based on Article 39.1 GDPR, the data protection officer must (a) inform and advise the data controller about its obligations under the GDPR and other Union or Member State data protection provisions, and (b) monitor compliance with the GDPR, other Union or Member State data protection provisions, and the policies of the data controller or processor regarding the protection of personal data, including the allocation of responsibilities, awareness-raising and training of staff involved in processing, and relevant audits.  
74. The documents from the respondent do not indicate that the data protection officer was involved in the obligations under Articles 32.1 and 32.2 (see section II.2). The officer

II.5. Article 30.1 GDPR  
II.5.1. Findings in the Inspection Report  
76.  
The Inspection Service finds regarding the register of processing activities of the respondent that it does not meet the minimum requirements as imposed by Article 30.1 GDPR. Specifically, the Inspection Service identifies the following violations in this regard:  
- The description of the categories of data subjects and the categories of personal data is incomplete (Article 30.1.c) GDPR) as in the tab "Export processing register" of the register of processing activities of the respondent, the categories of data subjects and personal data are only briefly listed instead of being described. This is the case for the columns "categories of data subjects (whose personal data is being processed?)", "data categories: basic data" and "data categories: sensitive data." It is therefore unclear what is meant precisely;  
- The respondent does not demonstrate that its register of processing activities is current. The Inspection Service refers in this context to the fact that the respondent mentions "see export processing register (date: 30/08/2022)" while the register was provided to the Inspection Service via email on 19/09/2022. Consequently, the Inspection Service received the register of processing activities from the respondent on 19/09/2022, which was last updated on 30/08/2022.  
II.5.2. Position of the Respondent  
77.  
The respondent argues that the GDPR states that the data controller and processor are required to (i) maintain a register to demonstrate compliance with the regulation and (ii) cooperate with the supervisory authority and provide this register upon request. Furthermore, the GDPR does not provide further clarification regarding the specification, design, and/or content of a register of processing activities.  
78.  
Consequently, the respondent states that the GDPR does not specify to what level the entries in the register of processing activities must be described. The GDPR allows the data controller, i.e., the respondent, considerable freedom in this regard, provided that the register (i) makes sufficiently clear which processing activities are being carried out by stating a number of mandatory elements and (ii) is structured in such a way that the GBA can carry out checks on the (non-)compliance with the GDPR based on it. The respondent believes that the register is indeed detailed enough that there is little more that can be described/clarified regarding the categories of data subjects and personal data. All terms used, especially when read in conjunction with all other mentioned categories, are sufficiently clear regarding who processes which personal data.  
79.  
Regarding the current status of the register, the respondent confirms that the export provided to the Inspection Service indeed dated from August 30, 2022, despite being submitted on September 19, 2022. After all, there was no request for an export that should not exceed a certain age, and moreover, this was indeed the current version of the register, in the absence of a need for adjustment during the period from August 30, 2022, to September 19, 2022.  
II.5.3. Assessment by the Dispute Chamber  
80. Pursuant to Article 30 GDPR, each data controller must maintain a register of the processing activities carried out under its responsibility. Article 30.1.a) to g) GDPR stipulates that, with regard to the processing carried out in the capacity of data controller, the following information must be available:  
a) the name and contact details of the data controller and any joint data controllers and, where applicable, of the representative of the data controller and of the data protection officer;  
b) the purposes of the processing;  
c) a description of the categories of data subjects and of the categories of personal data;  
d) the categories of recipients to whom the personal data have been or will be disclosed, including recipients in third countries or international organizations;

e) where applicable, transfers of personal data to a third country or an international organization, including the mention of that third country or international organization and, in the case of transfers referred to in Article 49.1, second paragraph of the GDPR, the documents regarding appropriate safeguards;  
f) if possible, the intended timeframes within which the different categories of data must be deleted;  
g) if possible, a general description of the technical and organizational security measures as referred to in Article 32.1 of the GDPR.  
81. The Dispute Chamber establishes that the respondent provides a listing in its register of processing activities for:  
- The categories of data subjects (Article 30.1.c) GDPR), namely residents, non-residents, internal staff, external staff, children.  
- The categories of personal data (Article 30.1.c) GDPR), namely on the one hand basic data such as first and last name, address details (street, house number, box, municipality, country), telephone number, identification codes (national register number), birth data (date and place of birth), vehicle data, login data, and sensitive data such as health data, judicial data.  
82. The Dispute Chamber – referring to previous decisions14 – must rule on whether Article 30.1.c) GDPR requires a description of the categories of personal data and the categories of data subjects to be provided in the register of processing activities, or whether a listing suffices.  
83. The Dispute Chamber establishes that Article 30.1.c) GDPR requires that a description of the categories of data subjects and the categories of personal data must be included in the register of processing activities.  
84. The Dispute Chamber reminds that the purpose of the register of processing activities is to effectively apply the obligations contained in the GDPR. It is essential that the data controller (and the processors) have an overview of the processing of personal data they carry out. This register is primarily a tool to assist the data controller in complying with the GDPR for the various data processing activities they perform, as the register makes the main characteristics of these activities visible. The Dispute Chamber is of the opinion that this processing register is an essential instrument in the context of the aforementioned accountability obligation (Article 5, paragraph 2, and Article 24 GDPR) and that this register underlies all obligations imposed on the data controller by the GDPR.  
85. The Dispute Chamber establishes that neither the text of the GDPR nor the objectives of the GDPR require that more than a listing of the categories of personal data and the categories of data subjects be included in the register of processing activities, and that a more detailed description would therefore not be necessary.  
86. Regarding the categories of recipients, the Dispute Chamber refers to a recommendation from the Commission for the Protection of Privacy15 and the doctrine16 which outlines that while it is not necessary to mention the individual recipients of the data, they can be grouped by category of recipients. Mutatis mutandis, this assertion can also be applied to the categories of personal data and data subjects.  
87. However, the Dispute Chamber points out that the filling out of the register of processing activities must always be evaluated on a case-by-case basis to determine whether the description or listing included therein is sufficiently clear and concrete.  
88. In the present case, the Dispute Chamber establishes that the listings included in the register of processing activities were sufficiently concrete. According to the Dispute Chamber, there can be little doubt about the meaning of the elements listed above in the context of the processing activities mentioned in the register of processing activities.  
89. Regarding the second finding of the Inspection Service concerning the currency of the register of processing activities, the Dispute Chamber points out that the register of processing activities must also be updated in accordance with the development and evolution of the activities of the relevant company or organization. If the data controller initiates a new processing activity or modifies an existing processing activity, the register of processing activities must be adjusted accordingly.  
90. Since the period between the export of the register of processing activities and its submission is limited to just under 3 weeks, and since there are no elements indicating that the register of processing activities would not have been current, the Dispute Chamber is of the opinion that no infringement has been demonstrated.

III. Sanctions  
92.  
Based on the documents in the file, the Dispute Chamber finds that there are multiple infringements of the GDPR. Firstly, the infringement of Articles 5.1(f), 32.1 and 32.2 in conjunction with Articles 5.2, 24.1, and 25.1 GDPR; secondly, those of Articles 35.1, 35.2, 35.3, and 35.7 GDPR; and finally, those of Articles 38.1 and 39.1 GDPR.  
93. Having the necessary processes in place to achieve and demonstrate compliance with the GDPR is one of the fundamental principles of the GDPR. The data protection impact assessment is an important accountability tool because it not only helps data controllers to comply with the requirements of the GDPR but also demonstrates that appropriate measures have been taken to ensure compliance with the GDPR. The data protection officer also plays a crucial role in data protection at a data controller.  
94. The Dispute Chamber is of the opinion that there are sufficient elements to impose a reprimand, which constitutes a light sanction and suffices in light of the infringements of the GDPR established in this file. In determining the sanction, the Dispute Chamber takes into account the fact that the respondent received (incorrect) advice regarding its qualification as a data controller but, after internal analysis, took the necessary steps to comply with its obligations as prescribed by the GDPR. The respondent has already rectified the infringements and provides evidence thereof. Furthermore, the Dispute Chamber points out that it is not authorized to impose an administrative fine on public authorities, in accordance with Article 221, § 2 of the Data Protection Act.  
95. The Dispute Chamber proceeds to a dismissal regarding the other grievances and findings of the Inspection Service because it cannot conclude, based on the facts and the documents in the file, that there are infringements of the GDPR in this regard. These grievances and findings of the Inspection Service are therefore considered manifestly unfounded within the meaning of Article 57, paragraph 4 of the GDPR.
