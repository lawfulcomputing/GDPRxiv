II. Motivation  
II.1. Regarding the Quality of the Defendant  
23.  
The Disciplinary Chamber notes that the defendant is a non-profit organization established as an external service for prevention and protection at work (SEPPT) within the meaning of Article 40 of the Well-being Act.  
24.  
Employers are obligated to promote the well-being of their workers and must adopt appropriate preventive measures to avert psychosocial risks at work. This obligation also entails the creation of an internal service for prevention and protection at work. Employers may also call upon external services for prevention and protection at work (SEPPT) such as the defendant.  
25.  
Furthermore, the employer must establish procedures allowing a worker who believes they have suffered a psychosocial risk to request either an informal psychosocial intervention or a formal psychosocial intervention. When an SEPPT is involved, as in this case, these requests for intervention can be directly submitted to a prevention advisor from this external service. This SEPPT can, as will be explained below, also call upon an independent prevention advisor. In response to the request for psychosocial intervention, the prevention advisor drafts an opinion, intended for the employer, recommending the adoption of individual measures concerning the requesting individual (such as the complainant in this case) or the implementation of collective measures.  
26.  
Although the defendant is contractually engaged with the employer of the complainant (namely with the Higher Education Institution), the defendant had the obligation to carry out its legal missions independently. Its status as a data controller arises from the missions assigned to it by the regulations concerning well-being at work. The defendant is thus a data controller within the meaning of Article 4.7 of the GDPR. The Disciplinary Chamber

II.2.3. Assessment of the Contentious Chamber  
39. The Contentious Chamber recalls that pursuant to Article 5.1 a) of the GDPR (lawfulness, fairness, transparency), any processing of data must be lawful.  
40. This requirement of lawfulness means that the processing of personal data must be carried out in accordance with all applicable legal rules. This implies not only compliance with data protection rules and the mobilization of a lawful basis as defined in Article 6.1 of the GDPR but also, for example, compliance with obligations under labor law, contract law, consumer protection, professional secrecy, or non-discrimination rules. The processing of personal data, even authorized under one of the lawful bases provided for in Article 6.1 of the GDPR, cannot contravene applicable law.  
41.  
In this case, the Contentious Chamber is of the opinion that the defendant could rely on Article 6.1 e) of the GDPR22 to communicate to Ms. Z1 the personal data concerning the complainant necessary for her to process the psychosocial intervention request made by him as an independent prevention advisor.  
42.  
The defendant is indeed entrusted with the execution of a public interest mission, based on a legal basis23.  
43. The Contentious Chamber notes that the activity of a SEPPT such as the defendant is regulated by the "Well-being Law" and the "Well-being Code," as previously mentioned. The missions entrusted to it by the legislator aim, among other things, to prevent psychosocial risks at work. More generally, the SEPPT is expected to assist the employer in ensuring and improving the health, safety, and well-being of its workers. For these reasons, this mission can be qualified as being in the public interest.  
44. Regarding the quality of the legal basis that entrusts it, the Contentious Chamber recalls Recital 41 of the GDPR, which states that, “when this regulation refers to a legal basis or to a legislative measure, this does not necessarily mean that the adoption of a legislative act by a parliament is required, without prejudice to the obligations provided for under the constitutional order of the concerned Member State. However, this legal basis or legislative measure should be clear and precise, and its application

Reminder of principles  
67. Under Article 15.1 of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to obtain from the data controller confirmation as to whether or not personal data concerning them is being processed (1st aspect of the right of access).  
68. When this is the case, the data subject has the right to obtain a series of information listed in Article 15.1 a) - h) and 15.2 of the GDPR relating to the processed data as well as access to it (2nd aspect of the right of access)31.  
69. The GDPR provides in Article 15.3 that the data controller shall provide a copy of the personal data to the data subject. This obtaining of a copy is the main modality of granting access to the processed data.  
70. The Dispute Chamber is of the opinion that, notwithstanding the absence of reference to Article 15 of the GDPR in the email of October 13, 2021, from the complainant, the request made by the latter to "know what data is held by the defendant and by Ms. Z1 as well as to receive a copy of it" constitutes a request for access within the meaning of Article 15 of the GDPR.  
71. When receiving a request for the exercise of rights (here the right of access) from a data subject (here the complainant), the data controller (here the defendant) is always required to respond to the data subject:  
- Either by providing information on the measures taken in response to their request as soon as possible and in any event within one month of receiving the request. If necessary, this period may be extended by two months, taking into account the complexity and number of requests. In this case, the data controller shall nonetheless inform the data subject of this extension and the reasons for the delay within one month of receiving the request (Article 12.3 of the GDPR).  
- Or, if it considers that it should not respond to the request, including in support of Article 12.5 of the GDPR (see below on this point), by informing them without delay and at the latest

This context, as well as the outcome of these procedures, whether they rule in favor of or against the complainant, do not taint by extension the complainant's access request of October 13, 2021, as excessive within the meaning of Article 12.5 of the GDPR.  
93. The Contentious Chamber therefore does not share the view of the SI that the defendant was justified in not responding to the complainant based on the adage "No one can invoke their own turpitude," since the complainant would have created the conditions for a lack of response to him (finding 1 of the SI - point 14). This Latin adage ("Nemo auditur propriam turpitudinem allegans") and cited as a general principle of law, indeed presupposes, in addition to the fact that it is generally accepted to have a relatively limited scope, a wrongful behavior on the part of the person to whom it is opposed.  
94. However, in this case, the Contentious Chamber, in support of the reasoning above, has concluded that the complainant's behavior could not be deemed culpable as excessive in light of his access request. More specifically, the Contentious Chamber highlighted that the contextual elements reported by the defendant did not taint the access request of October 13, 2021, as excessive. The invocation of the adage is therefore not relevant in this case, and no repercussions on the complainant's right of access can be drawn from it.  
95. In conclusion, the Contentious Chamber decides, in support of the above, that the defendant was not justified in relying on Article 12.5 of the GDPR to refuse to respond to the complainant's request. It does not demonstrate the excessive nature of the request of October 13, 2021, from the complainant and has committed a violation of Article 12.5 of the GDPR.

118. Article 5.1. d) of the GDPR provides that personal data must be "accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable measures must be taken to ensure that personal data that is inaccurate, in relation to the purposes for which it is processed, is erased or rectified without delay (accuracy)."  
119. As a corollary of this principle to be respected and implemented by the data controller (Article 5.2. of the GDPR), the data subject has a right to rectification formulated as follows in Article 16 of the GDPR: "the data subject has the right to obtain from the controller, without undue delay, the rectification of inaccurate personal data concerning him or her."  
120. It follows that a request for rectification must therefore be addressed to the data controller. If the latter fails to respond or wrongfully refuses to comply, a complaint may be filed with the APD.  
121. The exercise of the right to rectification must, in order to be favorably addressed, concern inaccurate personal data. The Contentious Chamber recalls here that "subjective" personal data (such as evaluative data) are not, without questioning their qualification as personal data within the meaning of Article 4.1 of the GDPR, to be considered as erroneous within the meaning of Article 16 of the GDPR as long as the data subject does not share the opinion that they reveal, for example. This is different from "objectively erroneous" data, such as a misspelled name, an incorrect date of birth, etc.  
122. Regarding the data contained in the disputed psychosocial notice, the Contentious Chamber does not have elements allowing it to conclude that these are erroneous. It also notes that the complainant has contested this notice before the Commission of Psychologists.  
123. Regarding the geolocation data, the Contentious Chamber has no evidence to confirm or refute it.  
124. Specifically concerning personal data processed by the defendant in the context of the defenses it develops in its conclusions submitted in this procedure, the Contentious Chamber emphasizes that the GDPR is not there to alter the statements made in the conclusions. The rights of the defense and the possibility to respond are the avenues through which it is appropriate to contest their possible inaccuracy in the specific context of their processing as a defense means. This applies to both so-called "subjective" personal data and those that could be "objectifiable," such as in this case the exact number of proceedings initiated by the complainant or the subjects he actually teaches. The Contentious Chamber has also taken note of the arguments and evidence from both parties and is, in terms of the reasoning of its decision, free to follow the interpretation made or not. Even assuming that the Contentious Chamber orders the rectification of the data based on Article 100, 6° of the LCA, quod non, this injunction could only concern the processing of data carried out through the submitted conclusions, which would amount to modifying conclusions during the procedure, something the Contentious Chamber is not authorized to do. If these same personal data were or had been previously processed by the defendant, the Contentious Chamber does not have elements that would allow it to conclude that the defendant, with whom the exercise of rights must primarily be exercised, would not have or would not take them into account in the future.  
125. Regarding the request based on Article 5.1. f) of the GDPR, the Contentious Chamber notes, still in an abundant manner, that it has no evidence attesting to any violation thereof. In the conclusions through which he introduces this grievance, the complainant indeed limits himself to speculating about an undue communication of personal data concerning him by the defendant (and/or by Ms. Z1 who is not a party to the case) to Mr. Z2, counsel for his employer, the Haute École, due to similar biases in their respective writings.
