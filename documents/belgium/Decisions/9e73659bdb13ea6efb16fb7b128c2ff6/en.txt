32.  
The Court of Justice of the European Union specifies in this regard that the remedies provided for in the two aforementioned provisions can be exercised concurrently and independently.  
33. Thus, in the judgment Schufa Holding AG, the Court states that “the complaint procedure, which is not akin to that of a petition, is designed as a mechanism capable of effectively safeguarding the rights and interests of the concerned individuals.” The Court thus emphasizes the essential and autonomous nature of the remedies provided by the GDPR.  
34. Furthermore, in the case Fashion ID, the Court recalled that supervisory authorities must carry out their missions in complete independence. In the judgment Wirtschaftsakademie Schleswig-Holstein, the Court had previously emphasized that supervisory authorities must be able to exercise their intervention powers effectively and efficiently.  
35. In its judgment Shrems II, the Court states that even the existence of an adequacy decision from the European Commission cannot prevent a supervisory authority from examining a complaint from a concerned individual and verifying whether the transfer of their personal data to a third country complies with the requirements set by the GDPR.  
36. In light of the above, it is not the responsibility of the Data Protection Authority to verify the potential existence of parallel proceedings.  
37.  
It is therefore up to the parties to provide evidence of the existence of ongoing parallel proceedings if they deem it necessary.

44. This competence derives directly from Article 51 of the GDPR, which stipulates that each Member State must have one or more independent public authorities responsible for overseeing the application of the regulation.

These competences are not merely complementary but are exercised independently and in parallel to those of the SPF Economy.

As a result, fewer emails than expected were brought to the defendant's attention. The context in which all this was carried out, namely the pandemic caused by Covid-19, did not alert the defendant to the low number of emails it was receiving at that time.  
69. Moreover, the defendant adds that according to the guidelines of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), the calculation of the period referred to in Article 12.3 of the GDPR begins when a request under Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR reaches the official channels of a data controller. However, the defendant points out that due to the technical problem that occurred during the integration of the inbox of the old email address, the complainant's requests never reached it. It finally notes that it became aware of these requests only on September 1, 2020.  
b) Assessment of the Disciplinary Chamber  
70. The first four paragraphs of Article 12 of the GDPR provide as follows:  
“1. The data controller takes appropriate measures to provide any information referred to in Articles 13 and 14 as well as to carry out any communication under Articles 15 to 22 and Article 34 regarding the processing to the data subject in a concise, transparent, understandable, and easily accessible manner, using clear and plain language, particularly for any information specifically addressed to a child. The information is provided in writing or by other means, including, where appropriate, electronically. When the data subject requests it, the information may be provided orally, provided that the identity of the data subject is demonstrated by other means.  
2. The data controller facilitates the exercise of the rights granted to the data subject under Articles 15 to 22. In the cases referred to in Article 11, paragraph 2, the data controller does not refuse to comply with the request of the data subject to exercise the rights conferred on them by Articles 15 to 22, unless the data controller demonstrates that it is not in a position to identify the data subject.  
3. The data controller provides the data subject with information on the measures taken in response to a request made under Articles 15 to 22, as soon as possible and in any event within one month of receiving the request. If necessary, this period may be extended by two months, taking into account the complexity and number of requests. The data controller informs the data subject of this extension and the reasons for the delay within one month of receiving the request. When the data subject submits their request in electronic form, the information is provided electronically where possible, unless the data subject requests otherwise.  
4. If the data controller does not respond to the request made by the data subject, it shall inform the data subject without delay and at the latest within one month of receiving the request of the reasons for its inaction and the possibility of lodging a complaint with a supervisory authority and seeking a judicial remedy.”  
71.  
The Disciplinary Chamber notes that the defendant claims to have deleted the complainant's personal data – initially requested on February 26, 2020 – and to have informed him via an email dated September 18, 2020.  
72.  
In this regard, the Disciplinary Chamber agrees with the Supervisory Authority that the defendant's response of September 18, 2020, is not sufficiently transparent within the meaning of Article 12.1 of the GDPR. The response indeed mentions a maximum retention period of 10 years, in accordance with the law. It is thus not possible on this basis to determine more precisely the exact moment when the data will be deleted. Furthermore, the response uses the conditional and abstract terms, such as: “By ‘non-essential information,’ we mean all data that can be deleted unless you are no longer a customer with us. […]”. However, since this email was not sent to the complainant, there is no basis for finding a breach of Article 12.1 of the GDPR.

II.2.2. Regarding the right of access  
a. Position of the parties and the SI  
78.  
According to its investigation report, the SI concludes that the defendant violated Article 15 of the GDPR, given that this article provides neither exception nor limitation to the right of access in cases where the requested data has been deleted in accordance with Article 17 of the same Regulation.  
79.  
The defendant, on the other hand, argues that the actual request of the complainant consisted of the deletion of his data, supporting this position by the absence of mention of the complainant's access request in the complaint form. Therefore, the defendant concludes that by deleting the complainant's data, it complied with the complainant's true request.  
80. The complainant contests the defendant's argument and asserts that his request was not limited to the deletion of his data. In this regard, he relies on several emails through which he exercised his right of access. He further notes that some of these emails are included in the inventory of the defendant's documents and that some are reproduced in the defendant's conclusions. He specifies that it appears from the defendant's conclusions that he had specifically requested that the defendant respond to his access request before proceeding with the deletion of his data.  
b. Assessment of the Contentious Chamber  

Decision on the merits 169/2024 — 18/23  
81.  
It appears from the case file that the complainant exercised his right of access on February 26, 2020, and made reminders on February 27, 2020, and March 6, 2020, explicitly stating that his access request should be addressed before the deletion request – the complainant having reaffirmed in his reply conclusions his desire to access the data listed in Article 15.1 of the GDPR.  
82.  
The Contentious Chamber recalls that the right of access has three components. First, under Article 15.1 of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to obtain from the data controller confirmation as to whether or not personal data concerning him or her is being processed. Second, when personal data is being processed, the data subject has the right to obtain access to the said personal data as well as to a series of information listed in Article 15.1 a) – h). Third, under Article 15.3 of the GDPR, the data subject also has the right to obtain a copy of the personal data undergoing processing. Finally, Article 15.4 states that the right to obtain a copy referred to in paragraph 3 of the same article shall not adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others.  
83. In addition to Article 15.4 of the GDPR, Article 12.5 of the GDPR provides, as a limitation, that the data controller may refuse to comply with one of the requests referred to in Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR or may require a reasonable fee when the request is manifestly unfounded or excessive. In this case, the data controller is required to demonstrate the manifestly unfounded or excessive nature of the request. Article 23 of the GDPR provides for the possibility for national legislators to derogate from the rights provided for in Articles 15 to 22.  
84. The Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant did not invoke any of the aforementioned limitations.  
85. The execution of the complainant's right to deletion cannot therefore be held as an exception to the complainant's right of access.  
86. Consequently, the Contentious Chamber finds that the defendant violated Article 15 of the GDPR.

II.3.1. The position of the parties and the SI  
87.  
According to the investigation report, the SI concludes that the defendant did not inform the complainant as it should have done. It notes that although the defendant claims to have sent a confirmation email regarding the deletion of the complainant's data, it does not see the complainant's email address among the recipients of the email in question.

88. The defendant states that she became aware of the plaintiff's request for erasure only on September 1, 2020. On September 15, 2020, she sent an email to the plaintiff regarding the follow-up to the erasure request. However, this email was sent in Dutch. The plaintiff, not understanding the language, requested that he be responded to in French. On September 18, 2020, the defendant then sent the same email, but this time in French. However, the plaintiff's email address was not among the recipients of the email, as it had been erased, in accordance with Article 17 of the GDPR.  
89. Regarding the fact that she sent her first email to the plaintiff in Dutch, the defendant explains that she was under the impression that the plaintiff was Dutch-speaking, given that the Inspection Service had always communicated with her in Dutch.  
II.3.2. Assessment of the Contentious Chamber  
90. As stated in point 26, the Contentious Chamber decides not to examine this grievance in depth, as it is not included in the letter inviting the parties to conclude.  
91. The Contentious Chamber recalls, as it did in point 70, that the data controller must inform the data subject of the measures taken following a request made under Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR, and this within one month at the latest – unless exceptions apply. If the data controller were to refuse to comply with a request made under Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR, they would then have to inform the data subject of the reasons for this refusal, also within one month at the latest.  
92. The plaintiff exercised his right to erasure for the first time on February 26, 2020. The defendant erased the plaintiff's personal data in September 2020. However, she was unable to inform the plaintiff in French (but did so in Dutch).  
93. The Contentious Chamber notes that the plaintiff's email address was not among the recipients of the email by which the defendant informed of the follow-up to the plaintiff's erasure request. In this regard, it should be emphasized that the defendant, by sending her first email to the plaintiff in Dutch, did not commit any fault. In the absence of indication regarding the plaintiff's linguistic affiliation, and given that the Inspection Service had always communicated with the defendant in Dutch, it could not reasonably be expected of the defendant to address her email to the plaintiff in a language other than Dutch.  

Decision on the merits 169/2024 — 20/23  
94. However, the Contentious Chamber considers that after the plaintiff responded that he did not understand Dutch, it was incumbent upon the defendant to resend an email to the plaintiff in French regarding the follow-up to the erasure request. The GDPR indeed does not provide any exception to the respect of Article 12.3 on the grounds that a data controller has complied with an erasure request. Since the defendant received the plaintiff's email informing her that he did not understand Dutch, she then had the plaintiff's email address. Therefore, she should have organized herself in such a way as to comply with the obligation imposed on her under Article 12.3 before erasing the plaintiff's personal data.  
II.4. Regarding the breach of Article 31 of the GDPR  
II.4.1. The position of the parties and the Inspection Service  
95. The defendant indicates that she has not breached Article 31 of the GDPR. She first points out the absence of a definition of the term "cooperation" included in the aforementioned provision, whether in the GDPR or the LCA. In the absence of such a definition, the defendant at least relies on the doctrine defining this cooperation as an obligation for a data controller and/or a processor to comply with requests from a supervisory authority, which may consist of providing information or access to premises, information systems, and/or documentation.  
96. In this regard, the defendant states that she has consistently responded to the questions posed to her by the Inspection Service and even indicated that she remains at the latter's disposal for any further questions.  
97. Furthermore, the defendant explains that she could not be attributed any malicious intent in the erasure of the plaintiff's data. Firstly, she states that no element of the file allows for the conclusion that she destroyed evidence. While she admits to having erased the plaintiff's data during the present procedure, this was not done with the intention of destroying evidence. She states that this erasure was carried out in good faith, in order to respect the rights of the plaintiff, while noting that it was done under some pressure as an investigation was being conducted against her. Finally, as stated above, such accusations are unfounded as the defendant has shown herself to be open to providing additional answers.

II.4.2. The Assessment of the Contentious Chamber  
98. As stated in point 26, the Contentious Chamber decides not to examine this complaint due to the incomplete presentation made in the letter inviting the parties to conclude.  
99. However, the Contentious Chamber simply recalls that under Article 31 of the GDPR, "The data controller and the processor, as well as, where applicable, their representatives, shall cooperate with the supervisory authority, at the request of the latter, in the performance of its tasks."  
100. Although the GDPR does not precisely define what is meant by the term cooperation, the Contentious Chamber has already had the opportunity to decide that, among other things, failing to "comply with the exercise of the complainant's rights and inform the Contentious Chamber of the follow-up given to this decision"15, failing to "raise arguments against the injunction that had been given to it as permitted by Article 99 LCA"16, and failing to respond to an email from the Contentious Chamber informing it of the amount of the proposed administrative fine constitutes an attitude reflecting "a manifest lack of consideration and cooperation contrary to what is expected of a data controller, particularly in the application of Article 31 of the GDPR."17. More recently, the Contentious Chamber also decided that a data controller's failure to respond to letters—whether physical or electronic—from the SPL inviting it to a mediation procedure constitutes a breach of Article 31 of the GDPR18.  
101. Thus, the Contentious Chamber considers—without this being construed as any position taken by it regarding the present facts—that the deliberate deletion of personal data by a data controller with the intent to negatively affect the investigation of the SI is contrary to the principle of cooperation established in Article 31 of the GDPR.
