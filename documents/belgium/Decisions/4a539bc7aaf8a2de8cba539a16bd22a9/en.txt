The facts in the decision of the Dispute Chamber 102/2021 concerned the same ‘perpetrator’, namely the respondent, but are not connected to the facts in the current complaint in terms of time and place. The decision 102/2021 concerns facts from 4 years prior to the facts in this complaint and does not involve the same addressee and thus by definition not the same place where the mail was delivered and the photo was taken. The Dispute Chamber also concludes that it concerns similar facts, but not the same facts.

II.5. The procedure for the Dispute Chamber  
II.5.1. Violation of the principles of reasonableness, economy, and diligence  
31.  
The respondent argues that the Dispute Chamber violates the principles of reasonableness, economy, and diligence. He believes that the Dispute Chamber prioritizes this case without considering the impact of the processing and the efficiency of the potential intervention, thereby not acting as a normally prudent and careful administration. Regarding impact, he points to the Dispute Chamber's policy on non-prosecution, where none of the nine general criteria for significant societal impact and/or personal impact are applicable. The respondent considers the processing as a small-scale processing of the data of one person that does not fall into the category of special personal data. Regarding efficiency, the respondent refers to (i) the previously concluded procedure, where the complaint was deemed ‘manifestly unfounded’, (ii) a second procedure that is running concurrently for similar processes, (iii) the use on the GBA website of the implementing decree as an example of legal provision regarding the processing of data on an identity card, (iv) the publicity that the complainant has given to his complaint, (v) confusion between the concepts of identity document and identity card, (vi) the expansion of the scope of the complaint, and (vii) the failure to exercise the rights of the data subject by the complainant before filing the complaint.  
32.  
The oversight by the Dispute Chamber is not primarily aimed at resolving disputes between parties, but is one of the instruments of the GBA to ensure compliance with data protection rules, in accordance with the provisions in the EU Treaties, the GDPR, and the WOG. In his conclusions, the respondent states that he handles approximately 120,000 registered shipments per day and retains the photo for 13 months. The respondent theoretically therefore permanently has about 45,000,000 photos of identity cards available for inspection, including one or more photos of the complainant's identity card. Such processing involves processing with significant societal impact due to the number of individuals whose personal data are being processed and due to the sensitive data collected on the identity card. Moreover, it is within the discretionary power of the Dispute Chamber to choose what action to take in a particular case, which the respondent also acknowledges. The procedural elements raised by the respondent do not result in a violation of the rights of defense, as the respondent has had the opportunity to fully present his arguments through the response and rebuttal; furthermore, the respondent has been able to fully exercise his right to contest during the hearing of the Dispute Chamber. The respondent has thus suffered no disadvantage and the rights of defense have been respected.  
33.  
Furthermore, the respondent himself points to the impact of the processing of personal data under his responsibility and the efficiency of the Dispute Chamber's intervention when he states: “It should be noted that the Dispute Chamber, with its decision in this procedure, can contribute to restoring serenity in the public debate regarding the issuance of registered shipments by [the respondent]. Those expectations play a role in the assessment of the part of this complaint that concerns the legal basis for the processing.  
II.6. The legality of the processing  
II.6.1. Delimitation of the dispute  
37.  
Regarding the verification of the identity of the recipient of a registered shipment, the Dispute Chamber notes that the implementing decree speaks of two different but related processes. On the one hand, the implementing decree refers to the verification of identity by the respondent, and on the other hand, it refers to the proof of this identity verification carried out. The Dispute Chamber observes that the complainant does not oppose the identity verification using his identity card. However, he does oppose taking a photo of his identity card as proof of this identity verification. The Dispute Chamber will therefore only address the proof of the identity verification and will not comment on the identity verification itself carried out by the respondent.  
38.  
The Dispute Chamber also points out that the implementing decree refers to identity documents and identity cards, with the identity card being one of the options to serve as an identity document. In addition to the identity card, there are other identity documents listed in the implementing decree that may be useful for the respondent. The complainant has clearly stated in his conclusion that he specifically opposes the use of his identity card as proof of identity verification.  
II.6.2. The legal basis of legal obligation  
39.  
In his conclusion, the respondent states: “Firstly, it is the case that requesting the identity document of the addressee of a registered shipment is prescribed by law, specifically Article 9, §1 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022.”17  
40.  
The Dispute Chamber reminds that, in order to lawfully invoke the legal basis from Article 6.1.c of the GDPR, personal data may only be processed if this is necessary for the fulfillment of a legal obligation resting on the data controller. The processing must in these cases always have a basis in the law of the European Union or that of the relevant member state, which must also state the purpose of the processing. It must therefore be examined whether the conditions set out in that article are met in this case.  
A clear, precise, and predictable legal basis

41.  
According to recital 41 of the GDPR, this legal basis or legislative measure must be clear and precise, and its application must be predictable for the data subjects, in accordance with the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter 'Court of Justice') and the European Court of Human Rights (hereinafter 'ECHR').  
42.  
In this regard, the Dispute Chamber specifically refers to the judgment Privacy International18 of the Court of Justice dated October 6, 2020, in which the Court states that the legislation in question must contain clear and precise rules “on the scope and application of the measure in question, so that those whose personal data are concerned have sufficient safeguards that their data are effectively protected against the risk of misuse.” The Court adds: “That regulation must be legally binding under national law and in particular indicate under what circumstances and conditions a measure providing for the processing of such data may be taken, thus ensuring that the interference is limited to what is strictly necessary […]”19.  
43.  
Article 9, §1 of the implementing regulation requires the respondent to collect and retain proof of identity verification for 13 months. The respondent argues that the purpose of this processing is to ensure the legal guarantees associated with the registered mail. In the event of a dispute, the respondent must be able to demonstrate that the mail was indeed delivered to the correct recipient (or their authorized representative). This purpose is reflected in the definition of registered mail, which is included in Article 2, 9° of the Postal Services Act.  
44.  
The proof of identity verification consists of a “handwritten, photographic, or electronic copy of the identity document” or “any other evidence that he deems useful.” The same article defines in the second paragraph the identity document: “any document by which one can verify the identity of a person, without limiting it to an identity card, a passport, or a driving license. That document, which comes from a federal, regional, provincial, or municipal administrative authority, states the name and first name of the holder and is provided with a photo on which the holder is recognizable.”  
45.  
The Dispute Chamber finds that in this legal basis:  
• the purpose of the processing of personal data is clear, namely to verify the identity of the recipient of a registered mail and to obtain and retain proof of the identity verification, given the legal guarantees associated with the registered mail;  
• the category of data subjects is clear, namely the individuals who receive a registered mail (the addressee or the authorized representative);  
• the necessary personal data for this purpose are specified, namely the name, first name, and photo of the data subject;  
• the retention period is specified, namely 13 months;  
• the processing is predictable, namely a handwritten, photographic, or electronic copy of an official government document containing this personal data is taken at the time of delivery of the registered mail by the postal employee.  
Necessity  
46.  
Pursuant to Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR, processing is lawful if and to the extent that the processing is necessary for the fulfillment of a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject. When personal data are processed, they must be adequate and relevant for the purpose.  
47.  
The Dispute Chamber, together with the respondent, notes that the Postal Services Act assigns a special legal value to registered mail. One of these guarantees is that the postal employee verifies the identity of the addressee, so that the registered mail is reliably left with the correct person. To be able to prove this verification, the Dispute Chamber finds it necessary to take and retain a copy of the document from the government that was effectively used for the identity verification.  
Conclusion  
48.  
The Dispute Chamber concludes that there is a legal obligation on the part of the respondent under the Postal Services Act and the implementing regulation to collect and retain the name, first name, and photo of the complainant for the purpose of ensuring the legal guarantees of a registered mail. Therefore, the Dispute Chamber finds no infringement of Article 5.1.a) in conjunction with Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
49.  
This judgment is consistent with the decision of the Dispute Chamber of September 13, 2021.

II.7. The Minimum Data Processing  
50.  
In the legal basis for the processing, the King has left the choice of the medium for the necessary personal data to the respondent, as long as it is a copy of a government document that contains the name, first name, and photo of the recipient, or any other evidence that the respondent deems useful. The King thus leaves it to the respondent to assess which medium is most suitable. The Disputes Chamber agrees with the respondent when it argues that this choice belongs to him and not to the data subject, as the complainant seemed to insinuate.  
51.  
To make this choice, the respondent currently relies on the influence of the working conditions of postal employees, the number of registered mail items to be processed, and the authority of the copy as evidence. The respondent also opposes the choice of handwritten evidence due to possible errors in transcription and thus a potential infringement on the accuracy of the data in accordance with Article 5.1.d) of the GDPR.  
52.  
However, the Disputes Chamber notes that the respondent does not take into account the principle of minimum data processing from Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR in this assessment of the choice of medium. Moreover, the respondent argues that assessing minimum data processing would constitute a contra legem interpretation of the implementing decree since the choice of a photo from the identity card is literally included in this decree. The Disputes Chamber does not follow this reasoning of the respondent and points to the material scope of Article 2 of the GDPR and the primacy of Union law over national law. In this regard, the Disputes Chamber also refers to the 'Mediahuis' ruling of the Markets Court, in which the Court clearly states that a legal provision must be set aside if it is not in accordance with a clear Union legal provision.20  
53.  
According to the principle of minimum data processing, established in Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, personal data must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the purposes for which they are processed. Recital 39 of the GDPR adds: "Personal data may only be processed if the purpose of the processing cannot reasonably be achieved by other means."  
54.  
By taking a photo of the complainant's identity document, the respondent processes all personal data contained on this card and retains this data for 13 months. Since the name, first name, and photo of the recipient of the registered mail, in this case the complainant, are on the identity card, this processing is adequate for the purpose of processing.  
55.  
To perform an identity check, the postal employee only needs the data mentioned on the registered mail as a reference: the name, first name, and address. However, the identity card contains more data, such as the date of birth, place of birth, gender, nationality, identity card number, and validity period; the new e-ID also displays the national register number on the front of the card. All this data is not relevant for the respondent, as he has no reference data with which he could perform any additional checks or demonstrate. The respondent himself cites the identity card number in this context: "However, it can be argued that the identity card number can be considered a more sensitive piece of data, while the implementing decree of March 14, 2022 expressly provides for evidence (e.g., driving license) that does not contain such data from the identity card."21  
56.

62.  
In this case, the Disputes Chamber finds that the processing of a copy of the identity card constitutes a violation of the general principle of minimal data processing, one of the five core principles of the GDPR. The fact that the government has included this specific processing in the implementing decision is therefore not in accordance with this principle and must be set aside or resolved with adequate technical and organizational measures. Such an assessment by the Disputes Chamber does not involve a contra legem interpretation but rather an application of the primacy of Union law over a national legal provision that is in conflict with it.  
63.  
The Disputes Chamber rules that the respondent violates Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR by processing an excess of personal data of the complainant that is not relevant and is not limited to what is necessary for the purpose of processing as established in the legal basis for this processing.  
II.8. The Storage Limitation  
64.  
According to the principle of storage limitation, as laid down in Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR, personal data may only be retained as long as necessary for the purposes for which they were processed. The implementing decree stipulates in Article 9, §1 that proof of identity verification is retained for 13 months by the provider of postal services.  
65.  
The respondent argues that applying a shorter period than provided for in the implementing decree would constitute a shortcoming of the legal obligations resting on the respondent. They therefore securely retain this proof of identification for a duration of 13 months, after which it is automatically deleted.  
66.  
The Disputes Chamber notes that neither in the regulations nor by the respondent in their defenses is there any justification presented for why the retention period is set at 13 months. However, the retention period for proof of identification corresponds with the imposed retention period for proof of dispatch.  
67.  
Given the importance of the registered shipment and the purpose of processing the personal data, namely to provide proof of identification of the recipient of a registered shipment, and given that the retention period is explicitly mentioned in the regulations, the Disputes Chamber rules that the respondent does not violate Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR.

II.9. The Responsibility of the Data Controller  
68. The complainant accused the respondent in his complaint that the employees of the respondent were not aware of the organizational measures or did not apply these organizational measures.  
69.
