I. Facts and procedure  
1.  
On September 21, 2020, the complainant filed a complaint with the Data Protection Authority against the defendant. The subject of the complaint concerns the fact that the defendant did not appropriately respond to the complainant's request to delete his personal data, specifically his email address, which the defendant uses to send unsolicited advertising to the complainant. The complainant indicates that he has repeatedly requested deletion. The defendant only responded after some time, stating that the complainant's data had never been included in its system and that the error leading to the sending of emails had been found, after which the defendant apologized. Despite this statement, the complainant continues to receive direct marketing emails from the defendant.  
2.  
On October 7, 2020, the complaint was declared admissible by the Frontline Service based on Articles 58 and 60 of the LCA, and the complaint was forwarded to the Contentious Chamber under Article 62, § 1 of the LCA.  
3.  
On November 21, 2022, the parties were informed by the Contentious Chamber of Decision 166/2022 of November 18, 2022, which orders the defendant, under Article 58.2, c) of the GDPR and Article 95, § 1, 5° of the LCA, to comply with the complainant's request to exercise his rights, specifically the right to erasure (Article 17.1 of the GDPR), and to delete the personal data in question within 30 days from the notification of this decision, subject to the defendant submitting a request for substantive processing in accordance with Articles 98 et seq. of the LCA.  
4.  
On November 24, 2022, the defendant requested a copy of the file (Article 95, § 2, 3° of the LCA), which was transmitted to him on the same day, namely November 24, 2022.  
5.  
On December 14, 2022, the Contentious Chamber received from the defendant the request for substantive processing in accordance with Articles 98 et seq. of the LCA, following Decision 166/2022 of November 18, 2022. In this regard, the defendant essentially argues that due to a material error in the spelling of the email address, the complainant received messages that were intended for a client of the defendant. Specifically, the material error concerned a single letter in the surname of the complainant to whom the email address […] belongs, distinguishing him from the actual client of the defendant who has the email address […].

II. Rationale  
10.  
The defendant explains that the complainant received a payment reminder email for an  
outstanding invoice on June 16, 2020. This email was sent by a collaborator of the  
defendant and was followed by a new reminder sent by email on June 30, 2020, by the  
defendant's representative on duty. This communication resulted in the complainant's email  
address being integrated into the personal communication group of the representative, and  
this address thus ended up in the defendant's files. On June 30, 2020, the complainant  
reacted by stating that there was no connection between him and the unpaid invoices. On  
July 31, 2020, a new reminder email was sent by the defendant to the complainant, who  
immediately responded in the same vein. On September 14, 2020, the complainant received  
a promotional email from the defendant's representative, after which the complainant  
immediately requested to cease these unsolicited communications. The defendant reacted on  
the same day by apologizing to the complainant and indicating that the cause of the problem  
had been found and that it was a clerical error in transcribing the email address, which  
explained why messages had been improperly sent to the complainant. On October 2, 2020,  
the complainant received an email from the defendant's representative with promotions for the  
following weeks. Following this, the complainant requested the representative to delete his  
email address. On the same day, the complainant's email address was removed from the  
personal communication group of the defendant's representative.  
11.  
Although the defendant claims that the complainant's email address was never part of its  
automated marketing file, the defendant acknowledges that the complainant's email address  
ended up in its files, albeit inadvertently, but that this was simply due to a clerical error  
which subsequently led to the inadvertent sending of correspondence that was not intended  
for him.
Decision on the Merits 163/2024 — 4/6  
12.  
The Disciplinary Chamber draws attention to the fact that the presence or absence of intent does not constitute a criterion for the processing of personal data within the meaning of Article 4.2) of the GDPR1. Although it was not the defendant's intention to send the email to the complainant, the mere fact that the email was indeed sent to the complainant is sufficient to qualify this sending as processing. The defendant also expressly indicates that the email address was found in their files. The Disciplinary Chamber therefore finds that the sending of the email falls within the scope of the GDPR as defined in Article 2.1 of the GDPR2.  
13.  
Each processing of data must be lawful within the meaning of Article 5.1 a) of the GDPR in conjunction with Article 6.1 of the GDPR. The Disciplinary Chamber emphasizes in this regard that the defendant wrongly assumed that the complainant was the client whose personal data, and more specifically the email address, had been processed under Article 6.1 b) of the GDPR and that they could write to them to obtain payment for outstanding invoices. The recovery of unpaid invoices constitutes the purpose pursued in this regard (Article 5.1 b) of the GDPR).  
14.  
It is a fact that with respect to the complainant, neither the principle of lawfulness (Article 5.1 a) of the GDPR) nor the principle of purpose limitation (Article 5.1 b) of the GDPR) were respected, in the absence of any relationship between the complainant and the defendant. The complainant was indeed not part of the defendant's clientele, so no unpaid invoice could be related to them, nor could it give rise to any processing of the complainant's personal data.  
15.  
However, the Disciplinary Chamber considers that this finding should not lead to any sanction in this case, and it releases the defendant from any prosecution, given that there was no other reason for sending emails to the complainant than a simple spelling mistake in the complainant's name, which differs by only one letter from the actual client of the defendant. As soon as this was established, the defendant immediately took the necessary steps to rectify the material error both in the defendant's address file from which the complainant's data was deleted on September 14, 2020, and in the personal communication group of the representative on duty as a collaborator of the defendant from which the complainant's email address was deleted.

October 2, 2020. The removal of the defendant's personal communication group occurred later, as it was only later that it was found that the complainant's email address had also been improperly included. However, the removal of the complainant's email address was carried out immediately when the error was also identified in the personal communication group. The defendant also demonstrates that since October 2, 2020, no further communication has been sent to the complainant.  
16.  
Although the Contentious Chamber ordered the defendant to comply with the exercise of the complainant's right to erasure in the context of the preliminary proceedings leading to the decision on the merits (Decision 166/2022 of November 18, 2022), it appeared in the proceedings on the merits, based on the detailed presentation of the factual elements, that one could conclude a violation of the purpose limitation principle and the lawfulness principle, but that this was exclusively due to a human error in the spelling of the email address, so that the correspondence was addressed to the complainant rather than the actual recipient. Human errors can never be completely excluded. What is important in this case is that as soon as the human error was established, the defendant immediately took the necessary steps to delete the complainant's email address and that the latter subsequently received no further communication from the defendant. The Contentious Chamber therefore considers that a decision on the merits aiming for a dismissal regarding the defendant is appropriate.  
17.  
For the sake of completeness, the Contentious Chamber considers that the access obtained by the complainant to the personal data and outstanding invoices of the defendant's client is not related to insufficient technical and organizational measures that the defendant would have taken to protect the personal data of the client in question against security risks. The fact that the email intended for the defendant's client was sent to the complainant cannot be linked to a problem of security of personal data processed by the defendant. The Contentious Chamber believes that no security measure can completely exclude a human error whereby an email is sent to the wrong recipient. It cannot therefore be concluded that by sending the email intended for a client (who is not the complainant) to the complainant, the defendant would have taken insufficient measures to protect the personal data of the client against security risks, so that no violation of Articles 32 and 33 of the GDPR can be established.
