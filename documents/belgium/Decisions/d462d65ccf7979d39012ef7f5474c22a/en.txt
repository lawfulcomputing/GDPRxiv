25. Although the principle of impartiality applies to administrative authorities, including the Contentious Chamber, it should be noted that according to consistent case law from the Council of State: “the general principle of impartiality must be applied to any body of the active administration. It is sufficient that an appearance of bias may have raised in the applicant a legitimate doubt regarding the ability to address their case impartially. However, this principle only applies insofar as it is compatible with the specific nature, and notably with the structure of the active administration. Furthermore, the impartiality of a collegiate body can only be called into question if, on the one hand, specific facts that raise suspicions of bias regarding one or more members of that body can be legally established and, on the other hand, it appears from the circumstances that the bias of that or those members may have influenced the entire body. It is up to the party alleging that the authority did not act with independence, impartiality, and diligence to provide evidence of this.”  

26. It is therefore up to the party invoking the breach of the principle of impartiality to provide evidence of specific facts from which it should be concluded that the principle of impartiality has been violated.  

27. Objective impartiality is distinguished from subjective impartiality.  

28. Furthermore, the complainant acts as a concerned party, and this is outside of any connection with her profession as a lawyer, which she previously practiced for the law firm in question. Since the defendant does not provide further elements to demonstrate that the Contentious Chamber would have given the appearance of bias, the Contentious Chamber cannot conclude that it has violated the principle of objective impartiality.

II.2. Regarding the admissibility and receivability of the complaint  
II.2.1. Regarding the constitution of noyb  
31.  
Article 80.1 of the GDPR states that "the data subject has the right to mandate a body, organization, or non-profit association that has been validly established in accordance with the law of a Member State, whose statutory objectives are of public interest and is active in the field of protecting the rights and freedoms of data subjects in the context of the protection of personal data concerning them, to lodge a complaint on their behalf, exercise on their behalf the rights referred to in Articles 77, 78, and 79, and exercise on their behalf the right to obtain compensation referred to in Article 82 when the law of a Member State provides for it."  
32.  
Article 220, §2 of the Framework Law specifies that:  
"§ 2. In the disputes provided for in paragraph 1, a body, organization, or non-profit association must:  
1° be validly established in accordance with Belgian law;  
2° have legal personality;  
3° have statutory objectives of public interest;  
4° be active in the field of protecting the rights and freedoms of data subjects in the context of the protection of personal data for at least three years.  
§ 3. The body, organization, or non-profit association provides proof, by presenting its activity reports or any other document, that its activity has been effective for at least three years, that it corresponds to its social purpose, and that this activity is related to the protection of personal data."  
33. The Contentious Chamber has already had the opportunity to express doubts regarding the compatibility of certain aspects of the Belgian provision with that of the GDPR.  
34. The primacy of European law implies the exclusion of any national provision that cannot be interpreted in accordance with a European legal standard—this exclusion being a duty for all state bodies, including judicial and administrative authorities responsible for applying European law within their respective competences.  
35. If there are reasons to believe that a law—understood in the sense of Article 22 of the Constitution—would violate "a fundamental right guaranteed in a totally or partially analogous manner by a provision of Title II of the Constitution as well as by a provision of European law [...]", it is then the responsibility of the jurisdiction before which this situation arises to refer a preliminary question to the Constitutional Court.  
36. The CJEU has ruled that an incidental procedure for the constitutional review of national laws respects Union law, provided that this procedure meets four conditions, which are as follows:  
- Other national jurisdictions remain free "to refer, at any time during the procedure they deem appropriate, and even at the end of the incidental constitutional review procedure, any preliminary question they deem necessary to the Court";  
- Other national jurisdictions remain free "to adopt any necessary measures to ensure the provisional judicial protection of the rights conferred by the legal order of the Union."
Decision on the merits 131/2024 — 12/33  
-  
Other national jurisdictions remain free "to leave the national legislative provision in question inapplicable, at the end of such incidental proceedings, if they consider it contrary to Union law";  
-  
"It is for the referring court to verify whether the national legislation in question can be interpreted in accordance with these requirements of Union law."  
37.  
The Contentious Chamber notes that the special legislator has limited the scope of Article 26, §4 of the Special Law to ordinary and administrative jurisdictions only.  
38. However, the Contentious Chamber is not within the jurisdiction of the judiciary – it is indeed an administrative authority.  
39. Consequently, Article 26, §4 of the Special Law does not apply to the Contentious Chamber, and furthermore, it has neither the obligation nor the possibility to submit a preliminary question to the Constitutional Court.  
40. Given that there is no other incidental procedure for constitutional review, the Contentious Chamber must directly do everything necessary to give full effect to the European legal standards it must apply within the scope of its competencies – namely, Article 80.1 of the GDPR concerning the present case.  
41.  
In continuation of what has been stated in point 33, the Contentious Chamber asserts that Article 220, §2, 1° of the Framework Law is contrary to the aforementioned provision of the GDPR in that it restricts the scope of the latter, making them thus irreconcilable.  
42.  
Consequently, the application of Article 220, §2, 1° of the Framework Law should be set aside.  
II.2.2. Validity of the mandate  
43. Regarding the representation mandate, the Contentious Chamber notes that it mentions the data of the mandator and the mandatary, and that the former mandates the latter to represent her before the Data Protection Authority (DPA) and to take any necessary action to enforce her rights regarding the collection and processing of her data on the defendant's website. The Contentious Chamber adds that, in the annexes to the complaint form, the mandate is titled as follows: "Document 1 – Representation Agreement under Article 80(1) GDPR."  
44. First of all, concerning this last element, the Contentious Chamber cannot agree with the defendant's reasoning when it claims that noyb attempted to "conceal" the absence of reference to Article 80.1 of the GDPR in the body of the mandate. On the one hand, noyb asserts that this mention was already present at the time of the signing of the mandate. On the other hand, the Contentious Chamber notes that the validity of the mandate must, if necessary, be examined at the time of the filing of the complaint. Therefore, it does not seem plausible that noyb sought to "conceal" the absence of reference to Article 80.1 of the GDPR – to the strict extent that the absence of reference to Article 80.1 of the GDPR would render the mandate invalid – given that it would have been sufficient to redo the mandate. Consequently, the mandate should be read in light of its title as an annex. Read this way, there is no doubt that the mandate was concluded within the framework of Article 80.1 of the GDPR.  
45. Next, regarding the possible contradictions of the mandate that the defendant points out, the Contentious Chamber recalls that it could not assess the mandate too restrictively. As an administrative authority, the Contentious Chamber oversees the proper implementation of the GDPR. In this capacity, it is empowered to impose one or more of the sanctions listed in Articles 95, §1 or 100, §1 of the LCA – and this, in particular, to protect the fundamental rights of the data subjects. The mandate, as it appears in the case file, allows for the identification of the parties to this contract, the data controller against whom the complainant addresses her grievances, the supervisory authority with which it is intended to file a complaint, and a reference to Article 80.1 of the GDPR under which the mandate is adopted. These elements justify the actions that noyb undertakes on behalf of and for the complainant before the DPA. Imposing further conditions on the mandate would undermine the oversight duty of the Contentious Chamber, as well as the rights of the data subjects. For all practical purposes, the Contentious Chamber specifies that the elements of the mandate cited above cannot be interpreted as setting any minimum threshold.  
46. Finally, and in any case, the Contentious Chamber notes that Article 17 of the Judicial Code does not apply to the Contentious Chamber, which is an administrative authority as explained in point 38.  
II.2.3. The lack of signature on the complaint  
47.  
Article 58 of the LCA provides that "Any person may file a complaint or a written request, dated and signed, with the Data Protection Authority."
Decision on the merits 131/2024 — 14/33  
48. Article 60 of the same Law states that, in the context of examining the admissibility of the complaints it receives, the SPL verifies that the complaint is "drafted in one of the national languages," that it "contains a statement of facts and the necessary indications to identify the processing it concerns," and that the complaint "falls within the jurisdiction of the Data Protection Authority."  
49. Contrary to what noyb argues, these two provisions should not be read separately, but rather together. Thus, the formal conditions prescribed by Article 58 of the LCA must also be taken into account in the admissibility examination referred to in Article 60 of the same Law.  
50. The Contentious Chamber notes that the complaint form was signed by the chairman of the board of directors of noyb, with the following mention: "For noyb."  
51. In this regard, the Belgian legislator clarified that the signature must come from "the competent person in the matter," but not necessarily from the complainant. Thus, it must be understood that at the very least, the representative may sign the complaint form. This is also derived from Article 80.1 of the GDPR, which states that the data subject has the right to mandate "an organization, a non-profit organization, or an association [...] to submit a complaint on their behalf, exercise on their behalf the rights referred to in Articles 77, 78, and 79 [of the GDPR]."  
52. As a legal entity, noyb must be represented by one of its members in the acts it performs. Thus, the chairman of the board of directors of noyb signed the complaint form – in the exercise of this function, which empowers him to perform such acts.  
53. It cannot be inferred from the mention "For noyb" that noyb acts as a complainant, since this mention specifically aims to engage the responsibility of noyb, and not that of the chairman of the board of directors of noyb in his capacity as a natural person.  
II.2.4. The interest to act  
54. The Contentious Chamber is not unaware of the content of its decision 22/2024; however, it is necessary to highlight the differences that distinguish the facts arising from the aforementioned decision from the facts arising from the present decision.  
55. Although the complainants share, in both decisions, the fact that they completed an internship at noyb, and, in this context, consulted websites that subsequently motivated the filing of a complaint with the DPA, it should not be overlooked that at the origin of the facts examined in decision 22/2024, noyb had implemented a large-scale plan aimed at filing dozens of complaints against multiple data controllers with various supervisory authorities – including the DPA. Furthermore, the complainant had explicitly acknowledged being assigned various files, among which was the website of the defendant in the aforementioned decision. These elements – combined with others – led the Contentious Chamber to consider that the mandate concluded between the complainant and noyb was then fictitious. However, such a conclusion cannot occur in the present case. The complainant – a French speaker – indeed consulted the French-speaking website of the defendant based on a personal initiative, which does not fall within the framework of the other coordinated projects of noyb. There is no principle that opposes noyb representing one of its employees or interns.  
56. Moreover, no link can be established between the present complaint and the complaints filed against the 15 Belgian websites mentioned in noyb's press release from July 2023. While it is clear that some coordination took place to a certain extent in this case, it is not established that this coordination occurred before the emergence of the complainant's grievances. In any case, the elements of the present case do not allow for the establishment that noyb exerted any pressure on the complainant.  
57. Therefore, nothing supports the assertion that the mandate is fictitious.

The Internet has significantly evolved practices and occupies the time of the majority of citizens, particularly young people. Thus, a consent that could be described as routine is established. Internet users navigate from website to website, from page to page, and are consequently confronted with a large number of cookie banners. In doing so, the warning effect of the cookie banner diminishes, and the individuals concerned may grant their consent by default, due to the fatigue thus caused. This observation is exacerbated when it is taken into account that data controllers sometimes implement designs that encourage users to accept the placement of cookies.  
65. These reasons then impose on the Contentious Chamber the duty to examine the present case with the utmost sensitivity.  
II.3.1. Regarding the articulation of the GDPR and the Framework Law with the guidelines  
66. The Contentious Chamber intends to respond here to the arguments put forward by the defendant regarding violation type 1 (see points 72 to 80, "Regarding the absence of a 'Reject All' button at the first level of the cookie banner") and 2 (see points 81 to 95, "Regarding the misleading use of button colors"), whereby the defendant asserts that neither the GDPR nor the Framework Law require the establishment of a 'Reject All' button at the first level of the cookie banner or the use of "buttons and characters of the same size, importance, and colors." The defendant adds that the guidelines of the EDPB and the supervisory authorities have no binding value as they constitute soft law.

It cannot be expected that the European legislator, in adopting this Regulation, intended to define in detail the specific modalities of all practices related to this act. On the contrary, it has established general and abstract rules to which the persons and entities concerned must comply. The supervisory authorities, in particular, must apply these principles and rules to concrete cases, which take place in the digital society, where technological developments have been made.

II.3.3. Regarding the misleading use of button colors  
81.  
Regarding the first argument of the defendant, concerning the fact that neither the GDPR nor the Framework Law would require data controllers "to use buttons and characters of identical size, importance, and color," the Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant is mistaken in thinking that the complainant would support this idea. The grievance in this regard claims that the cookie banner takes a form that encourages data subjects to click on the "Accept and Close" button, such that the consent granted does not meet the conditions set forth by the GDPR. Moreover, the Contentious Chamber refers the parties to points 66 to 80.  
82.  
Next, the Contentious Chamber observes that on the defendant's cookie banner (see below), three colors appear. The text uses a white font color, and the background of the banner is dark blue. The "Learn More" button, which ultimately allows the rejection of cookie placement, is the same blue as the background but is separated by white borders. The "Accept and Close" button is a striking orange color.  
83. As stated in point 72, consent must have been given freely, specifically, informed, and unequivocally. In line with what has been developed in point 71, requiring a data controller to ensure that the colors of the buttons used are not likely to clearly direct users towards the choice of consenting to the placement of cookies constitutes a requirement for respecting the notably free and unequivocal nature that consent must have as understood in Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
84. In this case, the Contentious Chamber is of the opinion that the use of colors by the defendant is likely to clearly encourage users to click on the "Accept and Close" button, in that the button stands out particularly from the rest of the cookie banner, and that it is thus on this button that users' attention will primarily gravitate. Therefore, the consent of the complainant collected by the defendant regarding the placement of cookies is not valid.

II.3.4. Regarding the modalities for withdrawing consent  
96. Article 7.3 of the GDPR states that “The data subject has the right to withdraw their consent at any time. The withdrawal of consent shall not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal. The data subject shall be informed thereof before giving consent.
