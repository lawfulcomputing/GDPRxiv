21. Employers have the obligation to promote the well-being of their workers and must adopt appropriate preventive measures to prevent psychosocial risks at work.10 This obligation also implies the creation of an internal service for prevention and protection at work. Employers may also call upon external services for prevention and protection at work.  
22. In this context, the defendant had entered into a contractual relationship with the employer of the complainant.  
23. For the Contentious Chamber, this point calls for several comments. First of all, the Contentious Chamber notes that the legislator, regarding the part of the opinion containing collective measures, considers that "it does not constitute personal data within the meaning of the law of December 8, 1992 since it does not concern information about a natural person but rather information related to the general prevention of the company." The Contentious Chamber recalls that the definition of personal data in force at the time the bill was adopted, contained in the law of December 8, 1992, is identical to that contained in Article 4.1 of the GDPR, which defines it as "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person."

31. It logically follows that to ascertain whether a document contains personal data, a concrete assessment must be made based on the information in question and its link to an identifiable person, directly or indirectly. The legislator's position of considering that a certain document does not inherently contain personal data and automatically excluding it from the material scope of the GDPR is not compliant with the latter and with the case law of the CJEU.  

32. Furthermore, the legislator adopts a contradictory position when it excludes this document from the scope of the GDPR but also provides for an exception to the right of access concerning it. These two positions are irreconcilable. The establishment of an exception to the right of access regarding certain data requires as a sine qua non condition that these data fall under the scope of the GDPR. The system set up by the legislator in the law on occupational well-being seems to create a confusion, whether intentional or not, between the right of access to documents within the framework of the occupational well-being law and the right of access as defined by the GDPR.  

33. However, the Court of Justice of the European Union clearly distinguishes between these two instruments, as it states that “the term ‘copy’ [in the sense of Article 15.3 of the GDPR] does not refer to a document as such, but to the personal data it contains, which must be complete.” The right of access as defined in Article 15.3 of the GDPR is therefore not a right to obtain access to documents but rather to data. This does not exclude, however, that a person may, when exercising the right of access, “obtain a copy of extracts from documents or even entire documents or extracts from databases that contain, among other things, said data, if the provision of such a copy is essential to enable the data subject to effectively exercise the rights conferred on them by this regulation, it being emphasized that account must be taken, in this regard, of the rights and freedoms of others.”  

34. Despite the apparent contradictions in the law on occupational well-being, the Contentious Chamber acknowledges that it has indeed established a derogatory regime to the right of access in Belgian legislation. The legality of this arrangement and its application by the defendant must therefore be analyzed by the Contentious Chamber under Article 23 of the GDPR.

II.2. On the merits of the case  
39. The grievances raised by the complainant fall into two categories. First, the complainant contests the refusal by the defendant to provide him with a copy of certain documents (namely, the personal notes of the prevention advisors, the opinions issued by the prevention advisors, including the annexes to these opinions) and claims access to a pre-interview completion form dated March 2015. The Disciplinary Chamber will first examine the scope of the complainant's right of access and then whether the access actually granted by the defendant complied with the provisions of Article 12 of the GDPR.  

II.2.1. On the violation of the right of access (Article 15 of the GDPR)  

II.2.1.1.  
Position of the parties  
40. The complainant has repeatedly requested a copy of the personal notes of the prevention advisors, the complete opinion, and the annexes of the opinions issued by the prevention advisors in the context of the interventions initiated at his request. According to him, the defendant could not refuse him access to these documents. He believes that the exception to the right of access provided for by the occupational health law is not compliant with the GDPR. He also complains that he did not receive the March 2015 completion form.  
41. The defendant argued that it could not legally grant him access to certain documents requested by the complainant due to an explicit exception to the right of access provided for in Article 32septiesdecies of the occupational health law, which restricts access to the following documents: the personal notes of the prevention advisors, the opinions and annexes issued by a prevention advisor following a formal intervention. It does not contest the obligation to provide the March 2015 completion form, but claims that it no longer has it.  
42. The complainant raised the fact that Article 32septiesdecies refers to the law of December 8, 1992. According to the complainant, exceptions to the GDPR are framed by Article 23 of the GDPR and must be specified in Chapter III of the law of July 30, 2018, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data (hereinafter "the law of July 30, 2018"). However, the exception in Article 32septiesdecies is not included there. And Article 32septiesdecies bases its exception on the law of December 8, 1992, which was repealed by the law of July 30, 2018. Therefore, according to the complainant, this exception to the right of access is no longer applicable, and the defendant cannot invoke it against him.  
43. According to the defendant, Article 32septiesdecies, as an exception to Article 15 of the GDPR, is still in force and implements Article 23 of the GDPR. The defendant argues that the list of exceptions provided for in Chapter III of the law of July 30, 2018, is not exhaustive.  

II.2.1.2.  
Position of the Disciplinary Chamber regarding compliance with the right of access and its limitations (Articles 15 and 23 of the GDPR)  
44. As the data controller, the defendant is required to comply with data protection principles and must be able to demonstrate that these are respected. It must also implement all necessary measures to this end, in accordance with the principle of accountability.  
45. Under Article 15.1 of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to obtain from the data controller confirmation as to whether or not personal data concerning them is being processed. When that is the case, the data subject has the right to access the said personal data as well as a series of information listed in Article 15.1, a) - h). The GDPR further provides, under Article 15.3, that the data subject has the right to obtain a copy of the personal data undergoing processing. Article 15.4 provides that this right to a copy must not adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others.  
46. The Disciplinary Chamber recalls that the right of access is one of the major requirements of the right to data protection: it constitutes the "gateway" that allows the exercise of the rights of the data subject.  

To this end, the data subject submits a dated and signed request to the data controller or to any other person designated by the King. The information is communicated without delay and at the latest within forty-five days of receipt of the request. The King may set the modalities for exercising the right referred to in paragraph 1.

While it is true that Article 280 of the law of July 30, 2018, states that the law of

This legislative measure does not necessarily have to be a law, an order, or a decree adopted by a parliament, but any legal norm that is sufficiently clear and precise, the application of which must be foreseeable for any concerned party.33 In this case, the derogation is provided for by a norm having the force of law. This law establishes a clearly defined list of documents to which the right of access does not apply and also provides for certain exceptions to this derogation. The derogation is deliberately limited and restricted to specific documents and provides for exceptions to this derogation. The law therefore uses sufficiently clear terms to indicate to everyone in what circumstances and under what conditions the limitation on the right of access occurs. The Contentious Chamber finds Article 32septiesdecies sufficiently clear, precise, and foreseeable. The first condition is thus met.  
52. Secondly, the derogation to the right of access provided for by Article 32septiesdecies of the Occupational Well-Being Act must pursue one of the reasons exhaustively listed in Article 23. Article 32septiesdecies is part of a law aimed at ensuring the safety and health of workers. In this context, prevention advisors must assist employers in detecting risks to workers and in applying protective legislation for workers. The Contentious Chamber therefore considers that this objective aims to ensure the protection of the concerned person or the rights and freedoms of others, as stated in Article 23.1, i). Recital 73 of the GDPR further indicates that the protection of the concerned person referred to in Article 23 includes social protection.

II.2.2. Regarding compliance with the modalities for exercising the right of access (Article 12 of the GDPR)  

II.2.2.3.  
General considerations  
70. Article 12 of the GDPR concerning the modalities for exercising their rights by data subjects provides that the data controller must facilitate the exercise of the rights of the data subject (Article 12 §2 of the GDPR) and provide information on the measures taken in response to their request as soon as possible and, at the latest, within one month of the request (Article 12 §3 of the GDPR). For more complex requests, or when the data controller receives a large number of requests, this initial one-month period may be extended by two months (Article 12 §3). When the data controller does not intend to comply with a request, they must notify the data subject of the reasons for their refusal within one month, along with information that a complaint against this refusal can be lodged with the data protection supervisory authority (Article 12 §4 of the GDPR).  
71.  
In the event of a refusal to comply with a request, the Contentious Chamber emphasizes the importance of the reasoning behind this refusal. Article 12 §4 of the GDPR must be read in conjunction with Article 12 §2: to facilitate the exercise of the rights of data subjects, a data controller must clearly and simply state the reason for their refusal. If this refusal is based on a legal provision, the relevant legal provision must be communicated to the data subject. Indeed, it would be difficult for a data subject to assess the relevance of a refusal and exercise their rights if the reasoning for such a refusal is based on an incorrect or missing legal basis.  
II.2.2.4.  
Position of the parties  
72.  
The complainant argues that the defendant granted him late access to the requested documents: his request was made on February 15, 2019, and the copy was sent to him on October 20, 2020. According to the complainant, the defendant did not comply with Article 12 of the GDPR. For its part, the defendant contests any violation of Article 12 and argues that it complied with the deadlines prescribed by the GDPR. Furthermore, the defendant asserts that its refusal to grant access cannot be confused with a lack of response.  
73.  
During the hearing, the defendant argued that the Contentious Chamber could not examine a potential violation of Article 12 of the GDPR on its part since only compliance with Articles 15 and 23 of the GDPR had been included in the letter from the Contentious Chamber dated May 27, 2021, informing the parties that the case would be treated on the merits based on Article 98 LCA (hereinafter, letter Article 98). The defendant refers in this regard to the case law of the Market Court.  
74.  
The Contentious Chamber first asserts that the defendant was able to address the potential violation of Article 12 of the GDPR in its conclusions, responding to the complainant's conclusions. It cannot therefore claim that this article was not raised by the complainant, and it should be noted that it was able to present its defense in this regard. Furthermore, given the obvious link between Article 12 of the GDPR, which prescribes the modalities for exercising the rights of the data subject, and Article 15 of the GDPR which regulates the right of access, the Contentious Chamber considers itself competent to rule on the alleged violations of Article 12 of the GDPR.  
75.  
Regarding the complainant's first exercise of the right of access for the personal notes of the prevention advisor, the complainant submitted his request on February 15, 2019. The defendant's agent responded to him on February 15, 2019, with a refusal based on the Royal Decree of April 10, 2014, concerning the prevention of psychosocial risks at work. Subsequently, on February 28, 2019, this refusal was justified and based on Article I.3-31, paragraph 2 of the Code on Well-being at Work, and Articles 32quaterdecies and quinquiesdecies of the law on well-being.  
76.  
The Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant responded to the complainant within the one-month deadline and justified its refusal. However, the legal basis invoked by the defendant is not relevant to justify this refusal. Indeed, it is Article 32septiesdecies that prohibits access to the personal notes of prevention advisors by workers. Article I.3-31 indicates the content of an individual file but is not relevant to limit the scope of the right of access of a data subject. Therefore, the defendant violated the provisions of Article 12.4 of the GDPR by invoking an irrelevant legal basis to justify its refusal.  
77.  
Regarding the second exercise of the right of access for his medical file55 and his psychosocial file on February 15 and 21, 2019, the Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant responded to this request on February 21, 2019.
