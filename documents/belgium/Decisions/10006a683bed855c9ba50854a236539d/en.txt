II. Reasoning  
II.1. Description of the Processing  
23.  
For clarification, the Disputes Chamber summarizes the various processing activities in the context of the innovation project "noise in the student neighborhood" (hereinafter: the 'innovation project') based on the technical documentation and the inspection report.  
24.  
In the context of the City of Things project of the second interested party, the defendant had requested and received subsidies to set up 30 sound sensors as a pilot project in the student neighborhood. These sensors would record all sounds from April 2022 to December 2022 and activate a type of 'nudging'6 in response to human noise disturbances for a period of several weeks in June and October 2022.  
25.  
The aim of the project is "to map and remediate noise pollution in the student neighborhood of Antwerp using technology."7 For this purpose, the data controller engages a processor who, through sound sensors and a server cluster with three sub-processors, will additionally train an existing AI model to classify environmental sounds and activate a nudging setup in the case of certain sound classes. A statistical analysis will evaluate the causes of noise pollution and the effects of the nudging.  
26.  
The existing AI model of the processor was already able to distinguish the following sound classes: people, birds, bell, engine, alarm, dog, glass, shot, siren, whistle.

II.1.1. Storage of raw audio files  
27.  
Once installed, the sound sensors continuously recorded ambient sound 24/7. These analog signals were converted within the sensor into successive digital signals of ten seconds (hereinafter referred to as ‘raw audio files’). These raw audio files were encrypted and stored on the sound sensor for a period of eight hours, including the coordinates of the sensor and the time of recording.  

II.1.2. Objective trigger  
28.  
From this raw audio file, the sound level was immediately calculated and sent to the Google cloud to serve as one of the 'triggers' for the further course of the innovation project. The sound level did not contain any personal data. When the recorded sound exceeded a certain threshold of sound level between 7 PM and 7 AM, thus activating the trigger, the sound sensor generated MEL-spectrograms of audio fragments of 10 seconds of the sound. These MEL-spectrograms are necessary because the classification of the sound is based on them.  

II.1.3. Subjective trigger  
29.  
The innovation project provided an app for local residents to report when noise disturbances occurred in their neighborhood. Based on these 'subjective' reports, a trigger was activated from the Google cloud to send the appropriate ten-second fragments of raw audio files from the sound sensor's memory to the server cluster to enable the training of the AI system.  

II.1.4. Determining sound classes: training of the AI system  
30.  
When an (objective or subjective) trigger was activated, a "recording" command from the Google cloud was sent to the sound sensor, which forwarded the relevant 10 seconds of raw audio files via a server to the processor. Employees of the processor listened to the fragments to label them in the predetermined classes (see paragraph 26). This labeling was forwarded to a third server, where the label was combined with the calculated MEL-spectrogram of the raw audio file. The AI model was trained on this third server using the labeled MEL-spectrograms.  

II.1.5. Statistical analysis  
31.  
Based on the classification of the sounds that had activated a trigger, an analysis was made of the occurrence of noise disturbances in the student neighborhood. This analysis could be consulted via a dashboard.  

II.1.6. Automatic nudging  
32.  
Based on the sound class determined after the activation of an objective trigger, the AI system, located in the server cluster, could activate a nudge in real-time in the immediate vicinity of the sound sensor where the objective trigger was observed. Since this procedure was still under development, only two limited periods were provided during the duration of the innovation project to set up and test a nudge installation. The ultimate goal of the innovation project was, on the one hand, to sufficiently train the AI model to (eventually) attempt to perform the classification of sounds entirely on the sensor itself and, on the other hand, to evaluate based on this classification which nudges would be effective in proactively addressing human nighttime noise.

The Dispute Chamber first assesses the processing of personal data within the innovation project. It distinguishes between the recording and processing of raw audio files and the calculation and processing of voiceprints.  
II.2.1. Raw Audio Files  
36.  
The Inspection Service establishes that sounds of professional quality are recorded 24/7 from thirty different sensors for a duration of forty weeks, without distinguishing between sounds with or without voices. These sounds are divided into ten-second fragments and digitized into raw audio files on the sensor itself. Under these circumstances, the Inspection Service states that it is inevitable that “an unpredictable range of special categories of personal data as referred to in Article 9.1 GDPR”13 is processed. This applies to both the separate ten-second fragments and the consecutive fragments of each ten seconds.  
37.  
Both the processor and the respondent assert that no processing of personal data took place, at most that such processing was purely speculative/hypothetical and was in no case demonstrated. Both primarily rely on the requirement of Article 4.1 of the GDPR that a person must be identified or identifiable, which would not be the case. They also state that any potential processing of personal data would be limited because the raw audio files were only forwarded to the server if a certain sound level was exceeded.  
Assessment of the Dispute Chamber  
38.  
Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person.” The Court of Justice has already ruled in several judgments that the words ‘any information’ indicate the intention of the EU legislator to give this concept a broad meaning, “which potentially extends to any kind of information, both objective information and subjective information, in the form of opinions or assessments, provided that this information ‘concerns’ the data subject.”14

39.  
First, the Dispute Chamber establishes that voices are being recorded. The inspection report demonstrates that the sound sensors permanently record sound and store it on the sensor for eight hours with professional quality and within the frequency range of the human voice.15 The European Data Protection Board (hereinafter ‘EDPB’) points out that “the voice of the data subject itself constitutes personal data”16. In this case, the location of the person speaking can also be inferred based on the location of the sensor that processed the voice.  
40.  
Second, the Dispute Chamber rules that the substantive information from the recorded conversations may contain personal data. Given that most of the thirty sound sensors are not higher than the first floor and that they permanently record sound with sufficient quality to capture conversations nearby, it can be stated with near certainty that conversations between passersby in this busy student area are recorded audibly, as stated by the Inspection Service. It is inevitable that during such conversations, information is exchanged between the participants about themselves or about an identified or identifiable person.  
41.  
In this context, the Dispute Chamber finds that it was not necessary for the purposes of the innovation project to process intelligible voices or conversations. The technical analysis of the Inspection Service has shown that it is possible to reduce the quality and duration of the recordings such that classification of sounds would still be possible, but no voices or conversations could be recognized. Neither the respondent nor the processor demonstrates that they have taken measures to make the processing of intelligible voices and conversations impossible.

II.10. Duty of Care Regarding the Processor

97. II.11. Transfer to third countries outside the EEA  
172. When personal data is transferred to countries outside the European Economic Area (hereinafter ‘EEA’), this constitutes a transfer of personal data. For the transfer of personal data to countries outside the EEA, the GDPR stipulates that this is only permissible if the level of protection provided by the GDPR is not undermined. This is the case if the country outside the EEA has an adequate level of data protection or provides additional safeguards for the transfer of data. If the European Commission has not made an adequacy decision, then appropriate safeguards must be established in another way to provide a sufficiently high level of protection.  
173. The Inspection Service finds that the processing did not comply with Chapter V of the GDPR since no risk assessment was conducted, upon which any appropriate safeguards could be based, while biometric personal data (voiceprints) were unsecured and available in the Google cloud.  
174. The respondent does not take a position regarding a possible violation of Chapter V of the GDPR.
