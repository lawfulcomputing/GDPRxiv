I. Facts and procedure  
I.1.  
The complaint  
1. On December 9, 2019, the complainant filed a complaint with the Data Protection Authority against the defendant.  
2. The facts underlying the complaint consist of direct marketing calls that continued despite the complainant exercising her right to object.  
3. On April 4, 2019, the complainant exercised her right to object (Article 21.2 of the GDPR) following unsolicited calls received and made, according to the complainant, by the defendant.  
4. On the same day, the defendant confirmed to the complainant that she had recorded the request to object in her database. Furthermore, the defendant asked the complainant to provide the calling phone number, considering the fraud and phishing activities that prevail. Thus, the defendant expressed the idea that the calling phone number might not belong to her but rather to a third party attempting to impersonate her.  
5. Still on the same day, the complainant responded that she could not provide the defendant with the calling phone number, given that the call had been made using the "masked call" function.  
6. On December 3 and 6, 2019, the complainant sent reminders to the defendant.  
7. On the same day, the defendant replied that in order to initiate an investigation, she needed, at a minimum, to know the calling phone number.  
8. On January 7, 2020, the complaint was declared admissible by the First Line Service based on Articles 58 and 60 of the LCA, and the complaint was forwarded to the Contentious Chamber under Article 62, § 1 of the LCA.  
9. On January 28, 2020, in accordance with Article 96, § 1 of the LCA, the request from the Contentious Chamber to conduct an investigation was forwarded to the Inspection Service, along with the complaint and the inventory of documents.  
10. On December 9, 2020, the investigation by the Inspection Service was concluded, the report was attached to the file, and it was transmitted by the general inspector to the President of the Contentious Chamber (Article 91, § 1 and § 2 of the LCA).

Despite the confirmation dated April 4, 2019, of the consideration of the complainant's opposition, Y continued to process the complainant's personal data for direct marketing purposes.  

The complainant indeed continued to receive telemarketing calls on her mobile number from call centers acting on behalf of Y at least until March 3, 2020 (step 3 / document 7).  

In his letter dated June 29, 2020 (step 8 / document 13), the DPO provides a very convoluted justification regarding the continued processing of the complainant's data for direct marketing purposes. He essentially states that:  
- the complainant's subscription request of November 6, 2018, did not result in the creation of a CRM file (note: but rather a customer account!) within Y;  
- her subscription modification request of May 29, 2019, did, however, result in the creation of a CRM file in her name;  
- the opposition request of April 4, 2019, being prior to the insertion of the complainant's data into the CRM file, did not pertain to the CRM file in her name but to the CRM file in her mother's name (!) for which the complainant's contact data was included;  
- the creation of a CRM file in the complainant's name after her opposition request would justify the continuation of telemarketing towards her.  

The Inspection Service considers that the very peculiar management of Y's CRM database cannot justify ignoring the complainant's opposition request.  

The argument that the complainant's opposition request was only reflected for the customer account in her mother's name while the complainant herself held a Y subscription in her name is unacceptable and does not excuse Y's shortcomings in this matter. It is worth noting in this regard that the complainant's mother no longer had a Y subscription at the time of the events (step 6 / document 11).  

Decision: Based on these elements, the Inspection Service finds that Y did not respect the complainant's right to oppose and continued to unlawfully process her data for direct marketing purposes, contrary to Articles 5.1.a, 6.1, and 21.3 of the GDPR.

a.2. Second finding  
The second finding made by the Inspection Service is drafted as follows:  

"Finding 2: Failure to facilitate the exercise of the complainant's rights  

In its email dated December 6, 2019, in response to the complainant's third complaint regarding a new call from a call center acting on behalf of Y for direct marketing purposes, the Y Data Protection Office asked the complainant to provide the call center's phone number in order to intervene (cf. step 1 / document 1).  

However, the complainant had previously explained during the email exchange with the Y Data Protection Office that the calls were coming from withheld numbers.  

Furthermore, by responding in this manner, the DPO service clearly did not take the trouble to check in Y's database the processing of the complainant's personal data that continued to be carried out for direct marketing purposes.  

By asking the complainant to provide the (withheld) call number of a call center operating on its behalf while neglecting to verify in its database the actual processing of the complainant's data, Y failed in its obligation to facilitate the exercise of the rights of the data subject.  

Decision: Based on these elements, the Inspection Service finds that Y did not comply with Article 12.2 of the GDPR in this instance."  

a.3. Third finding  
The third finding made by the Inspection Service is drafted as follows:  

"Finding 3: Absence of data protection by design and data protection by default

c.1. Administrative procedure of the Litigation Chamber of the Data Protection Authority

The present settlement proposal is made without prejudicial acknowledgment and does not in any way bind the Litigation Chamber regarding the position it is free to choose in the event of a refusal of the settlement proposal. In this regard, the Litigation Chamber refers in particular to the powers conferred upon it to ascertain or not the existence of an infringement and to impose sanctions, if necessary, under European law12 and Belgian law13. In this case, it is up to the Litigation Chamber to pursue the matter by other means, in cases where the conditions are not accepted by the defendant or where the Litigation Chamber decides to withdraw its settlement proposal.

When the settlement proposal is refused by the party to whom it is addressed, the Litigation Chamber continues to process the case.

It is understood that the procedure before the Litigation Chamber cannot be interpreted in a criminal sense; the settlement provided for in Art. 100, §1, 4° of the LCA cannot be confused with any of the alternative dispute resolution methods as they exist in criminal law14. In conclusion, the settlement has, in light of the LCA, a sui generis character.

c.2. Scope of the settlement

Furthermore, the Litigation Chamber states the specific facts situated in time and space on which this settlement proposal is based. Although it is not concluded here and now to ascertain the existence of infringements by the Litigation Chamber within the framework of this settlement proposal, it remains that the latter is conditioned by the facts stated in the complaint, which the complainant alleges. Therefore, the settlement proposal concerns certain facts precisely delineated in time, all being considered in a specific technical context. Facts that fall outside this strict scope are not taken into account for the settlement. The disputed period thus lies between the date of filing the complaint and the date of the settlement decision following this present proposal.

c.3. Steps and elements of the settlement procedure

The general framework of the settlement procedure of the Litigation Chamber is as follows:

1. The settlement under the LCA is made between:
- On the one hand, the Litigation Chamber, which commits to ending the procedure, thereby waiving the imposition of any corrective measures;
- And, on the other hand, the settling party, which commits to paying a sum of money and/or complying with certain additional conditions.
2. The settlement involves an acknowledgment of the facts by the defendant, but not an acknowledgment of the alleged breaches of the law in force.
3. The scope of the settlement is in any case limited to the facts that occurred during a strictly defined period. The settlement proposal or, where applicable, the settlement decision, clearly establishes this period.
4. The complainant had the opportunity to express their views on the settlement proposal, which was communicated to them on 20/09/2024.15 Nevertheless, the complainant did not respond within the prescribed time.
5. A settlement is formalized by a settlement decision, which transparently outlines the course of the procedure leading up to it and explicitly includes all the terms of the initial and modified settlement proposal following the

The settlement proposal constitutes the starting point of the settlement procedure.
