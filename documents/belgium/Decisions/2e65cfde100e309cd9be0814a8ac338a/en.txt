II.2.3. Assessment of the Contentious Chamber  
42. The Contentious Chamber recalls that in execution of Article 12.3 of the GDPR, "the data controller provides the data subject with information on the measures taken following a request made under Articles 15 to 22, as soon as possible and in any event within one month of receiving the request. If necessary, this period may be extended by two months, taking into account the complexity and number of requests. The data controller informs the data subject of this extension and the reasons for the delay within one month of receiving the request. When the data subject submits their request in electronic form, the information is provided electronically where possible, unless the data subject requests otherwise."2

43. The complainant having made a request to exercise their right to erasure based on Article 17 of the GDPR, Article 12.3 of the GDPR is applicable. The Dispute Chamber will therefore verify that the conditions of this article have been duly respected.  
44. Regarding the calculation of the one-month period within which the data controller must provide information on the follow-up to a request to exercise rights under the aforementioned Article 12.3, this calculation must be carried out in accordance with Union law. Furthermore, it is the date of receipt of the request by the data controller that triggers this period. In other words, the one-month period begins to run when the data controller has received a request, and it is not necessary in this regard that they have actually acknowledged it. The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) considers it good practice for data controllers to confirm receipt of requests in writing, for example, by sending emails (or postal mail, where applicable) to the individuals who made the request, confirming that their requests have been received and that the one-month period runs from day B to day C.  
45. Receipt by the data controller also implies that the request has reached them through one of their relevant channels as specified below.  
46. The Dispute Chamber reminds that according to the aforementioned Guidelines, the EDPB encourages data controllers to provide the most appropriate and user-friendly communication channels, in accordance with Articles 12.2 and 25 of the GDPR, to enable the data subject to submit an effective request. This may include, for example, communication details of the data controller provided in their communications directly addressed to the data subjects or contact details publicly provided by the data controller, for instance, in their privacy policy or in other mandatory legal notices of the data controller (e.g., the contact details of the owner or the company on a website).

Nevertheless, the EDPB specifies that if a data subject submits a request using a communication channel provided by the data controller but different from the one indicated as preferable, this request must, in general, be taken into consideration, and the data controller must process it accordingly. Data controllers must indeed make all reasonable efforts to ensure that the exercise of the data subject's rights is facilitated.

According to the EDPB, the data controller is, however, not obliged to respond to a request sent to a random or incorrect email address (or postal address) not directly provided by the data controller, or to a communication channel that is clearly not intended to receive requests concerning the rights of the data subject, if the data controller has provided an appropriate communication channel to the data subject.

54. Regarding the sending on June 2 to the address [address 2], which is not specifically dedicated to the exercise of the rights of the data subject according to the defendant, at the very least in light of the articles published on the website of newspaper A, the Contentious Chamber is of the opinion that if this address is indeed not the one referenced by the privacy policy of newspaper A, the request of the complainant addressed to it should nonetheless have been processed by the defendant.  
55. Indeed, it appears from the examination of the documents that the address [address 2] is provided in a document titled "Privacy Policy – Y (name of the defendant)" (document 10 of the file produced by the defendant). As a reminder, the privacy policy available on the website of newspaper A indeed qualifies the defendant as the data controller and bears the same title as this document (document 9 of the file produced by the defendant). In the introduction of these two texts, in the second paragraph, there is an explicit reference to newspaper A in these terms: "Y (the defendant) is a group of companies active in the field of information (followed by a list of newspapers including newspaper A) etc." Further on in both documents, it is specified, under the purposes of processing pursued, that personal data is processed for journalistic purposes, including dissemination and archiving for the purpose of informing the public using any media. Generally speaking, the structure of the two privacy policies is identical, the headings of the sections are the same, and their content is similar, with the exception of point c) under section V.C. of the policy available on the website of newspaper A, which relates to audience measurement of several sites, including that of newspaper A. However, it cannot be inferred by someone consulting this privacy policy that it would be specific to newspaper A. Generally, the privacy policy available on the website of newspaper A does not specify at any time that it is limited to the data processing carried out by said newspaper. Such information is also not found on the site as such. The mere fact that this privacy policy is available on the website of newspaper A is not sufficient to dismiss outright the relevance of the other privacy policies of the defendant's group, particularly for the reasons related to their similarity outlined above. Another difference between the two privacy policies lies in the fact that the one available on the website of newspaper A mentions the contact address [address 1], while the one available on the defendant's group website mentions [address 2]. The difference is subtle, and in both cases, the internet user clearly identifies the terms "privacy" and "Y," that is, the name of the defendant, in each of these two addresses. Moreover, these are not mentioned in the section "How to exercise

56. In support of the above, the Contentious Chamber considers that the address [address 2] provided by the defendant in the privacy policy available on the website bearing its name should also be regarded as an official channel for exercising the rights of the data subjects. Therefore, the complainant cannot be criticized for also sending her requests to this address, especially in light of the reminder in the absence of any response to her previous communication sent 6 weeks earlier to the address specifically referenced by the A newspaper's website. The request from the complainant sent to this address should have been processed. We are far from the scenario described by the EDPB of sending to a random address or to an address clearly not intended to receive such requests.  
57. In any case, these emails from June 2, 2020, which further relay the previous communications from April 17, 2020, meant that the defendant could not, as of June 2, at the very least, ignore that the request had previously been sent to her on April 17, 2020, notably to the address [address 1] which she qualifies as "correct." The receipt of the message at the defendant's DPO address on June 2, 2020, is also not contested.  
58. In light of all the elements and findings that precede, the Contentious Chamber considers, on the one hand, that at the very least as of June 2, 2020, the defendant had indeed received the complainant's request which was addressed to her via her DPO as well as to the address [address 2] which she could not ignore, and on the other hand, that she refrained from responding to this request within the month prescribed by Article 12.3 of the GDPR since her first response to the complainant dates from September 30, 2020. Consequently, the defendant violated Article 12.3 of the GDPR.

The withdrawal is merely one element that the Contentious Chamber may take into account when deciding, if necessary, to dismiss the complaint.

In this case, the Contentious Chamber notes that this withdrawal is motivated by the execution, confirmed by the complainant, of the judgment of the French-speaking Court of First Instance of Brussels dated (…).
