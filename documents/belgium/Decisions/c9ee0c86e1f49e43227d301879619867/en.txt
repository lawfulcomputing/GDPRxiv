II. Reasoning  
II.1. Context  
46. Since its creation in 2010, Freedelity has offered technology aimed at simplifying the collection and updating of personal data, notably using data contained in the chip of the Belgian electronic identity card (hereinafter, "eID").  
47. Freedelity's services allow for the centralization of commercial benefits offered by various brands to consumers, as well as the loyalty cards or proof of purchase of these consumers. These services are offered both (i) by Freedelity to its clients of the "brands" type (under a B2B model, within the framework of the CustoCentrix service), and (ii) by Freedelity to consumers (under a B2C model).
Decision on the merits 146/2024 – 7/67  
48. If the article in L'Echo mentioned above focused on the presentation of CustoCentrix, from the inspection phase, the investigation concentrated on the "Freedelity file," maintained by Freedelity as the data controller, this file being largely fed by data collected by the brands.  
49. As explained by Freedelity in its responses to the inspection service (document 5 of the file), "the Freedelity file indeed allows for the personal identification of each consumer, access to the Myfreedelity portal, as well as the management of the process of mutualizing the updating of consumer data among Freedelity's clients."  
50. The Inspection Service analyzed only the compliance of the processing related to the Freedelity file, and consequently (1) the collection of personal identification and contact data, (2) the mutualization of this personal data, and (3) the transfer of identity data contained in the Freedelity file to third parties such as Z1 and Z2.  
51. The Contentious Chamber will successively analyze the legality of the processing (1) and (2) mentioned above in section II.5. However, it notes that the investigation report of the Inspection Service does not contain sufficient elements to allow for an in-depth examination of processing (3), particularly regarding possible legal bases and the roles of the actors involved. Consequently, this decision will not address this processing (3).

II.3.1. The questioning of the director of the General Secretariat (at the time, also president of the Management Committee) by the director of the Knowledge Center  
54. The defendant believes that the director of the Knowledge Center at the time had no authority to question the director of the General Secretariat, whether in his capacity as a member of the Management Committee or as the director of the Knowledge Center.  
55. For all useful purposes, the Contentious Chamber recalls that the Management Committee is composed of the five directors of the APD (Article 12 of the LCA). As such, the director of the Knowledge Center was, alongside this role, a member of the Management Committee at the time of the questioning of the director of the General Secretariat. Furthermore, the director of the General Secretariat at the time was also the president of the Data Protection Authority, and thus, in this capacity, chaired the Management Committee in accordance with Article 13§1 of the LCA.  
56. Article 10§1 of the LCA states that "The Management Committee monitors developments in technological, commercial, and other areas that impact the protection of personal data" (emphasis added).  
57. It appears from the documents in the file that the director of the Knowledge Center (a member of the Management Committee) proposed to the president of the Management Committee to examine a service from Freedelity, the operation of which was described as innovative in an article from L'Echo published on July 8, 2019, titled "Freedelity proposes to mutualize customer data management."  
58. The APD, through its predecessor the Commission for the Protection of Privacy (hereinafter "CPVP"), was aware of the existence of the service proposed by Freedelity, consisting of an innovative technology allowing access from one's identity card to economic benefits (e.g., points accumulated on a loyalty card). However, the article from L'Echo presented an evolution of the technological service in question: "Today, the company from Nivelles is taking it a step further. With Custocentrix, it is launching a cloud platform that allows retailers to better organize and keep up to date all personal and behavioral data of their customers."  
59. It should be noted that an evolution of a technological service offered by a private company requiring the use of the Belgian identity card, which contains a highly sensitive and regulated unique identifier (the national register number), has an impact on the protection of personal data in Belgium. If the members of the Management Committee of the APD were not able to understand the extent of

the impact of this development based on a simple newspaper article, nor whether the handling of the national registration number was involved in this development, they fully assumed their responsibility by deciding to analyze said development based on Article 10 of the LCA. Any contrary interpretation of this article would limit this monitoring power specifically granted to the members of the Steering Committee and would render it meaningless. The director of the Knowledge Center was therefore justified in formally requesting the president of the Steering Committee to include this discussion on the agenda of the Steering Committee meeting on August 20, 2019.  
60. Under the conditions mentioned in the two preceding paragraphs, any member of the Steering Committee is authorized to formally request the president of the Steering Committee to include a discussion regarding such monitoring on the agenda of the Steering Committee meetings. For the Contentious Chamber, this request falls within the framework of the obligation to monitor developments in technological fields, which is incumbent upon the APD and therefore, primarily, upon the members of the Steering Committee. Such monitoring requires the possibility of an internal request among members of the Steering Committee and is thus perfectly justified.  
61. A member of the Steering Committee wished to place on the agenda a discussion concerning the new service proposed by Freedelity, in order to allow the Steering Committee to monitor this development in the technological field. This legitimate request occurs within the framework of Article 10§1 of the LCA and is therefore in accordance with the law.  

II.3.2. Sending a letter of request for information to the defendant by the president of the General Secretariat (at the time, also president of the Steering Committee)  
62. First, the defendant believes that following the decision of the Steering Committee to monitor the Freedelity service, the General Secretariat exceeded its authority by sending a letter of request for information to the defendant (document 2 of the file). It is based in particular on a report from the Court of Auditors which, according to it, criticizes the fact that the General Secretariat assumes a "filtering role for files." Secondly, the defendant argues that sending this letter of request for information in Dutch to the defendant is contrary to the law on the use of languages in administrative matters and that, by application of Article 58 of the law of July 18, 1966, the nullity of this act must be recognized and, by extension, that of the entire procedure.

63. Firstly, the Contentious Chamber emphasizes that during the meeting of the Steering Committee, its members decided that a letter could be sent based on Article 20§1,1° of the LCA, after consulting the files that had previously been opened regarding the defendant with the First Line Service (“SPL”). The first letter was drafted by the General Secretariat and sent to the defendant on August 30, 2019.  
64. The president of the APD (and thus of the Steering Committee) was assisted in the execution of his tasks by the General Secretariat (Article 13§3 of the LCA). Furthermore, the General Secretariat has its own mission of "monitoring" technological developments that impact the protection of personal data (Article 20§1,1° of the LCA). This mission presents a certain redundancy with that of the Steering Committee, which consists of "monitoring" developments in the technological field affecting the protection of personal data (Article 10 of the LCA). However, the mission of "monitoring" of the General Secretariat implies, a priori, a more active level of control than that involved by the notion of "monitoring" incumbent upon the Steering Committee. Moreover, the Steering Committee does not have its own department and cannot ensure its monitoring without going through another body of the APD for assistance, which in this case was the General Secretariat, whose director chaired the Steering Committee.  
65. The Contentious Chamber does not share the defendant's interpretation that the General Secretariat would have arrogated to itself "investigative" powers (a mission solely incumbent upon the Inspection Service) or "filtering of files." In the present case, the General Secretariat sent a dozen neutral questions regarding the operation of the new Freedelity service, in order to understand its functioning. The General Secretariat is indeed authorized to exercise such monitoring under Article 20§1,1° of the LCA, which is entirely distinct from the investigative power reserved for the Inspection Service (Article 28 of the LCA).  
66. Thus, the criticisms of the Court of Auditors regarding the fact that the General Secretariat would operate a role of filtering files are not relevant to the present case, since in this instance, the file was opened at the request of a member of the Steering Committee and the Steering Committee decided to send a letter on August 20, 2019. The General Secretariat merely assisted the president of the Steering Committee while remaining within the limits of its legal competencies, and, in this way, allowed the Steering Committee to ensure the monitoring of the developments in the technological field in question.

67. Prohibiting the General Secretariat from contacting the relevant stakeholders regarding technological developments in order to fully assume its mission of "oversight" would amount for the APD to seeking the intervention of another body (for example, the Inspection Service) to ensure this oversight. Such a mode of operation would be entirely contrary to the spirit of the LCA, and to the logic of the internal distribution of competencies among the bodies of the APD, as explained above: it is not only provided in the text that the General Secretariat follows technological developments, but it is also logical that the General Secretariat assists the Steering Committee in the execution of its tasks, particularly when Article 13§3 of the LCA stipulates that the president of the APD – thus the president of the Steering Committee – is assisted in the execution of his tasks by the General Secretariat. Furthermore, the Inspection Service could not be seized at this stage, since its referral cannot originate from a request from the General Secretariat (Article 63 of the LCA) and no procedure justifies the sharing of an investigation by the Inspection Service with the General Secretariat, which must remain confidential by principle (Article 28 of the LCA).  
68. For these reasons, the General Secretariat remained within the limits of its competencies by contacting Freedelity, in the context of its oversight mission, in order to personally assess the impact of a technological development on data protection, as provided by Article 20§1,1° of the LCA, to inquire about the technological development that constitutes the new service offered by Freedelity, a service whose impact was certain due to the sensitive nature of the processing involved requiring the use of the identity card. Therefore, sending a letter requesting information regarding the processing of personal data in the context of the new service from Freedelity constitutes a proportionate measure within the framework of the oversight mission of the General Secretary and the monitoring of technological developments under the purview of the Steering Committee.  
69. (free translation). In this case, Document 2 of the file was handled by the defendant without difficulty, and its sending occurred at a stage prior to the investigation by the Inspection Service.

II.3.3. Sending a note to the Management Committee by the General Secretariat (“DIRCO Report – Preliminary Information Letter – Freedelity”)  
74. First, the defendant criticizes the fact that the General Secretariat drafted a note (“Preliminary Information Letter”) for the Management Committee based on the responses provided by the defendant (document 6 of the file). She also criticizes that this note uses elements from the contacts between the CPVP and the responses provided by Freedelity to the questions from the General Secretariat. Secondly, the defendant claims that the document titled “probe letter” attached to the decision of the Management Committee of December 6, 2019, is not included in the file. She adds that the use of the term “probe” demonstrates the establishment of exploratory, abusive, unfair, and arbitrary procedures. She concludes that, in this case, the APD had no serious indication at the time it contacted Freedelity.  
75. First, the Contentious Chamber notes that following the defendant's response to the request for information letter, an opinion was drafted by the General Secretariat for the Management Committee based on the responses provided (document 6 of the file). The Management Committee decided on this basis, on December 6, 2019, to request the Inspection Service to open an investigation.  
76. As previously stated, the Contentious Chamber recalls that the General Secretariat has a supervisory mission (Article 20§1, 1° of the LCA). When this supervisory mission follows a request for follow-up from the Management Committee (Article 10 of the LCA), it is logical that a document be established based on the responses to the questions posed, to enable the Management Committee to ensure the continuity of its follow-up (Article 10 of the LCA).  
77.  
Indeed, to follow “the developments in the technological field” (emphasis added) in the sense of Article 10 of the LCA, it is necessary for the Management Committee to take into account all relevant information for understanding such developments. The Management Committee therefore legitimately cross-referenced and used the information provided by Freedelity during its contacts with the CPVP, of which the APD is a continuation.  
78. The provision of necessary information for follow-up cannot be carried out by the Management Committee itself since it does not have a department - as previously indicated - and must therefore necessarily be carried out by an organ of the APD. In this case, the General Secretariat was the natural department of the APD for this purpose: the request for information already came from the General Secretariat, which logically ensured the follow-up of its correspondence, not to mention that this organ is entrusted by Article 10 of the LCA with a mission of monitoring technological development.  
79. The Contentious Chamber concludes that the General Secretariat was perfectly justified in drafting an opinion for the Management Committee by gathering the information made available to it so that the Management Committee could decide to open an investigation subsequently. It is only based on the information reported by the General Secretariat that the Management Committee was able to assess the follow-up to be given to the procedure. No opinion or recommendation binds the Management Committee, which is sovereign in its assessment. In this instance, the Management Committee decided that the follow-up to the procedure would be the implementation of Article 63.1° of the LCA.  
80. Secondly, the document titled “probe letter,” the legality of which the defendant contests, is indeed document 6 of the inventory, which has been part of the file since the beginning of the case and was provided to the defendant when she requested a copy of the file.  
81.

125. As a preliminary matter, the Contentious Chamber does not dispute that the principle of impartiality applies to administrative authorities. However, it should be emphasized that the principle of impartiality "must be reconciled with the structure of the active administration," as will be described below in paragraph 127.  
126. The Contentious Chamber of the APD is presided over by Mr. Hijmans. The proper functioning of the Contentious Chamber and the APD requires that the president be available and able to sit on decisions on the merits (articles 33§1 and 92 to 107 of the LCA), as well as within the Management Committee, as evidenced by the dual competence of the directors of the APD bodies established by article 12 of the LCA.  
127. According to consistent case law from the Council of State:  
"The general principle of impartiality must be applied to any body of the active administration. It is sufficient that an appearance of bias may have raised a legitimate doubt in the applicant regarding the ability to address their case impartially. However, this principle only applies insofar as it is compatible with the specific nature, and notably with the structure of the active administration. Furthermore, the impartiality of a collegial body can only be called into question if, on the one hand, specific facts that raise suspicions of bias regarding one or more members of that body can be legally established and, on the other hand, it appears from the circumstances that the bias of that or those members may have influenced the entire body. It is up to the party alleging that the authority did not act with independence, impartiality, and diligence to provide proof of this."  
128. Therefore, it is up to the party invoking the breach of the principle of impartiality to demonstrate the existence of specific facts from which it should be concluded that the principle of impartiality has been breached. If the facts concern a member of a collegial body, the party invoking the breach of the principle of impartiality must demonstrate that the facts they identify are likely to have affected the impartiality not only of their author but also of at least a majority of the members of the collegial body in question.

2. On the responsibility for processing  
215. A data controller is defined as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing” (Article 4.7 of the GDPR) (emphasis added). This is an autonomous concept specific to data protection regulation, which must be assessed based on the criteria it establishes: determining the “purposes” and “means” of processing involves deciding on the “why” (the reason or objective of the processing) and the “how” (the manner in which this objective will be achieved)53.  
216. In some cases, responsibility is considered joint. For there to be joint responsibility in data processing, it is necessary for two or more entities to participate together in determining the purposes and means of the processing. This participation can manifest itself through a common decision or through converging decisions that complement each other and are essential for the realization of the processing. A key criterion is that the processing could not take place without the contribution of each party, which means that the processes are inseparable and inextricably linked54.  
217. The Contentious Chamber reminds that in the case of joint responsibility, Article 26 of the GDPR requires joint controllers to ensure, through a contract, that they mutually comply with the GDPR.  
218. According to the EDPB guidelines on the concepts of controller and processor55, “Joint participation may take the form of a common decision made by two or more entities or arise from converging decisions adopted by two or more entities, when the decisions complement each other and are necessary for the realization of the processing in such a way that they have a concrete effect on the determination of the purposes and means of the processing. An important criterion is that the processing would not be possible without the participation of both parties in the sense that the processing by each party is inseparable from that of the other, that is to say, inextricably linked.

In other words, only the data essential for achieving the objectives of the processing should be collected, thus avoiding any excessive collection of information that would not be directly useful or justified by the intended purposes. As stated previously, Article 25.1 of the GDPR establishes the obligation of data protection by design and by default, implying the implementation of concrete measures that must be applied from the definition of the means of processing.

II.5.6. Finding 5: Principle of limitation of the retention of personal data (art. 5.1.e, 5.2, 24 and 25.1 of the GDPR)  
279. The defendant claims that retaining data for a period of 8 years in the context of managing the Freedelity file is appropriate. According to the defendant, the starting point for this retention period is calculated from the last "activity" of the individual concerned (e.g., checkout).  
280. It argues that the 8-year data retention period is justified firstly by the specific purpose of the Freedelity file, which requires regular updates and immediate accessibility of the data. Secondly, it emphasizes the legitimate economic interest of retaining the data for a sufficient duration to allow for optimal use of its service. This retention period is also justified by accounting and tax considerations, both for the clients and for the company itself. It highlights that the legal warranty for consumer goods of 2 years, which can be extended by merchants, necessitates a data retention period longer than the strict 2 years proposed by the Inspection Service.  
281. The Litigation Chamber recalls that under Article 5.1.e of the GDPR, data must be retained in a manner that ensures they are not kept in a form allowing the identification of individuals for longer than necessary for the purposes for which they are processed. The data controller is responsible for complying with this principle and must be able to demonstrate that it is being adhered to.

respected (arts. 5.2 and 24 of the GDPR). The Contentious Chamber refers to its previous findings regarding art. 25 of the GDPR (see paragraph 263).  
282. The Contentious Chamber finds that the arguments of the defendant are insufficient to justify such a long retention period. Indeed, while the regular updating of the file and the immediate accessibility of the data are necessary to ensure quality customer service, it should be emphasized that these objectives can be achieved with a retention period significantly shorter than 8 years.  
283. Regarding the retention periods invoked by the defendant, the Chamber notes that these primarily apply to purposes distinct from the management of the Freedelity file, for which Freedelity does not act as data controller. Indeed, the legal provisions concerning guarantees mainly relate to the relationships between the consumer and the seller. 285. In this case, the Chamber believes that a retention period of a maximum of 3 years from the last activity would be sufficient to meet the needs of the defendant while respecting the rights of the data subjects.
