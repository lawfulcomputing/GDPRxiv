II.2. On the procedure
II.2.1. Referral of the Steering Committee by the General Secretariat:
26. The defendant argues in its conclusions that the General Secretariat of the APD is not competent to handle notifications of data breaches under Articles 19 and 20 of the LCA. Consequently, the competence of the General Secretariat to analyze notifications of data breaches and then draft a note for the Steering Committee is not established. In any case, said note should have been sent at least one week before the meeting of the Steering Committee in accordance with Article 4 of the Internal Regulations; however, the note from the General Secretariat is undated.
Decision on the Merits 166/2024 — 7/50

27. The Dispute Chamber recalls that Article 4 of the Internal Rules of Procedure (IRP) provides that "For all cases requiring a strategic decision from the management committee, a proposal is prepared by the competent director through a note. Unless in cases of urgent necessity, the file will be made available to the members at least one week before the meeting, if necessary with the required annexes. During the meeting, the file will be explained by the competent director."

28. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) stipulates in Article 33§1 that "In the event of a personal data breach, the data controller shall notify the breach to the competent supervisory authority..." without distinction of the department or service of the authority competent to handle said breach. The Dispute Chamber notes that neither the Law on the Protection of Personal Data (LCA) nor the IRP assigns exclusive competence to a department of the Data Protection Authority (DPA) to handle notifications of personal data breaches. Contrary to what the defendant claims during the hearing, there is no legal vacuum on this point, as the GDPR provides that such competence lies with the supervisory authorities. If, as in this case, national law does not provide anything, it remains the responsibility of each authority to carry out the missions conferred upon it by the GDPR, in order to give useful effect to the text.

29. The General Secretariat, one of whose executive tasks is to formulate opinions within the framework of Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) (Article 20§1, 3° of the LCA), has the necessary experience to assess the risks associated with data processing implemented by data controllers. Furthermore, it has the executive task of monitoring technological developments that impact the protection of personal data (Article 20§1, 3° of the LCA), which requires monitoring the technologies used by hackers, which can be described in the data breach notification forms.

30. In fact, the General Secretariat is a department of the DPA with privileged expertise to analyze the risks arising from a data breach notified to the DPA.

31. On the other hand, the director of the General Secretariat has the competence, like any other director, to present a file to the Management Committee for review with a view to possibly activating Article 63.1° of the LCA. Given the developments outlined above (paragraphs 29 and 30), it appears that the director of the General Secretariat was competent, without excluding that other directors could have done the same, to propose a note to the Management Committee, based on his analysis of the data breach notification form, in accordance with Article 4 of the IRP.

32. Furthermore, the Dispute Chamber observes that while Article 4 of the IRP requires the competent director to submit the file to the members of the Management Committee at least one week before the meeting, the IRP does not require the director to date the note itself. In this case, the DPA proposed to the defendant to acknowledge before the hearing that this provision of the IRP had been observed, since the note was sent by email one week before the meeting of the Management Committee. The defendant did not contest this date during the hearing.

33. In any case, this provision falls under the internal organizational rules of the DPA and cannot lead to the nullity of the procedure, as claimed by the defendant. This time limit, which is an indicative and not mandatory deadline, aims to protect the directors in their functions and not to confer rights to the parties in a procedure. Therefore, this deadline has no effect regarding third parties and cannot be invoked by the defendant to request the annulment of the present procedure. It should also be noted that this deadline was respected.

34. In conclusion, the director of the General Secretariat has validly complied with the procedure prescribed in Article 4 of the IRP by drafting a note to the Management Committee. This note was submitted to the members of the Management Committee at least one week before the meeting. The arguments of the defendant are therefore unfounded.

II.2.3. Serious Indications Retained by the Management Committee:  
45. The defendant argues that the indications retained by the Management Committee to establish that there were serious indications of the existence of a practice likely to lead to a violation of the fundamental principles of personal data protection are erroneous. It contests that the reasoning of the Management Committee is established by reference to the note from the General Secretariat. Finally, it explains that the fact that the hospital was the victim of a cyberattack does not stem from any fault on its part and does not constitute a serious indication within the meaning of Article 63.1° of the LCA.  
46. The Disciplinary Chamber recalls that under the powers conferred on the Management Committee, it is up to them to determine whether serious indications reveal the existence of practices that may lead to a violation of the fundamental principles of data protection (Article 63.1° of the LCA). As the Market Court has recalled, this is a discretionary power of the DPA.  
47. The Council of State accepts reasoning by reference, provided that the document referred to is part of the file, which is the case here (Council of State ruling of May 7, 2013, No. 223.440). Indeed, the decision of the Management Committee refers to the note that was provided to it and is based on it to retain the following indications:  
a. The shutdown of the hospital's computer network, which led to the cancellation of scheduled operations and consultations as well as the closure of the emergency services.  
b. The degree of sensitivity of the data involved in the breach, including in particular the national number, the social security identification number, genetic and biometric data, data related to health, care, sexual life or sexual orientation, criminal convictions, extracts from the criminal record, the content of electronic communications, and financial data.  
c. The indication in the form of the possible impact of the data breach on the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals, such as the unavailability of data or treatments preventing the continuation of the expected service.  
d. The large number of people affected, potentially 300,000 individuals.  
e. The existence of a previous significant data breach of the "ransomware" type dated [date] 2019. On this point, the defendant confirmed that no ransom had been paid.

48. This information was based on the description of the data breach, as outlined in the initial and supplementary notification forms submitted to the DPA by the hospital. It was only through the subsequent investigation conducted by the Inspection Service that the latter was able to somewhat contextualize the extent of this breach and the previous breach from 2019.  
49. However, the investigation did not fully clarify the exact circumstances of the breach (see paragraph 106 and following regarding the preservation of logs). In any case, if erroneous information was communicated to the DPA through these notifications, the Management Committee cannot be criticized for using it to characterize serious indications justifying the opening of an investigation. At the time of the Management Committee's decision and based on the elements available to it upon reviewing the initial notification, the Management Committee sovereignly determined that there were serious indications justifying the opening of an investigation. The Litigation Chamber cannot exercise any control over the Management Committee's decision in this regard other than a marginal review. Moreover, the Management Committee did not exceed its powers in assessing the seriousness of the indications in question in this case.  
50. Furthermore, the defendant contests that the Management Committee took into account a previous data breach that occurred in 2019 by the same hospital. However, the Management Committee was entitled to rely on the information made available to the DPA through the data breach notification forms received from the hospital. These forms constitute an essential and legitimate source of information for the DPA, allowing for the characterization of serious indications of potential violations of data protection legislation. The recurrence of data breaches within the same institution, particularly when they occur at close intervals and involve high risks to the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals, reasonably strengthens the presence of serious indications within the meaning of Article 63.1° of the LCA.  
51. The Management Committee sovereignly assessed that the fact that a hospital experienced two data breaches, each presenting "high" risks to the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals within a span of two years, constituted an additional serious indication revealing the existence of practices that could lead to a violation of the fundamental principles of data protection.  
52. Moreover, the defendant seems to confuse the notion of "serious indications" with the finding of a violation of the GDPR. The Management Committee does not have the power to establish a violation of the GDPR, and such a finding is not required for it to invoke Article 63.1° of the LCA. The competence to establish a violation of the GDPR is reserved for the Litigation Chamber. It is only when it has a complete file, which contains the parties' conclusions and a possible investigation report, that the Litigation Chamber can determine whether a cyber attack constitutes a violation of the GDPR. At the time of the activation of Article 63.1°, only the existence of serious indications is necessary, which has been sovereignly assessed (see paragraphs 49 to 51 above).  
53. In conclusion, the Management Committee was justified in finding the seriousness of the indications in question sufficient, based on the information provided by the defendant (Article 63.1° of the LCA).  
II.2.4. Transmission of the notification form within the departments of the DPA:  
54. The defendant argues that the data breach notification form does not need to be shared within the departments of the DPA, due to the absence of a legal provision for this purpose. Furthermore, it claims that neither the GDPR nor the LCA provide for the possibility of a data breach notification leading to proceedings before the Litigation Chamber. Finally, it asserts that the European Data Protection Board (hereinafter "EDPB") does not foresee that such a notification can be used to justify the referral to the Inspection Service and to accuse the notifier of other breaches.  
55. The Litigation Chamber contests the arguments raised in the defense's conclusions and reminds that the GDPR provides, in its Article 33§1, that "In the event of a personal data breach, the controller shall notify the breach to the competent supervisory authority...". Furthermore, "Such notification may lead a supervisory authority to act in accordance with its tasks and powers set out in this Regulation" (recital 87 of the GDPR).  
56. Therefore, any supervisory authority is legally authorized to know the content of a data breach notification form that is submitted to it. Contrary to the assertions raised in defense, the GDPR indeed provides that such a notification may lead an authority to exercise its investigation and enforcement powers. Considering the structure of the DPA, this circumstance necessarily justifies the possibility of transmitting the form between its competent services.

II.3. On the Merits
II.3.1. Finding 1: On the Data Protection Impact Assessment

60. First, the defendant argues that since the processing related to the electronic messages of staff was the only processing affected by the data breach, it was not subject to the obligation to carry out a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) for this processing, which does not require the processing of sensitive data. During the hearing, the Contentious Chamber further questioned the defendant on its reasoning asserting the absence of processing of sensitive data in the context of the processing of electronic messages of hospital staff. The defendant maintained that sensitive data were likely exchanged via electronic messages but stated that this sensitive data was encrypted.

61. Secondly, it argues that the performance of a DPIA does not have to take a specific form, and that it is incorrect to assert that no DPIA was conducted since the hospital implemented security measures that are the result of risk analyses.

62. Thirdly, it claims that since the processing was implemented before the entry into force of the GDPR in May 2018, it is not subject to the obligation to carry out a DPIA, and even if it were, it would have been exempted from this obligation due to the "grace period" of three years granted by the EDPB.

63. First, the Contentious Chamber recalls that a DPIA is required in several cases, notably in the case of large-scale processing of special categories of data (Article 35.3.b of the GDPR). A DPIA must at least contain (Article 35.7, and Recitals 84 and 90 of the GDPR):
a. a systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes of the processing;
b. an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations;
c. an assessment of the risks to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects;
d. the measures envisaged to:
i. address the risks;
ii. demonstrate compliance with the GDPR.

64. In this case, the data controller processes sensitive data (including health data and genetic data) of vulnerable individuals (patients) on a large scale (300,000 patients in the database), which imposes upon it the obligation to comply with this requirement. The Contentious Chamber recalls that one of the purposes of conducting a DPIA is to allow the data controller to proactively identify security weaknesses and adopt appropriate measures to protect personal data against data breaches.
Decision on the merits 166/2024 — 16/50

65. Contrary to the defendant's allegations, the fact that the attack focused on the staff's email rather than on the patients' medical records does not exempt the defendant from its duty to conduct a DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment). The Litigation Chamber reminds that the definition of "processing" under the GDPR is broad and includes, in particular, the storage, collection, consultation, use, and provision of personal data (Article 4 of the GDPR). Even if the attack only targeted the staff's email accounts, the fact that these accounts allow for the processing of sensitive data of vulnerable individuals on a large scale imposes on the defendant the obligation to comply with its duty to conduct a DPIA concerning this processing, regardless of whether this data is encrypted during the sending of emails. In any case, this finding is not limited to examining the conduct of a DPIA concerning the specific processing related to the staff's email accounts.

66. Furthermore, it appears irrelevant on the part of the defendant to claim that only the staff's email accounts were affected, as it emerges from the investigation report that the hospital's servers were paralyzed (including the software supporting the medical records), and that it ceased to be operational due to the attack. Moreover, it is clearly indicated in the investigation that traces were found on the "radiology server." According to the defense's conclusions: "The Hospital was able to gradually but quickly restart its services without risks, including the radiology service," and "Finally, the thorough investigations conducted by the Hospital and the service providers it engaged showed that during the attack, only 5 gigabytes of information were exported from one of the Hospital's servers to the Internet. However, the server in question contains all the X-rays (...)." Therefore, the data breach indeed involved sensitive categories of personal data, namely the health data of the hospital's patients processed on a large scale.

67. The Contested Chamber recalls the obligation imposed on the data controller to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, including the ability to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, availability, and constant resilience of processing systems and services (32.1.b) of the GDPR). III.2.1. Reminder of the applicable legal provisions

148. As an independent administrative authority, the Litigation Chamber has the exclusive power to determine the corrective measures and appropriate sanctions in accordance with the relevant provisions of the GDPR and the LCA. This competence specifically derives from Articles 58 and 83 of the GDPR, as confirmed by the case law of the Market Court in its judgments of July 7, 2021, February 19, 2020, or even December 20, 2023, which highlighted the extent of the discretionary power of the Litigation Chamber regarding the choice of sanctions and the magnitude of the fine.

149. According to the guidelines of the EDPB and the GDPR, the supervisory authority has the discretionary power to impose a fine. The GDPR requires each supervisory authority to ensure that the administrative fines imposed are effective, proportionate, and dissuasive in each individual case (Article 83.1 of the GDPR).

150. Furthermore, when determining the amount of the fine, the supervisory authority must duly consider, for each individual case, several specific elements, such as “the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing concerned, as well as the number of affected individuals and the level of damage they have suffered” (Article 83.2.a) of the GDPR); as well as “the intentional or negligent nature of the infringement” (Article 83.2.b) of the GDPR); and “the categories of personal data concerned by the infringement” (Article 83.2.g) of the GDPR).

151. Consequently, any fine must be assessed taking into account all the factors set out in Article 83.2, points a) to k) of the GDPR, without exceeding the maximum legal amount set out in Articles 83.4 to 83.6 of the GDPR.

152. In accordance with Recital 148 of the GDPR, sanctions, including administrative fines, must be imposed in addition to or instead of appropriate measures in cases of serious breaches, even when it is the first finding of a breach. Thus, the fact that an infringing behavior is only observed for the first time in the case of a defendant does not prevent the Litigation Chamber from imposing an administrative fine, in accordance with Article 58.2.i) of the GDPR.

Indeed, even though the initial amount of the fine of 390,000 EUR represents only about 0.54% of a (rounded) turnover of 72,000,000 EUR, this amount must be reduced by about one-third in this case to primarily take into account (i) the financial difficulties of the hospital, particularly the conclusion of its recognition file as a "Company in Difficulty" and the appointment of a crisis manager, (ii) the new mitigating circumstance considered by the contentious chamber, namely the energy crisis and inflation, which also impact the financial capacity of the company.  
- Evidence of loss of value: There is no indication that the imposition of the fine as revised would endanger the viability of the company, resulting in a significant loss of the value of its assets or threatening its ability to continue its operations in a viable manner. There must be a direct link between the fine and this loss of value, and it is not automatically accepted that bankruptcy or insolvency leads to such a loss, given the amount of the revised fine. In the absence of such tangible evidence demonstrating this correlation, an additional reduction of the fine does not seem justified.  
- Specific social context: The social context of the time, marked by the COVID-19 health crisis in 2021, is a factor considered by the contentious chamber in determining the amount of the fine to be imposed.

▪ Deterrence  

198. The deterrent nature of the fine must have two dimensions. It must deter the person against whom the fine is imposed from repeating the violations found in the future, but also deter any other person from reproducing the infringing behaviors committed by the first.  
199. Several factors determine the deterrent nature of a fine: the nature, the amount of the fine, and the likelihood of its imposition are determining elements in this regard. A fine must be sufficiently high to have a significant financial impact on the offending company while remaining proportionate to the severity of the violation. In other words, the criterion of deterrence overlaps with that of effectiveness.  
200. If a supervisory authority believes that a fine is not sufficiently deterrent, it may consider increasing it. In certain cases, it may even apply a deterrent multiplier to enhance its deterrent effect. This multiplier can be adjusted at the discretion of the supervisory authority to ensure that the deterrent objectives are fully achieved.  
201. In this case, taking into account the reassessment of the duration of the violation, the new mitigating circumstance considered, as well as the financial capacity of the hospital, the final amount of the fine will therefore be definitively reassessed to 200,000 EUR.  
202. This amount remains sufficiently deterrent to prevent the defendant from reoffending in the violation of GDPR rules. Moreover, it also seeks to deter other companies from committing similar violations. This fine, proportionate to the severity of the violation and taking into account the defendant's turnover, is designed to have both a specific and general deterrent effect.
