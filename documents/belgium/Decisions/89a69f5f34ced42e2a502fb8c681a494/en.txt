34. In this regard, the defendant opposes the invocation of new provisions of the GDPR at such a late stage, for the first time during the hearing, without having had the opportunity to defend against them. Furthermore, it considers that it cannot be required to de-reference other URLs that are not specifically identified by the complainant. Such a request cannot be acceptable, as it would not be permitted by the GDPR, with Article 17 of the GDPR applying only to personal data that is actually processed, and not to hypothetical or future processing; it would also constitute a surveillance measure contrary to Article 8 of the DSA; finally, it would also represent an action ad futurum, which would not be permitted under common law in the absence of a born and current interest.  
35. The Contentious Chamber recalls that, in accordance with the case law of the Market Court, it follows from the principle of good administration and respect for the rights of the defense that the Contentious Chamber must, from the outset of the proceedings, inform the party being prosecuted of all the factual and legal elements that are alleged against them. In this regard, the Market Court recently specified the following:  
"It is necessary for the person concerned to understand what behavior is being reproached to them. However, this does not necessarily imply that the authority must specify the precise qualifications of the facts or indicate the statutory provisions that may have been violated at the time of their commission (the Contentious Chamber emphasizes)."  
36. In this case, the Contentious Chamber delineated the scope of the debates, in its invitation letter to conclude dated August 6, 2024, to the alleged violation of Articles 12.1 and 12.4 of the GDPR, as well as the applicability of Article 17.3.a) of the GDPR to the complainant's de-referencing requests. While it is true that Article 21 of the GDPR is closely related to Article 17, in that the exercise of the right to object can ground a right to erasure within the meaning of Article 17.1.c), it should be emphasized that the complaint, as well as the complainant's conclusions, exclusively targeted the de-referencing of two URLs, explicitly relying on Article 17 — an article commonly invoked in cases related to the right to be forgotten. On this basis, the defendant could not reasonably understand that it was also required to defend itself against a potential violation of Article 21 of the GDPR, which it did not do in this instance.  
37. Furthermore, the complainant's request, which essentially seeks to order the defendant to de-reference all URLs, current and future, related to the complaint of sexual harassment against them, raises several points. First, it is important to recall the material scope of the GDPR, which only applies to the processing of data, in accordance with Article 2.1 of the GDPR. 17.1 of the GDPR can only be conceived in relation to an existing and determined processing activity. Thus, a request for de-referencing can only be exercised concerning the search results that actually appear when the complainant's name is entered into the defendant's search engine. The complainant's request would amount to de-referencing any link pointing to data related to the complainant, which would be contrary not only to the spirit but also to the letter of Article 17 of the GDPR. Requests for the right to be forgotten must be examined in concreto. They require, on the part of the search engine, an individual case-by-case analysis of the referenced links, in order to adequately balance the interests at stake. Consequently, the balancing could differ depending on the referenced content. Finally, a general de-referencing injunction would, in practice, be difficult to apply for the defendant, particularly because it is not the originator of the content it references. Thus, it is only ex post, following a request made by the data subject, that the search engine operator must carry out such verification, under the supervision of the Contentious Chamber.  
38. Therefore, the Contentious Chamber considers that the aforementioned requests from the complainant cannot be upheld and will only address in this decision the provisions of the GDPR that the Contentious Chamber had expressly indicated as being at issue in its letter of August 6, 2024, which excludes Article 21 of the GDPR. It will also limit its examination to the URLs identified by the complainant in his complaint, namely the disputed URL No. 1 and No. 2.  
II.2. Regarding compliance with Articles 12.1 and 12.4 of the GDPR  
39. The complainant requests that the Contentious Chamber finds that the defendant has not complied with Article 12 of the GDPR by failing to provide him with clear, understandable, and accessible information, which did not allow him to fully understand the reasons for the refusal that underpinned the decision not to respond to his de-referencing requests (point 17).  
40. In its letter of August 6, 2024, the Contentious Chamber delineated the framework of the discussions regarding the grievance raised below by the complainant concerning the alleged violation of Articles 12.1 and 12.4 of the GDPR due to the insufficient reasoning for Google's refusal of the complainant's de-referencing requests in the exchanges that took place prior to the filing of the complaint.  
41. The Contentious Chamber recalls that it is the responsibility of the data controller to respect the principle of transparency enshrined in Article 5.1.a) of the GDPR. Recital 39 of the GDPR specifies that the principle of transparency requires that any information and communication relating to the processing of personal data be easily accessible, easy to understand, and expressed in clear and simple terms. This principle is notably formalized in Article 12 of the GDPR, which defines the obligations of the data controller regarding the modalities of information and communication in the context of the exercise of the rights of the data subject. The Contentious Chamber therefore wishes to emphasize that Articles 12.1 and 12.4 condition the effective application of different rights of the data subject, particularly in the context of the present case, of Article 17 of the GDPR.  
42. Article 12.1 of the GDPR states that:  
"The data controller shall take appropriate measures to provide any information referred to in Articles 13 and 14 as well as to carry out any communication under Articles 15 to 22 and Article 34 regarding the processing to the data subject in a concise, transparent, understandable, and easily accessible manner, using clear and simple language, particularly for any information specifically addressed to a child. The information shall be provided in writing or by other means, including, where appropriate, by electronic means. When the data subject requests it, the information may be provided orally, provided that the identity of the data subject is demonstrated by other means (the Contentious Chamber emphasizes)."  
43. Article 12.4 of the GDPR states that:  
"If the data controller does not respond to the request made by the data subject, it shall inform the data subject without undue delay and at the latest within one month of receiving the request of the reasons for its inaction and the possibility of lodging a complaint with a supervisory authority and seeking a judicial remedy (the Contentious Chamber emphasizes)."

II.2.2. Regarding the comprehensibility of the responses provided by the defendant  
48. It being established that Articles 12.1 and 12.4 of the GDPR apply to the responses initially provided to the complainant by the defendant to inform him of the refusal to comply with his two de-referencing requests, it is therefore the responsibility of the Contentious Chamber to examine compliance with these provisions, particularly regarding the requirement of comprehensibility, given that the complainant reproaches the defendant for these standardized responses not allowing him to fully understand the reasons for the refusals.  
49. In its guidelines on transparency under the GDPR, the Article 29 Working Party (hereinafter "Article 29 Group") defines the requirement of "comprehensibility" as follows:  
"The requirement that this information be 'comprehensible' means that it should be understandable by the majority of the intended public. Information should be concrete and reliable; it should not be phrased in abstract or ambiguous terms nor leave room for different interpretations."

90. The Contentious Chamber believes that the indexing of contentious content should not create a kind of "virtual criminal record." Suspicions of sexual harassment against the complainant did not lead to a conviction; however, the continued indexing of the contentious content, which conveyed an outdated and incomplete image of the facts attributed to him, continued to cause him disproportionate moral harm. IV. Publication of the decision  
95. Given the importance of transparency regarding the decision-making process of the Contentious Chamber, this decision is published on the website of the Data Protection Authority, with the removal of direct identification data of the complainant and the cited individuals, whether natural or legal, excluding the defendant. The Contentious Chamber believes that the publication of this decision identifying the defendant serves several objectives.  
96. It primarily aims at a public interest objective, considering the importance of the search engine "Google" for many internet users and the fact that a very large number of people residing in Belgium are referenced in one way or another by the search engine "Google." The Contentious Chamber finds it relevant to give this decision publicity that raises awareness among internet users of the rights they have under the GDPR. In this respect, even though the decision directly concerns only the complainant (whose identification data is not published), it is also of interest to a large part of the general public.
