I. Facts and procedure  
1. The subject of the complaint concerns the photographic capture of the identity cards of recipients of registered mail (hereinafter, "registered mail delivery practice").  
2. On October 28, 2022, the complainant, in order to receive a registered letter addressed to him, is compelled to be subject to the registered mail delivery practice of the defendant. The complainant refuses and nevertheless offers to show his identity card without accepting the photographic capture. The postal agent reiterates, following a call with his supervisor, that the photographic capture is necessary for the delivery of the registered mail. Following this altercation, the complainant files a complaint with the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter, "DPA") against the defendant.  
3. On November 24, 2022, the First Line Service (hereinafter, "SPL") informs the complainant of the proper receipt of his complaint by the DPA and invites him to submit a request to exercise his rights to the DPO of the defendant.  
4. .II.1.3. The principle of ne bis in idem  
46. In its judgment of March 22, 2022, the CJEU explains that “the condition ‘idem’ requires that the material facts be identical. Conversely, the principle of ne bis in idem is not intended to apply when the facts in question are not identical, but merely similar. Indeed, the identity of material facts is understood as a set of concrete circumstances arising from events that are, in substance, the same, in that they involve the same author and are indissociably linked to each other in time and space.”  
47. Regarding decision 102/2021, the Contentious Chamber notes that the facts involve the same author as in the present case, i.e., the defendant, but that they are not indissociably linked to each other in time or space. The latter occurred a year apart, do not concern the same complainant, and consequently took place in different locations. Therefore, the Contentious Chamber argues that the facts between the present case and those of decision 102/2021 are similar but not identical.  
48. Regarding decision 38/2023, the Contentious Chamber observes that the contact form in question did not request a copy of the identity card, contrary to the one challenged in the present case. The identity of facts, required by the principle of ne bis in idem, is therefore not satisfied here either.  
49. Since the condition of “idem,” constitutive of the principle of ne bis in idem, is not fulfilled in this case, the Contentious Chamber considers that this principle does not apply.  
50. However, in accordance with the principle of legal certainty and legitimate expectation, the Contentious Chamber specifies that the content of the aforementioned decisions will be part of the assessment of the grievances in the present case.

.II.2. First grievance: Compliance of the practice of distributing registered mail with Articles 5.1.a) and 6 of the GDPR; and Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR  
55. First, the defendant argues that its practice of distributing registered mail is prescribed by Article 9, §1 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022, which constitutes its legal basis.  
56. Second, the defendant adds that it accepts other forms of identity proof provided for in this article, as long as they meet the legal conditions of Article 9, §2 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022. Moreover, the wording of the aforementioned Article 9, §1 offers it the possibility to choose the means of proof that it deems useful. Consequently, it considers it impossible to assert that the processing was carried out in violation of the principle of data minimization.  
57. The Contentious Chamber specifies that it will first evaluate whether the defendant validly bases this practice on Article 9, §1 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022, in accordance with Articles 5.1.a) and 6.1.c) of the GDPR. Secondly, it will assess the compliance of this processing with the principle of data minimization of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR.  
.II.2.1. The basis of the practice of distributing registered mail on a valid legal basis  
58. The basis for processing under Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR is subject to two conditions: a clear and precise legal basis in Union law or Belgian law, and the necessity of the processing for the fulfillment of that legal obligation.  
59. The Contentious Chamber notes that Article 9, §1 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022 delineates the defendant's practice of distributing registered mail.

clearly, precisely, and predictably as understood by Article 6.3 of the GDPR. The legal provision on which the defendant bases this practice is therefore valid.  
60. Regarding the necessity of this practice, the Litigation Chamber notes that Article 9, § 1 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022 requires the defendant to provide proof of verification of the identity of the recipients of registered mail through photographic, handwritten, or electronic capture of an identity document. An identity document is defined as "any document that allows for the verification of a person's identity [...] issued by an administrative authority [...] [and] mentions the name and surname of the holder and is provided with a photo or the holder is recognizable."  
61. The Litigation Chamber notes that the identity card is cited as an example of an identity document whose photographic capture allows for the verification of the identity of the recipient of registered mail.  
62. Therefore, the practice of distributing registered mail by the defendant is prescribed by Article 9, § 1 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022 and meets its obligations.  
63. In conclusion, the Litigation Chamber considers that the defendant validly bases this practice on Article 9, § 1 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022, in accordance with Articles 5.1.a) and 6.1.c) of the GDPR.  
.II.2.2. Data minimization in processing induced by Article 9, § 1 of the Royal Decree of March 14, 2022  
64. The principle of data minimization – Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR – requires the processing of data necessary for achieving the identified purpose and ensuring that this processing cannot be carried out by less intrusive means.  
65. In this regard, the Litigation Chamber notes that a postal agent only has access to the data on the registered mail (name, surname, address) to verify the identity of the recipient. However, an identity card contains more data: date of birth, place of birth, sex, nationality, card number, validity period, and national register number for cards issued after January 14, 2020. It should be emphasized that postal agents do not have any reference base to verify the accuracy of all the data collected on the identity card.
