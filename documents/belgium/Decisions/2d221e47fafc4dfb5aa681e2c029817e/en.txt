Decision on the merits 89/2025 — 6/16  
II. Motivation  
II.1. Preliminary remarks  
30. It is undisputed by the parties that the defendant, based in Brussels, is the data controller of the complainant's data within the meaning of the GDPR.  
31. The processing in question concerns the consultation of the complainant's National Register file as well as his photo.  
32. As a preliminary matter, the defendant argues in its conclusions that in the absence of bad faith on the part of the agent during the consultation of the complainant's personal data, this excludes any liability on the part of the defendant.  
33. The Contentious Chamber cannot accept this reasoning. Processing is defined by the GDPR as "any operation or set of operations performed or not by automated means and applied to personal data or sets of personal data, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of making available, alignment or combination, restriction, erasure or destruction."  
34. Therefore, any consultation of data constitutes processing, regardless of the agent's intent or the voluntary nature of the act. Furthermore, the defendant's argument that the consultation lasted only 20 seconds cannot be upheld by the Contentious Chamber. The GDPR does not set a minimum duration for processing to be recognized.  
35. Finally, the close ties between Belgium and Morocco leading to an overload at the Casablanca office cannot be used as justification for an error, as this fact does not exempt the data controller from the responsibilities incumbent upon it under the GDPR.  
II.2. Regarding the principle of accountability (art. 5.2 and 24.1 of the GDPR)  
36. The complainant asserts in his complaint that the consultation of his National Register occurred in a manner non-compliant with the GDPR, as no control of the purpose limitation principle was foreseen at the time of the disputed facts.  
37. The defendant invokes several measures intended to demonstrate the compliance of the disputed consultation of the National Register with the GDPR. It notably mentions the annual transmission of the list of users with administrator rights to the Data Protection Authority and a procedure requiring individualization of the request for granting access rights for each new assignment. However, the authorization of the person who conducted the consultation is not questioned by the Contentious Chamber.  
38. The SI believes that the defendant did not comply with articles 5.2 and 24.1 of the GDPR in this case because no reason for consultation was recorded in the processing register at the time of the facts, and the defendant does not have an adequate control mechanism ensuring that its authorized agents consult the National Register solely for the purposes for which this access has been granted.  
39. The Contentious Chamber recalls that the principle of accountability, as enshrined in articles 5.2 and 24.1 of the GDPR, requires that the data controller be able to demonstrate the compliance of its processing with the principles set forth by the GDPR. Such demonstration must particularly address the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures. The data controller ensures to review and update these technical and organizational measures as necessary (article 24 of the GDPR).  
40. The analysis of the Contentious Chamber focuses on the demonstration of the existence of appropriate technical and organizational measures reported by the defendant to demonstrate its compliance with the GDPR: the signing of a confidentiality clause by the persons authorized to consult (Section II.2.1), and the establishment of a module that requires indicating the reason for the consultation (Section II.2.2).  
41. To be considered valid, the Contentious Chamber recalls that these measures must aim to prevent the consultation of the National Register for purposes other than those for which the data must be processed. The choice of appropriate measures falls to the data controller.  
II.2.1. Regarding the confidentiality clause  
42. The signing of a confidentiality clause is an organizational measure of a contractual nature, which legally binds its signatory. At the date of the disputed facts, the Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant demonstrates the obligation for its employees to sign a confidentiality clause.  
43. In this case, the confidentiality clause binds its signatories to consult the National Register only for strictly professional purposes and to refrain from disclosing to third parties any information they may become aware of in the course of their duties.  
44. However, the Contentious Chamber notes that the effectiveness of this clause is significantly diminished as it is not accompanied by other measures allowing for the monitoring of its compliance.

II.2.2. Regarding the Registration of the Reason for the Consultation  
45. The obligation to mention the reason for consulting the National Register is provided for by Article 17 of the law of August 8, 1983. This article came into force on December 23, 2018.  
46. At the time of the disputed facts (September 2019), the Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant had not yet implemented measures allowing for the mention of the reason for consulting the National Register.  
47. Furthermore, although the defendant indicates that only one year elapsed between the entry into force of this article and the establishment of a module allowing for the indication of the reason and purpose of the consultation, the Contentious Chamber cannot follow this argument. Indeed, given the crucial role of public institutions, they must be exemplary in the application of laws. Therefore, the defendant should have had control measures already in place at the time of the disputed facts.  
48. A recommendation from the Commission for the Protection of Privacy dated August 30, 2017, identified by the Inspection Service, already specified the importance of indicating the reason for the consultation. According to this recommendation, "the mention of the reason for the consultation constitutes a necessary and mandatory guarantee for accessing the National Register in a legitimate manner." It is therefore incorrect to assert that the defendant learned, upon the entry into force of the GDPR, that it was essential to allow the recording of the reason for each consultation of the National Register, as the reason enables the individuals concerned to understand the purpose of the processing of their data. The recording of this purpose is all the more important because, without it, no control can be exercised regarding compliance with the principles relating to the processing of personal data as provided for in Article 5.1. of the GDPR.  
49. In conclusion, the Contentious Chamber finds that at the time of the disputed consultation, Articles 5.2 and 24.1 of the GDPR were violated due to the absence of demonstration of the existence of appropriate technical and organizational measures to limit the consultation of the National Register to determined purposes.  
50. However, during its exchanges with the Inspection Service, the defendant indicates that it has implemented, subsequent to the disputed facts, a module allowing for the indication of the reason and purpose for which a National Register record is consulted.

It reminds that the data controller has the obligation to ensure that the technical and organizational measures implemented are appropriate.  
II.3. Regarding the Principle of Data Minimization (Art. 5.1.c) of the GDPR)  
II.3.1. Position of the Parties  
53. The complainant argues that the consultation of his photo is not relevant in light of the supposed purpose for which the data is processed.  
54. The Inspection Service follows the complainant's allegations on this point and considers that the automatic display of the photo when consulting a National Register record, without regard to the relevance of this data for the processing of the file, is a violation of Article 5.1.c. of the GDPR.  
55. The defendant argues that consulting the photo linked to the extracts from the National Register is necessary for the performance of its missions. It bases its argument, in particular, on the importance of the role of the National Register in the prevention of identity fraud. The defendant alleges in this case that the consultation of the complainant's National Register record took place in order to confirm an address, a family link, or a parentage in the context of a third party's visa application.  
II.3.2. Analysis of the Contentious Chamber  
56. The Contentious Chamber notes that the difficulty lies in the automatic consultation of the identity photo each time a National Register record is opened.  
57. First, the Contentious Chamber recalls that the principle of data minimization, as provided for in Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, implies that personal data must be "adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed."

58. Any personal data processed must therefore be necessary in light of the defined purpose. Thus, each data consulted in this case, including the consultation of a photograph of a citizen, must be necessary for the accomplishment of this mission, which is, in this instance, justified by the needs to process a third party's visa application.  
59. Secondly, the Contentious Chamber wishes to remind that the National Register contains highly sensitive data, although not considered a special category of personal data within the meaning of Article 9 of the GDPR, insofar as this information allows for the certain and unequivocal identification of the concerned person. An appropriate level of protection must therefore be considered.  
60. Thirdly, the deliberation 34/2012 of the sectoral committee of the National Register, highlighted by the defendant, does not aim to legitimize all consultations of photographs from the National Register. Indeed, the deliberation of the sectoral committee concerns the consultation of the National Register within the framework of a passport application procedure. In this case, the Contentious Chamber notes that the complainant was not involved in any visa application procedure and was never asked to produce his identity documents.  
61. Fourthly, the Contentious Chamber specifies that the consultation of the photo linked to a National Register record is not automatically unlawful. Indeed, this processing of the photo may, in certain cases, be necessary to achieve a specific purpose. This consultation could be justified, in particular, by the need to confirm the information of presented identity documents.  
62. Based on the preceding elements, the Contentious Chamber finds that the consultation of the complainant's photograph was not necessary to verify an address, a family link, or a parentage of a third party and therefore, at the date of the disputed facts, Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR was violated.  
63. The defendant informs the APD that, following the report from the SI and since May 11, 2021, the consultation of the photo attached to a person's record is no longer automatic and must now be subject to a separate request to be consulted.  
II.4. Regarding the respect of the right of access (Article 15.1 of the GDPR)  
64. The right of access to data exercised by the complainant is provided for by Article 15.1 of the GDPR and aims to allow the concerned person to "be informed about the processing and to verify its lawfulness."8

II.4.1. Regarding the purpose of the processing  
65. The complainant wished to know the purposes of the processing of his data in this case. In his first communication with the defendant on this matter, he formulates his request as follows: "My national register was opened by the SPF Foreign Affairs, by a record 25 and 08 (photo) on the date and unless I am mistaken of 4/9/19 around 17:52. Could you let me know the reason for this consultation?"  
66. The defendant considers that it adequately responded to the complainant's access request by informing him that his file had been consulted in the context of a long-term visa application. It later specified that "the data from the National Register can be consulted for a person who is not directly involved in a visa application," notably to "verify certain elements such as a family link, parentage, or an address." It argues that it is materially impossible for it to identify with certainty the specific file in which the complainant's data was consulted. Although a hypothesis was formulated during its communication with the SI, it explains that this possibility could only become a certainty if it consulted the national register records of other people.  
67. The SI considers that the defendant has failed in its obligation of transparency, as provided for in Articles 12.1 and 15.1 of the GDPR. Indeed, it limited itself to informing the complainant that his data had been consulted by the Consulate General of Belgium in Casablanca in the context of a visa application, mentioning only a possibility of consultation for the purpose of verifying "certain elements such as a family link, parentage, an address...", without being able to precisely identify the individual file concerned. However, in the context of the investigation conducted by the SI, it was possible to establish precisely in which file the data of the concerned person had been consulted, which demonstrates that this identification was indeed feasible.  
68. The Contentious Chamber reminds that the data controller is obliged to communicate the purposes of the processing to a concerned person exercising their right of access (Articles 12.1 and 15.1.a). The purpose of the processing must be communicated to the concerned person in a precise and specified manner regarding the specific case of the concerned person.9  
69. In this case, the defendant was able, in the context of its collaboration with the Inspection Service, to make a reasonable assumption regarding the specific file within the framework of which the complainant's data had been consulted, which had not been possible to respond to the complainant's request.

70. The information regarding the purpose must be sufficiently precise and specific to allow the data subject to assess the lawfulness of the processing of their data. By responding in general terms that the data had been consulted in the context of a long-term visa application, without having conducted a concrete verification due to a lack of knowledge of the exact file concerned, the defendant did not provide a sufficiently precise and specific response to the access request of the complainant.  
71. The Contentious Chamber therefore observes that the material impossibility of retrieving the context of the consultation alleged by the defendant was not so much a strict impossibility of retrieving this information, but rather a lack of thorough examination, which was not carried out during the exercise of the complainant's right of access.  
72. The Contentious Chamber thus concludes that there has been a violation of Articles 12.1 and 15.1 of the GDPR, due to the failure to provide the necessary information in the context of an access request, specifically regarding the purpose of the processing, allowing the complainant to assess the lawfulness of the processing of their personal data.  
73. Furthermore, the privacy statement provided by the defendant does not mention at any time the possible processing of third-party data in the visa granting procedures. Although the Contentious Chamber does not sanction this situation in the context of the present decision as the parties were not asked to conclude on this point, it draws the defendant's attention to the fact that the data controller should have provided this information as it constitutes a complete purpose for which the data is processed. This proactive information is all the more essential when the processed data has not been collected directly from the data subject.  
II.4.2. Regarding the identity of the person who conducted the disputed consultation  
74. The complainant also wishes to be informed of the identity of the person who conducted the disputed consultation. The defendant opposes this request, stating that it does not contest its status as a defendant and that this information is not useful for the complainant.  
75. The Contentious Chamber recalls that the Court of Justice of the European Union has ruled on the interpretation of Article 15.1 of the GDPR in the context of the disclosure of log files from a database consultation. It holds that, although the date and purposes of the consultation must be provided to the person exercising their right, the right of access provided for in Article 15.1 of the GDPR "does not establish such a right regarding information relating to the identity of the employees of the said controller who carried out these operations under its authority and in accordance with its instructions, unless such information is essential to enable the data subject to effectively exercise the rights conferred on them by this regulation and provided that the rights and freedoms of those employees are taken into account."  
76. The Contentious Chamber has also previously ruled on a similar situation and, citing the aforementioned judgment of the Court of Justice, understands that "the protection of the identity of the employee acting under the authority of their employer is superior to that of the employee who consulted the data for their own purposes."  
77. In this case, it is not contested that the employee who conducted the disputed consultation was authorized to consult the National Register and did so in the course of their duties.  
78. Based on these considerations, the Contentious Chamber reaffirms its previous position and finds that there is no justification for providing the complainant with the identity of the employee who conducted the disputed consultation since they acted under the authority of their employer and in accordance with their instructions. The refusal to disclose the identity of the person who conducted the disputed consultation does not prevent, in this case, the effective exercise of the complainant's right of access. Indeed, the exercise of the complainant's right of access concerns the purpose of the processing of their data. The identity of the person who consulted them does not provide any clarification regarding the understanding of this purpose. This does not allow for verification that the consultation is lawful.  
79. The Contentious Chamber therefore finds that the non-disclosure of the identity of the person who conducted the consultation acting under the authority and in accordance with the instructions of their employer is a proportionate limitation on the complainant's right of access in light of the right to the protection of personal data of the employee who conducted the consultation. It therefore does not find a violation of Article 15.1 of the GDPR concerning the identity of the agent who conducted the consultation.
