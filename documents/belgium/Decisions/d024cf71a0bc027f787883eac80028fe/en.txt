Decision on the merits 65/2025 — 2/9  
I.  
Facts and procedure  
1.  
Between January 24, 2020, and January 28, 2020, thousands of eID certificates (both signature and authentication certificates) were registered in a certificate transparency log system (CT-logs) of Google. The certificates contained personal data, including the national register number (as the serial number of the subject of the certificate), the name and first name of the citizen, and the fact that it concerns a Belgian national or a foreigner.  
2.  
On October 22, 2021, the Executive Committee of the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter “GBA”) decided to initiate a case with the Inspection Service of the GBA based on Article 63, 1° WOG1 as it was applicable at the time of the facts that led to the present procedure (hereinafter referred to as WOG) since the Executive Committee was of the opinion that there were serious indications pointing to a practice that could lead to a violation of the fundamental principles of the protection of personal data.  
3.  
On September 8, 2022, the investigation by the Inspection Service was completed, the report was added to the file, and the file was forwarded by the Inspector General to the Chair of the Dispute Chamber (Article 91, § 1 and § 2 WOG). The report contains findings regarding the subject of the complaint and concludes that there is:  
1. a violation of Article 5.1, f) in conjunction with Article 32 GDPR as there would be no adequate security of personal data;  
2. a violation of Article 5.2 and Article 24.1 GDPR as the respondent would not fully assume responsibility;  
3. a violation of Article 25 GDPR as the respondent would not apply data protection by design;  
4. a violation of Articles 33 and 34 GDPR as the respondent would not have reported the data breach and would not have informed the affected citizens; and  
5. a violation of Article 35 GDPR as the respondent conducted an impact assessment regarding data protection in the context of the processing with the eID but there would be neither an update of the impact assessment in light of the issues of this file nor a plan for updating it.

II. Reasoning  
18.  
The law of July 19, 1991, concerning the population registers, identity cards, foreigner cards, and residence documents regulates, among other things, the electronic identity card (hereinafter: “eID”). The eID contains two certificates: the signature certificate and the authentication certificate, which contain various personal data of the citizen, including the national register number.  
19.  
The respondent is the data controller for the issuance of eIDs and also places these certificates on them in accordance with the European Regulation (EU) 2019/1157 and the European eIDAS Regulation. These authentication and signature certificates are public.  
20.  
Every citizen can decide whether to activate the certificates and with whom to share the certificates. The citizen shares his certificates with a third party when he needs to identify himself and, if applicable, authenticate (such as when logging in online to a web application or when scanning the eID) or when he digitally signs a document and provides this document to a third party. These certificates contain the national register number of the citizen as the serial number. The electronic signature and/or authentication certificates that contain the national register number may be retained without prior consent as long as necessary to provide proof of the

21.  
The certificates, which are freely available for citizens to use for identification/authentication or to place an electronic signature, contain personal data, making the GDPR applicable to the processing of this personal data. Publishing it elsewhere or sharing it with third parties or otherwise processing it is therefore not permitted without proper authorization. The National Register Act even prohibits the unlawful use of the national register number without authorization and for purposes other than those for which the authorization was granted (Article 8, §8, paragraph 2 of the National Register Act). Criminal sanctions apply to this (Article 13, paragraph 2 of the National Register Act).  
22.  
The disputed processing in this case is the unlawful publication of approximately 40,000 signature and authentication certificates of Belgian citizens in one of Google’s CT logs, which violated the confidentiality of the personal data. The question arises as to who should be designated as the data controller for this disputed processing.  
23.  
According to Article 4.7) GDPR, the data controller is to be understood as: “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data.” The data controller must therefore have decision-making authority and influence over the relevant processing of personal data.  
24.  
The Inspection Service states in its report that the respondent must be qualified as the data controller for the disputed processing. To this end, the Inspection Service presents the following arguments. First, the respondent is responsible for safeguarding the identity of the citizen: the respondent produces and distributes, among other things, the eIDs in accordance with what is stated on the respondent's website. Second, the Inspection Service points out that the respondent, after consulting with the Federal Public Service Policy and Support (FOD BOSA), responded to the questions during the inspection investigation. The Inspection Service notes that information from the Crossroads Bank for Enterprises (KBO) also indicates that the respondent should be considered the data controller. They attach an excerpt of this to the report.

25.  
The respondent disputes this qualification as data controller and refutes the findings of the Inspection Service. They acknowledge that they are indeed responsible for the issuance of, among other things, the eID cards but argue that this does not say anything about the disputed processing in this case, namely the publication of the certificates on the CT logs. The respondent places the certificates on the citizens' eIDs, but neither the respondent nor FOD BOSA, nor their subcontractors publish these certificates at any other location. Regarding the fact that they answered the questions from the Inspection Service, the respondent points out that they consulted with FOD BOSA and that during that consultation it was agreed that they would respond to the questions posed. According to the respondent, a qualification as data controller for the publication of the certificates on the CT logs cannot be inferred from the fact that they answered questions from the Inspection Service. Moreover, they have repeatedly indicated in their communications with the Inspection Service that they are not the data controller for the disputed processing. Regarding the last finding concerning the excerpt from the KBO, the respondent is unclear about what the Inspection Service means by this. The excerpt as included in the Inspection Report contains no substantive indications regarding the processing of personal data in any context, let alone about the eID and more specifically about the publication of the certificates on the CT logs, the respondent concludes.  
26.  
It is up to the Dispute Chamber to assess whether the respondent should be designated as the data controller for the disputed publication of the certificates on the CT logs.  
27.  
The Dispute Chamber points out that a data controller is responsible for its own processing “carried out by or on behalf of it” (Recital 74 GDPR). The Court of Justice has ruled on this point that a data controller cannot be held responsible for earlier or later processing for which it has not determined the purpose and means:  
“On the other hand, that natural or legal person, without prejudice to any civil liability provided for by national law in this regard, cannot be considered to be responsible in the sense of that provision for processing operations that take place earlier or later in the processing chain and for which it has not determined the purpose and means.”  
28.  
The guidelines 07/2021 on the concepts of “data controller” and “processor” in the GDPR from the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) also state that the qualification as data controller or processor must be assessed with

29.  
The Dispute Chamber does not have elements indicating that the respondent decided to proceed with the publication of the certificates in the CT logs, nor does it have information on how and why this publication would take place. The Inspection Service also states in its report that “the processing by the CT ecosystem was indeed not determined as a purpose by the [the respondent].”  
30.  
After investigation by the respondent and other involved actors such as Google, it is unclear who published the certificates in the CT logs. Since CT logs are an open-source platform and are intended to prevent erroneous or fraudulent certificates, anyone can (and must) upload certificates to the CT logs. Google does not keep extensive logs of who publishes something in its CT logs. Consequently, Google itself cannot determine who published the certificates in the CT logs.  
31.  
In light of the above, the Dispute Chamber concludes that it is not established that the respondent de jure or de facto determined the purpose and means of the disputed publication of the certificates and that the respondent cannot therefore be designated as the data controller in this context.  
32.  
Consequently, the Dispute Chamber does not uphold the findings of the Inspection Service and decides to proceed with a technical dismissal in accordance with its dismissal policy.11  
33.  
However, the Dispute Chamber points out that neither the National Register Act nor the GDPR allows for the publication of these certificates and the associated personal data in CT logs without an applicable legal basis. The personal data in question includes the national register number (as the serial number of the certificate), the unique key to many authentic personal data of the citizen. The use of these certificates makes the national register number public, as in the case of a digital signature or online authentication.
