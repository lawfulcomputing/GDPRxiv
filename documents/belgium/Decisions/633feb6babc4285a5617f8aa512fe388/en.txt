I.  
Facts and procedure  
1.  
On March 20, 2019, the complainant filed a complaint with the Data Protection Authority against the defendant, explicitly requesting to preserve his anonymity during the processing of the complaint.  
2.  
The subject of the complaint concerns how the defendant, a professional organization, processes the personal data of its members and trainees. The defendant published the personal data of members and trainees via a public search engine on its web application. The defendant allegedly used the data of its members and trainees during the period leading up to the elections held within the professional organization, for direct marketing purposes, including the sending of electoral propaganda emails by various candidates for the elections within the professional organization, as well as for invitations to events with commercial companies.

a) Legal basis

Public search engine  
18.  
In the first part of the complaint, the complainant argues that the defendant has published the personal data of members and trainees on its web application via a public search engine. The complainant asserts that he has never given his explicit consent to the defendant for the publication of his personal data on the defendant's website as a mandatory member of the defendant's organization.  
19.  
In its report, the Inspection Service emphasizes that regarding the publication of the personal data of members, the defendant invokes several legal bases for the same processing, namely Article 6.1 a), 6.1 c), and 6.1 f) of the GDPR.  
20. The investigation by the Inspection Service indeed revealed that the defendant relies on the legal obligation (Article 6.1 c) of the GDPR) to publish data recorded in its internal register and specifically refers to Article 44, paragraph 5 of the law of April 22, 1999, concerning accounting and tax professions. The Inspection Service notes in this regard that, while the aforementioned provision certainly constitutes a legal basis for the processing of personal data of members and trainees in the form of automated processing, there is, however, no sufficient legal basis for the publication of this personal data in a public register, accessible via the public search engine.  
21.  
The report of the Inspection Service also indicates that the privacy statement mentions another legal basis for the publication of this same personal data, namely the legitimate interest of the defendant (Article 6.1 f) of the GDPR).  
22.  
Following the investigation by the Inspection Service regarding the use of personal data included in the public register for the election of the defendant's members (see

The public responsibilities vested in data controllers are often not based on precisely defined obligations or legislative standards, more specifically the definition of the essential characteristics of data processing. Processing occurs rather on the basis of a more general authorization to act, as necessary for the fulfillment of the mission. As a result, in practice, the legal basis in question often contains no provision concretely describing the necessary data processing. Data controllers wishing to invoke Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR on the basis of such a legal basis must then perform their own balancing between the necessity of processing for the public interest mission and the interests of the data subjects.  

32. Based on the above, the Contentious Chamber considers that the legal provisions (Articles 44 and 46 of the law of April 22, 1999) invoked by the defendant are limited to determining the purpose of the register of individuals exercising the profession, namely the maintenance of the register of certified accountants and certified tax advisors, as well as the list of trainee accountants and trainee tax advisors. As the defendant points out, these provisions aim to subject the exercise of the profession to strict ethics and approval conditions that ensure, promote, and protect quality, professionalism, and the trust relationship with the (future) client. The public register must contribute to this. However, the Contentious Chamber notes that the legal provisions in question contain no indication obliging the defendant to publish the register of the concerned professionals online via the search engine. On the contrary, the defendant proceeded with the publication solely on the basis of a balancing that he performed himself, which he also acknowledges in the response conclusions. The defendant expressly states in this regard that the legislator himself did not provide any clear modalities regarding the form of the register and how it should be accessible to achieve the intended purpose. The defendant published it because he believed that at the present time, the availability of the register at the headquarters did not allow for achieving the intended objective. The defendant therefore did not act on the basis of an obligation imposed by the legislator.  

33. The correct legal basis on which the IPCF should have relied was the execution of the public interest mission with which it was entrusted (Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR), and not the legitimate interest (Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR) on which it subsequently thought it could base the publication of personal data via the search engine, following

There is, according to the Contentious Chamber, no violation of personal data within the meaning of Article 4.12 of the GDPR in relation to the defendant. One cannot speak of a data breach in the sense of Articles 32 and 33 in conjunction with Article 4.12 of the GDPR, as the defendant himself published the data via the search engine. The reuse for electoral purposes was not due to a security breach within the meaning of Article 4.12 of the GDPR in the absence of appropriate technical and organizational measures. However, the defendant has nothing to do with the processing of the data reused for electoral purposes and is therefore not responsible for this processing. There was a lack of legal basis for the publication; this constitutes a violation in itself and does not imply that one can also deduce a lack of measures to conclude a violation of Articles 32 and 33. On the other hand, there was indeed improper use of the published personal data of members and trainees by various candidates in the elections, but not by the defendant. The Dispute Chamber recalls that the role of a subcontractor does not arise from the nature of an entity processing data, but rather from its concrete activities in a specific context. The same entity may simultaneously act as a data controller for certain processing activities and as a subcontractor for others; the qualification of data controller or subcontractor must be assessed based on specific data sets or data processing activities. The nature of the service determines whether the processing activity amounts to processing personal data on behalf of the data controller within the meaning of Article 4.8 of the GDPR.  
In accordance with the Guidelines of the European Data Protection Board23, the Dispute Chamber considers that the service provided by the publishers consists of printing publications and distributing printed materials. This service does not specifically aim at processing personal data but primarily focuses on the printing and distribution of the defendant's publications and only secondarily on the processing of the recipients of the publications, namely the members and trainees.  
63. Consequently, in this case, the publishers must be regarded as distinct data controllers, capable of independently determining the purposes and means of the processing necessary to provide the service. This is also evident from the fact that the publications are offered independently by the concerned publishers, notably through their online store, where not only the members and trainees of the defendant but also other individuals can place an order directly with the publisher. This applies insofar as the offering of the publications involves the processing of data of individuals who have placed an order in order to deliver the ordered publication to them.  
64. This leads the Dispute Chamber to conclude that no violation of Article 28.3 of the GDPR has been committed by the IPCF. Regarding the ITAA, it appears from the case file that subcontracting contracts have been concluded with various partners, primarily IT communication partners, partners for events, and professional associations accredited for training purposes, so there is also no violation of Article 28.3 of the GDPR.
