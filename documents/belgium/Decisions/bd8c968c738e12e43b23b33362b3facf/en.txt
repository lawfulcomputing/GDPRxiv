II. Motivation  
II.1. Context  
23. In light of the specific elements of this case, the Contentious Chamber must establish some contextual observations as a preliminary matter.  
24. On May 31, 2021, noyb publishes an article5 expressing its desire to put an end to the "cookie banner terror," and indicates that it has sent 500 "pre-contentious letters" to European companies benefiting from certain publicity that use cookie banners non-compliant with the GDPR. The detection of the disputed cookie banners and the drafting of the pre-contentious letters are carried out using an automated system.  
25. According to noyb, companies make cookie banners a real headache for the individuals concerned, contrary to what is expected by the provisions of the GDPR. More specifically, these banners adopt a form that prevents individuals from refusing the installation of non-essential cookies as easily as they can accept them. noyb thus hoped to send pre-contentious letters to nearly 10,000 companies before the end of 2021.  
26. On August 9, 2022, noyb publishes a new article6 following the aforementioned article. It announces the filing of 226 complaints with 18 supervisory authorities under Article 51 of the GDPR due to insufficient or even non-existent compliance.  
II.2. Regarding the Competence of the DPA  
27. The DPA is the Belgian authority responsible for monitoring compliance with the GDPR in accordance with Article 8.3 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, Article 16.2 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU), and Article 51 of the GDPR.  
28. Pursuant to Article 3.1 of the GDPR, it applies in cases where the data controller is established in the territory of the European Union.  
29. According to Article 77 of the GDPR, it appears that the complainant – residing in Austria – has the right to lodge a complaint with the supervisory authority of her choice, even if the latter is located in a member state where the complainant does not have her habitual residence or place of work.  
30. In this case, the territorial competence of the DPA and the applicability of Belgian law are not in dispute, as the data controller identified by the SI in its investigation report has its establishment in Belgium.  
II.3. Regarding the Qualifications of the Representative  
31. Article 80.1 of the GDPR states the following: "The data subject has the right to mandate a body, organization, or association that is not for profit, which has been validly constituted in accordance with the law of a Member State, whose statutory objectives are of public interest and is active in the field of protecting the rights and freedoms of data subjects in the context of the protection of personal data concerning them, to lodge a complaint on their behalf, exercise on their behalf the rights referred to in Articles 77, 78, and 79, and exercise on their behalf the right to obtain compensation referred to in Article 82 when the law of a Member State provides for it."  
32. Under Article 220 of the Framework Law, it is specified that the body, organization, or association that the data subject wishes to mandate must, among other things, be validly constituted under Belgian law and be active in the field of protecting the rights and freedoms of data subjects in the context of the protection of personal data for at least three years. The Contentious Chamber notes the existence of doubts regarding the compatibility of these conditions with Union law, as the European legislator did not call for any precision of this kind concerning Article 80.1 of the GDPR. That said, the Belgian provision is of no consequence in the present case.

II.4.2. Position of the Contentious Chamber  
42. The Contentious Chamber immediately recalls that Article 80.1 allows any concerned person to mandate "an organization, a non-profit organization, or an association" – NOYB being constituted in this latter form.

43. The Contentious Chamber qualifies the complainant as a concerned person since she visited the defendant's website, and her personal data was processed in this context.  
44. Moreover, the Contentious Chamber notes that a mandate designating NOYB as representative was established in German and English on August 2, 2021 (see point 6) to represent the complainant in the cases referred to in Articles 80.1, 77, and 78 of the GDPR.  
45. Going even further than the position of the SI (see point 35), the Contentious Chamber considers that the mandate presents a fictitious character, for the reasons set out below:  
46. First, the Contentious Chamber raises that the complainant was indeed an intern at the time she visited the defendant's website, as well as at the time she mandated NOYB. More specifically, and in light of the complainant's statements during the hearing (see point 23), it appears that the visit to the defendant's website took place as part of a mission assigned to her by NOYB – the association where she completed her internship. This mission involved visiting 5 Belgian websites – of which the defendant's was one – to potentially ascertain the presence of a non-compliant cookie banner under the GDPR. Therefore, the Contentious Chamber cannot agree with NOYB's reasoning when it asserts in its conclusions that "the complainant selected and consulted the website autonomously and noted and documented the violation."9 On the contrary, the complainant herself acknowledged during the hearing that she did not select the defendant's website, but that it was part of the 5 cases assigned to her and that she was thus required to address (see point 34). The complainant having acted within the framework of an internship mission, the Contentious Chamber questions the aforementioned statement by NOYB since everything suggests that she acted as a collaborator of NOYB.  
47. Notwithstanding this first reason, the Contentious Chamber notes that the complainant refrained from revoking her representation mandate after the end of her mission at NOYB, and due to the fact that she expressed her intention to file a complaint with the APD. The Contentious Chamber does not deny this, and does not claim to judge the motivation of complainants in exercising their right to file a complaint with a supervisory authority. The question here is distinct.  
48. Indeed, in the field of data protection, the Contentious Chamber is particularly attentive to the respect of the conditions for obtaining consent in the case of data processing based on this legal basis (Article 6.1.a in conjunction with 4.11 of the GDPR). Although in this case, the Contentious Chamber does not examine the

consent as a basis for the lawfulness of processing personal data, the concept of consent presents a useful analogy.  
49. In order to determine whether the consent obtained is valid, the Dispute Chamber examines, among other things, whether the data subject has freely consented to the processing of their data (art. 4.11 of the GDPR). In this regard, the Dispute Chamber observes whether the data subject had a genuine freedom of choice or whether they were able to refuse or withdraw their consent without suffering any detriment (recital 42 of the GDPR). Furthermore, the Dispute Chamber also takes into account specific cases that may compromise the freedom of their consent, particularly when there is a manifest imbalance between them and the data controller (recital 43 of the GDPR).  
50. In its Guidelines 5/2020 on consent, the EDPB has interpreted that such a manifest imbalance that may affect the freedom of consent can manifest itself in the context of employment relationships. Indeed, the EDPB considers that it is unlikely that an employee can refuse to give their consent to their employer for the processing of their data without suffering, or fearing to suffer, negative consequences. The EDPB specifies that employees "can only freely give their consent in exceptional situations, when absolutely no negative consequences will result from their refusal to give consent." Therefore, in view of the imbalance of power dynamics between an employer and their employees, and the resulting hierarchical dependence, the EDPB considers it improbable that an employee is able to respond freely to a request from their employer without feeling obliged to consent.  
51. In this case, the Dispute Chamber again notes that the defendant was indeed an intern at noyb at the time the mandate was issued to noyb and the complaint was filed with the DPA. The Chamber therefore finds that there was a hierarchical link between the complainant and noyb at the time of the events. The Dispute Chamber also notes that it was in the context of her internship that noyb assigned the task to the complainant to visit websites to identify potential violations of the GDPR. And that it was following these visits ordered – or at least facilitated – by noyb that the decision to file a complaint with the DPA was made. The Chamber thus observes that the mandate given to noyb is situated within a professional context that may have influenced the complainant.  
52. Therefore, by analogy to the rules regarding the protection of personal data, the Dispute Chamber considers that the fact that the complainant was an intern at noyb may have influenced her ability to give free consent.

noyb was able to substantially affect the freedom of its consent during the execution of the mandate. The Chamber takes this additional element into account in its decision.  
53. Secondly and finally, the complainant stated during the hearing that the mission entrusted to her is part of a broader "plan," which aims to verify the compliance of several websites with the GDPR. Furthermore, the Contentious Chamber adds that the visit to the defendant's website lasted precisely 3 minutes and 48 seconds. Based on these two elements, the Contentious Chamber concludes that there is no spontaneous nature to this visit; rather, it had no other purpose than the mere observation of a potential non-compliance of the defendant's cookie banner with regard to the GDPR, followed, if applicable, by the sending of a "pre-contentious letter" (see point 4) to the defendant and, if applicable, followed by the filing of a complaint with the DPA.  
54. The Contentious Chamber thus considers that noyb acts not as a representative based on Article 80.1 of the GDPR, but rather as a complainant based on Article 80.2 of the GDPR, which states: "Member States may provide that any body, organization, or association referred to in paragraph 1 of this Article, regardless of any mandate conferred by a data subject, has the right in the Member State in question to lodge a complaint with the supervisory authority competent under Article 77, and to exercise the rights referred to in Articles 78 and 79 if it considers that the rights of a data subject under this Regulation have been violated as a result of the processing."  
55. The Contentious Chamber notes that the Belgian legislator deliberately chose not to implement this open clause.  
56. The Contentious Chamber adds that Article 58 of the LCA allows a legal entity – as is the case for noyb in this instance – to file a complaint with the DPA provided that it demonstrates a sufficient interest, as expressed by the Belgian legislator in the preparatory works of the LCA.  
57. In previous decisions, the Contentious Chamber has had the opportunity to outline the scope of this notion. Although the Contentious Chamber, in its capacity as an administrative authority, deals with cases on a case-by-case basis, it notes that a sufficient interest must be understood as an interest that is concrete in nature and is therefore not specifically public. This is the case when an employer – in its capacity as a legal entity – wishes to file a complaint regarding a potential breach of the GDPR concerning a member of its staff.  
58. In this case, it appears that the breaches specifically concern a member of noyb's staff, given that the complainant was an intern at the time of the events (see point 35).  
59. However, the Contentious Chamber cannot limit its examination to this technical finding. Indeed, while it acknowledges that the breaches of the GDPR for which noyb wishes to file a complaint concern a person who was an intern at the time of the events, it cannot ignore the artificiality of the situation, which would ultimately amount to completely emptying Article 80.1 of the GDPR of its substance, as well as erasing the distance that separates it from Article 80.2 of the same Regulation.  
60. Although the objective pursued by noyb constitutes a major concern within the European Union – and beyond – and aligns with the missions pursued by the DPA, the Contentious Chamber considers that based on the facts of the present case, noyb has used the complainant as a catalyst in order to act before the DPA and thus defend its objectives, which are by nature and necessarily of a public order.  
61. Consequently, the Contentious Chamber notes that noyb does not demonstrate a sufficient interest to act as it pursues a public interest.  
62. The Contentious Chamber concludes that it is not necessary to continue the examination of the present case, given that the aforementioned elements allow it to ascertain a lack of standing to act on the part of the actual complainant.

The refusal of consent is not situated at the same level as the granting of consent; it is naturally more difficult for a concerned individual to not consent than to consent to the processing related to cookies.  
65. In terms of its investigation report, the Supervisory Authority concludes secondly to a violation of Articles 5.1.a. and 6.1.a. of the GDPR read in conjunction with Article 10/2 of the LCA in that analytical cookies – not strictly necessary – were installed without obtaining the prior consent of the concerned individual (“Type I violation”).  
66. The Supervisory Authority explains that the “Cookie Choices” option on the cookie banner directs the concerned individual to a second screen that lists various categories of cookies. Among them are “Strictly Necessary Cookies” as well as “Analytical Cookies.” Both are “Always Active” so that it is not possible to deactivate them. Furthermore, analytical cookies include cookies that enable marketing targeting, and are therefore to be considered as personal data.  
67. In terms of its investigation report, the Supervisory Authority concludes thirdly to a violation of Article 7.3 of the GDPR read in conjunction with Articles 24 and 25 of the GDPR in that it is not as easy to withdraw consent as it is to give it (“Type K violation”).  
68. In said report, the Supervisory Authority details in this regard that granting consent consists only of an action to be performed on a “Allow All” button – namely, a “click.” In contrast, to withdraw consent globally, it is necessary to perform 4 such actions. For the withdrawal of consent to be as easy as its granting, the number of actions required should be equal for both choices – which is not the case in this instance.  
69. In terms of its investigation report, the Supervisory Authority concludes fourthly and lastly to a violation of Articles 5.1.a. and 6.1.a. of the GDPR read in conjunction with Articles 4.11 and 25 of the GDPR and Article 10/2 of the LCA in that the use of colors and contrasts by the defendant is likely to negatively affect the conditions for granting consent by the concerned individuals, and that there is a misleading design of links on the first level of the cookie banner (“Type C, D, and E violations”).

77.
