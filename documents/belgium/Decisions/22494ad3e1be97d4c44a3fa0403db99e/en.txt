2.  
In her complaint and the clarifications provided in terms of conclusions and during the hearing (see below), complainant No. 1 reports the following facts.  
3.  
In early October 2020, wishing to make an appointment with her dentist without calling by phone, complainant No. 1 found that the website […] (hereinafter "Z" or "the platform" or "the platform Z") allows for direct online appointment booking with health professionals. The contact details of her dentist are listed on the platform, and complainant No. 1 creates an account to be able to make an appointment with her.  
4.  
Complainant No. 1 reports that it was only after creating her account that she received the information that it was not possible for her to make an appointment with her dentist, as only the contact details of the latter were listed on the site without the possibility of making an appointment with her via the platform itself.  
5.  
For the proper understanding of this decision, the Contentious Chamber specifies here that the platform "Z" aims to facilitate quick contact between patients and healthcare professionals – including but not limited to doctors – particularly through online appointment bookings. When a patient accesses the platform, two options are available to them:  
-  
Either only the contact details of the sought practitioner are listed on the platform without the possibility of making an appointment with them via the platform. This is referred to as a "non-registered practitioner/professional." In this case, the patient can make an appointment with this practitioner through their own contact channels based on the contact details listed on the platform. No account is necessary for this purpose.  
-  
Or it is possible to make an appointment directly via the platform with the sought practitioner. This is referred to as a "registered practitioner/professional" on the platform. To make such an appointment, the patient must create a patient account for which they are asked to provide the following personal data: their last name, first name, email address, phone number, and National Registry Number (NRN).  
6. On October 21, 2020, having been unable to make an appointment with her dentist (non-registered) via the platform, complainant No. 1 requests the deletion of her account and the personal data she provided during its creation. In this regard, the Litigation Chamber recalls that in a judgment of October 7, 2021, the Court of Cassation stated:  
“3. It is undeniably clear from all the aforementioned legal provisions that a data subject has the right to lodge a complaint with the Data Protection Authority against a processing practice that they believe violates their rights under the GDPR (...). This is also the case when the personal data of the data subject themselves have not been processed, but the latter has not obtained the benefit or service because, precisely due to the existence of the practice that presumably constitutes a violation, they refused to consent to the processing.”

II.1.2. The assessment of the Contentious Chamber  
55.  
The Contentious Chamber is not bound by the status claimed by the first defendant, nor by that which would be attributed to it by the SI. It must assess the reality of this qualification and, if necessary, dismiss it if its analysis shows that it cannot be upheld.  
56.  
The Contentious Chamber recalls that a data controller is defined as "the natural or legal person or any other entity that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data" (Article 4.7 of the GDPR). This is an autonomous concept specific to data protection regulation, the assessment of which must be based on the criteria it sets forth: the determination of the purposes of the relevant data processing as well as that of the means thereof.  
57.  
The Contentious Chamber also recalls that the essential criterion for there to be— as argued by complainant No. 1—joint responsibility for processing is the joint participation of two or more entities in determining the purposes and means of a processing operation. More specifically, joint participation must encompass, on the one hand, the determination of the purposes and, on the other hand, the determination of the means. Joint participation in this determination implies that more than one entity exerts a determining influence on the question of whether, for what purpose, and how the processing takes place. In practice, joint participation may take the form of a common decision made by two or more entities or result from converging decisions adopted by them regarding the essential purposes and means of the processing.  
58.  
In this case, the Contentious Chamber notes that the elements provided by complainant No. 1 do indeed show, as highlighted by the SI and not contested by the first defendant, that the second defendant invested in the project to create the platform by the first defendant (then a young start-up). However, the status of joint data controller requires, as previously noted, participation in the determination of the purposes and means of the processing operations. The mere act of financially supporting a project does not necessarily imply that there is a determination of these purposes and means.

of assessing the degree of intrusion of the

processing envisaged in the individual sphere, by measuring its impact on the private life of individuals (processing of sensitive data, processing concerning vulnerable persons, profiling, etc.) and on their other fundamental rights (freedom of expression, freedom of information, freedom of conscience, etc.) as well as the other concrete impacts of the processing on their situation (monitoring or surveillance of their activities or movements, exclusion from access to services, etc.). These impacts must be measured in order to determine, on a case-by-case basis, the extent of the intrusion caused by the processing into the lives of individuals. The principle of data minimization will also be taken into account.  
102. The data controller must then consider, in weighing its legitimate interest against the rights and interests of the individuals concerned, the "reasonable expectations" of those individuals. This consideration is essential for processing that can be carried out without the prior consent of the individuals: in the absence of a positive and explicit act on their part, the legitimate interest requires not to surprise individuals in the modalities of implementation as well as in the consequences of the processing.  
103. In this case, the Contentious Chamber finds that the data processed are indeed publicly accessible and professional data. This nature of the processed data is a factor that can contribute to tipping the balance in favor of the data controller, provided that the individuals concerned can reasonably expect their data to be used for the purpose pursued by the said data controller and this, without their prior consent.  
104. The Contentious Chamber is of the opinion that in this case, the disputed processing does not fall within the reasonable expectations of the concerned unregistered healthcare professionals. Their contact details are published on their own website or on that of their practice, or even on that of a hospital where they provide services. They have a direct link with this practice or hospital. The Contentious Chamber starts from the idea that they have expressed their agreement for these publications which fall within the framework of professional relationships they have themselves established. That the first defendant may have thought that increased visibility for healthcare professionals could be beneficial to them can certainly be understood. However, the Contentious Chamber concludes that in this case, the fact that the processing cannot be considered as falling within the reasonable expectations of these practitioners is decisive and tips the balance in favor of the rights and freedoms of the latter. Therefore, the "balancing test" is not satisfied.

At the end of the analysis above, the Contentious Chamber concludes that there has been a violation of Article 5.1. a) (lawfulness requirement) and Article 6.1. of the GDPR by the first defendant in that it could not validly rely on the lawful basis of Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR to legitimize the processing of data of healthcare professionals not registered on its platform and therefore did not have a valid lawful basis to justify said processing.  
II.5. Regarding the processing of the National Register Number (DOS-2020-05649 and DOS-2021-05271)  
II.5.1. The parties' viewpoints  
106. Both complainant No. 1 and complainant No. 2 denounce in their respective complaints the processing of the NRN by the first defendant and question the lawfulness of its processing in light of the LRN.  
107. Complainant No. 1 highlights that, in principle, the use of the NRN is prohibited. Only the persons exhaustively listed in Article 5.1. of the LRN may use it, subject to authorization from the Minister of the Interior. By way of exception, Article 8.3. of the LRN provides that "an authorization to use the national register number is not required if the national register number is exclusively used for the identification and authentication of a natural person within the framework of a computer application offered by a private or public institution of Belgian law or by the authorities, institutions, and persons referred to in Article 5, § 1." Complainant No. 1 believes that the first defendant wrongly relies on this exemption since the patient's introduction of their NRN on the platform does not allow for identification or authentication (the NRN seems rather to be used as a user number for the data subject), the first defendant not having access to the National Register or the patient's electronic identity card. Therefore, according to complainant No. 1, it should be considered that the first defendant processes the NRN of the platform users unlawfully.  
108. As for the first defendant, it states in its conclusions that it opted for the NRN to identify and authenticate the users of the platform securely, as it is a unique identifier of which only the data subject has, in principle, knowledge (point 22). It deemed it essential, given the (health) data that could be exchanged through the platform between a patient and a healthcare professional, that the user be uniquely and reliably identified and authenticated. The first defendant indicates that it believed it could rely on the aforementioned Article 8.3. of the LRN while stating that it does not access the national register.
Decision on the merits 149/2023 — 27/34  
109. During the hearing, the first defendant indicated, as already mentioned in point 28, that she had made the decision to refrain from requesting the National Registration Number (NRN) from users of the platform, thereby breaking with a choice that had been made at the time of the platform's creation by the previous management.  
II.5.2. The assessment of the Contentious Chamber  
110. Article 87 of the GDPR provides that "Member States may specify the specific conditions for the processing of a national identification number or any other identifier of general application. In this case, the national identification number or any other identifier of general application is only used subject to appropriate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the data subject adopted under this Regulation."  
111. Under Belgian law, the processing of the NRN, which constitutes such a national identification number, is strictly regulated in the aforementioned National Registration Law (LRN). Article 5 of the LRN exhaustively determines the authorities, bodies, organizations, or persons who may access it.  
112. Article 8 of the LRN states the necessity of being, unless an exception applies, authorized by the Minister of the Interior to, as in this case, use this number. An exemption from authorization is provided if, as mentioned above, the NRN is exclusively used "for the purposes of identifying and authenticating a natural person within the framework of a computer application offered by a private or public institution under Belgian law or by the authorities, bodies, and persons referred to in Article 5, § 1."  
113. As the Privacy Protection Commission (CPVP) emphasized in its opinion 19/2018 of February 28, 2018, concerning a draft law introducing, among other things, amendments to the LRN, including the aforementioned Article 8.3, "the authentication of a person consists of verifying that they have the identity they claim to have. It is the authentication certificate (including the national registration number) present on the electronic identity card that allows this through cryptographic techniques" (point 31 of the opinion).  
114. In this case, it appears from the explanations provided by the first defendant herself that she was not identifying or authenticating the user of the platform based on the NRN that the latter was invited to provide. Indeed, the first defendant admits not accessing the National Register. She therefore does not verify, through access to the RN data and reading the electronic identity card, the authenticity of the information provided.

120. The Contentious Chamber specifies beforehand the following regarding the principle of "ne bis in idem" invoked by the first defendant concerning the complaint regarding the lawfulness of the processing of the NRN. The first defendant indeed expresses surprise at having been invited to conclude on this issue in the context of the two complaints (point 22) and stated during the hearing that they were questioning the respect of this principle by the Contentious Chamber if it were to sanction the same breach twice over the same period.  
121. The Contentious Chamber recalls that the general legal principle "non bis in idem" entails that "no one can be prosecuted or punished a second time for an offense (even if otherwise qualified) for which they have already been acquitted or convicted by a final judgment in accordance with the law and the criminal procedure of each country." In other words, second prosecutions are prohibited for identical or substantially identical facts that, having been subject to previous prosecutions, have resulted in a final decision of acquittal or conviction. By "identical or substantially identical facts," one must understand a set of concrete factual circumstances related to a...

The respect due to this general principle of law does not in any way prevent the Contentious Chamber from inviting the concerned parties to defend themselves regarding the same grievance raised in different pending proceedings in which it has not yet made a decision. The Contentious Chamber is free to invite conclusions in a pending case following a complaint, even if subsequent to another that raises the same grievance during the same time period. It remains free to adopt its decisions according to the priority schedule it establishes. The Contentious Chamber adds that while in the specific case, the nature of the sanctions it imposes may, as the case may be, be classified as penal, this is not necessarily always the case.

In this case, however, the Disciplinary Chamber wishes to respond to the fact that in its reply and summary conclusions, the first defendant argues that the Disciplinary Chamber would not be justified in imposing an administrative fine on the grounds that the fine is only the 13th sanction in the enumeration of Article 100 of the LCA, as well as based on the considerations expressed by the Market Court (CdM) in its judgment of January 27, 2021 (RG 2020/AR/1333, p.19), as well as those in which, in a judgment of May 26, 2021, the said Court would dismiss the possibility for the Disciplinary Chamber to impose a fine from the first infringement committed inadvertently (GR 2021/AR/163).  
128. The Disciplinary Chamber recalls that Article 58.2 of the GDPR states that each supervisory authority has the power to adopt all the corrective measures listed. The administrative fine is listed in the penultimate position (9th – litera i)), just before the 10th which allows for the suspension of flows. To consider that there is a form of hierarchy among the measures finds no support in the text of the GDPR; on the contrary, since litera j) specifies that each supervisory authority may impose an administrative fine in accordance with Article 83, in addition to or instead of the measures referred to in this paragraph, depending on the specific characteristics of each case. Furthermore, the measure referred to in litera j) (suspension of flows) cannot, of course, be conditioned by the prior existence of a fine that would have been imposed. That would make no sense.  
129. Regarding Article 100.1 of the LCA implemented under the GDPR, the same reasoning applies. It should be noted in this regard that the LCA does not reproduce the full terms of Article 58.2 of the GDPR, namely that the administrative fine may be ordered in addition to or instead of the measures referred to in this paragraph, depending on the specific characteristics of each case. Certainly, the fine is mentioned in 13th position in litera 13, but this must be read with the precision that has been omitted by the Belgian legislator. The measures referred to in litera 14, 15, and 16 (publication of the decision) of Article 100.1 of the LCA are moreover not designed to only occur "after a fine."  
130. In summary, the position of the fine in the list of corrective measures/sanctions provided for in the GDPR and the LCA does not mean, a fortiori, in support of the text of Article 58.2.i) of the...
