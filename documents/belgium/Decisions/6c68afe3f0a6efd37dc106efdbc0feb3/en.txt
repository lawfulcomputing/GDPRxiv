I. Facts and procedure  
1.  
On January 8, 2020, the complainant filed a complaint with the Data Protection Authority against the defendant. Previously, the complainant had submitted the case to the First Line Service for mediation, but he claims that this mediation procedure did not yield a satisfactory result.  
2.  
The subject of the complaint concerns the receipt of an unsolicited commercial email sent on October 2, 2018, to the complainant by the defendant. Following this, the complainant contacted the defendant on October 3, 2018, asking how his personal data had been obtained without his knowledge, given that he had never provided it to them himself. He therefore requested access to his personal data with an explicit request for the manner in which the defendant obtained his personal data as well as the legal basis on which the processing of this data is based. According to the complainant, the defendant did not provide a satisfactory response to his access request; they only specified that his personal data had been deleted, although the complainant had not requested this. Furthermore, the complainant asserts that unsolicited emails were still sent to him, despite the notification of the deletion of his data by the defendant.  
3.  
The complaint is part of a bankruptcy in which the curator first transferred the member file of the bankrupt football club to SA W on June 8, 2018, and the file was subsequently transferred to the defendant on June 13, 2018. The personal data of the subscribers of the bankrupt club was already in the database of Z, a limited liability company under Dutch law, prior to the bankruptcy, as the bankrupt club had decided to collaborate with Z regarding communication with supporters. The defendant subsequently decided to continue the collaboration with Z.  
4.  
On February 10, 2020, the complaint was declared admissible by the First Line Service based on Articles 58 and 60 of the LCA, and the complaint was forwarded to the Contentious Chamber under Article 62, § 1 of the LCA.

19. In accordance with Article 5.1(b) of the GDPR, the processing of personal data for purposes other than those for which the personal data were initially collected should only be permitted if it is compatible with the purposes for which the personal data were initially collected. Given the circumstances, the Chamber Contentieuse assesses whether the new purpose for which the members' file is processed in the context of bankruptcy can be considered compatible with the initial purpose communicated to the complainant at the time of their affiliation with the club as a supporter.
Decision on the merits 108/2024 — 7/30  
Regarding the criteria set out in Article 6.4 and in Recital 50 of the GDPR, it must therefore be verified whether the subsequent processing, namely the transfer of the members' file - containing the complainant's data - to the curator and then to a purchaser of the assets falling within the estate (in this case, the defendant) is compatible with the initial processing consisting of the registration of football supporters' affiliation. The Disciplinary Chamber concludes that the complainant entrusted his personal data to the declared bankrupt club to affiliate with the club as a subscriber and could not reasonably expect that this same data would be used in the context of a potential subsequent bankruptcy for a transfer to a curator without being able to oppose it and without being informed, regardless of the purpose for which the curator would subsequently process the obtained personal data. In this regard, the Disciplinary Chamber specifies that when assessing the complainant's reasonable expectations, it takes into account the fact that the curator may in turn monetize the members' file thus obtained in the context of his legal mission for any other purpose that must be distinguished from the initial purpose, and this mission is not limited to the realization of a revival of the club. Given that the curator is not obliged to solely facilitate a revival but that it is possible, as soon as a bankruptcy occurs, for the curator to monetize the members' file for any other new purpose, and this against the will of the concerned individuals, the processing of data by the curator in the event of bankruptcy simply cannot be considered as compatible subsequent processing.  
20. The finding that there is no question of compatible subsequent processing implies that a distinct legal basis is required for the transfer of the members' file by the curator to the defendant to be deemed lawful.  
21. Processing of personal data, and therefore also incompatible subsequent processing as in this case, is indeed lawful only if there is a legal basis for this purpose. For incompatible subsequent processing, it is necessary to rely on Article 6.1 of the GDPR as well as on Recital 50 of the GDPR. Recital 50 of the GDPR indicates that a distinct legal basis is required for the processing of personal data for purposes that are not compatible with the purposes for which the data were initially collected.

In accordance with Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR and the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter "the Court"), three cumulative conditions must be met for a data controller to validly invoke this legal basis, namely, firstly, the pursuit of a legitimate interest by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data is communicated; secondly, the necessity of processing the personal data for the realization of the legitimate interest pursued; and thirdly, the condition that the rights and fundamental freedoms of the data subject do not prevail" (judgment "Rigas"5).  
25.  
In other words, in order to invoke the legal basis of "legitimate interest" in accordance with Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR, the data controller must demonstrate that:  
1) the interests it pursues with the processing can be recognized as legitimate (the "purpose test");  
2) the intended processing is necessary to achieve those interests (the “necessity test”);

26.  
Regarding the first condition (the so-called "purpose test"), the Contentious Chamber considers that liquidating a bankruptcy must be regarded as being carried out in pursuit of a legitimate interest. This allows the trustee to fulfill his mission rooted in Book XX of the Economic Law Code (CDE), namely to proceed with the liquidation of the bankruptcy estate as specified in Article XX.98 of the CDE6. This legal mission implies that the trustee is obliged to sell the assets still present in the estate in order to honor the debts of the bankrupt towards his creditors. In this regard, it is clear that the trustee is attempting to sell at the highest possible price, which implies, in a certain sense, a commercial interest. Such a commercial interest may be a legitimate interest in accordance with Recital 47 of the GDPR7. This is also supported in Opinion 06/2014 of the "Article 29" Working Party on Data Protection8 (the legal predecessor of the EDPB). Therefore, the first condition set out in Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR is fulfilled9.  
27.  
To fulfill the second condition, it must be demonstrated that the processing is necessary for the achievement of the pursued purposes. This means more precisely that it must be asked whether the same result can be achieved by other means, without processing personal data or without substantial processing that is unnecessary for the individuals concerned.

It is particularly important to assess whether "the data subject can reasonably expect, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that such data will be processed for a specific purpose."  
30. This aspect is also emphasized by the Court in its judgment "TK v. Asociaţia de Proprietari bloc M5A-ScaraA" of December 11, 2019, which specifies the following:  
"The reasonable expectations of the data subject that their personal data will not be processed are also relevant for the purposes of this balancing test when, in the circumstances of the case, that person cannot reasonably expect further processing of such data."  
31.  
The Contentious Chamber verifies whether the interest of the defendant is proportional to the impact on the freedoms and fundamental rights of the data subjects, including the complainant. In this regard, the Contentious Chamber notes that it is clear that the legal mission of the curator is to defend the interests of the creditors. The curator will therefore wish to achieve the highest possible profit by selling the entirety of the bankrupt's assets, including the complete membership file, to the person responsible for the relaunch. The membership file must indeed be considered essential for the person responsible for the relaunch, given that having the contact details of the subscribers constitutes the starting point for enabling a relaunch.  
32.

In this context, it should, however, not be overlooked that in the normal expectation scheme of the complainant, in the event of bankruptcy, the curator proceeds to sell the assets along with the related membership file in view of the anticipated relaunch of the club. Regarding the first condition (the so-called "purpose test"), the Contentious Chamber considers that the purpose pursued by the defendant, which consists of contacting former subscribers once by email to offer them a subscription with the acquiring club during the relaunch, can be regarded as a legitimate interest within the meaning of recital 47 of the GDPR. Therefore, the first condition set out in Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR is fulfilled.  
41.  
Concerning the second condition (the so-called "necessity test"), the defendant has put forward arguments that make it plausible that, with the alternatives that would be, according to the Inspection Service, less intrusive for the former subscribers concerned than the direct mail that was sent, the pursued purpose would not have been achieved. While the Inspection Service refers to a media campaign and leaflets, the defendant emphasizes that it is impossible to reach all interested supporters through a media campaign (supporters who are not yet aware of the offer would then directly contact the defendant, which would not be feasible). The distribution of leaflets in the stadium is also not an option, according to the defendant, given that supporters must necessarily first be informed of a match in order to attend and then be able to receive a leaflet there. III. Corrective Measures and Sanctions  
54. Regarding the identified violation of Articles 5.1.a), 12.1, 14, and 21.4 of the GDPR as well as Articles 12.3, 12.4, and 15.1 of the GDPR, the Contentious Chamber decides to impose an administrative fine under its powers based on Article 83 of the GDPR and Article 100, § 1, 13° of the LCA.  
55. The EDPB adopted on May 24, 2023, the Guidelines 04/2022 on the calculation of administrative fines under the GDPR (hereinafter: the Guidelines), pursuant to Article 70, paragraph 1, e) of the GDPR. The Guidelines are directly applicable as they do not provide for a transitional provision for procedures that were already underway at the time of their adoption.  
56. The Guidelines describe a methodology for determining the amount of the fine as follows:  
Step 1: Identify the processing activities and violations that need to be assessed;  
Step 2: Set the starting amount for calculating the fine for the identified violations (starting amount);  
Step 3: Assess any aggravating or mitigating circumstances that may require adjustment of the amount set in Step 2;  
Step 4: Determine the applicable maximum amounts for the violations and whether any increases from the previous step do not exceed these amounts;  
Step 5: Determine whether the final amount of the calculated fine is effective, proportionate, and dissuasive and adjust this amount to these criteria if necessary.  
57. The Contentious Chamber determines the amount of the administrative fine using this methodology. On July 4, 2024, the Contentious Chamber informed the defendant, via the sanction form, of its intention to impose an administrative fine of 8,000 euros. On August 13, 2024, the defendant provided its response to the sanction form to the Contentious Chamber. This will be examined in more detail below.  
Step 1: Identify the processing activities and violations that need to be assessed  
58. In order to determine the starting amount of the fine, it is first necessary, as described in the Guidelines, to examine whether there is one or more behaviors that may be sanctioned. The starting point in this context is that the same processing operation or related processing operations may constitute a single behavior. The term "related" refers to the principle that a single behavior may consist of several actions carried out within the framework of a unified intent and that are so closely linked contextually (notably concerning the identity of the data subject, the purpose, and the nature), spatially, and temporally that, from an objective point of view, they would be considered as constituting a coherent single behavior.  
59. The violations concerning the defendant relate to the failure to comply with the principle of transparency, the obligation to provide information (Articles 5.1.a), 12.1, 14, and 21.4 of the GDPR), and the infringement of the right of access (Articles 12.3, 12.4, and 15.1 of the GDPR).  
60. In accordance with the Guidelines, the Contentious Chamber explains below that, in this case, there are circumstances that constitute a single behavior, but that this behavior results in not just one but several violations, which implies that the attribution of one violation excludes the attribution of another violation. Indeed, the behavior that led to the complaint and from which the aforementioned violations arose consists of sending the unsolicited email to the complainant. Under the principle of specialty, it is established that the principle of transparency (Article 5.1.a) of the GDPR) as a fundamental principle constitutes a general concept that is concretized in Article 12 of the GDPR in the form of an obligation to provide information on the part of the data controller, namely the defendant. Furthermore, the Guidelines refer to the principle of consumption in cases where the violation of one provision regularly leads to the violation of another, because the first violation constitutes a preliminary step to the other. Thus, the Contentious Chamber concludes that the lack of transparent information and communication (Articles 5.1.a) and 12.1, 14, and 21.4 of the GDPR) by the defendant subsequently deprived the complainant of their right of access, despite Articles 12.3, 12.4 in conjunction with Article 15.1 of the GDPR.  

The principle of specialty (specialia generalibus derogant) is a legal principle that implies that a more specific provision (derived from the same legal act or from several legal acts with the same legal force) takes precedence over a more general provision, even though both provisions aim at the same objective. 63. As mentioned in the Guidelines, almost all obligations of the data controller are qualified in the provisions of Article 83, paragraphs 4 to 6 inclusive of the GDPR. The GDPR distinguishes between two types of violations. On one hand, violations subject to penalties under Article 83, paragraph 4 of the GDPR, which may incur a maximum fine of 10 million euros (or in the case of a company, 2% of the annual turnover, whichever is higher); on the other hand, violations subject to penalties under Article 83, paragraphs 5 and 6 of the GDPR, which may incur a maximum fine of 20 million euros (or in the case of a company, 4% of the annual turnover, whichever is higher). By this distinction, the legislator has provided an initial indication in abstracto of the severity of the violation: the more serious the violation, the higher the fine.

Given that the Disciplinary Chamber finds that the defendant has committed a violation of the principle of transparency and the related obligation to inform (Articles 5.1.a, 12.1, 14, and 21.4 of the GDPR), while also disregarding the complainant's right of access (Articles 12.3, 12.4, and 15.1 of the GDPR), it is not only the basic principles of processing that have been violated, but also the rights of the data subject, which are fundamental and whose violation is consequently sanctioned, in accordance with Article 83.5.a) and b) of the GDPR, with an administrative fine of up to 20 million euros or, in the case of a company, up to 4% of the annual global turnover of the previous financial year, whichever is higher.

with the fine not being less than 6,000 euros or exceeding 12,000 euros38.  
89. Taking into account the minimum and maximum amounts set in the Guidelines by  
level, the relevant gross margin of the defendant, and the factors listed in the  
section "III. Step 2", the Contentious Chamber decides to set the final starting  
amount for the established violation (falling under Article 83.5 of the GDPR with a  
medium degree of severity) at 9,000 euros, which is 0.30% of the theoretical  
starting amount of 3 million euros.  
Step 3: Assessment of Aggravating and Mitigating Circumstances  

i)  
Assessment of any aggravating or mitigating circumstances  
90. As mentioned in the Guidelines, it is then necessary to assess whether, in the  
circumstances of the case, there is a need to set a fine higher or lower than the  
starting amount defined in this regard. The circumstances to be considered are  
mentioned in Article 83, paragraph 2 of the GDPR. Each of the circumstances  
mentioned in this provision can only be assessed once39. In the previous step,  
the nature, severity, and duration of the violation40, the intentional or negligent  
nature of the violation41, and the categories of personal data42 have already been  
taken into account. Therefore, only parts c) to f) inclusive and h) to k) inclusive  
of Article 83, paragraph 2 of the GDPR remain.  
91. Relevant violations previously committed by the data controller or the processor  
(art. 83.2.e) of the GDPR) — The Contentious Chamber takes into account the fact  
that to date, it has not been seized of any other proceedings against the defendant.  
Referring to the Guidelines, the Contentious Chamber states that the absence of  
previous violations must be deemed neutral and cannot be considered as a  
mitigating factor, given that compliance with the GDPR is the norm43.  
92. The manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the violation  
(art. 83.2.h) of the GDPR) — Given that the Contentious Chamber became aware of  
the violation following a complaint, this element is deemed neutral in accordance  
with the Guidelines44.  
93. The degree of cooperation established with the supervisory authority in order to  
remedy the violation and mitigate its potential negative effects (art. 83.2.f) of the  
GDPR) — The Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant has shown  
cooperation towards it. Although the report from the Inspection Service finds a  
violation of the obligation to cooperate (art. 31 of the GDPR), based on the finding  
that, according to the Inspection Service, the defendant did not respond in a timely  
manner to the questions posed, but that from the time of the intervention of its  
lawyer, the previously unanswered questions were indeed answered and that a  
timely response was also given to the follow-up questions, the Contentious  
Chamber concludes that this constitutes a sufficient element to assert that the  
defendant has provided the required cooperation. Furthermore, the Contentious  
Chamber notes based on the documents in the file that the defendant has followed  
up on the findings of the Inspection Service not only by drafting a privacy policy  
and a cookie policy in accordance with the GDPR but also by ensuring that every  
future email sent by the defendant will contain a link to the privacy policy for  
effective information. This measure is likely to mitigate the negative effects on the  
rights of the data subject and is therefore considered a mitigating factor45.  
94. Other mitigating or aggravating circumstances — The other factual elements in  
the file are not of a nature to be considered as a mitigating or aggravating  
circumstance. The context mentioned by the defendant in which the data file was  
obtained, namely the bankruptcy of the former football club, as well as the timing  
of the acquisition of this file, shortly after the GDPR came into effect, have already  
been considered above. The Contentious Chamber therefore concludes that this  
circumstance must be regarded as neutral.  
95. Finally, the Contentious Chamber draws attention to the fact that the other criteria  
of Article 83.2 of the GDPR are not, in this case, likely to lead to any other  
administrative fine than that defined by the Contentious Chamber in this decision.

Step 4: Review of Exceeding Maximum Amounts  
97. As stated above, for the identified violations, a maximum fine amount of 20 million euros applies. The administrative fine proposed by the Disciplinary Chamber in this decision is significantly below the threshold of this legally established maximum amount.  
Step 5: Assessment of Effectiveness, Proportionality, and Deterrence  
98. Under Article 83.5.a) and b) of the GDPR, the Disciplinary Chamber may impose an administrative fine for the violations described above. As outlined in the Guidelines, the imposition of a fine can be considered effective if it achieves the objectives for which it was imposed. These objectives may include, on the one hand, sanctioning unlawful behavior and, on the other hand, promoting compliance with the applicable regulations. Regarding the deterrent effect, the Disciplinary Chamber asserts that the administrative fine aims, on the one hand, to discourage recidivism on the part of the defendant and to encourage the latter to take measures aimed at transparent information in respect of the rights of the data subject, particularly concerning communication by email addressed to all individuals listed in a membership file. On the other hand, the deterrent effect also applies to other data controllers, particularly those engaged in similar sporting activities, in order to assess internal operations and, if necessary, take appropriate measures to avoid similar violations. Furthermore, the Disciplinary Chamber considers that the administrative fine is proportionate given the nature, severity, and duration of the violation, as well as the other factors in Article 83.2 of the GDPR as assessed in this decision. The Disciplinary Chamber therefore concludes that both objectives have been achieved and that the administrative fine to be imposed is thus deterrent and proportionate.
