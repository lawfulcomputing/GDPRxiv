Facts and Procedure  
1.  
The subject of the complaint concerns the failure to delete the complainant's personal professional email address following his dismissal by the defendant.  
2.  
The defendant is a company active in the real estate sector, notably acting as a property manager.  
3.  
The defendant states that it dismissed the complainant in October 2020, without notice. The defendant characterizes this cessation of the complainant's activities as abrupt and contentious. According to the defendant, it occurred without the preparation of files or handover to a successor.  
4.  
The parties disagree on the reasons for this dismissal. The defendant highlights a context of takeover and, on this occasion, the discovery of professional errors committed by the complainant. The complainant formally denies any wrongdoing on his part. He indicates that at the time of the takeover, 7 months before his dismissal, he was informed that due to his age and the proximity of his retirement, he was not part of the company's future and that his files would be gradually taken over by colleagues from a sister company of the defendant. From that moment on, the complainant states that his work was systematically hindered by the contradictory directives he received from the new management.  
5.  
The defendant reports that the complainant managed about thirty residences before his dismissal and played a key role regarding the legal obligations of the property manager as well as in the functioning of the company. His tasks were numerous (financial management of properties, administrative management of properties, and ensuring the proper maintenance of these properties), regularly spanning several years. In the performance of his duties, the complainant was in direct contact with the co-owners of the properties he managed.

The defendant highlights the context of the health crisis related to the COVID-19 pandemic, the absence of a transition and handover period related to the abrupt departure of the complainant, the recent resumption of the company, as well as the fact that the complainant was her only full-time employee for a significant number of residences.  
II.2.2. The Perspective of the Dispute Chamber  
27.  
As the data controller, the defendant is required to comply with data protection principles and must be able to demonstrate that these are respected (accountability principle – Article 5.2. of the GDPR). Furthermore, in her capacity as data controller, she must implement all necessary technical and organizational measures to this end (Article 24 of the GDPR).  
28. Article 5.1 b) of the GDPR enshrines the principle of purpose, which requires that data be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not be processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes.  
29.  
It is against the backdrop of purpose that other principles also enshrined in Article 5 of the GDPR may apply: the principle of minimization – according to which only data that is adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purpose may be processed (Article 5.1 c) of the GDPR) – and the principle of limited retention – according to which data may not be retained in a form that allows...

the identification of the data subjects only for a duration not exceeding that necessary in light of the purposes for which they are processed (Article 5.1 e) of the GDPR).  
30. These principles and the obligations that arise for the data controller resonate in terms of rights for the data subject, as in particular, pursuant to Article 17.1 a) of the GDPR, the data subject has the right to obtain from the data controller the erasure of personal data concerning them when such data are no longer necessary in relation to the purposes for which they were collected or processed.  
31.  
The disputed email address of the complainant is personal data within the meaning of Article 4.1 of the GDPR. It is indeed information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person within the meaning of this article. In this case, it relates to the complainant.  
32.  
This address, created for professional purposes in the context of the defendant's activities, was intended to allow the complainant to receive and send emails in the context of their activities within the defendant.  
33. The Contentious Chamber is of the opinion that in order to comply with the principle of purpose limitation (Article 5.1 b) of the GDPR), combined with the principles of data minimization (Article 5.1 c) of the GDPR) and storage limitation (Article 5.1 e) of the GDPR), it is the responsibility of the data controller to block the email accounts of holders who have ceased their functions no later than the day of their effective departure. This blocking must occur after they have been notified in advance and after an automatic message has been inserted. This automatic message will inform any subsequent correspondents that the data subject no longer holds their position within the company and will provide the contact details of the person (or the generic email address) to reach in their place, for a reasonable period (initially 1 month). Depending on the context and, in particular, the level of responsibility held by the data subject (such as a position of managing director or another key role that they are the sole holder of, as in this case), a longer period may be accepted, ideally not exceeding 3 months. This extension must be justified and carried out with the consent of the data subject or, at a minimum, after notifying them. An alternative solution must also be sought and implemented as quickly as possible without necessarily waiting for the ultimate deadline of this extension.  
34. The Contentious Chamber believes that this approach should be favored over the automatic forwarding of emails to another company email address. In the case of automatic forwarding, especially without informing the sender of the message, there is indeed no control over incoming emails or "in". Furthermore, in this case, potentially sensitive private information could

II.3.2. The view of the Litigation Chamber  
41. Article 6.1 of the GDPR requires that any processing be based on a legal basis. In other words, the data controller cannot process data without relying on one of the legal bases listed in Article 6.1 of the GDPR, which embodies the principle of legality set out in Article 5.1 a) of the GDPR.  
42. The Litigation Chamber has, in light of the preceding developments, found that the purpose for which the data constituting the email address was processed ceased with the termination of the complainant's activities with the defendant. Pursuing a legitimate interest in compliance with the conditions of Article 6.1 f) of the GDPR, the address may, as indicated by the SI, remain active for a certain period to ensure the proper functioning of the business and the continuity of the defendant's services. Beyond this period, no legal basis allows for the continuation of the processing.  
43. The Litigation Chamber recalls that in order to rely on the legal basis of "legitimate interest" under Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR, the data controller must,

the first defendant in this case must demonstrate that a) the interest it pursues through the processing of the concerned data can be recognized as legitimate (the "purpose test"); b) that the proposed processing is necessary to achieve this interest (the "necessity test"); and c) that the weighting of this interest against the interests, freedoms, and fundamental rights of the concerned individuals weighs in its favor or in favor of the third party (the "balancing test").  
44. The Contentious Chamber will verify whether, in this case, these 3 tests are satisfied regarding the disputed processing.  
Purpose Test  
45. The Contentious Chamber recalls that in order to be qualified as "legitimate," the interest pursued by the data controller (or the third party, but this is not the case in the present instance) must be lawful under the law, determined in a sufficiently clear and precise manner, be real and current, and not fictitious or hypothetical (purpose test).  
46. In this case, the Contentious Chamber is of the opinion that the use of the complainant's email address for a brief period intended to ensure business continuity and contacts with the managed co-ownerships while implementing transitional measures constitutes a legitimate interest on the part of the defendant.  
Necessity Test  
47. Regarding the necessity test, the Contentious Chamber recalls that the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) has ruled, among other things, in the "TK" judgment on this condition of necessity of processing, emphasizing the strict interpretation of this condition, which is not specific to Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR but common to all the lawful bases listed in Article 6.1 of the GDPR, except for the consent provided for in Article 6.1(a) of the GDPR.  
48. The CJEU also notes that the condition regarding the necessity of processing must be examined in conjunction with the so-called "data minimization" principle enshrined in Article 6, paragraph 1, under c), of Directive 95/46, according to which personal data must be "adequate, relevant, and not excessive in relation to the purposes for which they are collected and for which they are subsequently processed."

Balancing Test  
52.  
The Contentious Chamber recalls that, in addition to the two aforementioned conditions, Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR can only be invoked if the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail over the interest pursued by the data controller or the third party. In other words, the data controller must conduct a balancing exercise, weighing the rights and interests at stake, and verify in this context that the interests (commercial, property security, anti-fraud, etc.) it pursues do not create an imbalance to the detriment of the rights and interests of the individuals whose data is being processed. If the interests and rights of these individuals prevail, Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR cannot be used.

This involves assessing the degree of intrusion of the proposed processing into the individual sphere, by measuring its impact on the privacy of individuals (processing of sensitive data, processing concerning vulnerable persons, profiling, etc.) and on their other fundamental rights (freedom of expression, freedom of information, freedom of conscience, etc.), as well as the other concrete impacts of the processing on their situation (monitoring or surveillance of their activities or movements, exclusion from access to services, etc.). This consideration is essential for processing that can be carried out without the prior consent of the individuals: in the absence of a positive and explicit act on their part, the legitimate interest requires not to surprise individuals in the modalities of implementation as well as in the consequences of the processing.  
55. Generally speaking, regarding the continuation of processing a professional email address after the departure of an employee or other actor of the company, the Contentious Chamber is of the opinion, as reiterated by the SI in its investigation report and in point 33 of this decision, that it is appropriate to set up an automatic message informing any correspondent that the individual concerned (here the complainant) no longer holds their position within the company and to provide as soon as possible the contact details of the person (or the generic email address) to be contacted in their place, for a reasonable period (initially 1 month). II.4. Regarding the follow-up to the complainant's request for deletion (Articles 12.3, 12.4, and 17.1 of the GDPR)  
II.4.1. The viewpoint of the SI and the parties  
57. As mentioned above, the SI notes a breach of Articles 12.3 and 12.4 of the GDPR on the part of the defendant in that it failed to respond to the complainant's request for deletion dated November 11, 2020, the circumstances invoked by the defendant not undermining this finding.  
58. Similarly, the complainant denounces this total lack of response. None of the arguments put forward by the defendant seem to him to be valid. The argument of the hasty cessation of its activities cannot be upheld since it is a decision that comes from the defendant itself. The argument concerning the resumption of activities by a different management cannot be upheld either, as this resumption occurred 7 months before his dismissal. The complainant contests any errors made in the context of his services. Finally, the complainant emphasizes that the commitments of the new management were made under the pressure of the services of the APD while the complaint was pending and not spontaneously.  
59. For its part, the defendant does not contest that it did not respond to the complainant's request dated November 11, 2020, for the deletion of his professional email address [professional email address of X]. In addition to the context of the pandemic and other circumstances already mentioned, the defendant insists that it did not remain inactive as it implemented an automatic response system for the complainant's email address and then disabled it. It also worked on replacing all posters and plaques containing the complainant's contact details in the residences it managed. Finally, it indicates that it verified that the diversion of emergency calls to the complainant's private phone was indeed stopped.  
II.4.2. The viewpoint of the Contentious Chamber  
60. The Contentious Chamber notes that the defendant did not comply with Article 12.4 of the GDPR, which states that "if the controller does not respond to the request made by the data subject, it shall inform the data subject without delay and at the latest within one month of receiving the request of the reasons for its inaction and the possibility of lodging a complaint with a supervisory authority and seeking a judicial remedy."  
61. Indeed, as soon as it receives a request for the exercise of the rights of a data subject (here the complainant), the data controller (here the defendant) is always required to respond to the data subject:  
- Either by providing information on the measures taken in response to the request as soon as possible and in any case within one month of receiving the request in accordance with the provisions of Article 12.3 of the GDPR. If necessary, this period may be extended by two months, taking into account the complexity and number of requests. In this case, the controller must nonetheless inform the data subject of this extension and the reasons for the delay within one month of receiving the request.  
- Or, as recalled above, if it believes it should not respond to the request made by the data subject (Article 12.4), it must inform the data subject without delay and at the latest within one month of receiving the request of the reasons for its inaction and the possibility of lodging a complaint with a supervisory authority and seeking a judicial remedy.  
62. In other words, the data subject must never be left without any response, regardless of the controller's intention regarding the follow-up it gives or intends to give to the request for the exercise of a right addressed to it.  
63. In this case, it is undisputed that the defendant refrained from responding to the complainant's request for deletion dated November 11, 2020.  
64. The circumstances already invoked by the defendant related to workload, the particular context of the pandemic, or the consequences of the complainant's hasty departure do not eliminate this breach. At most, these circumstances could be taken into account by the Contentious Chamber in determining the appropriate sanction for the established breach.  
65. There has therefore been a violation of Article 12.4 of the GDPR on the part of the defendant, which, believing itself justified (which it is not – see above) in continuing to process the complainant's email address, and consequently in refusing his request for deletion, nonetheless should have responded to him and explained the reasons for this refusal as well as informed him of the possibility of lodging a complaint with the APD and seeking a judicial remedy.  
66. Finally, as the Contentious Chamber has already stated above, the principles of purpose, minimization, and limitation of retention, as well as the obligations arising therefrom...

In the absence of the data controller complying with these obligations spontaneously, given the cessation of the processing purpose (Article 5.1. b) and e) of the GDPR), the data subject may obtain erasure by exercising the right recognized to them in Article 17.1 a) of the GDPR. II.5. Additional remarks  
69. The Litigation Chamber notes that the defendant has taken the necessary measures to inform the security company that the complainant's phone number should no longer be called in case of an incident, as the latter has left the company. Generally, it was the defendant's responsibility to inform those to whom the complainant's data had been communicated in the exercise of their functions that the complainant was no longer exercising them, ideally and if possible, proactively, but in any case as soon as the complainant made the request.  
70. For the record, Article 19 of the GDPR provides that "the data controller shall notify each recipient to whom the personal data have been communicated of any rectification or erasure of personal data or any restriction of processing carried out in accordance with Article 16, Article 17, paragraph 1, and Article 18, unless such communication proves impossible or involves disproportionate effort. The data controller shall provide the data subject with information on these recipients if the data subject requests it."  
71. Regarding the signs displaying the complainant's contact details posted in the various buildings for which they were responsible, the Litigation Chamber also notes that the complainant's contact details as displayed—constituting personal data concerning them within the meaning of Article 4.1 of the GDPR—have gradually been erased and replaced by a general contact address.
