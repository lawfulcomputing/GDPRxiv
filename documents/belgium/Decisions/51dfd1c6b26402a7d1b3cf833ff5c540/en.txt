II. Motivation  
II.1. Regarding the review of the case by the Contentious Chamber 
38. The main argument of the defendant is based on the fact that the plaintiff does not present any new factual and/or legal arguments that could lead to a different decision than that already adopted by the Contentious Chamber concluding to a dismissal of the four complaints under Article 95, § 1, 3° of the LCA and that it should not review the case and take another decision (point 26).   
39. In its preliminary decision prior to the substantive treatment of the case No. 138/2023, the Contentious Chamber concluded to a dismissal of the four complaints under Article 95, § 1, 3° of the LCA and specified the following: 
“(...) The plaintiff, if he disagrees with the content of this decision and believes that he can present factual and/or legal arguments that could lead to a different decision, may submit a request to the Contentious Chamber for substantive treatment of the case (...)”5. 
40. The Contentious Chamber wishes to clarify that delisting requests invariably balance the right to data protection and the freedom of expression and information and thus constitute a complex exercise, as indicated by the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter “CJEU”)6. In order to carry out this delicate balancing of competing interests, it is essential that the Contentious Chamber has the developed positions of the parties involved. 
41. 
In this case, within the framework of the so-called “light” procedure, or “preliminary procedure prior to substantive decision,” the defendant had the opportunity to express itself, at the request of the Contentious Chamber, through its letter of April 24, 2023. In this letter, it was able to detail the reasons why it considers that Article 17.3.a) of the GDPR applies to the URLs for which it refused delisting. However, in the context of this “light” procedure, the plaintiff did not have the opportunity to respond to these arguments. To ensure respect for the principle of adversarial proceedings, the plaintiff was informed in the decision of the possibility of submitting a request to the Contentious Chamber for substantive treatment of the case, which he did on October 30, 2023.  
42. 
Although the plaintiff's position had already been known to the Contentious Chamber, both in his requests to the defendant and in his complaints, the Contentious Chamber believes that the substantive treatment of the case undoubtedly allowed him to develop his arguments through his conclusions and allowed him to be heard during the hearing.

II.3. Regarding the refusal to delist links by Google LLC  
52. The complaint submitted for examination by the Contentious Chamber concerns the exercise by the complainant of their right to erasure. Their request pertains to links referenced in the results provided by the search engine "Google Search" when their name is entered as a search criterion.  
53. It is necessary to assess the delisting requests made by the complainant in light of Article 17 of the GDPR, the criteria and rules established by the CJEU in its judgment Google Spain8, the guidelines of the Article 29 Working Party related to this judgment (hereinafter the guidelines of Group 299), the teachings from the CJEU judgment in the case GC and others v. CNIL of September 24, 201910, and the Guidelines 5/201911 on the criteria for the right to be forgotten under the GDPR in the context of search engines by the EDPB (hereinafter the EDPB guidelines), in order to ensure a fair balance between, on the one hand, the fundamental rights to respect for private life and to the protection of personal data, enshrined in Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU, and, on the other hand, the fundamental right to freedom of information for internet users, guaranteed by Article 11 of the Charter. In the pursuit of this fair balance, various criteria will be taken into account, which will be analyzed below. This right to erasure aims to protect the data subject against processing that would not be or is no longer legitimate.  
54. It should be noted at the outset that while an infringement of privacy caused by referencing can be amplified due to the essential role of search engines in accessing information via the Internet, similarly and for the same reason, a delisting can impact the freedom of information of internet users. A balance between interests must therefore necessarily be struck.

In this case, based on the relevant criteria to be considered according to the case law of the CJEU and the European Court of Human Rights (hereinafter "ECHR"), and in support of all the elements presented to it, the Contentious Chamber argues that, in this instance, the result of the balance of interests to be struck, pursuant to Article 17.3. a) of the GDPR, between the right to data protection, on the one hand, and the right to freedom of information, on the other hand, leans in favor of freedom of expression and information, as well as the maintenance, via the search engine, of the accessibility of the said information. The Contentious Chamber indeed considers that the indexing by the defendant of the disputed URLs is necessary for the exercise of the right to freedom of information of internet users for the reasons set out below (points 58 to 92).

II.3.1. The role played by the complainant in public life
58. Following the opinion of the CJEU and the guidelines of the EDPB, the Contentious Chamber considers that the quality of being a person who plays or does not play a role in public life is a decisive element for balancing the right to private life and the right to public information. Indeed, according to the EDPB, which reiterates the words of the CJEU: "The Court has also considered that the rights of the concerned individual generally prevail over the interest of internet users in accessing information through the search engine provider. However, it has identified several factors that may influence this balancing, including the nature or sensitivity of the information, and in particular the interest of internet users in having access to this information, which may depend on the role played by the concerned individual in public life (the Contentious Chamber emphasizes)"14.

In this case, contrary to the complainant's assertion, the publication in certain press articles of a photo of his expired diplomatic passport does not constitute the processing of biometric data. The Dispute Chamber recalls that, in accordance with Articles 4.14), 9.1, and Recital 51 of the GDPR, only data processed by specific technical means for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person falls within the definition of biometric data, so that the processing of photographs should not be systematically considered as processing of special categories of data. In this regard, it is important to distinguish the respective roles and responsibilities of the press publisher and the search engine. The press publisher, faced with a request for deletion, will be able to remove certain data from the disputed articles if it cannot invoke the journalistic exception and its legitimate interest in processing this information. As for the search engine, it can only intervene in the indexing of the relevant articles. In this case, and regarding the defendant, the Dispute Chamber cannot consider that the presence of this information, mentioned marginally in certain disputed press articles, justifies the complete de-referencing of the relevant articles. Such a measure would be disproportionate, especially since there are less intrusive alternatives to the freedom of information of internet users, notably by directly addressing the publisher of the content in question.

II.3.3. Regarding the Accuracy of the Disputed Content  
84. The question of the accuracy of the disputed content referenced is also an important element to consider in the assessment of the conditions for application provided for in Article 17.3.a) of the GDPR, in order to evaluate whether the right to information of internet users and the freedom of expression of the content provider can prevail over the rights of the applicant for de-referencing. Indeed, if the referenced content is found to be predominantly inaccurate, the right to inform and the right to be informed cannot prevail.  
85. It should be noted that the CJEU specifies in its ruling TU and RE v. Google LLC what is expected from the person requesting de-referencing due to the inaccuracy of referenced content: "It is for this person to establish the manifest inaccuracy of the information contained in that content or, at the very least, of a part of that information that does not present a minor character in relation to the entirety of that content. However, in order to avoid placing an excessive burden on this person that could undermine the effective exercise of the right to de-referencing, it is only incumbent upon them to provide the evidence that can reasonably be required of them, taking into account the circumstances of the specific case, to establish this manifest inaccuracy (the Contentious Chamber emphasizes)."  
86. In this ruling, the CJEU also clarifies the obligations and responsibilities incumbent upon the operator of a search engine. The Court ruled that, when the inaccuracy does not appear manifestly in light of the evidence provided by the concerned person, the search engine operator cannot be required to take on an active role. It specifies the following: "the operator of the relevant search engine cannot be imposed an obligation to investigate the facts and, for this purpose, to organize an exchange."
