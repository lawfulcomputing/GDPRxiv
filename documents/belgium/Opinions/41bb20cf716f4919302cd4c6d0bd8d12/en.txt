I. SUBJECT OF THE REQUEST FOR OPINION

1. The Minister of Social Affairs and Public Health (hereinafter the requester) seeks the opinion of the Commission regarding a draft law establishing the Information Security Committee and amending various laws concerning the implementation of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (hereinafter the draft law).

2. The draft law aims to implement several aspects of the aforementioned Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of April 27, 2016 (hereinafter General Data Protection Regulation or GDPR).

3. Initially, the draft law seeks to create an Information Security Committee, which is intended to compensate for the disappearance of sectoral committees within the Commission, following the law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority.

4. The draft law also aims to provide a legal basis for the creation, by social security institutions and social inspection services, of a data warehouse for the purposes of data matching and data mining, which should enable targeted controls and analyses in the context of combating social fraud.

5. Finally, the draft law intends to offer several control and inspection services in the social, financial, and economic sectors the possibility to limit certain rights generally held by data subjects in the context of the protection of their personal data, in accordance with Article 23 of the GDPR.

II. EXAMINATION OF THE REQUEST FOR OPINION

1. ESTABLISHMENT OF AN INFORMATION SECURITY COMMITTEE

6. In accordance with Articles 109 and following of the law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority, the sectoral committees integrated within the Commission will be abolished on May 25, 2018.

7. The draft law thus creates a new body: the Information Security Committee (hereinafter ISC), consisting of a Social Security and Health Chamber (hereinafter SS&H Chamber)
Notice 34/2018 - 3/21  
and of a chamber Federal Authority (hereinafter chamber AF), which is responsible in particular (but not exclusively)1:  
-  
to render deliberations regarding certain communications of personal data2 and  
-  
to oversee the training and functioning of data protection officers (hereinafter DPO) of social security institutions and public institutions and federal authority institutions.  

1.1. Status of the CSI and its decisions  

8. According to the Explanatory Memorandum of the draft law (p. 7), the CSI must be considered as "a measure to implement the basic principles of the GDPR regarding 'privacy by design' and 'privacy by default'."  
Furthermore, the Explanatory Memorandum (p. 6) also stipulates what the CSI certainly is not:  
-  
it is not a supervisory authority;  
-  
it does not replace the respective DPOs;  
-  
it is not a data controller3.  

9. In its deliberations, the CSI will proactively verify whether, during the contemplated communications of personal data, the conditions of purpose limitation, proportionality, and security defined in the GDPR are met (Explanatory Memorandum, p. 4).  

10. The Commission therefore notes that the creation of the CSI can be perceived as a measure by the federal/state legislator of Belgium aimed at assisting in certain cases the data controllers in the public sector to comply with their responsibilities (articles 5.2 and 24 of the GDPR).  
The Commission also notes that the possibility of 'prior authorization' from the supervisory authority itself, as provided for in Article 36.5 of the GDPR, has remained untapped. However, it agrees with the added value of prior deliberations regarding communications of personal data that may pose certain risks to the rights and freedoms of the data subject, especially in the social security and health sectors. It further notes that the GDPR leaves it to the legislator

11. The Commission notes in this regard that articles 40 and 80 of the draft law explicitly provide that the deliberations issued by the CSI "have general binding force towards third parties." The Commission acknowledges, as has already been specified, that the deliberations may have normative power, but it also believes that the Data Protection Authority (hereinafter the DPA) must be able to freely exercise the missions and powers entrusted to it (see articles 57 and 58 of the GDPR) when assessing a processing of personal data. In this case, it is desirable that the DPA can notify the CSI in a reasoned manner and request a reconsideration of the deliberation. For reasons of legal certainty, this request has no retroactive effect.  
Proposed text:  
"The Data Protection Authority (DPA) has the power to confront the deliberations of the CSI with higher legal standards.  
Without prejudice to its other powers, the DPA, when it reasonably finds that a deliberation is not in compliance with a higher legal standard, may invite the CSI to reconsider the deliberation within 45 days on the points it indicates.  
A deliberation thus reconsidered must then be submitted to the DPA by the CSI within the 30 days preceding the prior binding opinion."

It is recommended to also include this power in the DPA's mandate in the law of December 3, 2017, establishing the Data Protection Authority, so that all its powers are not scattered across several legislations.

13. The Commission finally emphasizes that the issuance of a deliberation by the CSI for the data controller concerned does not imply any obligation to communicate personal data. The latter retains all the freedom to judge for itself the appropriateness - as rightly indicated in the Explanatory Memorandum (p. 14).

1.2. Training and functioning of the Data Protection Officer (DPO)

14. Another important mission of the CSI, provided for in the draft law (articles 40 and 80), in addition to issuing deliberations regarding data flows, is to "monitor whether data protection officers receive adequate ongoing training and work in a coordinated manner and, if not, take all necessary support measures to ensure this adequate training or achieve the coordination, including technical coordination."

15. The Commission assumes that in this context, the CSI does not substitute for the specific data controller (nor for the processor), who, under article 38 of the GDPR, have the mission to:  
- involve the DPO, in an appropriate and timely manner, in matters relating to the protection of personal data;  
- assist the DPO and provide him with the necessary resources to carry out his missions and enable him to maintain his specialized knowledge.  

In order to exclude misunderstandings in this regard, the Commission proposes to reformulate the cited provisions as follows: "concretely assist data protection officers, including by providing adequate ongoing training and formulating recommendations, particularly at the technical level."

16. The Commission further notes that article 25 of the draft law also allows assigning to the DPO of a social security institution, in addition to his advisory mission, "the execution of tasks entrusted to him by the person responsible for daily management."

17. The Commission still wishes to remind in this matter that the DPO must always be able to act independently. Article 38.3 of the GDPR explicitly states that the data controller and the processor must ensure that the DPO does not receive any instructions regarding the exercise of his missions, and article 38.6 of the GDPR adds that the tasks assigned to him must not lead to a conflict of interest for the DPO.
Opinion 34/2018 - 6/21  
18. The Commission recommends that the draft law explicitly emphasizes the independence of the DPO. Specifically, the Commission proposes to add the following wording: “To the extent that it does not compromise the independence of the DPO and provided that the content and quantity of other tasks assigned to him allow him to carry out his DPO duties in accordance with the GDPR.”  

19. The Commission also considers it advisable that any potential intervention by the King to set the rules under which the DPO of a social security institution must carry out complementary tasks (see Article 25, final part, of the draft law) be provided after consultation with the APD.  

20. Finally, the Commission still has reservations regarding the obligation included in the draft law (see Articles 10 and 24) for social security institutions to communicate the identity of the DPO to the SS&S chamber of the CSI (in addition to the notification in this regard to the BCSS). Indeed, Article 37.7 of the GDPR already provides for the communication of the identity and contact details of DPOs to the APD. It is preferable to avoid administrative overload and the risk of inconsistencies accompanying such a dual notification obligation.  

1.3. Composition of the CSI  

21. The Commission believes that in the enumeration of the members of the CSI in Article 2, § 2 of the draft law, it is preferable to start with the 2 members who are part of both chambers (i.e., members 1° and 4°), followed by the members who are part of one or the other chamber (i.e., members 2°, 3°, 5°, and 6°).  

22. Furthermore, the Commission also considers that in Article 2, § 2, last paragraph of the draft law, it should be explicitly defined:  
- either which of the 2 members referred to in Article 2, § 2, 5° and 6°, is part of the joint chambers,  
- or that all members of the two chambers are part of the joint chambers.  

23. The Commission believes that in Article 3 of the draft law, it should be added that a candidate cannot also be a member of the federal government, a community or regional government, nor hold a position in a strategic cell of a minister.

2. CREATION OF A DATA WAREHOUSE(S) IN THE FIGHT AGAINST SOCIAL FRAUD  

26. Article 12 of the draft law aims to create a general legal basis for social security institutions, social inspection services, and the Administrative Fines Directorate of the Legal Studies, Documentation, and Litigation Division of the SPF ETCS, either for their respective concerns or jointly, in execution of their respective legal missions, with a view to the creation of a data warehouse(s) for data matching and data mining purposes, allowing them to carry out targeted checks based on risk indicators and to conduct analyses on relational data from different institutions to achieve effective action against social fraud.  

27. Regarding the personal data that will need to be aggregated in the data warehouse(s), the Explanatory Memorandum states that "data may be drawn from any relevant database, public or private, in compliance with the required deliberations."  

28. The Explanatory Memorandum further specifies that the CSI, in accordance with the powers conferred upon it, will pronounce on the various aspects of the communication of personal data to social security institutions and social inspection services and will verify in particular whether the communication of personal data actually complies with the regulations regarding the protection of privacy. It will thus be checked whether the principles

of purpose and proportionality are respected and if, during processing, sufficient security measures are provided.

29. Article 12 of the draft law formulates a very broad purpose of 'data matching and data mining for effective social fraud prevention' for the creation of one or more data warehouse(s) in which (which) personal data from any 'relevant' public or private database can be grouped. Such a broad and global formulation indeed offers very few guidelines for the individual whose data will be included in the data warehouse(s). Neither Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights (hereinafter ECHR), nor Article 22 of the Constitution, nor the GDPR, particularly Articles 6.3 and 22, allow for such a 'blank check'.

30. When the legal basis for processing personal data is a legal obligation or serves a public interest mission entrusted to the data controller, this legal basis contains, in accordance with Article 6.3 of the GDPR, several specific provisions regarding:  
- the types or categories of data subject to processing;  
- the data subjects;  
- the entities to which personal data may be communicated and the purposes for which they may be communicated;  
- the limitation of purposes;  
- the retention periods;  
- the operations and processing procedures, including measures to ensure lawful and fair processing (notably including pseudonymization and encryption).  
The draft law presents serious shortcomings in this regard.

31. The Explanatory Memorandum refers to both Article 8 of the ECHR and Article 22 of the Constitution, which allow for a limitation of the right to respect for private life (including the protection of personal data) provided that it is provided for by law and under the conditions set by it. It goes without saying that any interference by a public authority in the right to respect for private life must be prescribed in a 'sufficiently precise legal provision' that meets an imperative social need and is proportionate to the pursued purpose.

Such a precise legal provision must define the essential elements of personal data processing that accompany the interference of the public authority. In this context, it concerns:  
- determined, explicit, and legitimate purposes;  
- (categories of) personal data that are relevant and not excessive;  
- the maximum retention period of recorded personal data;  
- the designation of the data controller.

32. Under Article 12 of the draft law, data mining and data matching applied to personal data grouped in the data warehouse (including 'personal social data related to health' as defined in Article 9 of the draft law) must allow for targeted checks based on risk indicators and perform analyses on relational data from different institutions. Therefore, it may involve automated individual decision-making, including profiling.

33. The Commission recalls Article 22 of the GDPR, which defines the principle that everyone has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, producing legal effects concerning them or significantly affecting them in a similar manner.  
Such a decision is, however, possible, provided that it is authorized by Union law or the law of the Member State to which the data controller is subject and which also provides for appropriate measures to safeguard the rights and freedoms and legitimate interests of the data subject.  
Insofar as sensitive data is used for this decision within the meaning of Article 9 of the GDPR, Union law or the law of the Member State must pursue an important public interest, ensure the proportionality of the pursued objective, respect the essence of the right to data protection, and provide appropriate and specific measures to safeguard the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject.

3.1. General Provisions  

36. Article 23 of the GDPR allows Member States to provide, within certain determined limits and for specific objectives, exceptions to the rights of the persons concerned. The specific objectives for which this is possible are listed in Article 23.1 of the GDPR; these include public interest objectives of the Union or a Member State, including significant economic or financial interests of the Union or a Member State, including in the areas of monetary, budgetary, and fiscal policy, public health, and social security, particularly a mission of control, inspection, or regulation related, even occasionally, to the exercise of public authority.

37. Any legislative measure providing for limitations on the rights of the person concerned must at least contain specific provisions relating to the elements listed in Article 23.2 of the GDPR, such as:  
- the purposes of the processing or categories of processing,  
- the categories of personal data,  
- the extent of the limitations introduced,  
- the safeguards intended to prevent abuse or unlawful access or transfer,  
- the identification of the data controller(s) (or categories of data controllers),  
- the retention periods,  
- the risks to the rights and freedoms of the persons concerned, and  
- the right of the persons concerned to be informed of the limitation.  

38. In order to determine the scope of the margin of appreciation that the legislator has in this context, it is important to recall the case law of the Court of Justice regarding Article 13 of Directive 95/46/EC, which provided for a similar exception basis. In the Smaranda Bara ruling, the Court confirmed that these exceptions could only be established by "legislative measures." Subsequently, the Court clarified that Member States could adopt these exceptions only insofar as they are "necessary." Given the unchanged intention of the European legislator to ensure a high level of protection, this means that exceptions to the rights of the persons concerned must remain within the limits of what is strictly necessary. The necessity and proportionality of the measures concerned must therefore be interpreted restrictively.

3.4. Limitations in the context of inspection and control by the services whose competencies are defined in the Economic Law Code  

51. Articles 82 and 83 of the draft law introduce several amendments to the law of February 28, 2013, which introduced the Economic Law Code. In Title I, Chapter I of Book XV ("The exercise of supervision and the investigation and detection of offenses"), two new articles are inserted regarding how to respond to the request of the individual whose personal data is processed by the inspection and control services of the SPF Economy in order to exercise their rights provided for in Articles 12 to 22 of the GDPR, in application of Article 23 of the GDPR.  

52. The draft law provides in Article 82 for a limitation of all rights of the data subjects, more specifically those provided for in Articles 12 to 22 and 34 of the GDPR, and this is always to ensure "Article 23.1, d, g, and h" in relation to the "inspection and/or control services whose competencies are defined by the Economic Law Code."  

53. The Commission first observes that the wording of Article 82 (particularly the first and third paragraphs) of the draft law leaves much to be desired in terms of clarity; moreover, the text in Dutch does not correspond to the text in French.  

54. Article 82 of the draft law provides for a general exception formulated in an extremely broad manner to all the rights provided - in Articles 12 to 22 and 34 of the GDPR - of the data subjects in relation to all control and inspection services within the SPF Economy (no specific provision identifying the actual data controllers) with a simple reference to Article 23.1, d, g, and h of the GDPR without the slightest precision regarding the regulatory missions of the control and inspection services referred to. The GDPR does not authorize such a 'blank check.'  

55. Article 83 of the draft law also does not take into account the specific minimum provisions required by Article 23.2 of the GDPR and barely offers added value to ensure the maintenance of the essence of freedoms and fundamental rights in a democratic society (except possibly the indication of the retention period - however very broad - which cannot exceed the limitation periods for the offenses sought/targeted).  

Notice 34/2018 - 17/21  
Indeed, the Commission is not in favor of an indirect access system with intervention from the Data Protection Authority (DPA) where the data subject only receives a message stating that "the necessary checks have been carried out." This is indeed a cumbersome and administratively burdensome system that excludes a real possibility of recourse in this matter with the DPA. Furthermore, no justification is provided for why such an indirect access system (which is hardly applied anywhere in Europe) would be essential for the economic inspection services, whereas social and financial inspection services can clearly function perfectly well without such a system.  

56. The Commission must conclude that Articles 82 and 83 do not pass the test of Article 23 of the GDPR. The Commission takes the liberty of referring to how Article 23 of the GDPR is applied in the draft law for social inspection services (see Articles 62 to 68 of the draft law) and the control and inspection services of the SPF Finance (see Articles 73 to 79 inclusive of the draft law) and suggests a similar application for the economic control and inspection services (which must still be defined).

58. The Commission initially recommends replacing 'professional secrecy' with an 'obligation of confidentiality' in the last paragraph of Article 5 of the draft law.

Previously, it served "to conduct research that may be useful for the knowledge, design, and management of social security." Under Article 11 of the draft law, it may serve any historical, statistical, scientific, or policy support purpose.

Insofar as it is provided that social security institutions, social inspection services, and the Directorate of Administrative Fines of the Legal Studies, Documentation, and Litigation Division of the SPF ETCS may also subsequently process, within the framework of another legal mission (without further specification), each personal data they process in the execution of their legal missions, Article 13 of the draft law violates Articles 6.3 and 6.4 of the GDPR.

63. Indeed, when processing for a purpose other than that for which the personal data was collected is not based on the consent of the data subject or on the law of a Member State, the compatibility of the intended purpose with the purpose for which the data was collected must be assessed by the data controller, taking into account:
- the link between the two purposes;
- the relationship between the data subjects and the data controller;
- the nature of the personal data, in particular whether the processing concerns special categories of personal data (Articles 9 and 10 of the GDPR);
- the possible consequences of the intended processing for the data subjects;
- the existence of appropriate safeguards, which may include encryption or pseudonymization.

64. Article 11 of the draft law assigns the role of 'intermediate organization' to the BCSS, and Article 46 of the draft law does the same for the eHealth platform. The Commission believes that the right to restrict processing, as provided for in Article 18 of the GDPR, does not hinder the proper execution by social security institutions of their legal and regulatory missions within our social security system. Article 20, point b of the draft law may lead to the circulation of incorrect data, insofar as it provides that corrections and deletions of personal social data will only be communicated to the data subject themselves. III. CONCLUSION

68. Given that the Commission is committed to an initiative where DPOs and data controllers are supported by consultative deliberations, it is in favor of the creation of the CSI. Nevertheless, the Commission believes - in light of the above - that the draft law is not entirely compliant with the provisions of the GDPR, particularly considering:
