EXAMINATION OF THE PROJECT  

II.1. Principles of Transparency and Legality, and Subsequent Processing of Data  

II.1.1. Transparency and Legality  

5.  
Under the principles of transparency and legality enshrined in Articles 8 of the ECHR and 22 of the Constitution, a decree must clearly specify under what circumstances the processing of personal data is authorized, and consequently determine what data is processed, the individuals concerned, the conditions and purposes of such processing, the duration of data retention, and the individuals who have access to it. The Authority has already had the opportunity to recall these principles. When the basis for processing relies on a legal basis of national law, Article 6, 3. of the GDPR also specifically requires that the purposes be defined by this basis.

6.  
In this context, a delegation to the King or, in this case, to the Governments, "is not contrary to the principle of legality, provided that the authorization is defined with sufficient precision and pertains to the execution of measures whose essential elements are established in advance by the legislator."

7.  
The principles just outlined must be applied taking into account the general and abstract nature of the project which essentially establishes a framework for the exchange in Wallonia between public authorities of data from authentic data sources by allowing a unique collection from citizens and businesses, and for the control of data processing carried out by these authorities, without directly providing for specific data processing (with some nuances, see below, point No. 24). Thus, beyond this general purpose, the project does not itself establish the determined and explicit purposes of the processing of data from authentic sources, which may arise from other texts that may be future. In light of the principles of transparency and legality, the project calls for the following comments.

II.1.2. Generalization of the Subsequent Processing of Data from Authentic Sources

8. The project is designed in such a way that it organizes the principle of reusing data from authentic sources for purposes other than those for which these sources and databases were originally established. Thus, the labeling implies that data from the authentic source will be "communicated to other public authorities and reused by them for purposes other than those pursued by the initial collection" (Article 4, paragraph 1, subparagraph 1 of the project), with the project intending to "deem" such "subsequent processing" as "compatible" (Article 4, paragraph 1, subparagraph 2). And when data from an authentic source is made available by the BCED, "public authorities are obliged to go through it to access it" (except in cases of exception) (Article 6, paragraph 1, subparagraph 1 of the project). Once authorized by the CCED to access the relevant data via the BCED, the public authority in question can no longer collect the data from citizens, businesses, or others (Article 8, paragraph 1 of the project).

9. Article 6, 4. of the GDPR provides for three limiting hypotheses under which data may be subsequently processed for a purpose other than that which justified their collection: that of the consent of the data subject, that of processing based on Union law or national law (or that of a federated entity) constituting a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society to guarantee the objectives set out in Article 23, 1. of the GDPR, and finally, the hypothesis in which the data controller in this case assesses whether the contemplated subsequent processing is compatible with the initial processing in light of a series of elements to be taken into account (Article 6, 4., points a) to e) of the GDPR).

10. The project relates to the second hypothesis by organizing, through a cooperation agreement between two federated entities, the reuse of data from authentic sources. In doing so, and in any case, unless it violates Article 6, 4. of the GDPR, it cannot automatically deem the subsequent processing based on the rules of the project and the rules related to it (rules governing the authentic source and rules governing the missions of the public authority seeking to access the data) as compatible. To judge otherwise would deprive Article 6, 4., subparagraph 1 of its useful effect. Furthermore, it must be noted that this proposed provision does not meet the quality criteria required as explained in the previous point. Article 4, paragraph 1, subparagraph 2 of the project must be deleted, and the validity of each subsequent processing must be evaluated in concreto, in light of the applicable normative framework.

II.1.3. Authentic data source and quality of the standard creating the  
authentic data source  

12.  
Authentic source and database derived from authentic sources. The draft  
defines the authentic data source as the “database established by a decree  
or order of the Government containing uniquely valuable data, labeled and made  
available to public authorities by the Data Exchange Crossroads Bank” (Article 2, 1°). It  
then defines the database derived from authentic sources as the “database  
established by a regulatory provision, grouping a set of data derived notably from  
authentic sources intended to be reused by public authorities after being made  
available by the Data Exchange Crossroads Bank” (Article 2, 2°).  

13.  
Database derived from authentic sources. The Authority primarily questions  
the creation of databases derived from authentic sources. Indeed, first of all, as the  
Commission for the Protection of Privacy has been able to argue, the Authority is of the opinion that, in principle, the  
authentic data source is unique and should not be duplicated. For enhanced  
protection of the privacy of the individuals concerned, the same Commission has also clearly  
positioned itself in favor of the integration of services rather than the integration of data. In  
other words, for these reasons, the use of databases derived from authentic sources should be  
excluded.  

14.  
This is all the more true as the Authority notes that, given the current definition of the  
concepts in the draft (Article 2, 1°, 2° and 5°), it is permitted that the “authentic sources” of the  
database derived from authentic sources may come from external authentic sources (for example, a  
federal database). In this context, the Authority, on the one hand, questions the competence and thus  
the legitimacy of a federated entity to duplicate in this way a database created and organized by  
another entity not party to the cooperation agreement. A case-by-case justification will be necessary  
on this point. In any case, on the other hand, such duplication and the subsequent reuse of the  
data can only take place to the extent permitted by the applicable normative framework  
(including implementing provisions) for the relevant external authentic source.

II.1.4. Specific Transparency Obligation

21.  
The draft enshrines in its provisions a series of specific transparency obligations that the Authority supports. Thus, the public authority requesting information from individuals must indicate the type of data they are consulting about them, from authentic data sources (Article 8, paragraph 1, of the draft); they must pre-fill the information requests addressed to individuals and indicate the source of the data (Article 8, paragraph 2, of the draft); and the managers of authentic data sources must implement technical means that provide the individuals concerned with the possibility, in substance, to exercise certain of their rights (consultation of data, rectification, and identification of the public authorities that accessed it), at least partially, electronically, in addition to the obligations arising from the GDPR (Article 9 of the draft).

22.  
While the Authority supports this latter process, it is, however, subject to the following. Indeed, it does not constitute the straightforward implementation of the right of access under the GDPR, but rather a complementary measure. Thus, the electronic technical means in question will allow, among other things, the individual concerned "to know all the public authorities that have, during the past six months, consulted or updated the personal data concerning them, with the exception of the administrative and judicial authorities responsible for the investigation and prosecution of offenses, as well as the State Security and the General Intelligence and Security Service of the Armed Forces." Regardless of the formulation issues (the data "of a personal nature" are concerned and probably more generally, the authorities responsible for the investigation and prosecution of offenses), this possibility opened to the individual concerned is indeed more limited than the right they enjoy under Article 15 of the GDPR.

23.  
The Authority emphasizes in this context the importance of properly informing the individuals concerned that this is not a tool allowing them to exercise their right of access in the sense of the GDPR, a right of access that can otherwise be exercised within the scope recognized by the GDPR and the implementing Belgian law, and regarding which they must be informed from these technical and electronic means, unless there is a risk of creating harmful confusion. Furthermore, the Authority considers that this six-month period could be extended given the potential scope of application.

Articles 6, 2. and 3., and 9, 4., of the GDPR, raise legally and practically, in the opinion of the Authority, the following questions related to its implementation.  

34.  
Personal competence of control. First, should the scope ratione personae of this control competence be defined according to the quality of the data controller (a public authority established under the law of the federated entity), an "organic criterion," according to the basis of processing (processing necessary for compliance with a legal obligation under the law of the federated entity or necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or related to the exercise of public authority of the federated entity, referred to in Article 6, 1., c) and e), of the GDPR) that this data controller carries out, a "processing criterion," or according to a combination of these organic and processing criteria (namely, a public authority established under the law of the federated entity that carries out data processing based on the law of the federated entity)? Undoubtedly, the "organic criterion" appears to be more practicable and secure from the perspective of legal certainty.  

35.  
Material competence of control. Secondly, what about the scope ratione materiae of the control competence: would the control be limited to the specificities and particularities enshrined in the law of the federated entity within the margin of maneuver offered by Articles 6, 2., and 9, 4., of the GDPR, to the exclusion of the minimal and general framework (see above, point no. 29), a "specific control," or would it extend, in the relevant area, to all applicable data protection rules concerning processing, a "global control"?  

36.  
The answer to these questions depends on the interpretation to be given to Article 22, paragraph 1, of the Constitution, which itself raises the following questions.  

37.  
Which authority is competent regarding the organization of the control of the minimal and general framework referred to in Article 22, paragraph 1, of the Constitution? Before the entry into force of the GDPR, this minimal framework was composed of the law of December 8, 1992, which transposed the previously applicable European rules to the GDPR in this area, namely Directive No. 95/46, which, in other words, formed an integral part of this minimal framework. It is logical that the authority competent to establish this minimal framework is also the one competent to establish a supervisory authority and its powers. The Council of State has thus recognized in its aforementioned opinion that "it is the responsibility of the federal authority, by virtue of its competence regarding general regulation concerning the limitation of the right to respect for private life, to lay down the rules concerning the supervisory authority responsible for ensuring compliance with this general regulation."

However, on the one hand, this assimilation raises questions: what specialty can this minimal and general framework have? However, the Authority seriously questions the practicability of such a solution, which would involve, regarding the same processing, competing supervisory authorities whose competence would be distributed, from the perspective of data protection, according to a relatively artificial criterion, and which is likely to generate legal uncertainty and significantly harm the transparency of the normative framework applicable to data protection, to the detriment of data controllers and data subjects. Indeed, how can one distinguish the specific rules enacted by the federated entities from the rules of the GDPR, of which they are merely the specification or the framed derogation, and to which they must comply?  

44.  
This last aspect of compliance raises an additional problem. Indeed, the establishment of a "minimal" framework inevitably implies a (partial) hierarchy among the rules, with those of the specific framework not being able to depart from the general framework (unless they are more protective). However, this hierarchy could not extend from the perspective of control competence, unless control authorities were established that would no longer be independent and which in concreto would have a competing binding power regarding the same processing from the perspective of the data controller (thus the general rule of the GDPR specified by a federated entity in the context of a processing constitutes for it the same command), while their competences should be exclusive. The risk of contradictory decisions would create harmful uncertainty for data controllers and data subjects.  

45.  
An alternative would then be to consider that the GDPR does not fall under this minimal and general federal framework, and that it constitutes an autonomous European framework. Only the provisions enshrined in the LTD and LCA would then remain the minimal and general framework to be taken into account by the federated entities under Article 22, paragraph 1, of the Constitution. And when the federated entities act within the scope of their competences to adopt rules in the cases provided for in Articles 6, 2. and 3., and 9, 4., of the GDPR, they would also be legitimate in establishing supervisory authorities responsible for ensuring compliance with the GDPR in general, in these cases.

46.  
Again, the "organic criterion" would be excluded, but in this case, the authorities of the federated entities would have a power of "overall control" (see above, points 34-35). While this solution seems more feasible, it would nevertheless significantly reduce the scope of Article 22, paragraph 1, of the Constitution regarding the applicability of the GDPR, unless it inherently carries the possibility of removing any effect concerning the rules adopted by the federated entities: what remains, in fact, of the minimal and general federal framework that imposes itself on the federated entities and whose control falls under federal authority (the organization of appeal processes? The determination of sanctions for violations of data protection rules – see below on this subject, point no. 66 -?). This solution also remains a source of legal uncertainty. Certainly, it is to a lesser extent than the previous solution, since it would no longer be necessary to distinguish control according to data protection rules. However, it is still risky to consider that a priori, any concerned processing activity can easily be identifiable and subject to distinct treatment, according to its basis. And finally, the risk of contradictory decisions from competing control authorities, as just mentioned, remains, since a minimal and general framework still exists, albeit restricted. In short, legal certainty is once again undermined from the perspective of data controllers and the individuals concerned.  

47.  
It would be even more so by resorting to the "organic criterion." Indeed, the same provisions of the law of a federated entity can have two facets. On one hand, they will establish the public interest mission of the concerned administration and justify the necessity for it to process personal data (Article 6, 1., e), of the GDPR). On the other hand, they may require a private data controller to process personal data in order to fulfill the obligations that these provisions impose on them. In other words, once again, divergences of opinion between control authorities could subject the same data controller to contradictory imperatives: on one side, those resulting from the law of the federated entity deemed compliant with data protection rules by the control authority of that entity; on the other side, those resulting from data protection rules interpreted differently by the federal control authority.  

48.  
Ultimately, assuming that the "processing criterion" could apply without difficulty, which is not the case, when the GDPR is excluded from the minimal and general framework (see above, points nos 45-46), the same data controller could be subject, in Belgium, to different interpretations of the GDPR depending on the processing activities they carry out.

Conclusion. In conclusion, the previous developments illustrate that there is currently a damaging uncertainty regarding the scope of the rules allocating competencies concerning the creation, at different levels of government, of several control authorities and the organization of their competencies. Even if the applicant rightly claims the competence to create a control authority in the sense of the GDPR, the scope of its

II.2.3. Application to the project

50.  
Subject to the issues previously discussed regarding the distribution of competence, it is ultimately the responsibility of the applicant to make coherent choices in the creation and organization of a supervisory authority that comply with the GDPR. In this case, the project transforms the Wallonia-Brussels Commission for Data Exchange Control [CCED] into a supervisory authority within the meaning of the GDPR. In this regard, the Authority makes the following considerations.

Competence

51.  
Public authorities. The competence of the CCED must be clearly outlined in the project. Article 3 of the project must be adapted to take into account the competence as delineated in Article 22, paragraph 1, subparagraph 3, of the project (see also Article 3, 6°, of the project, defining the CCED): the CCED "is responsible for overseeing the application of regulations concerning the protection of personal data by public authorities in the Walloon Region and the French Community." The applicant therefore chooses an "organic criterion" of competence (regarding this criterion, see supra, points nos. 34, 42, 46, and 47), that of "public authorities."

52.  
The explanatory memorandum of the project specifies that the "definition of public authority is taken from other legislations to avoid misinterpretation." According to the provisions of the project, it refers to "a) the services of the Governments; b) the local authorities present in the territory of the Walloon Region; c) public law bodies and persons, regardless of their form and nature, which: i) have been created to specifically meet [the] needs of general interest that are not industrial or commercial in nature; and ii) are endowed with legal personality; and iii) depend on the Governments or local authorities referred to in this point b), in one of the following ways: - either their activities are predominantly financed by the Governments or local authorities referred to in this point b); - or their management is subject to control by the Governments or local authorities referred to in this point b); - or more than half of the members of the administrative, management, or supervisory body are appointed by the Governments or local authorities referred to in this point b); d) associations formed by one or more entities referred to in a; b or c." This definition is thus inspired by the definition of the concept of "contracting authority" as defined in Article 2, 1°, of the law of June 17, 2016, on public procurement (see also Article 5 of the LTD).

II.3.3. Allocation of responsibilities regarding the processing of personal data  

104.  
In the context just outlined, the draft does not explicitly provide for an allocation of responsibilities regarding the processing of personal data as such, but rather more generally regarding the processing of data from authentic sources and databases from authentic sources. However, within the framework of this draft, the determination of responsibilities regarding the processing of personal data undoubtedly constitutes an essential element of the processing which, in accordance with the principles recalled previously (see supra points nos 5-6 and 87-89) and for obvious reasons of legal security, must be resolved.

105.  
Use of data from an authentic source by a public authority. This is a basic hypothesis of data sharing between public authorities. The manager "A" manages an authentic source within which there is data whose processing is necessary for public authority "B," in order to fulfill its public interest mission or related to the exercise of public authority, for example, the granting of a permit to the concerned individual. Authorized by the CCED, "B" accesses the data from "A" via the BCED in accordance with the cooperation agreement and the decree that created the authentic source, and then processes the data in accordance with the rules governing the granting of the relevant permit. The data sharing system established by the cooperation agreement is merely the support/means for collecting the data necessary for authority "B," and all processing operations involved constitute a single processing operation aimed at the purpose of issuing the permit to the concerned individual. 

106.  
In light of the tasks of the manager of the authentic source (see above, point no. 90), the missions of the BCED (in particular, those of service integrator, see above, points nos. 91-96 and 98), and the mission of the public authority accessing the relevant data (which will be defined in the standard establishing its missions), the Authority is of the opinion that these three actors, at the very least, are jointly responsible for the processing. The project remains free, within the limits permitted by the GDPR, to allocate individual responsibilities among them, for example, in the manner in which it currently allocates responsibilities regarding the various processing operations in general (i.e., regardless of whether the data concerned is personal or not) (collection, storage, updating, security, provision, etc.).

II.3.3. Technical and organizational measures – security – and obligation to use the BCED  

114.  
Given the central role played by the BCED, which as its name indicates, constitutes a true hub for data exchange, the Authority believes that the framework cannot be silent regarding its obligation to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures in light of the risks to which the rights and freedoms of the data subjects are exposed (Article 32 of the GDPR).  

115.  
In this regard, the Authority supports the provisions set forth in the project on this matter (see Articles 10, 11, paragraph 2, 8), g), 12, paragraph 4, 13, 16, and 20), and in particular the fact that as a service integrator, the BCED must offer "highly secure" access services to authentic sources in compliance with applicable regulations (Article 2, 3°, b) of the project).  

116.  
The Authority nevertheless considers that this requirement for a high level of security should more generally be enshrined in Article 16 of the project, first sentence, which states that the BCED "ensures at all times the security of data and their transmission." More generally, the framework should provide that the BCED must ensure a high level of security for the processes related to the exercise of its missions. Beyond this specific point, rules and concepts enshrined in applicable legislation in other areas could, if necessary, be invoked to strengthen the framework in terms of the technical and organizational measures to be implemented by the BCED.

III.  
CONCLUSION

FOR THESE REASONS,

the Authority is of the opinion that the draft cooperation agreement amending the cooperation agreement of May 23, 2013, between the Walloon Region and the French Community regarding the development of a joint initiative in data sharing and the joint management of this initiative must be adapted. The necessary adaptations can be summarized as follows.

Before summarizing the various adaptations to be made to the draft, the Authority wishes to draw the applicants' attention to the following three elements, which are of particular importance:

- first, authentic data sources cannot be duplicated by a service integrator or another public authority, except in disregard of their principle and potentially the rules that establish and regulate them, particularly when these sources are governed by another level of power;

- second, the involvement of multiple service integrators without a proper division of their respective tasks and without mutual commitments regarding information security is contrary to the obligations established in the GDPR regarding information security;

- third, finally, the Authority is opposed to the draft in that it also grants a supervisory authority a general normative authorization competence concerning all data flows from authentic sources between public authorities, a competence that should fall under the responsibility of the data controllers and, where applicable, an entity established for this purpose, under the principle of "accountability."

Regarding the concepts used by the draft, the generalized reuse of data established, and the principles of transparency and legality:

- the concept of databases from authentic sources should be abandoned as it is not in line with the very logic of authentic sources, particularly as it could also include external authentic sources, organized at another level of power. In any case, on the one hand, it could not be...
