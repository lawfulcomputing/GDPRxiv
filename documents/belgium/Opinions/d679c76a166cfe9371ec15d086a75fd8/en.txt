Notice 106/2018 - 2/15

I. OBJECT AND CONTEXT OF THE REQUEST FOR NOTICE

1. The Committee on Internal Affairs of Parliament invited the Data Protection Authority for a hearing on October 16, 2018, regarding the draft law containing various provisions concerning the National Register and population registers – DOS 54 3256.

2. The Data Protection Authority expressed its opinion on the draft law containing various "Internal" provisions in its notice 19/2018 of February 28 of this year.

3. Given that this draft law includes measures that have a significant impact on the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned, the Authority issues this notice on its own initiative to analyze the follow-up that has been made to its aforementioned notice 19/2018. On many points, the draft law containing various provisions concerning the National Register and population registers (hereinafter "the draft law") follows the notice 18/2018 of the Privacy Protection Commission (CPVP). In light of the limited time available to the Data Protection Authority (DPA), this notice is limited to addressing the points not followed up from notice 18/2018 of the CPVP as well as, without necessarily aiming for completeness, remarks on new provisions that present a negative impact in terms of guarantees for the individuals concerned regarding their level of protection of their fundamental right to the protection of personal data.

4. The attention of parliamentarians is particularly drawn to Article 27 of the draft law, which provides for the insertion of fingerprints into the electronic chip of the identity card. Given the impact of this measure concerning the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, a specific chapter has been drafted on the subject. It is included at the end of this notice.

II. SUBSTANTIVE EXAMINATION

5. It follows from Article 2, §2 of the draft law that new sub-registers may be created by the National Register (hereinafter "NR"). The wording of this provision does not meet the criteria of predictability and quality of laws governing the processing of personal data as stated by the European Court of Human Rights. Article 2, §2 must explicitly specify what these sub-registers are, other than those already mentioned in Article 2 §1 of the draft LRN (Population registers, registers of foreigners, waiting register, and consular registers) and in Article 6bis of the 1991 law on Population Registers (Identity card register and Foreigners' card register). According to the explanations included in the explanatory memorandum, it seems to concern the Protocol Register and the new sub-register created by this draft law, the Register of persons appearing on a Belgian civil status act.

6. Regarding the creation of this new Register of persons appearing on a Belgian civil status act, ambiguity surrounds its creation on several aspects; this must be corrected to meet the required quality criteria:
a. It is necessary to limitatively specify the civil status acts concerned.

b. Furthermore, the justification for the creation of this last Register must clearly appear in the explanatory memorandum; otherwise, it cannot be subject to an analysis of legitimacy and proportionality.
i. How does being mentioned in a civil status act justify the inclusion of the person in the NR concerning the purposes of the National Register?
ii. A priori, the rationale for registering a person in the National Register is linked to the repeated contacts that this person will have with Belgian administrations. Is this criterion met for every person appearing in a civil status act?

c. In addition to the issue of the lack of concrete justification for the creation of this new register, Article 2, §4, 3° of the draft LRN describes the new categories of persons to be registered in this register in a vague and impractical manner ("persons who are married or who intend to marry (Criterion too vague and unverifiable) with a person registered in the NR, who cohabit (de facto or legal?) or who intend to make (Criterion too vague and unverifiable) a declaration of legal cohabitation with a person registered in the NR, or who are concerned by a recognition (What types of recognition are we talking about?), but who do not have an NR number"). According to Article 22 of the Constitution, it is not for the King but for the legislator to determine exhaustively and precisely the persons concerned by a registration in this centralized register.

d. The same remarks can also be made regarding Article 5 of the draft law, which inserts a new Article 2ter into the LRN: "The following persons shall be mentioned in the National Register, starting from a date determined by the Minister of the Interior, the natural persons mentioned on a Belgian civil status act established by a civil status officer but who are not subject to a registration or mention in the

e. According to Article 9 of the draft law, the data from this register ("Civil Status") will only be retained for archival purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research, or for general statistical purposes. There is a contradiction here with Article 2 of the LRN, which describes other purposes for which the data from the RN may be used. This point needs clarification (in addition to the necessary justifications mentioned above).  

f. This same article specifies the data concerning these individuals that will be included in the National Register but in a partially variable manner, namely the data referred to in Article 3, paragraph 1, items 1 to 3 of the LRN and the other data referred to in Article 3 of the LRN, provided they are available in the relevant civil status act. This is not compatible with the quality and predictability criteria of the ECHR and risks being judged contrary to Article 10 of the Constitution. The personal data that will be centralized in this register must be determined by law in a restrictive manner.  

7. Article 10 of the draft law amends Article 5 of the LRN to adapt it following the abolition of the Integrated Sectoral Committee within the Commission for the Protection of Privacy. It is now the Minister of the Interior who will grant access authorizations to the National Register and the use of the identification number of the RN; this aligns with his role as the data controller of this Register.  

a. The draft law refers, in several of its articles (see in particular Articles 10 and 11), to the conditions and modalities for access authorization to the RN "provided for in § 1 of Article 5 § 1 in draft." However, these conditions are found in Article 15 of the draft LRN and are reduced compared to the conditions currently provided in the LRN in paragraphs 2 and 3 of Article 5 of the LRN. It is necessary, on the one hand, to correct the reference made (also referring to the future Article 15 of the LRN) and, on the other hand, to complete the conditions and modalities for the authorization referred to in the draft Article 15 of the LRN with the terms "the verification of whether access to the requested data and the use of the requested RN number comply with the GDPR." This is a prerogative of the Minister of the Interior as the data controller of the National Register.

b. Regarding the verification of the security measures adopted by users of the National Register, the explanatory memorandum specifies that the Minister will not carry out any prior verification in this regard and that it is the mission of the Data Protection Authority. Whether for the Data Protection Authority in the exercise of its control powers or for the Minister of the Interior in the exercise of his verification competence regarding the conditions to be authorized to access the data of the National Register, it is true that the means to be made available to them to be able to audit on-site the security measures adopted by all users of the National Register should be very high and costly.  
It is therefore appropriate for the legislator to explicitly specify the minimum security requirements required of users of the National Register. This will provide a clear line of conduct in this regard and hold them accountable for the measures to be taken. To do this, the legislator could, for example, draw inspiration from the forms that were used by the Sectoral Committee of the National Register by requiring applicants for authorization to provide a sworn statement that they meet the following minimum conditions to be included in the LRN: (1) Conducting a risk assessment of the personal data processed and defining security needs accordingly, (2) maintaining a written version of the information security policy specifying the strategies and measures adopted to secure the personal data processed, (3) identifying all media involving the personal data processed, (4) informing internal and external personnel involved in the processing of data about their confidentiality and security duties regarding the processed data arising from both legal provisions and the security policy, (5) adopting physical security measures for the data to prevent unnecessary or unauthorized physical access to the media containing the processed personal data, (6) adopting physical and environmental security measures to prevent physical damage that could compromise the processed personal data, (7) adopting measures to protect the networks to which the equipment processing personal data are connected, (8) maintaining an updated list of the different persons authorized to access the personal data in the context of processing, specifying and justifying their respective level of access concerning their function performed (creation, consultation, modification, destruction), (9) implementing logical access security to the data via an access authorization mechanism designed so that the personal data processed and the processing concerning them are not accessible.

c. Finally, it is necessary to coordinate the requirement for prior authorization by ministerial decree with Article 20 of the law of July 30, 2018, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data, which provides that "unless otherwise provided in specific laws, in execution of Article 6.2 of the GDPR, the federal public authority that transfers personal data based on Article 6.1.c or 6.1.e of the GDPR formalizes this transmission for each type of processing through a protocol between the initial data controller and the data controller receiving the data."

8. Article 5, §2 of the draft LRN grants the Minister the power to authorize access to the data of the Population Registers, via the RN services. It is specified, as is currently the case in the LRN, that this data is not retained in the National Register.  
It is necessary to correct this fiction insofar as it is impossible to act as an intermediary in a data flow without retaining the concerned data for a minimum period of time. According to the explanatory memorandum, it is specified that this formulation should be understood in such a way that the data from the Population Registers accessed via the services of the National Register should not be considered as "legal" data within the meaning of the LRN. What does this mean concretely and what are the concrete consequences in terms of processing this data and accessibility?

9. Article 5ter of the draft LRN provides for the opening of the National Register to the private sector for the updating of their files or customer data banks in exchange for payment for the services of the National Register.  
a. Most of the comments made on this subject by the CPVP have been followed, except for those concerning the need to impose the adoption of minimum security measures on the private and public bodies that will be involved in receiving the transfers from the National Register on this basis. Article 5ter, §2, 6° of the draft only provides for the obligation to designate a data protection officer as well as to keep a "data security plan" available to the data protection authority without further specification. Given the completely insufficient nature of this last measure, it is appropriate that the legislator specifies the minimum security conditions that these bodies must meet to receive data updates. In this regard, reference is made to point 7.b above. A sworn commitment to the adoption of these measures could be provided for by the legislator.

b. Article 5ter, §2, 3° of the draft should be corrected as requiring the deletion of the basic identification data of the concerned person at the end of the contract may not be compliant with the GDPR. For example, in the case of managing disputes related to this contract, a longer retention period may be justifiable and compliant with the GDPR. The terms "in any case, the data must be immediately destroyed at the end of the contract" should be removed.

c. To ensure the effective nature of the obligation for the concerned bodies to notify the RN services of the cessation of the contractual relationship for which the data transfers from the RN will be communicated to them, as provided in Article 5ter, §3, it is necessary to provide for a specific criminal sanction for non-compliance. This obligation is important in light of the right to the protection of personal data as it ensures the legitimacy of the data flow over time. Without this specific criminal sanction, there is a certain risk that this obligation will remain a dead letter and that the National Register services will continue to communicate to these bodies irrelevant data without a basis of legitimacy.

The same remark applies to Article 5ter, §5 of the draft LRN, which prohibits the sale, communication to third parties, and use for advertising purposes of the data that will be made available to the bodies based on this draft Article 5ter.

d. Article 5ter § 4 of the draft LRN provides that the "list of all bodies authorized by natural persons to automatically receive from the RN the modifications made to their data as well as the purposes for which said modifications are communicated is established, kept up to date, and available on...

the website of the National Register". To the extent that the determination of these bodies and the aforementioned purposes of data communication are linked to the consent of each individual concerned, this list can only be accessible to each individual and not publicly on the internet. If the legislator's intention is to communicate the names of the bodies that wish to potentially benefit from this system, it is necessary to reformulate this provision so that it accurately reflects the legislator's intent. In its current formulation, the publication envisaged by Article 5ter §4 of the draft LRN will consist of a processing of personal data contrary to the GDPR.

10. In order to ensure a correct minimal level of security for users of the National Register, Article 10 of the draft LRN must specify what the minimum security measures required are. Reference is made to point 7.b. of this notice.

11. The draft law removes the current Article 12 § 2 of the LRN, which requires any beneficiary of access authorization to the RN to designate by name their bodies or agents who, due to their responsibilities, have access to the RN and to keep an updated list of these individuals. No justification is provided in the explanatory memorandum regarding this removal, whereas this obligation requires users of the National Register to limit the members of their staff authorized to consult the National Register to what is strictly necessary.

12. The authority notes with satisfaction the establishment of the legal obligation to maintain correct logging files of access to the National Register (loggings) by users of the National Register, as well as the penalization of non-compliance with this obligation. This will greatly improve the situation that the CPVP has often faced during its inspections regarding suspicions of illegal use of data from the National Register; data controllers often citing the impossibility of verifying who used the data due to the absence of logging.

In addition to providing for this logging obligation, Article 17 paragraph 5 of the draft LRN provides for the maintenance by the RN services of a register of user consultations and communications made, indicating the identification of the user who accessed the data, the data consulted, and the date and time of consultation. As with any processing of personal data specifically regulated by law, it is necessary to specify the concrete purpose for which this internal register is created.

13. Regarding Article 27 of the draft law, which amends Article 6 of the 1991 Population Registers Act (1991 Act) by providing for the inclusion of fingerprints, reference is made to the chapter of this notice specifically dedicated to this subject (points 19 and following).

14. Article 6 § 4 of the draft 1991 Act regulates the reading and recording of the data on the identity card by providing that all data contained therein - except for the photo, the RN number, and the fingerprints - may be read or recorded in accordance with the legal provisions on privacy and personal data protection. It is specified that the photo and the RN number may only be used with legal authorization or by ministerial decree.

The explanatory memorandum specifies that the legislator cannot impose the determination of the authorities authorized to read the photo on the identity card - as will be done for reading the image of the fingerprints - and justifies its position based on the fact that the photo is visible to the naked eye on the identity card.

This justification is not convincing. To the extent that the automated reading and recording of the photo on the identity card generates a particular risk in terms of identity fraud; the Authority considers that the authorities authorized to carry out such processing should be determined restrictively by law and specific technical protection measures should surround the automated reading of the photo on the identity card.

15. Article 6 §4 al. 2 in fine of the draft 1991 Act conditions the reading and use of the electronic identity card on the necessity of obtaining the prior free, specific, and informed consent of its holder, and paragraph 4 of this same provision provides that "without prejudice to Article 1 of the AR of 25/03/2003, the holder of the identity card may refuse that their data be read/recorded except in the situations determined by AR deliberated in the Council of Ministers." The consideration 78 of the CPVP's notice has not been followed. The government interprets the AR of March 25, 2003 contrary to that of the CPVP by considering that this AR also applies to private sector actors.

However, the Authority recalls that this AR of March 25, 2003 (and more specifically its Article 1) was adopted in execution of Article 6, §7 of the law of July 19, 1991, relating to population registers and identity cards, which delegates to the King the task of determining "the authorities and public officers at whose request the identity card must be presented"; which reinforces the position of the CPVP and its remark made in cons.78: contrary to what is stated in the Explanatory Memorandum, this AR is not applicable to the private sector.
Notice 106/2018 - 10/15

Consequently, according to the wording of Article 6, §4 of the draft law of 1991, all private sector actors will be required to obtain the prior consent of the individual concerned to read or use a person's electronic identity card. This is likely to severely impact private actors who are bound by a legal obligation (prior to this draft law) to collect the identity of individuals through their identity card. To avoid this, it is appropriate to add at the end of paragraph 2 of this future Article 6, §4, "Unless otherwise provided by law."

Furthermore, apart from the legal provisions allowing for the reading of the identity card, certain types of contracts (generally bilateral contracts with successive performances as long as necessary) may require ensuring the correct identity of the individual concerned at the time of their conclusion and constitute a legitimate basis for requesting an individual to present their identity card. However, by limiting the option of consent for this purpose, it may also put some private sector actors in a position where they cannot verify the identity of their contracting party through this means. In this regard, reference is made to the Recommendation of Initiative 03/2011 from the CPVP regarding the copying of identity cards as well as their use and electronic reading.

16. Article 28 of the draft law grants the Minister of the Interior the power to authorize access to the Identity Card Register, but in doing so, it also extends the potential beneficiaries of said authorizations—currently limited to public authorities—without any justification.

Given that these identity card registers are more sensitive compared to the National Register due to the data they contain and in light of the risk of fraud and identity theft that may result from the misuse of the identity card photo, these registers must have restricted accessibility compared to the National Register. A logical option would be to limit their accessibility to the competent authorities for the purposes of detecting criminal offenses, conducting investigations, and pursuing legal actions in this regard or enforcing criminal sanctions.

17. Article 29 of the draft law modifies Article 6ter of the 1991 law in such a way that it is no longer provided in the law that the electronic functions of stolen or lost identity cards are suspended or withdrawn directly by the Municipality. The determination of these modalities is now delegated to the King. The rationale for delegating this to the King is not apparent. This should be provided for directly in the law. It is important that these functions are directly suspended in the event of theft or loss. Furthermore, the delegation to the King made in the draft Article 6ter, §4 also includes the determination of the authorities to which these theft reports must be made, whereas paragraph 1 of the draft Article 6ter already determines this.

18. Regarding Article 30 of the draft law related to the Checkdoc application, the Commission's opinion—recommending that users of this application who will have access to personal data of citizens (information on whether or not their identity card is still valid) be previously identified and authenticated—is not followed.

The explanatory memorandum notes the impossibility of identifying and/or authenticating due to the need for the widest possible accessibility of the service. In doing so, citizens will be unable to know the recipients of their personal data (information on whether their identity card or passport is still valid or has been stolen, lost, or expired) who use this Checkdoc service if they request access; which cannot be acceptable.

Moreover, the version of the draft law of this article provided that the services of the National Register retain for 10 years from the date of verification the data related to the documents whose validity has been verified as well as the users of the Checkdoc application. It is important that the draft law also includes this precision for the sake of predictability for Checkdoc users.

If these remarks are not followed, it is at least important that a risk assessment of such an opening of the use of this Checkdoc application (risk of identity fraud, forgery of identity documents, etc.) be conducted beforehand and that the chosen option be justified based on the conclusions of this risk assessment.

19. Regarding the insertion of fingerprints of the entire population into the electronic chip of the identity card (art 27 draft law)  

20. The draft law has not addressed the criticisms raised on this matter by the Data Protection Authority in its opinion 18/2018 dated February 28, 2018 (points 62 to 71).  

21. Even though it is provided that fingerprints will only be stored on the chip of the identity card, the attention of parliamentarians is specifically drawn to the points developed below.  

22. Contrary to what is stated in the Explanatory Memorandum, the European Commission has not issued a recommendation on the insertion of fingerprints into identity cards but has only submitted a proposal for a European Regulation from the European Commission dated April 17, 2018, on the insertion of biometric data into European identity cards. This proposal for a European Regulation - which, unlike the Belgian draft law currently under discussion, does explicitly and restrictively specify the purposes for which biometric data (facial image and fingerprint) may be used - has been the subject of very critical opinions from the European Data Protection Supervisor1 and still needs to continue its European legislative process.  

23. There is still no real justification for the measure contemplated in the explanatory memorandum, despite this being requested by the Data Protection Authority. Our identity card already has anti-counterfeiting features (hologram, etc.) as well as a biometric element (the facial image). In what concrete way is this insufficient? What statistics does the government have that support the proposed measure?  

24. In its aforementioned opinion, the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS) noted that the statistics do not support the European Commission's proposal, which aligns with that of the government. Statistics from the European Border and Coast Guard Agency (Frontex) reveal only 38,870 cases of fraudulent use of national identity cards for the period 2013-2017. Furthermore, there has been a decrease in the use of fraudulent residence permits by individuals from third countries since 2015 of at least 11%.

25. The assimilation of identity cards with passports, which is put forward by the government to justify this measure, is not acceptable: even though identity cards can also be used as travel documents within the European Union, they are currently not subject to systematic checks for these travels due to the principle of freedom of movement within the European Union. Furthermore, unlike passports, national identity cards offer many other uses (applications in the private sector, etc.). This point has also been raised by the EDPS in its opinion. Given the differences between identity cards and passports, the introduction of security elements in identity cards that could be considered appropriate in the case of passports cannot be automatic but requires thorough reflection and analysis that does not seem to have been conducted.

26. No prior data protection impact assessment (DPIA) appears to have been carried out regarding the project to insert the image of citizens' fingerprints into the chip of the electronic identity card. The explanatory memorandum simply states that a "DPIA will (is) done" "zullen worden uitgevoerd" in the Dutch version.

However, the proposed measure must be subject to a DPIA under Article 35.2.b of the GDPR, and this must be done prior to its legal framework since it is highly likely that the outcome of this analysis will lead to the adoption of other measures or, as was the case with the impact assessment of the European Commission's proposed Regulation - to limiting the mandatory nature of the insertion of biometric data in identity cards to the facial image of the holder and specifying the optional nature of the insertion of fingerprints.

27. The prohibition on processing biometric data can only be lifted based on Article 9.2.g of the GDPR, which requires not only the reason of significant public interest but also the proportionality of the measure in relation to the objective pursued and the adoption of appropriate and specific measures to safeguard the fundamental rights and interests of the individuals concerned. These are currently insufficient:

a. The government's choice to collect and store the digitized image of fingerprints in the chip of the card is, according to the EDPS, not the most opportune choice given the risk of identity theft in the event of hacking of the data on the electronic chip of the card. This choice should be reconsidered, and the fingerprint data stored in the identity card chips should be limited to a subset of features extracted from the fingerprint image or to trace-less biometric techniques (hand contour, vein pattern of a finger, etc.).  
b. Instead of delegating to the King the task of determining the authorities that will be authorized to read the fingerprints, it is the formal legislator's responsibility to do so.  
c. The law should also specify that the reading of this data can only be done to verify the authenticity of the identity card. It is advisable to already provide in the law for limiting measures for card readers that will allow reading fingerprints.  
d. What specific protective measures will be taken to minimize the risk of hacking the certificate of the identity card that will contain the image of the fingerprints, both in terms of securing the chip in which this data will be inserted and securing the readers of this data?  
e. What are the protective measures for the temporary database that will centrally store the fingerprints for 3 months, and who will be the data controller?  
f. Finally, as noted by the EDPS, children under the age of 14 should not be subjected to this measure.

FOR THESE REASONS,

In addition to the aforementioned remarks, the Authority issues an unfavorable opinion on the draft law primarily due to its Article 27, which provides for the inclusion of fingerprints in the electronic chips of identity cards.

For the reasons explained in this opinion (points 19 and following), it is recommended that parliamentarians request the removal of this measure from the draft law due to the necessity and legal obligation, as stipulated in Article 35.3.b of the GDPR, to conduct a prior analysis of its impact concerning data protection and to have statistics and concrete elements justifying the inadequacy of our current identity card model in relation to the fight against identity card forgery, given that it already contains a biometric element (the facial image) and anti-forgery devices (hologram, etc.).

Instead of anticipating the European Regulation proposal from the European Commission, it would be preferable to wait for this proposal to complete its European legislative process, especially since this project has been subject to a very critical opinion from the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS Opinion 07/2018 of August 10, 2018).
