Opinion 167/2018 - 2/11

I. OBJECT OF THE REQUEST AND CONTEXT

1. The Minister of Social Affairs and Public Health submits to the Authority for opinion, an "article of the draft law containing various provisions on health," article X, whose objective is to establish in law the power of the King to impose the processing of personal data, including health data, "in the context of making available to patients, human medicines for which no marketing authorization [(MA)] or registration has been granted or which are not marketed in Belgium, referred to in article 6quater, § 1, al. 1, 1° to 3°, of the law of March 25, 1964 on medicines" [the Medicines Act] (explanatory memorandum of article X). Article X inserts a § 1/1 in article 6quater of the Medicines Act [article 6quater, § 1/1]. This project was adopted due to the Opinion of the Council of State No. 55.790/3 of April 17, 2014, on a draft royal decree "amending the royal decree of December 14, 2006 concerning human and veterinary medicines" [the opinion of the Council of State]1.

2. The three hypotheses for making available referred to by the Medicines Act are essentially as follows: compassionate use of medicines2, execution of emergency medical programs3, and finally, response to special needs when the patient cannot be adequately treated with authorized and available medicines in Belgium. The objective of article X, as stated in its explanatory memorandum, is to establish and frame the power of the King regarding "any processing of personal data" in this context.

II.  
EXAMINATION OF THE PROJECT  

II.1. Principle of Legality and Authorization of the King  

3.  
The Council of State had, in its opinion (see points 3.6 and following), primarily raised a difficulty regarding the principle of legality enshrined in Article 22 of the Constitution. In this regard, under the principles of transparency and legality established in Articles 8 of the ECHR and 22 of the Constitution, a draft law must clearly specify the circumstances under which the processing of personal data is authorized, and consequently determine what data is processed, the individuals concerned, the conditions and purposes of such processing, the duration of data retention, and the individuals who have access to it. The Authority has already had the opportunity to recall these principles, following the CPVP.  

4.  
In their view, a delegation to the King "is not contrary to the principle of legality, provided that the authorization is defined with sufficient precision and pertains to the execution of measures whose essential elements are predetermined by the legislator."  

5.  
Important note: the applicant does not refer to other norms of the rank of law governing the processing of personal data in the context in which they intend to legislate (see above, point no. 2) and seems to do so de novo; the Authority will not seek elsewhere than in its project for potential legal bases for these processes. It is the applicant's responsibility to verify when, for example, in their project, they refer to missions of institutions provided for in other norms, whether these other norms do not already govern (and to what extent) the processing of personal data by these institutions. Only after this exercise can it be decided what needs to be provided for or not in the present draft of Article 6quater, § 1/1.  

6.  
Before applying the aforementioned principles in more detail to Article 6quater, § 1/1, in draft form, it can already be emphasized that the principle of the very existence of data processing must be determined by the legislator and not left to the discretion of the King, as Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 1, provides.

II.2. Elements to be integrated into Article 6quater, § 1/1, in draft  

7.  
In accordance with the principles just recalled, it is primarily the legislator's responsibility to determine which data (Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 1) may be processed and for what purposes (Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 2), distributing the former to the latter, rather than delegating this task to the King (Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 3). In other words, the framework must articulate the data and the purposes together. Only once this exercise is completed can the Authority determine whether this data complies with the requirements of Article 5, 1., c), of the GDPR.  

8.  
These purposes must also be determined, explicit, and legitimate (Article 5, 1., b), of the GDPR). Among these, three of the specified purposes relate to the exercise by the AFMPS of its missions in Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 2, 1°, 4° and 5°. Appropriately, 4°8 and 5°9 explicitly refer to the legal bases of the AFMPS's missions (however, references to 4° and 5° should refer respectively to Article 4, § 1, paragraph 3, 3° and 4°, of the law of July 20, 2006, concerning the creation and functioning of the Federal Agency for Medicines and Health Products). The 1° should also explicitly refer to the legal basis defining the relevant mission of the AFMPS (control and evaluation of the availability of medicines) – which is not the case as it stands. The same applies to the mission of the INAMI in this matter.  

9.  
The two other purposes provided for in Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 2, 2° and 3°, lack clarity. What are the "files" that need to be evaluated (the concept is not defined) and by whom should they be evaluated and in relation to what (does this also involve targeting a specific mission defined elsewhere?)? What about, in the same terms, the evaluation of the quality and cost of care covered by the cohort decision referred to in Article 25quater/1 of the law on compulsory health care insurance and benefits coordinated on July 14, 1994? Furthermore, who is responsible for monitoring the traceability of medicines (the concept is also not defined, although understandable; is this traceability regulated elsewhere? How is it organized? Must the King organize it?)? The lack of clarity in the text is such that it also prevents identifying the individuals who will have access to the data. However, these individuals must also be clearly indicated in the framework or at least in its economy, or in other rules to which this framework would refer. Again, concerning the public authorities that may be involved, either it pertains to the implementation of a mission already defined in another text to which it is appropriate to refer, or it pertains to a new mission that needs to be defined.

10. 
Regarding data concerning health (Article 4, 15 of the GDPR), at least in part, each processing envisaged must fall under one of the hypotheses of Article 9, 2. of the GDPR. Without prejudice to any other possible options, the explanatory memorandum of Article 6quater, § 1/1, refers a priori (the indeterminacy of the purposes does not allow for a definitive conclusion, see above, points nos 8-9) rightly to Article 9, 2., i), of the GDPR (processing necessary for reasons of public interest in the field of public health). However, the Authority reminds that this same provision also requires the relevant national law to provide "appropriate and specific measures for the safeguarding of the rights and freedoms of the data subject, in particular professional secrecy." However, apart from pseudonymization (see below, points nos 17-19), Article 6quater, § 1/1, does not provide anything specific.

11. 
The applicant will also pay attention to the question of whether it is desired or not to establish a central registry and, if so, regarding which data processing (purposes). The opinion of the Council of State (point 3.6) illustrates the legal importance of choosing such a form for data processing. In this case, it is a means of processing that should result from the terms of the provision. However, at this stage, the explanatory memorandum only emphasizes that "the processing of the concerned personal data may, for example, take the form of a central registry for the administrative data of patients, while efforts will be made to avoid such registries for the processing of health-related data, which are more sensitive" (italics added by the Authority). The centralization or decentralization of the storage of health-related data is indeed an essential characteristic of the processing. If the present draft of Article 6quater, § 1/1, aims to create a central database to be fed and used by the actors in the production and distribution chain of medicines for compassionate purposes, for example, this must be reflected in the provision of the draft. The decision to establish such a means of processing is also decisive regarding the identification of data recipients and the data controller(s), points which, moreover, contain weaknesses (see above, point no 9 and below, point no 12).

12. 
On the point of the designation of the data controller(s) specifically, Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 5, merely empowers the King to designate "the" data controller "who...

Just as data and purposes must be articulated (see above, point no. 7), responsibilities must be allocated concerning each processing operation and, where applicable, among joint controllers of processing. The Authority perceives that the difficulty in achieving this allocation lies particularly in the uncertainty surrounding the means of processing that will be implemented.

13.  
As a reminder, the data controller is the person, public authority, or any other body that determines alone or jointly with others the purposes and means of processing (Article 4, 7, of the GDPR). And when the purposes and means of processing are determined by national law, "the data controller may be designated or the specific criteria applicable to its designation may be provided by [national] law." In this regard, while Member States may specify the application of GDPR rules in particular areas where they legislate to ensure coherence and clarity in the normative framework applicable to data processing, they cannot, for this reason, derogate from the GDPR or depart from the definitions it establishes. If national law determines joint responsibility for processing, it may also determine the respective obligations of joint controllers (Article 26, 1., of the GDPR). In light of these principles, for example, the authorities responsible for control missions will generally be considered data controllers. If a central register were to be established, joint responsibilities could, for example, be contemplated (with a subsequent distinction in the allocation of responsibilities among joint controllers, between, for instance, the person who collects the data and the one who retains and makes it available).

14.  
The law must at least determine the essential elements of the data retention period. As for this, Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 4, in draft form lacks precision by limiting itself to setting a maximum retention period valid without nuance in any case (whether the data concerns the treating physician or the patient, and regardless of the purpose): the term of a thirty-year period commencing on the day of the last availability of the medication.

15.  
Two additional remarks can be made in this regard. First, the explanatory memorandum raises that the deletion of personal data "thirty years after the end of the availability of the medication [...] is consistent with the retention period of the patient's medical record and is justified concerning a medication that has not been authorized, knowing, moreover, that the side effects of a medication may manifest long after its administration."

It is recalled in this regard, on the one hand, that the retention of medical records in a hospital is, for example, thirty years12, and that the Medical Code of Ethics developed by the National Council of the Order of Physicians (November 23, 2018) provides in its Article 24 that "patient records must be kept for thirty years after the last contact with the patient" (italics added by us). The starting points of these deadlines are presumably quite different. And in any case, on the other hand, it is the responsibility of the applicant to assess whether they are equally relevant for all the purposes pursued in their project (see above, points nos. 8-9).

16.  
Secondly, the explanatory memorandum in its penultimate paragraph maintains a certain ambiguity regarding the concepts of personal data, pseudonymized data, and anonymized data. To recall, on the one hand, anonymized data – unlike pseudonymized data – are not personal data, thus falling outside the scope of the GDPR13 and therefore do not need to be deleted after a certain period under this regulation.

17.  
As for anonymization, it is made mandatory to a certain extent, like pseudonymization, by Article 6quater, § 1/1, paragraph 3, which states: the King "provides that the data are pseudonymized and anonymized, insofar as pseudonymization and anonymization do not undermine the purposes pursued by the processing." This provision calls for two comments.

18.  
On the one hand, if processing can be carried out based on anonymized data, this implies that personal data cannot be processed for its purpose – they would not be necessary, and their processing would lead to a violation of Article 5, 1., c), of the GDPR. In other words, in such cases, what the legislator must foresee as such is the anonymization processing of the concerned personal data. It is then at the stage of the proposed article that it is appropriate to identify the processes for which anonymous data are sufficient and to authorize the anonymization processing.

19.  
On the other hand, in the same vein, the legislator could also foresee the cases in which the data must be pseudonymized. That being said, the Authority is of the opinion that it is permissible for it not to carry out this exercise and to impose on the King the requirement to provide for the pseudonymization of personal data when it does not undermine the pursued purpose. The Authority is in favor of enshrining such an obligation in Article 6quater, § 1/1. This is an obligation

20.  
This point provides an opportunity to emphasize, despite the inaccuracies regarding the purposes of the relevant processing (see above, points nos 8-9), that the latter presents a high inherent risk to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned within the meaning of Article 35 of the GDPR. Indeed, in this case and from a principled perspective, failures in every aspect of information security are likely to pose a severe risk to the individual concerned. The following developments illustrate this with regard to patients. For example, from the perspective of confidentiality, health-related data is involved, and moreover, concerning patients affected by serious illnesses (disabling, chronic, or severe, or that significantly weaken health, see above, point no. 2). Unauthorized access to such information can be particularly detrimental to the treated patient, both privately and professionally.  

21.  
From the perspective of integrity and availability, erroneous or unavailable data could, for example, complicate the communication to the patients or treating physicians concerned regarding any newly discovered serious adverse effects that could call into question the treatment protocol. Conversely, erroneous data could lead to the premature and unjustified interruption of such protocols. As the explanatory memorandum recalls (first paragraph), it concerns "medicines for human use for which no marketing authorization or registration has been granted or that are not marketed in Belgium." These considerations support the applicant's objective in establishing data processing in this area, as much as they remind of the importance of framing these with adequate technical and organizational measures.  

22.  
The data anonymization processes that would be considered will also be particularly sensitive since their result, the anonymized data, will be excluded from the scope of the GDPR and will consequently be usable without constraints arising from this regulation. They thus also contribute to the identification of a high inherent risk to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned.  

23.  
This finding of a high inherent risk implies two consequences to be taken into account. Firstly, the Authority believes that the draft legislative text should not be silent regarding the technical and organizational measures governing the security of the relevant processing. The obligation of pseudonymization is a step in the right direction (see above, point no. 19) that would benefit from being supplemented, although it cannot obviously be expected that the legislator will legislate in detail on this matter.

24.  
Secondly, this type of processing requires an impact assessment regarding data protection (Article 35, 1., of the GDPR)15. The Authority will generally wait at the stage of its referral for an opinion based on Article 23 of the LCA, for an impact assessment to be carried out concerning the elements of processing determined by the proposed regulation. The impact assessment is part of the exercise of the proportionality test in data protection (8 ECHR, 22 C°, Charter, GDPR). Its purpose at the level of the data controller is to be able to adapt the processing in such a way that the rights and freedoms of the data subjects are duly protected in light of the inherent risks of the processing. When the processing is created by the State, the law must enshrine the essential elements as has been recalled (see above, points nos 3-5) and applied in this case. The leeway of the data controller is limited by these legislative choices and the consequent regulatory choices, which at their level become intangible variables. Therefore, the legislator who does not analyze the impact of the elements of processing they determine on the rights and freedoms of the data subjects conducts a partial proportionality test and risks placing the data controller in an inextricable situation of conflict of norms, when at the end of such an analysis, it would have emerged that these elements must be modified. Indeed, the data controller is then on the one hand bound by the law that provides for the processing, and on the other hand, by compliance with the GDPR. This situation creates legal uncertainty detrimental to the data controller and the data subjects, in which the useful effect of the obligation that the data controller has under Article 35 of the GDPR to carry out an impact assessment is compromised.  

III.  
CONCLUSION  

25.  
Overall, the Authority recognizes that Article 6quater, § 1/1, in draft form pursues a legitimate objective and for a significant part at least (see for example above, point no. 21), aims to serve the interest of the data subjects who are the patients benefiting from the hypotheses referred to in Article 6quater, § 1, al. 1, 1° to 3°, of the law of March 25, 1964, on medicines.  

26.  
However, and within the limits of its analysis (see above, points nos 5 and 7), the Authority is of the opinion that it must be revised in several respects concerning the essential elements of the planned processing, in order to meet the requirements of Articles 8 of the ECHR and 22 of the Constitution (see above, points nos 3-4), as well as the GDPR.

27.  
Thus, above all, it is up to the legislator to decide whether or not to implement the proposed processing (see above, point no. 6). It is also their responsibility to precisely determine the purposes of the processing (see above, points nos 8-9) and to link them to the relevant data (see above, point no. 7). Regarding health data, the text of the device itself will enshrine the necessary appropriate guarantees (see above, point no. 10).  

28.  
The choice of a possible central register for all or part of the proposed processing is not addressed in the device when it should be if that is the legislator's intention (see above, point no. 11), and it will also prove decisive regarding the responsibilities related to the processing and the recipients of the data. As for the latter, the absence of choice regarding a possible central register and the imprecision of the purposes just recalled create a situation of uncertainty incompatible with the principles mentioned above. And concerning the responsibilities regarding the processing, they must also be attributed to individuals based on the processing (see above, points nos 12-13).  

29.  
The duration of data retention must be specified and also be considered in relation to the processing (see above, points nos 14-15), the device of the article in draft form must include the essential elements.  

30.  
From the perspective of technical measures, pseudonymized data remains personal data, while anonymized data is not (any longer), and anonymization constitutes a processing of personal data, which has consequences for the article in draft form (see above, points nos 16-19). Pseudonymization is part of the technical and organizational measures that the King must implement, under the article in draft form, to ensure the security of the processing. The planned processing presents a high inherent risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, particularly given that health data is involved, the question of measures governing the processing must receive specific treatment in the proposed device (see above, points nos 10 and 20-21), and an impact assessment must be carried out (see above, point no. 24).

FOR THESE REASONS,  

given the remarks mentioned above, in points 25-30, the Authority issues an unfavorable opinion on Article X. of the draft law containing various provisions regarding health, inserting a paragraph 1/1 into Article 6quater of the law of March 25, 1964, on medicines.
