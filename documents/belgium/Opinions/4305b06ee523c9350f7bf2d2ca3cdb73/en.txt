I. 
SUBJECT AND CONTEXT OF THE REQUEST FOR AN OPINION

1. The President of the Chamber of Representatives (hereinafter "the requester") has urgently requested the Authority's opinion regarding a draft law establishing a database at Sciensano in the context of the fight against the spread of the coronavirus COVID-19 (hereinafter "the draft law").

2. According to the "Developments" accompanying the draft law, the Government wishes, in the context of a deconfinement strategy, to assign to the Belgian Institute of Public Health Sciensano the "mission [...] to collect health data from patients from various healthcare providers or health or care organizations, as part of its surveillance activities provided for by law, and to process them in a database. [...] Furthermore, personal data concerning individuals with whom the patient has come into contact are also recorded [...]". According to these "Developments", "The objective of this data collection is threefold: First, it aims to be able to locate the affected patients and contact them. Secondly, the data collection is necessary for scientific, statistical studies and/or support for future policy, after pseudonymization or anonymization. Finally, data is transmitted to health inspection services in the regions as part of initiatives aimed at combating the harmful effects caused by infectious diseases."

3. The draft law intends to provide a legal framework for the establishment of this database (Article 1 of the draft law). It determines, in particular, the purposes of the establishment of this database (Article 3 of the draft law), the categories of data included therein (Article 2 of the draft law), and their retention period (Article 5 of the draft law). The draft law stipulates that any communication of data from the database it creates to third parties must be subject to deliberation by the Information Security Committee (social security and health chamber) (Article 4 § 1 of the draft law). The draft law also provides for the possibility of access by Sciensano to the national register as well as to the registers of the Crossroads Bank for Social Security (Article 4 § 2 of the draft law) to complement the database. It also stipulates that all individuals who have access to the database must take the necessary measures to ensure that the personal data contained therein will be processed confidentially and solely for one of the three purposes of the database (Article 4 § 3 of the draft law). The draft law requires Sciensano to appoint a data protection officer (Article 4 § 3 of the draft law). Finally, it provides for the deletion of data by Sciensano five days after the publication of the royal decree.

4. The text of the proposed law is very similar to the text of the draft royal decree that was submitted to the Authority for opinion by Mr. Philippe De Backer on April 23, 2020, regarding which the Authority issued an opinion dated April 29, 2020. However, some elements have been added, either to clarify certain provisions of the initial draft or for other purposes. The main gaps in the initial text have, however, not been filled.

5. This notice from the Authority is also formulated in extreme urgency and based on the information available to it. The Authority insists once again on being consulted if significant changes are to be made to the proposed law. It also invites the applicant to conduct a thorough review of the proposed law to eliminate logical, grammatical, and spelling errors and to include definitions to allow for better understanding.

6. This new notice is structured in two parts. In the first part, the Authority makes a general remark regarding the predictability and proportionality of data processing, which are lacking in this case (II). In the second part of its opinion, the Authority provides its comments article by article (III).

II.  
GENERAL REMARK  
REGARDING  
PREDICTABILITY  
AND  
PROPORTIONALITY OF DATA PROCESSING  

1. The creation of a centralized database, particularly when it includes sensitive data (namely, in this case, health data), from various sources and pursuing multiple distinct purposes, constitutes a significant interference with the right to the protection of personal data. The Authority reiterates that any interference with the right to respect for the protection of personal data, especially when the interference is significant, is only permissible if it is necessary and proportionate to the objective(s) it pursues and is framed by a sufficiently clear and precise standard whose application is predictable for the individuals concerned.

2. Thus, any standard governing the processing of personal data, particularly when it constitutes a significant interference with the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned, must meet the requirements of predictability and precision so that, upon reading it, the individuals concerned can clearly foresee how their data will be processed and the circumstances under which data processing is authorized. In accordance with Article 6.3 of the GDPR, read in conjunction with Articles 22 of the Constitution and 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms, the essential elements of data processing must be described with precision. This includes, in particular, the specific purpose(s) of the processing; the identity of the data controller(s); the categories of data processed, which must be – in accordance with Article 5.1 of the GDPR, “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed”; the categories of data subjects (individuals about whom data will be processed); the data retention period; the recipients or categories of recipients to whom their data are communicated and the circumstances under which and the reasons why they will be communicated, as well as all measures aimed at ensuring lawful and fair processing of this personal data.

3. The Authority generally finds that the proposed law does not sufficiently meet the requirement of clarity and predictability of the standard.

Indeed, the purposes of the data processing provided for in the proposed law are neither determined nor explicit.

4. First, the purposes, as currently described in the proposed law, are not sufficiently determined and explicit. Article 3 of the proposed law identifies three purposes:

“1° to search for and contact the individuals referred to in Article 2, §§2, 3, and 4 by a contact center in the context of combating the spread of the coronavirus COVID-19;  
2° to conduct scientific, statistical, and/or policy support studies, after pseudonymization, by epidemiologists associated with the COVI-19 Risk Assessment Group or by epidemiologists accredited by the Information Security Committee.  
3° to communicate data to the health inspection services of the regions concerned (…) in the context of initiatives aimed at combating the harmful effects caused by infectious diseases.”

5. These purposes are not defined in a sufficiently determined and explicit manner to meet the requirement of Article 6.3 of the GDPR, read in conjunction with its Article 5.1.b).

6. The first purpose is described in such a way that it does not allow for an understanding of what data will be processed by whom, how, or to achieve what objective. Reference is made in this regard to the questions raised by the Authority in its aforementioned opinion regarding the draft royal decree.
Notice 42/2020 - 5/19  
The applicant is required to reformulate the description of this first purpose to address these questions. A formulation related to the following could, for example, be used:  

« §Art. 3 §1.  
1° The [indicate the data or categories of data concerned] are made accessible to the four regional contact centers responsible for contacting individuals reported or presumed to be infected with Covid-19 in order to offer them the opportunity to provide data regarding the individuals referred to in Article 2§2 with whom they have had physical contact in the three weeks preceding the date of [specify], whether by phone, email, or home visit; »  

7. A fourth purpose should be added after the first. It could be presented as follows:  

« 2° The [indicate the data or categories of data concerned] are made accessible to the four regional contact centers responsible for contacting the individuals referred to in Article 2§4 with whom the individuals referred to in Article 2§2 have had physical contact, by phone, email, or home visit, in order to provide them with hygiene and prevention guidelines [and/or to impose or suggest a quarantine, invite them to get tested for COVID-19, or require such testing].  

8. The wording of the current second purpose should also be improved. An example could be (subject to what is explained further regarding the necessity of providing this in the legislative proposal):  

« 2° The [indicate the data or categories of data concerned] may be processed by research organizations to enable them to conduct scientific or statistical studies regarding the fight against the spread of the coronavirus COVID-19 and/or to support policy in this area in compliance with Title 4 of the law of July 30, 2018, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data.  
And this, after anonymization or pseudonymization in cases where their anonymization would not achieve their objectives. »  

9. That said, the Authority considers that this purpose does not need to be specifically framed in the legislative proposal, as compliance with the relevant provisions of the GDPR and the LTD regarding research is sufficient. The addition of this epidemiological research purpose detracts from the understanding of the project and risks causing confusion among citizens. The Authority therefore recommends the removal of the provisions of the legislative proposal related to the framing of research.  

Notice 42/2020 - 6/19  

10. The wording of the current third purpose is also too vague and should be revised. An example could be:  

« 3° The four regional contact centers communicate [indicate the data or categories of data concerned] to the health inspection services of the regions referred to in Article 3.3° in order to enable them to [specify what is meant by “as part of initiatives aimed at combating the spread of harmful effects caused by infectious diseases”].  

11. Some of the questions raised by the Authority in its previous notice remain unanswered: what should be understood, concretely, by “initiatives aimed at combating the spread of harmful effects caused by infectious diseases”? Do these initiatives only target the harmful effects caused by COVID-19, or can the data also be used in the fight against other infectious diseases? As mentioned above, the Authority also wonders why the data communicated to the health inspection services of the regions must be centralized at Sciensano. Why is it not sufficient to provide for direct communication between the contact centers and these services?  

12. In order to ensure the predictability of the standard, it is necessary not only to review or clarify the pursued purposes, the sources of the collected data, the categories of collected data, and the categories of recipients (see also the “comments on the articles” below), but it is also essential to better structure the legislative proposal and clarify, for each purpose, the categories of data that will be processed, the source(s) of this data, the identity of the data controller(s), the data retention periods, as well as, where applicable, the categories of recipients to whom this data may be communicated and the circumstances under which and the reasons why it will be communicated. The current wording of the legislative proposal, which does not distinguish these different elements according to the pursued purposes, severely undermines the transparency and predictability of the processing it intends to regulate.  

13. In addition to improving the predictability of the legislative proposal, its restructuring “by purpose” will better ensure that the processing regulated by the legislative proposal complies with the principles of necessity and proportionality. Indeed, this restructuring of the legislative proposal will imply, in particular, that the applicant ensures that the data collected and included in the legislative proposal are strictly adequate, relevant, and necessary in relation to the purpose pursued (data minimization principle). Thus, for example, if the recording of anonymous data, or at least pseudonymized data, is sufficient to achieve the purpose, the legislative proposal must provide for the recording of such anonymized or pseudonymized data.

III.  
COMMENTS ON THE ARTICLES OF THE PROPOSED LAW  

In addition to the fundamental and general remarks that precede, the Authority must make more specific comments regarding certain provisions of the proposed law.  

  
Article 1 of the proposed law  

1. The proposed law concerns "the creation of a database at Sciensano in the context of the fight against the spread of the coronavirus COVID-19." In line with this title, the content of the proposed law essentially details the operations of collection and recording by Sciensano of data within a database (as well as the communication of this data to third parties). A thorough reading of the text reveals that the actual objective of the proposed law is to establish a contact tracing system through contact centers under the authority of regional agencies (mentioned in the last paragraph of Article 3), which involves the collection and use by these operators of a large amount of personal data, including health-related data (referred to as "sensitive"). Without prejudice to the competence of the Regions regarding the establishment of the practical details of their tracing system, the proposal must regulate the fundamental aspects; otherwise, the legality of making the data included in the central database available to the regional agencies cannot be guaranteed. The proposed law should also stipulate that these agencies can only access the necessary data contained in the Sciensano database if a regional legal framework detailing the modalities of the tracing operations has been established.  

2. Thus, the proposed law should, in any case, contain distinct and detailed chapters describing:  
  
The principle of communication - apparently made mandatory for doctors (and other providers mentioned in Article 2§1) - of medical data to Sciensano, which indeed requires a clear legal framework providing for this communication obligation, in derogation of medical confidentiality, as recently emphasized by the Order of Physicians. Both the rules regarding data protection and medical confidentiality oppose the allowance of such processing of medical data or, even more so, making it mandatory by a resolution of any committee, outside of any legal framework;  

Notice 42/2020 - 8/19  
  
the fundamental aspects of the tracing system that have a fundamental impact on the protection of rights and freedoms (potential home visits, injunctions for self-isolation, sanctions for non-response, incorrect response, etc.);  
  
the possible opportunities for third parties (notably epidemiologists), whose categories would be listed in a restrictive manner, to be communicated certain data (the categories of which would also be listed in a restrictive manner) by the agencies responsible for the contact centers (in their capacity as responsible for the processing carried out by these centers) for research, statistical, or archiving purposes. In this regard, and as developed below, the Authority already emphasizes that nothing indicates that it is required to centralize this data in a database held by a third party (Sciensano), such centralization not being in compliance with the principles of necessity, proportionality, and minimization, and therefore not acceptable.  
In this regard, the Authority recommends:  
  
to reformulate the title of the proposed law as follows:  
"Proposed law on the establishment of a system known as 'contact tracing of (presumed) infected persons by the coronavirus COVID-19'"  
  
and to reformulate its objective (and Article 1 of the proposed law) as follows:  
"Art. 1. The objective of this law is to establish a system known as 'contact tracing' in order to break the chain of contamination of the coronavirus COVID-19, through the provision, by telephone contact, digital means, or home visits, of recommendations to individuals who have had high-risk physical contacts with infected patients or those presumed to be infected."  

To conclude on this point, the Authority insists that the proposed law reflects its objective and the data processing envisaged. The current proposed law does not frame the data processing carried out by the main actors of the project, namely the contact centers responsible for tracing and the doctors or other officials required to provide the data to be recorded in the database. It rather seems to be written with the aim of compelling regional agencies to engage a "mandatory" provider to retain the data they will use or generate, even though (1) a centralization of this data is not required (and thus not permitted) and (2) these agencies must remain free in their choice of means used for their data processing and the choice of their potential subcontractors.  

The Authority recalls that the designation of Sciensano as the data controller (Article 1 of the proposed law) implies that it is responsible for complying with all obligations that

The scope of this designation should also be clarified. Sciensano acts as a data controller only concerning the aforementioned operations of collection, recording in the database, and communication of data to third parties. Not for what concerns the so-called "tracing" operations (contacting infected individuals and their contacts) per se.

Article 3§ 2 of the bill proposal  

12. Article 3 of the bill proposal provides that individuals who have been in contact with infected persons will be contacted by contact centers that will provide them with "appropriate recommendations."  

13. The Authority notes the lacunae in this provision, which nonetheless targets the very purpose of the bill proposal, namely the tracing operations by contact centers. The vague terms used do not allow the citizen to understand how their data will be used. As mentioned above, the bill proposal should stipulate that these agencies can only access the necessary data contained in the Sciensano database if a regional legal framework detailing the modalities of tracing operations has been established, including answers to the following questions: regarding individuals who have had contact with infected persons, will they merely be encouraged to monitor the onset of symptoms or be required to self-isolate? They will be contacted "electronically." If they will be contacted by email, how is the collection and recording of their phone number and physical address necessary and therefore justified? If they will be contacted by phone, why not indicate this and why collect their physical address nonetheless? In the event of no response or refusal to provide their contact data, will an infected or presumed infected patient be re-contacted? How many times? If there are multiple contacts and considerable insistence is shown for these patients to cooperate (as reported by the press), this should certainly be reflected in the bill proposal. If home visits are conceivable based on the data contained in the database, as seems to be the case, this should be mentioned in the bill proposal.  

14. The same applies to the following paragraph of the bill proposal that mentions the involvement of referring doctors or administrative officials of communities. If it is contemplated to transmit health-related data of individuals infected with the virus to "administrative officials" of the community to which they belong (university, prison, school, shelter) and to charge these officials with conducting internal contact tracing or even taking specific measures to combat the spread of the virus in their establishment, this must be subject to comprehensive legislative regulation (detailing the modalities of this system and legitimizing the data processing it will entail) and cannot rely on the mere mention of this system in three paragraphs of the bill proposal.

Article 4 § 1 of the draft law  

15. The Authority still wonders whether each doctor, hospital, laboratory, and contact center will have to systematically submit each of its data communications to Sciensano for deliberation by the Information Security Committee. This approach seems very administratively burdensome.  

16. In any case, the Authority reminds that it is the draft law that must determine the categories of data that must be communicated to Sciensano for each of the pursued purposes. The determination of the categories of data processed for each purpose cannot therefore be made by the social security and health chamber of the Information Security Committee. Indeed, as the Authority has already pointed out, neither Article 8 of the ECHR, nor Article 22 of the Constitution, nor the GDPR, particularly Article 6.3, allow for such a "blank check." As the Authority has already emphasized above, any processing of personal data must be framed by sufficiently precise regulation, which must respond to an imperative social need and be a measure proportionate to the (legitimate) purpose pursued. This regulation must be precise and define, at a minimum, the essential elements of the processing, including the determined, explicit, and legitimate purposes; the (categories of) personal data that are relevant, adequate, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the pursued purposes; the maximum retention period for recorded personal data; and the designation of the data controller.  

17. This provision also indicates that any communication of personal data by Sciensano to third parties must also be subject to deliberation. Article 22 of the Constitution requires that the essential elements of data processing (and certainly when they are likely to present a high risk to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned, as in this case) be specifically framed by a legal or regulatory text (decree), and therefore not by a deliberation of the Information Security Committee. The draft law must itself determine who the "third parties" are to whom Sciensano may communicate the data it designates and the reasons for which this data will be communicated to them. Certainly in light of the quantity and sensitivity of the data in question (health-related data, data related to a presumption of infection following contact) and the possibility for their potential recipients to make cross-references between these different types of data. Article 4 § 3 of the draft law  

22. The Authority reiterates that it does not understand the objective of the obligation imposed on "All persons who have access to the database [to take] the necessary measures to ensure that the personal data recorded is processed confidentially and only for the purposes mentioned in Article 3." If this refers to staff members of Sciensano, or even to the staff of data recipients handling the data, the Authority reminds that this obligation to ensure the confidentiality of the data is already imposed on the data controller, Sciensano, which is responsible for passing it on to its staff members and its subcontractors. If the intention here is to impose an obligation on contact center agents who are under the hierarchical control of the competent regional health agencies, why refer to the three purposes listed in Article 3 (and not just the first one)? In any case, if the objective of this provision is to guarantee the confidentiality of the data, the draft law should impose on the data controller (Sciensano) the obligation to take concrete measures in terms of training, entering into confidentiality agreements, and monitoring compliance with the obligations they provide for.  

23. The Authority reminds that under Article 37.1 of the GDPR4, Sciensano, which is a "public institution sui generis endowed with legal personality"5, must designate a data protection officer. Furthermore, in light of the missions assigned to it, it seems that the obligation to designate a data protection officer is also imposed on it under Article 37.3 of the GDPR, which requires data controllers and processors to designate a data protection officer when "[their] core activities consist of large-scale processing of special categories of data referred to in Article 9 [including health data]." Therefore, Article 4 § 3, paragraph 2, of the draft law has no added legal value compared to Article 37 of the GDPR. This provision violates the prohibition of transcription of the GDPR6 and must therefore be removed.

25. To the extent that it is not established at all that a royal decree proclaiming "the end of the COVID-19 coronavirus epidemic" will be published, this wording does not allow for a certain determination of the moment at which this erasure will take place.  

26. In this specific case, it should be provided in the bill that the data will be erased as soon as they are no longer necessary for tracing purposes. Specifically,  

a. data concerning infected or presumed infected individuals should be deleted when these individuals have been contacted and have, or have not, according to their choice, provided the requested information to the contact center agents; and  
b. data concerning individuals who have been in contact with infected or presumed infected individuals should be deleted as soon as these individuals have been contacted and have received the necessary recommendations.  

27. The Authority also notes that nothing is provided for the data retention periods of data that have been transmitted "to the health inspection services of the regions." Is the silence of the bill explained by the existence of regional legislations (if so, which ones?) determining the data retention period for data held by these services in the context of initiatives aimed at combating the harmful effects caused by infectious diseases?

FOR THESE REASONS,  

The Authority notes that the main grievances expressed in its opinion on the draft royal decree have not been addressed. It insists once again that the bill be restructured and that it distinguishes, for each of the pursued purposes, the sources of the collected data, the categories of collected data, the data controllers, the data retention periods, the categories of recipients to whom the data may be communicated, and the circumstances under which and the reasons why they will be communicated to them. During this restructuring of the bill, which should improve its readability and predictability, the applicant should also ensure that the processing it regulates complies with the principles of necessity and proportionality.  

More fundamentally, the Authority notes that the bill, despite a wording that lacks clarity and coherence, seems to want to establish a contact tracing system but actually organizes, without apparent necessity, the centralization of an indefinite (and indefinable) amount of data (including sensitive data or data whose access is restricted by specific laws) within a database established and managed by an entity that does not make use of it. The bill does not create a sufficient legal framework to establish the exception to medical confidentiality necessary to allow the communication of health data by doctors to the database that is being created. The bill must also provide that the data it plans to record in the database can only be made accessible to the contact centers once a regional legal framework detailing precisely the uses that will be made of it has been established. The Authority also notes that it omits the definition of a whole series of essential elements for the Information Security Committee, which is not its competence, rather than providing for these elements in the law as required by the constitution, the European Convention on Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms, and the GDPR. The bill must be fundamentally revised to clarify its purpose (establishment and management of a database vs. effective contact tracing), to clearly and precisely define the purposes of the establishment of this database, and to define other essential elements (without referring to
