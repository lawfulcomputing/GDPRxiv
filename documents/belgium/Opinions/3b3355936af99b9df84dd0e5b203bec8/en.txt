1. INTRODUCTION  

1. This notice concerns the draft law regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data (hereinafter referred to as the Draft).  

2. It was officially submitted for opinion to the Commission for the Protection of Privacy (hereinafter, the Commission) on March 20, 2018.  

3. The Draft is part of the reform of the regulation on the protection of personal data following the adoption, on April 27, 2016, of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (hereinafter the GDPR) and Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of prevention, investigation, detection or prosecution of criminal offenses, and the execution of criminal penalties, and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Framework Decision 2008/977/JHA of the Council (hereinafter the Directive).  

4. These two texts entered into force on May 24, 2016, and May 5, 2016, respectively, and each provides for a transitional period of 2 years.  

5. Thus, the GDPR will be applicable in a few weeks, specifically on May 25, 2018. The Directive, for its part, must be transposed by May 6, 2018.  

6. The Secretariat of the Commission had the opportunity to make certain suggestions during the preliminary phase of the preparation of the Draft.  

The Commission nonetheless regrets having to provide its opinion on a text of such scope and importance for the regulation of personal data within an extremely short timeframe.  

7. The Commission hopes that this opinion, however critical it may be, will allow for an improvement of the text and remains available to the requester to collaborate in achieving this objective.  

8. Regarding the GDPR, notwithstanding its nature as a regulation, it provides for a number of scenarios in which the national legislator must or may, as the case may be, intervene, within the limits permitted by the opening clause.  

Notice 33/2018- 6/129  
9. These opening clauses are of several kinds. Some of them allow the legislator to specify the application of the GDPR, others to provide for enforcement measures within the limits of the options they foresee, and others still to provide for enforcement measures aimed at complementing the GDPR. In each case, these measures may not contravene the GDPR. They must be notified to the European Commission, for the most part by May 25, 2018.  

10. As for the Directive, it must be transposed within the legally prescribed timeframe, that is, by the aforementioned May 6, 2018. In general, the Commission emphasizes that, to a very large extent, the text of the Directive is aligned with that of the GDPR while taking into account the specificities related to the scope of the Directive, namely the processing of data carried out for the purposes of prevention and detection of criminal offenses, investigations, and prosecutions in this regard or the execution of criminal sanctions by the competent authorities.  

11. In its work of proposing and adopting the enforcement and transposition measures for these two European texts, the legislator is required to respect the philosophy underlying them, the orientation intended by the European framework, and, of course, the letter of these texts which take precedence from the perspective of the hierarchy of norms as well as the general principles of data protection and those of the rule of law.  

12. The legislator indicates that it does not limit itself to proposing enforcement or transposition measures for the aforementioned European texts, but also adds "specific provisions."  

13. Nevertheless, the framework for data protection in Belgium must be in compliance with European texts and form a coherent whole capable of guaranteeing effective protection of the fundamental right to data protection.  

Key Points of the GDPR and the Directive  
14. The key points of the EU reform of the data protection framework are recalled below. It is also against this backdrop that the Commission has examined the provisions set forth by the Draft and formulated this opinion:  

- Harmonization: Regarding the GDPR, the European legislator has chosen the legal instrument that allows for the strongest harmonization, namely a Regulation (GDPR) rather than a Directive as it had done in 1995. In this same perspective, it also opts for a long list of harmonized definitions. Finally, the European legislator opts for a common protection regime for the private and public sectors while allowing the national legislator the possibility to specify the application of the text for the public sector, within the limits of the GDPR.
Notice 33/2018- 7/129

- The strengthening of the rights of data subjects is central to the reform. These rights must allow data subjects real informational control and effective protection as soon as data processing concerning them is carried out. The right to information of the data subject, an indispensable prerequisite for the exercise of their other rights, is reinforced. The strengthening of this right contributes to the overall objective of increased transparency also embedded in the text. The right to erasure is developed as well as the right to restrict processing. In the same spirit, the requirements surrounding the use of consent as a basis for the lawfulness of data processing are also strengthened. Furthermore, this strengthening of rights also stems from certain new obligations introduced by the GDPR, such as data protection by design and data protection by default (Article 25 GDPR). Finally, the GDPR offers data subjects new instruments to take legal action – such as collective representation in the sense of Article 80 GDPR – with the aim of enhancing the legal protection of citizens and asserting the respect of their rights before courts and tribunals.

- Removal of certain prior formalities – Accountability: the removal of prior formalities with respect to the data protection authority goes hand in hand with the principle of accountability developed in the texts. The data controller is required to comply with the GDPR and must be able to demonstrate this compliance. The removal of the prior declaration of processing is emblematic of this paradigm shift. It was not removed lightly but rather because it did not, in practice, fulfill either the objectives of transparency or those of reflection and accountability that had been assigned to it in 1995.

- New obligations for data controllers and processors – Risk-based approach: new obligations are imposed on data controllers and processors such as maintaining a Record of Processing Activities (Article 30 GDPR), stricter regulation of the use of subcontracting and subsequent subcontracting (Article 28 GDPR), the designation of a Data Protection Officer (Article 37 GDPR), conducting a Data Protection Impact Assessment (Article 35 GDPR - DPIA), and notifying data breaches to the authority and to the data subject (Articles 33-34 GDPR). Some of these obligations only apply when a (high) risk is associated with the processing or the incident. These obligations are also designed to help data controllers and processors comply with the texts.

- Strengthening the powers of the data protection authority: the abandonment of most prior formalities with the supervisory authority and the principle of accountability is accompanied by a strengthening of the powers of the supervisory authority, particularly its...
Notice 33/2018- 8/129  
corrective powers, including the ability to require the suspension or prohibition of data processing and the imposition of deterrent administrative fines. A resolutely post-facto control action is expected from the supervisory authorities. Its role in raising public awareness in general and informing data controllers and processors about their obligations remains essential.

-  
Strengthening international cooperation: in a globalized context, the data processing carried out by multinational companies, particularly those established in several Member States of the Union, is increasingly numerous. Joint investigations as well as mutual assistance between data protection authorities of the European Union are framed by the GDPR, for more effective action regarding these actors. The choice of a regulation intended to guarantee harmonized protection in practice is also accompanied by the establishment of a mechanism known as "the Consistency Mechanism," under which data protection authorities must agree according to procedures established in the GDPR on a harmonized interpretation and application of the provisions of the GDPR. This cooperation will take place, starting from May 25, 2018, within the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), in which a designated national supervisory authority must sit.

15. The main lines of the Directive are similar to those of the GDPR, taking into account, as already indicated, the specificities of its scope of application.

intelligence and security services  
16. The Project aims to frame all data processing operations, including those that do not fall within the scope of European Union law. This is particularly the case for processing carried out by "intelligence and security services," namely by the State Security and the General Intelligence and Security Service. This framework is provided for in Title III of the Project. In these areas as well, there is an international consensus to strengthen the framework for data protection. Indeed, a project to modernize the Convention of January 28, 1981, for the protection of individuals with regard to the automated processing of personal data (STE 108) is on the table of the Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe. Although it has not yet been formally adopted by the States Parties, this text has received general agreement in substance. It incorporates in its own way (it is an international Convention and not a directly applicable regulation) the main lines recalled above that are found in the GDPR and the Directive.

Methodology of the notice  
17. From a methodological point of view, this notice generally follows the structure of the Project, Title by Title. With regard to each Title, the analysis is presented, according to the needs of the notice and the concerns raised by the Project in the eyes of the Commission, article by article in a detailed manner or in a more global manner. Where applicable, the analysis of each Title concludes with an overall assessment by the Commission on the Title and a list of remarks and requests addressed to the legislator. The analysis of Title 7 also covers other articles scattered elsewhere in the project.

18. The remarks made regarding Title 7 are essential in that they concern the effectiveness of the control by the data protection authorities established, including the future Data Protection Authority, the legal successor of the Commission.

2. GENERAL SYNTHESIS  

Preliminary Remark  

19. This synthesis is limited to listing the major concerns of the Commission, its main alert points. Even though it contains a number of references, reading this synthesis should be accompanied by a thorough reading of the notice in its entirety for a comprehensive assessment of the Project.  

Objective of the Project  

20. The Project is an ambitious text that aims to both implement the GDPR (Title I), transpose the Directive (Title II), frame data processing not covered by the EU data protection framework (Title III), and provide specific provisions. The Commission notes that since this is a Regulation, only the provisions for which the European legislator has left an opening for the national legislator can be supplemented by provisions of this draft, and this within the limits authorized, if applicable, by the GDPR. In its analysis, the Commission has been particularly attentive to this.  

21. As mentioned, the legislator also aims to add specific provisions. The Commission believes that not all of them belong in the Project. In any case, if they rely on concepts from the GDPR, they must respect its nature and objective.  

Scope and Definitions  

22. The analysis of the different Titles highlights the need to clarify their precise scope.  

23. The Commission welcomes the legislator's attempt to provide a solution in the text of the Project for situations of conflict of law that may arise (Article 4 of the Project). Indeed, since the GDPR provides in several articles for the possibility for the national legislator to specify or complement the European regulatory framework it proposes, the question of which national law will apply must find an answer that guarantees legal certainty. The Commission believes that the solution proposed by the Project is not sufficiently robust and does not provide a real solution. The Commission makes an alternative suggestion in this notice.  

Notice 33/2018- 11/129  
24. Regarding definitions, which contribute to the construction of a coherent framework, the Project provides that the definitions of the GDPR apply "without prejudice to the definitions provided in this law" (Article 5). This clause is confusing to the extent that it seems to contradict the primacy of international law for the titles to which the GDPR and the Directive apply. In this regard, the notice emphasizes that Title 4, dedicated to processing for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research, or for statistical purposes, introduces a series of definitions, some of which diverge from those of the GDPR. The Commission requests the removal of these definitions. In general, overall coherence must be preserved regarding definitions (including, to the extent possible, for the processing referred to in Title III that is not covered by Union law).  

The Commission does not oppose the introduction of new definitions that clarify a concept not defined by the GDPR and for which reference is made to national law, or when the definition of undefined terms introduced is indisputable and likely to enhance legal certainty.  

25. Regarding the creation of different "supervisory authorities," the Commission believes that their respective competencies must be clearly and legally coherently delineated to enable them to carry out their missions effectively and efficiently.  

Unjustified Restrictions or Those Contrary to the GDPR, on the Rights of Data Subjects  

26. The Commission notes that the Project contains numerous restrictions on the rights of data subjects, among which a large number appear unjustified and contrary to the GDPR. The Commission recalls, as it did in the introduction, that one of the major axes of the European reform is precisely the strengthening of these rights.  

27. Article 85.2 of the GDPR "Processing and freedom of expression and information" allows the national legislator to provide exemptions or derogations to Chapter III of the GDPR dedicated to the rights of data subjects if and to the extent that they are necessary to reconcile the right to the protection of personal data with the freedom of expression and information. The Commission believes that the limitations provided in Articles 18 (right to restriction of processing) and 21 (right to object) of the GDPR are unjustified since the balancing of the right to data protection and the freedom of expression and information does not require a derogation from the exercise of these rights. Regarding the right to erasure, the Commission opposes the assertion included in the explanatory memorandum that this right would not be applicable to processing for the purposes of freedom of expression and information.
Notice 33/2018- 12/129  
28. Article 89 of the GDPR "Guarantees and derogations applicable to processing for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes" also allows Member States to provide for derogations to the rights referred to in Articles 15, 16, 18, and 21, subject to the conditions and safeguards referred to in Article 89.1, to the extent that these rights would be likely to make it impossible or seriously hinder the achievement of the specific purposes and where such derogations are necessary to achieve these archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research, or statistical purposes. The Commission is also of the opinion that the derogations made by the Project to the rights of the data subjects in Title 4 are contrary to the GDPR.  

29. In addition to the aforementioned articles and the derogations provided as applicable regarding each of the rights in the article dedicated to them, Article 23 of the GDPR allows Member States to limit, through legislative measures, the scope of the rights provided for in Articles 12 to 22 of the GDPR. Such limitation must respect the essence of fundamental freedoms and rights and constitute a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society for one of the reasons listed in the said article, such as national security or national defense, to name just two of the ten reasons retained. Such legislative measure must contain at least the elements listed in Article 23.2 of the GDPR (including, in particular, the identification of the purposes of the processing, categories of personal data, the safeguards intended to prevent abuse, etc.). These elements are lacking in the provisions that rely on Article 23.1 of the GDPR in the Project. Therefore, the Commission is of the opinion that these derogations do not meet the requirements of Article 23.2 of the GDPR regarding the quality of the legislative measure authorizing the derogation.  

30. Regarding the processing of health-related, genetic, and biometric data, the Commission regrets that the Project does not rely on Article 9.4 of the GDPR to maintain the additional safeguards provided by the current Belgian legal framework and thus preserve a level of protection equivalent to that currently in place for the benefit of the protection of the data subjects.  

31. As for the remedies (Chapter VIII of the GDPR), which complement the rights of the data subjects, the Commission regrets that the legislator did not wish to provide the data subjects with effective indirect means of action that are nonetheless permitted by the GDPR by implementing Article 80.2 of the GDPR.  

32. This provision indeed gives Member States the possibility to provide that any body, organization, or association referred to in Article 80.1, regardless of any mandate conferred by a data subject, has, in the Member State in question, the right to lodge a complaint with the supervisory authority that is competent under Article 77, and to exercise the rights.

33. In accordance with Article 58.5 of the GDPR, each Member State shall provide by law that its supervisory authority has the power to bring any violation of the regulation to the attention of the judicial authorities and, where appropriate, to take legal action to enforce the regulation. To enable a legally valid introduction of such actions, the Commission believes it is essential that the legislator supplements the Law of December 3, 2017, with a provision specifying that it is the responsibility of the Management Committee of the DPA to decide on such actions. Still with the aim of enabling effective protection of data subjects, the Commission requests the legislator to provide for the possibility of introducing an action to prevent a serious breach of the regulation from occurring, modeled on the action provided for in Article 1 of the Law of January 12, 1993, concerning a right of action in environmental protection matters.  

36. Finally, the absence of administrative fines applicable to the public sector, even though Article 83.7 of the GDPR allows the legislator to "establish the rules determining whether and to what extent administrative fines may be imposed on public authorities and public bodies established in its territory," combined with the criminal immunity of certain public legal entities (Article 84 of the GDPR) and exemptions—some of which are criticized in this notice (see in particular Article 3 of the Draft)—places the public sector in a privileged position, breaking with the principle of equality with other data controllers and processors in the "private sector." While it is true that the Commission has other forms of corrective powers regarding the said public sector, the effectiveness of its action will necessarily be diminished.  

Compliance with Articles 5.2 of the GDPR and Article 24 of the GDPR  

37. The principle of accountability is at the heart of the GDPR. It is stated in Article 5.2 as follows: the data controller is responsible for compliance with paragraph 1 (principles relating to the processing of personal data) and is able to demonstrate that it is complied with (accountability).  

Notice 33/2018- 14/129  

38. Article 24 states that, taking into account a number of factors, including the nature, scope, purpose of the processing, as well as the risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals, the data controller implements appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is carried out in accordance with the GDPR.  

39. In several places in the Draft, provisions contravene this Article 24 of the GDPR (Article 3 of the Draft is particularly problematic in this regard). On the other hand, the Commission welcomes the establishment of a framework for the transmission of personal data by "federal public authorities and federal public bodies," through agreements to be adopted by the relevant data controllers, after consultation with their respective data protection officers (DPOs). This could constitute a good accountability tool for the relevant data controllers. However, the Commission believes that the system should be made mandatory only for processing that presents a risk, in line with the risk-based approach that underpins certain obligations of the GDPR. In addition to certain improvements that are proposed, these protocols should also be made public.  

Unjustified restrictions on the obligations of data controllers and processors  

40. The Draft provides in its Chapter dedicated to processing for journalistic purposes and for academic, artistic, or literary expression exemptions from compliance with certain obligations imposed by the GDPR in favor of data controllers and processors (provision of the Register of data processing to the data protection authority, obligation to cooperate with it, obligation to notify data breaches, obligation to consult the DPA for prior data protection impact assessments where it appears that the residual risk of the processing remains high, exemptions in the chapter on cross-border data flows).  

41. Regarding the exemption from the obligation to provide the Register of processing activities upon request from the DPA (Article 30.4 of the GDPR), the Commission does not consider it necessary and thus contrary to the GDPR; indeed, the GDPR does not require the Register to contain the identity of the data subjects affected by the data processing. The balancing of the two aforementioned fundamental rights does not justify this exemption. The same applies to Articles 33 (notification of data breaches) and 36 (prior consultation with the DPA for DPIAs). Under no circumstances should the execution of these obligations lead to disclosing to the data protection authority the content of a press article that is in the process of publication.

On additional obligations to those of the GDPR for data controllers and processors that contradict the underlying objective of these obligations in the GDPR  

42. Data Protection Officer - Article 37.4 of the GDPR allows national legislators to add cases in which the designation of a Data Protection Officer (DPO) is mandatory. Article 37.1 of the GDPR lists three scenarios in which this designation is mandatory under the GDPR. These three cases can therefore be supplemented at the discretion of the national legislator. The Commission notes that the two additional scenarios for the mandatory designation of a Data Protection Officer provided for in Articles 25 and 191 of the Draft diverge from the philosophy of the risk-based approach of the GDPR. Indeed, the scenarios for the mandatory designation of a Data Protection Officer in Article 37.1 – whose actions certainly contribute to the effective implementation of the GDPR - are linked to a risk factor (processing of sensitive data, monitoring of individuals' behavior, large scale, core activities). In contrast, the new scenarios provided in the Draft are justified solely by the purpose of carrying out processing for archival purposes in the public interest, or for research or statistics of any kind (Article 191 of the Draft) or the use of a processor for public authorities (Article 25 of the Draft targets all legal entities under private law acting as processors for the aforementioned public or private federal bodies and/or collecting personal data from them) even though they themselves are required to designate a Data Protection Officer. This latter circumstance does not exclude that the processor must also be designated if it falls under the cases referred to in Article 37.1 of the GDPR. However, the Commission believes that making this a generalized obligation is excessive.  

43. Record of processing activities - The Commission also notes that the obligation – at least as it understands the provisions of Title I in this regard - to participate in a centralized register of processing operations carried out in the public sector and made accessible to the data subject does not, strictly speaking, constitute an implementation measure of the GDPR. The objective of transparency is commendable in principle but cannot be conflated with the obligation to maintain an internal record of processing activities as provided for in Article 30 of the GDPR. This additional obligation provided by the Draft diverges from the objective of the Register, which is not intended to directly inform the data subjects and creates in the Commission a sense that the obligation for prior notification of data processing to the supervisory authority is somehow being reintroduced, even though its removal was unanimous and no longer aligns with the principle of accountability enshrined in the GDPR. Indeed, this prior notification obligation did not fulfill, in the opinion of all, either its objective of transparency towards the data subjects or that of raising awareness and translating it into concrete measures to comply with data protection rules among data controllers.

National supervisory authorities  

 The establishment of three additional Belgian DPAs  
45. 46. The Commission takes note of these political choices, which correspond in themselves to the principle that several DPAs can be designated in a Member State (cf. art. 51.1 of the GDPR and art. 41.1 of the Directive). 47. The Commission believes it is crucial to define a convincing distribution of competencies among the different Belgian DPAs. In this regard, it is advisable to avoid as much as possible common or overlapping competencies due to the legal uncertainty that arises from it and their extremely ineffective nature.

4.3.  
Chapter III – Rights of the Data Subject  

105.  
An introductory remark to begin with: the Commission recommends changing the title of this chapter and formulating it as follows "Limitations on the Rights of the Data Subject" since the current title does not correspond to the actual intent of this chapter. In this chapter, the applicant intends to implement Article 23 of the GDPR, which allows Member States to provide, within certain limits and for specific purposes, exceptions to the rights of the data subject.  

The basic principles that the legislator must consider when implementing Article 23 of the GDPR  
106.  
To outline the scope of the discretion available to the legislator in this regard, it is important to recall the case law of the Court of Justice regarding Article 13 of Directive 95/46/EC, which provided for a similar exception. In the Smaranda Bara ruling, the Court confirmed that these exceptions could only be introduced by "legislative measures"10. The Court had previously clarified that Member States could adopt these exceptions only to the extent that they are "necessary"11. Given the unchanged intention of the European legislator to achieve a high level of protection12, it follows that exceptions to the rights of data subjects must remain within the limits of what is strictly necessary13. The necessity and proportionality of the measures in question must therefore be interpreted restrictively.  

107.  
Article 23 of the GDPR allows for legally framed exceptions through a specific legislative intervention, where the necessity for one of the purposes of Article 23.1 of the GDPR is clearly demonstrated. In the Belgian legal order, Articles 64 and 65 of the law of September 18, 201714 provide a good example of sector-specific motivated exceptions that align with the underlying philosophy of Article 23 of the GDPR. The Commission notes that the applicant acknowledges this starting point in its Explanatory Memorandum, indicating in particular that these exceptions must be included in "a legislative provision" and "that they must be as precise as possible"15. The Explanatory Memorandum even states that a specific analysis must take place to balance the rights of the data subject and the operationality of the concerned services16.  

108.  
Finally, any legal measure providing for exceptions to the rights of the data subject must include at least the elements set out in Article 23.2 of the GDPR.  

109.  
The legal articles of this chapter are therefore confronted with these basic principles.  

Articles 10 and 11 of the draft  
110.  
Articles 10 and 11 of the draft provide for a very broad and general exception to all the rights of the data subject that can be invoked by public administrations with competencies to detect, prosecute, and investigate criminal offenses. The Explanatory Memorandum mentions several non-exhaustive examples: social inspection services, economic inspection, inspection services within the Federal Public Service Finance, municipalities, etc. This exception would also apply to "other public administrations […] that have […] a need for a special regime"17. This special regime would be necessary because "a generalized belief circulates that the [GDPR] would be too strict" and to "maintain a certain flexibility for the public sector"18. The Commission believes that through this "generalized belief," the applicant undermines the GDPR. Therefore, Articles 10 and 11 of the draft do not pass the test of Article 23.2 of the GDPR.  

111.  
According to Article 23.2.a) and 23.2.e) of the GDPR, legislative measures must specify the categories of data controllers as well as the purposes. The joint reading of paragraphs 1, 2, and 3 of Article 10 of the draft does not allow for the precise identification of the data controllers who can invoke these exceptions. In particular, Article 10, § 2 violates this requirement by referring vaguely to any entity pursuing an objective within the meaning of Article 23.1.e) of the GDPR. This article does not implement the GDPR but instead creates a misleading standard that gives the impression that any public entity can invoke Article 23.1.e) of the GDPR to arbitrarily limit the rights of the data subject. Article 10 also does not specify the pursued objectives since the draft simply refers to Article 23.1 of the GDPR, without describing the legal tasks and missions of the concerned services.

Thus, the Commission noted in its opinion on Article 3, § 7 of the LVP that the exclusion of the right to rectification or deletion could lead to the tax authorities establishing their taxation based on inaccurate data or data whose processing is prohibited. The legislator must, within the framework of the applicable sectoral legislation, carry out similar assessments that allow for a distinct justification of the necessity of suspending each of the rights based on the missions of the service concerned. Therefore, the Commission does not understand why it would be necessary to systematically provide for an exception to Article 34 of the GDPR.

117.  
The Commission therefore considers that Articles 10 and 11 of the draft essentially embody a version stripped of meaning of the current Article 3, § 7 of the LVP to which an indeterminate number of unidentified public services could also resort – probably even for an indefinite duration. The attempt to gather a maximum number of public administrations under a single exception inevitably implies that the applicant has not been able to carry out the necessary balancing of interests between the specific needs of each service on the one hand and the rights of the data subject on the other. Contrary to what Article 11, § 4 of the draft suggests, it is primarily the responsibility of the legislator to create a proper balance between these conflicting interests in its implementing provisions of the GDPR. This paragraph therefore has no added value.

General conclusion for Chapter III of Title I  

145.  
In summary, all the exceptions listed in Chapter III of Title I are characterized by a great imprecision regarding a) who can invoke the exception; b) for what purposes; c) concerning which data; and d) for what duration. Moreover, there is a lack of sufficient legal guarantees – or references to existing guarantees – that must provide protection against arbitrary limitations on the rights of the individual concerned. Legal provisions with such imprecise scope not only do not meet the requirements of Article 23 of the GDPR but are also not reasonably foreseeable for the litigant in the sense of Article 8 of the ECHR due to their ambiguity. For all these reasons, the Commission's opinion on the entirety of Chapter III of Title I is unfavorable.

REMARKS REGARDING TITLE 2  

194.  
Title 2 transposes the Police and Justice Directive into Belgian law. It concerns the processing of personal data "by competent authorities for the purposes of prevention and detection of criminal offenses, investigations and prosecutions in this matter or enforcement of criminal sanctions, including protection against threats to public security and the prevention of such threats" (Article 1.1. of the Police and Justice Directive).  

195.  
The Police and Justice Directive does not apply to activities of processing personal data that do not fall within the scope of Union law nor to processing carried out by the institutions, bodies, and agencies of the Union (Article 1.3. of the Police and Justice Directive). Consequently, this Directive does not apply to the processing of personal data in the context of ensuring national security and defense, which are governed separately under Title 3.  

196.  
The distinction between the GDPR and the Police and Justice Directive mainly lies in a limitation of transparency obligations and the rights of the data subject, such as indirect access to personal data, the absence of a right to object or a right to erasure, and the absence of a right to data portability. Furthermore, no binding system of administrative sanctions is imposed either.

Delimitation in the context of the purpose: scope  

197.  
Article 32 of the Draft restates the purpose as described in Article 1.1. of the Police and Justice Directive. As indicated in Article 1.1. of the Police and Justice Directive, the personal scope is limited to "competent authorities." According to the Police and Justice Directive, this refers broadly to "any competent public authority" for the purpose described in Article 1.1. or "any other body or entity to which the law of a Member State assigns the exercise of public authority and public power prerogatives" for this purpose. Article 31.7.a) to g) of the Draft refers to the following entities:  

a) regular police services;  
b) judicial authorities, understood as courts and tribunals and the public prosecutor's office;  
c) the investigation service of the Committee P;  
d) the General Inspectorate of the Federal Police and local police;  
e) the General Administration of Customs and Excise, in the context of its mission related to the investigation, detection, and prosecution of offenses;  
f) the Passenger Information Unit, and  
g) the Financial Information Processing Unit.  

198.  
Regarding the material scope, the Explanatory Memorandum emphasizes that the Draft must be applied restrictively in order to best align with the personal scope of the Police and Justice Directive. According to the Explanatory Memorandum, this implies that other "administrative" authorities are not considered a competent authority within the meaning of this title, even if they are "competent for the control, inspection, or prosecution of criminal offenses." If the processing activities of these administrative authorities do not fall within the purpose of the Police and Justice Directive, the GDPR applies.  

199.

While efforts are made to find points of reference with the purpose of the Police and Justice Directive, it does not seem to be the case for all designated competent authorities. Thus, in Article 31.7.c) of the Draft, the Investigation Service of the Committee P is considered a "competent authority," "in the context of its judicial missions." The Committee P indeed has a dual mission, namely an administrative mission and a judicial mission, so that the mission

7. TITLE 4: PROCESSING FOR ARCHIVAL PURPOSES IN THE PUBLIC INTEREST, FOR SCIENTIFIC OR HISTORICAL RESEARCH, OR FOR STATISTICAL PURPOSES  

General Remarks  
255. The GDPR recognizes the societal interest in science, just as Directive 95/46/EC explicitly did. Regarding processing, Recital 113 of the Regulation states that "For scientific or historical research purposes or for statistical purposes, the legitimate expectations of society regarding the advancement of knowledge should be taken into account." The GDPR aims for a balance between the freedom of research and the protection of privacy.  

The Requirement for Appropriate Safeguards  
256. Article 89.1 of the GDPR clearly emphasizes that "Processing for archival purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes is subject, in accordance with this Regulation, to appropriate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the data subject." Researchers, statisticians, and archivists must comply with the Regulation and, like any other data controller, establish the necessary safeguards. Article 89.1 of the GDPR encourages data controllers to pay particular attention to pseudonymization and the further processing of anonymized data - where possible.  

Derogations from the Rights of Data Subjects: Limits  
257. Article 89.2-3 of the GDPR allows Member States to establish derogations from certain rights in the context of processing personal data for archival purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes. These legal derogations are permitted "to the extent that these rights would likely make it impossible or seriously impede the achievement of the specific purposes and where such derogations are necessary to achieve those purposes." The obligations of transparency are governed by Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR:  
-  
Article 13 governs the obligation of transparency in cases where a controller has collected personal data directly from the data subject and wishes to further process it for a new purpose. Article 13.3 of the GDPR states that "the controller shall provide the data subject with information regarding this other purpose and any other relevant information referred to in paragraph 2." Article 13 of the GDPR does not contain an exception for processing carried out for research purposes.

In principle, the controller must inform the data subject before proceeding with further processing for a purpose other than that for which the personal data were obtained (Article 14.4 of the GDPR). Gap: administrative competence for the decision to take legal action  
404. Both the DPA law and the draft bill lack a provision specifying which body of the DPA is competent to make the decision to take legal action. The question of who has the competence to make this decision cannot therefore simply be resolved in the internal regulations, as these have purely internal value and have no legal effect with respect to third parties. This question must therefore be resolved by law. A law that creates a state body must include a provision that specifies which body of that organization is competent to make the decision to take legal action (regardless of who will represent the organization in court).

Furthermore, the Commission draws attention to the fact that the competence of the R Committee in Article 130, § 1, first paragraph of the Project may potentially be interpreted quite broadly. The R Committee should indeed be responsible for "(…) the oversight of the processing of personal data carried out by the authorities and persons referred to in Article 109, first paragraph." Article 109, first paragraph of the Project lists the authorities involved in granting/revoking authorizations, certificates, and security opinions as referred to in the law of December 11, 1998144. These authorities also carry out other data processing, and to prevent the R Committee from being considered as the competent DPA for these processes as well, it may be advisable to formulate Article 130, § 1, first paragraph of the Project as follows: "(…) the oversight of the processing of personal data within the framework of Article 109, first paragraph, carried out by the authorities and persons referred to in that same article."  

D. Conclusion: unfavorable opinion  

Given the problems outlined above, the Commission issues an unfavorable opinion regarding the provisions of the Project concerning the three new DPAs145, given that:  
• the distribution of competencies among the 4 federal DPAs is governed in a chaotic and completely illogical manner (see above, point B);  
• the reform of the C.O.C. does not go far enough and is, in certain respects, not compliant with European rules (see above, point C.a. and below, point E).

The first proposal from the Commission is immediately the simplest and most effective. It is also the solution that has been favored in almost all other European countries. One could indeed choose not to create additional DPAs and thus grant the DPA full competencies. This would completely avoid the aforementioned problems. This proposal would also have the advantage that the oversight tasks – and thus also the expertise – in data protection would be centralized within a single organization. In this hypothesis, however, an appropriate solution would still need to be found for the existing C.O.C., which, as mentioned above, is nonetheless caught in a bind between the tasks and competencies of the Commission and those of Committee P.  

463.  
The Commission also sees a second possible solution, which it believes could also be operational, provided it is properly implemented: to assign to the DPA only the oversight of processing activities under the GDPR (and the national implementing provisions of the GDPR) and to create a second DPA competent for all other data processing, namely (mainly) the processing referred to in Article 2, second paragraph, and Titles 2 & 3 of the Project, with the exception of processing by judicial authorities.
