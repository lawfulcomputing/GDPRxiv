Director of the Agency for Personal Data Protection and Vice-President of the European Data Protection Board, Zdravko Vukić, participated in a professional panel discussion at the Privacy Symposium in Venice, during which he addressed one of the most important issues in the field of development and use of artificial intelligence – how to ensure lawful processing of personal data throughout the entire lifecycle of artificial intelligence systems.  
He emphasized that, regardless of whether it is at the design, development, or application stage of artificial intelligence, it is essential to identify a lawful basis for the processing of personal data. The principles from Article 5 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) fully apply to the processing of personal data in artificial intelligence systems, including the principles of lawfulness, fairness, purpose limitation, and data minimization.  
In his presentation, he particularly highlighted that data controllers must, if they wish to base the processing of personal data on legitimate interest, conduct a structured three-part test to demonstrate their legitimate interest.  
This specifically means that data controllers must demonstrate: that the interest is lawful, specific, and real, not speculative; that the processing of personal data is strictly necessary to achieve that interest; that this interest is not overridden by the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  
In accordance with the principle of data minimization, during the development and application of artificial intelligence models, the personal data used must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purpose of processing. This may include the processing of personal data to reduce the risk of potential biases and errors when such purpose is clearly and precisely defined, and the personal data is necessary for that purpose (e.g., when it cannot be effectively achieved by processing other data, including synthetic or anonymized data). The Agency strongly encourages the use of Privacy-Enhancing Technologies (PETs), such as synthetic data, federated learning, and differential privacy. These tools represent key mechanisms for reducing risks and ensuring compliance with data protection principles.  
When assessing whether the purpose is legitimate, specific, and explicit, and whether the processing is in line with the principle of data minimization, it should be particularly considered that different phases within the development and application of artificial intelligence models may represent the same or different processing activities and may involve successive data controllers or joint data controllers.  
In some cases, it is possible to determine the purpose that will be achieved during the application of the artificial intelligence model already in the early stages of development. Even when this is not the case, the specific context of future application must be at least partially known, and consideration should be given to how that context shapes the purpose of development.  
When reviewing the purpose of processing at a certain stage of development, the Agency will expect information from the data controller about the type of artificial intelligence model being developed, its expected functionalities, and all other relevant circumstances known at that stage of development.  
The context of application may include, for example, information about whether the model is being developed for internal use, whether the data controller plans to sell or distribute the model to third parties after development, and whether the purpose of the model is primarily research or commercial.  
Director Vukić concluded that the lawful use of personal data in artificial intelligence systems is important for the protection of the right to privacy and the right to personal data protection, but also for the protection of other fundamental rights. Unlawful processing can also lead to violations of other fundamental rights, including the rights to non-discrimination, the rights to freedom of expression, the rights to a fair trial, and even the right to life. The aim of data protection rules is not to prevent innovation but to protect the rights and freedoms of all individuals, especially vulnerable groups and children, in a rapidly evolving digital environment.
