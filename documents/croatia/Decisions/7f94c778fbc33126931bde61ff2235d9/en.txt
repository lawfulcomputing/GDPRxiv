In the broadcast of the Mayor on the business radio PoslovniFM, the Head of the Department for Supervision, Investigations, and Protection of Respondents' Rights, Mario Milner, was a guest discussing video surveillance in residential buildings. Many co-owners of buildings are still unsure when they are allowed to install video surveillance in the building, what they are allowed to record, and who is allowed to access the recordings from the video surveillance system. Listen to the show hosted and edited by Tin Bašić for answers to these and many other questions.

– Complete protection of personal data, that is, 100 percent, is not possible. The GDPR aims to empower citizens' rights to state what is or is not allowed. However, of course, to pay the price of some security and comfort, some rights are limited by certain regulations. One of those rights is restricted by the Postal Services Act, which mandates that every residential building must be marked and clearly specifies what is necessary for delivery to take place, emphasizes Milner.
– Fortunately, we can say that, regarding the number of reports, the issues in buildings have decreased. At the beginning of the application of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), we frequently received reports from residents, or respondents, related to the public disclosure of data on notice boards, such as announcements about debts. These notice boards are often located in some busy parts of buildings shared with hospitality establishments. Of course, we also have issues related to video surveillance, which is specifically regulated by the Law on the Implementation of the General Regulation. These are the two most common issues we receive, related to multi-apartment buildings, said Milner. Although, he notes, the issue of video surveillance is not only characteristic of residential buildings.

Video surveillance is very important for buildings, that is, for co-owners. It is clear that the decision to establish video surveillance is made by a two-thirds majority, and that regulations and procedures must be satisfied. However, representatives often say they have video surveillance and that co-owners ask to view those recordings because something has happened.
– We will clarify the meaning of this provision, and it will be much clearer. The purpose of the GDPR is to prevent abuse. Let’s say, theoretically, we have a not-so-large building, and three or four co-owners have access to the recordings, but all co-owners are responsible for that data processing. Thus, the co-ownership community is the data controller. It is not just the representative of the co-owners; he is merely the person authorized to view the recordings. There is a practice when video surveillance is introduced, co-owners make a decision at a meeting by a two-thirds majority. That is a legal requirement. They authorize the representative of the co-owners or someone else, a person who may be close to them, who has technical knowledge, to have access to the recordings. The purpose of the legal provision is not to limit someone's rights but to protect citizens, asserted Milner in the podcast Mayor, in a conversation with editor Tin Bašić.

Co-owners, for example, voted and have video surveillance in common areas, meaning everything that is common can be recorded. However, now each of them wants to see the recording – are they allowed?
According to current regulations, they are not allowed to do so unless it is clearly indicated in the decision made and there is a reason to view the recording, asserted Milner and Bašić, who is also a representative of the co-owners of his building.
– We also have video surveillance of public areas, workplaces, family homes, and this issue of video surveillance is quite common. It may not be important for residential buildings, but it is important to note the application of appropriate organizational and technical measures. This means that every data controller who processes personal data must take care of the risks that this processing entails and implement adequate organizational and technical measures. The most common data controllers are business entities who often think that some risk will not happen to them, so they do not apply these measures because they believe that financially it may not be profitable to introduce some safer technical solution, explains Milner. He noted that the General Regulation obliges every controller, or processor, to enable certain rights to the respondent, that is, the person whose personal data is being processed, whether it is the right to access, modify, or object to the processing of personal data. This may, he believes, have somehow remained overshadowed by the hype around the GDPR and all obligations, so we see that data controllers have not fully complied.

Who are the data controllers?
– Given that our topic is related to residential buildings, I will try to give such examples. The data controller is not the representative of the co-owners. The data controllers are all co-owners of the residential building, those who paid for the video surveillance with their money. If you are the owner of an apartment and record your apartment, then you, as an individual, are the data controller. Regarding residential buildings, the data controller is all co-owners of the residential building if they jointly made that decision. During the proceedings of the Data Protection Agency, we communicate with the representative of the co-owners since, based on a special law, he is designated to represent the co-ownership community, emphasizes Milner.

Thus, the representatives of the co-owners, or all co-owners, are the 'data controllers.' The processor, in this specific case, would be a company that processes personal data. This does not mean that if you hire a company to set up video surveillance and it never comes back to the building, does not service the system, and has no access to the data, that it is the processor. The processor would be a company, for example, a security company, that deals with this and has access to those recordings, can connect to your camera, retrieve recordings, and extract recordings.

When asked whether the Data Protection Agency will impose penalties immediately upon receiving a report or if there is a procedure that first orders the removal of deficiencies and if that is not done, then...
and are punished, Milner states that in six years of applying the General Regulation and the Act on the Implementation of the General Regulation, representatives of co-owners and co-owners have not issued a single monetary fine.  
– We always opt for some milder measure. Given that high fines are prescribed, representatives of co-owners, or co-owners designated to take care of the video surveillance system, always cooperate, and we can say that in most cases it is perhaps a misunderstanding, ignorance, that there is no violation of regulations that would indicate intent or abuse; these are rare cases, Milner says.  
Editor Bašić mentioned that they often receive questions on the portal ZGRADOnačelnik.hr about what is allowed and what is not. Namely, it is clear that, for example, a list of snow removal or debts cannot be published on the notice board in the entrance, as it actually contains the names and surnames of co-owners; this data can be sent to co-owners via email if requested. Or printed and placed in the mailbox. It is actually very clear that names and surnames should never be published in the hallways. However, surnames or names and surnames can be published on mailboxes and intercoms. He asked the guest to explain the difference as to why the former is not allowed, while the latter is.  
– We must distinguish between two things. One is that probably the president of the co-owners or the building manager publishes someone’s data, and the other is that you, as the owner, mark your own apartment. Thus, you as the owner can publicly disclose your data, and GDPR does not prevent you from doing so. In order to publish the data of another person, you must have some lawful purpose and a legal basis. There are several types of legal bases – for example, it can be a legitimate interest or consent. Consent must be demonstrable, meaning there must be someone’s signature. For instance, at some meeting, co-owners signed that “I, as a co-owner in this part, agree that my name appears on the notice board.” Thus, that is one legal basis. If you have not taken that step, some other legal basis would have to apply, which in this case actually does not exist. Therefore, the publication of names and surnames on notice boards is not a priori prohibited, but measures must be taken that present the least possible risk for the subjects (in this case, co-owners, editor's note), Milner explained. He added that the entire General Regulation on the Protection of Personal Data (GDPR) speaks about some risk assessment. Thus, if co-owners believe that the publication of names and surnames on the notice board does not pose any risk to them, they will give their consent for publication, asserted Milner.  
The editor added that this is only for those who have given consent, not for those who have not.  
The conversation shifted back to video surveillance, specifically recorded materials.  
In response to the editor's assertion that every co-owner can actually view the recordings and asked whether it follows that any co-owner can watch the recording at any time or if there must be some specific event for that – for example, a break-in or some damage, Milner replied that the purpose of video surveillance is the protection of persons and property.  
– One of the obligations when establishing video surveillance is an automated recording system, so-called logs. To review recordings where everyone is present, someone is walking around, you are intruding into someone’s privacy, there must be a justified reason. The agency can easily determine when someone accessed the recordings (logs), why they accessed them, and whether there was a harmful event. You must prove that this data, these recordings are used for that purpose. You will not be able to prove it if someone was playing in the entrance because that is not a harmful event; there must be records of contact with the police, minutes, and so on. Thus, you cannot prove the purpose for which the recording was viewed. You have used video surveillance outside of its purpose, and you are already in violation, Milner said, adding that this provision aims to prevent co-owners from finding themselves in some unprotected situation. For example, if some damage occurs, such as damage to a vehicle, what specifically can you do other than call the police?  
For instance, we have video surveillance in a multi-apartment building. Children are playing in the entrance and making noise, perhaps running around the entrance, banging on people's apartment doors, knocking, ringing the bell. We have a decision that all co-owners may view the video recording, not just those who signed the decision on the installation of video surveillance. And Ms. Marica or Mr. Toni comes and says that the kids bang on the doors every day. Is that a justified reason to view the recording?  
– The purpose is, I repeat, the protection of persons and property. When we talk about the protection of persons, it is the protection of their rights and freedoms. It is not just protection from immediate danger. If some events occur unlawfully, if we have a decision on house rules, if someone rings the bell once, would we define that as a violation? To define it as a violation, there must be a breach of some regulation. We are not talking about, for example, a loud conversation in the hallway of the building because loud talking is not prohibited. There must be some unlawfulness of certain actions. If someone is banging on the door, you may reasonably suspect that someone is attacking you because you live alone. There must be some justified reason. It is difficult to prescribe all situations because they depend on the subjective experience of the person. But if someone says – some kids are banging a ball in the entrance, they hit the ball five or ten times, that is not a justified reason to view the recording – Milner is clear. He emphasized that they have many reports where representatives of co-owners abuse that right, so AZOP through the log system sees when recordings were viewed, why, and what happened.  
– The purpose of the General Regulation is to protect the rights of the subjects. The General Regulation does not require any individual to do something, but it requires the data controller to do so. In this case, the co-owners are the controllers, so if we give rights to co-owners as data controllers, we jeopardize their other rights based on which they are subjects. Each of those co-owners is present in those recordings, so we recommend that video surveillance and viewing of recordings be used for justified purposes and that access to those recordings be limited to one or two persons.
**Maximum.** We recommend to managers and representatives of co-owners that we have encountered the least problems in those solutions where storage systems are in a locked cabinet, so that recordings are not located on a co-owner's computer. The system that stores video recordings must be additionally protected. If you have obtained consent for such a method of storage, it should be noted that this computer is likely used by your household members, children – advises Milner.  
He also recommends that the storage of video recordings be placed in a common room that is secured so that not everyone has access, that it is protected in a special cabinet, and that the key is held by an authorized person, that there is some record of usage, and that it is clearly stipulated when that room and recording will be used, how the recordings will be reviewed, and under what conditions.  
– The point is, I repeat, not to limit someone's rights, but to prevent abuses. We know what co-ownership relationships are like; sometimes they are ideal, sometimes they are not, so it is difficult to predict all circumstances. The authorizations that someone has in accessing the recordings carry responsibility, not just for one but for all co-owners. Penalties for abuse are up to 6,700 euros. That is a significant cost; that money can be spent in a much smarter way. I would also note that the installation of video surveillance is a legal possibility. If video surveillance is truly unnecessary for the building, the AZOP can prohibit such video surveillance, – says Milner.  
This means, the editor asked, that co-owners can decide on the installation of video surveillance in the building, but if there is no need for video surveillance, the AZOP can prohibit it for that building?  
– There is always a procedure to follow. It always starts with the least invasive method. The agency does not assess someone's safety or whether someone feels safe or not. We have guidelines issued by the expert subgroup of the European Data Protection Board that clearly state that video surveillance is one of the protective measures at the very end of the list, not at the beginning. Now, it is another matter that video surveillance is very accessible, very cheap, and very effective. What does that influence the co-ownership community to decide on the installation of video surveillance? There is probably some reason for it, and they can probably explain it to us, but it is not an absolute right. It is a legitimate interest that needs to be proven – Milner is clear. He adds that when two-thirds of co-owners say they want to install video surveillance, they must also prove the need for that video surveillance.  
– So, it is not enough to decide "because we said so," but there must be an explanation. For example, in a building with 10 co-owners, two can say that video surveillance is unnecessary because there is no reason for it; they can file a complaint with the AZOP, and their request can even be accepted? That is one of the possibilities; it is not likely, but it is a possibility. Therefore, the legal basis is a legitimate interest, and a complaint can be made against that legitimate interest, and the data controller must prove that right – emphasizes Milner.  
**Spyhole ante portas**  
After discussing video surveillance, the editor asked Milner if a co-owner can install a "spyhole" in their apartment door that is actually a camera? Does that camera record somewhere or just show the situation in real-time in front of the apartment? When is it activated? There are many questions that a lot of people ask.  
– The answer is both yes and no, and now I will explain in more detail. Our Law on the Implementation of the General Regulation more clearly defines certain chapters of personal data processing, and one of those is related to the processing of personal data through video surveillance, defining what video surveillance is, and here we "circle" around the recording. Video surveillance is a system for collecting certain data; it must be intended to do or is intended to do. If there is a possibility, whether that system is connected to the internet or you can insert a memory card into it. It is important to emphasize that it can constitute a storage system, i.e., recording that can be searched. Therefore, if it were a spyhole that only has a screen on the other side, is not connected to anything, and does not store or have any capability to store data, that is not video surveillance in the sense of our laws. Thus, if it is not video surveillance in the sense of our laws, the provisions of that law do not apply – explained Milner.  
The editor gave an example, saying that if a person is at work, their child is at home, someone rings the doorbell, and that person sees the image on their mobile phone of the person ringing?  
– And by the legal definition of video surveillance, that is video surveillance. You do not have to store recordings; it is important that you have that possibility. This practice has also been confirmed by the court, so it is a confirmed practice in court decisions. Technology has become so accessible that it seems normal to us, but it is questionable how much they comply with the legal framework for the protection of personal data, Milner explained. He pointed out that they have received such reports and generally prohibited such systems. A compromise solution is if that camera records directed and exclusively what is owned by the person, for example, only their apartment or only the entrance to their apartment.  
– Therefore, if such a camera records exclusively in front of someone's apartment, we can say that it is justified, that it is something used for protection. It is not justified and is prohibited by law to record the entire hallway without the consent of the co-owners. If you were to place such a camera on the door frame and record exclusively your door, the camera will only cover the necessary part in front of your apartment, that is justified. Therefore, it is necessary to position the camera so that the common part of the building is not visible – emphasizes Milner. He notes that care must be taken regarding what that camera can record.  
– If at some point you place a camera, at one moment it is directed at the door, and the next moment somewhere else, that is a different situation. In the case of family houses, for example, the problem is that on the market you have rotating cameras, motion detectors, and when we come to inspect, we will check the recording, and it may happen that the camera turned and recorded a public area. And you are already in violation – says Milner. He emphasizes that these are not simple matters and that in their practice, such combinations are most often prohibited because they are very
It is difficult to ensure compliance with regulations.  
– Some have cameras with which they record their parking, thus capturing public areas as well. Regardless of where that person's vehicle is parked, they are not a security guard, they are not a police officer, and they are not someone responsible for regulating anything in public areas. We prohibit such actions. Of course, in practice, another problem arises; we prohibit the processing of personal data, that is, recording, without mandating the removal of those cameras. For example, the misdemeanor law recognizes the seizure of items used to commit an offense. We conduct an administrative procedure, we prohibit the processing of data, yet in practice, the cameras can remain where they were. In such cases, we carry out compliance checks, we come for inspections unannounced and see whether the decision we issued has been complied with. If it has not been complied with, then we enter the realm of punishment – explains Milner.  
He emphasizes that they conduct the procedure according to administrative law, while the misdemeanor law provides certain powers that may be easier to enforce, and the bodies acting under the Misdemeanor Law have greater powers. This is not just a problem in Croatia but also in other member states and beyond.  
For example, in North Macedonia, we assisted in transposing the GDPR into their legal system. One thing we highlighted for them was precisely regarding video surveillance. They have the authority to order someone to remove a camera. As long as we do not have such a legal provision, we conduct supervision after we issue a decision. This supervision is quick and efficient – whether you have or have not complied with the decision. If you have not complied, you are in the zone of punishment – says Milner.  
– The right to process personal data through video surveillance is limited to the protection of individuals and property in the sense of this law. Of course, there are other laws, such as the Law on the Protection of Financial Institutions, which obliges certain institutions to have video surveillance. However, we are talking about the sphere of multi-apartment buildings, private individuals. You have the right to protect yourself in the area of your property, your real estate. The protection zone can be the boundary of your possessions. The law prohibits recording public areas; there is no justified reason for that. It is the same as if you were to run a red light in traffic and say you have a justified reason. You do not. It is prohibited, and you cannot justify it with any reason. Just like driving under the influence of alcohol, it is prohibited. The legislator has stated that private individuals are not authorized to record public areas; this is prohibited by law, and you cannot find a justified reason to do so, so do not record – concluded Milner in the podcast Zgradonačelnik.
