The question of the legality of processing personal data is crucial for compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation and is governed by Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation.
Anyone processing personal data must ensure the existence of one of the six legal bases for processing prescribed by Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation. If the data controller processes special categories of data, they must, in addition to the legal basis from Article 6, also ensure one of the exceptions prescribed by Article 9(2) of the General Data Protection Regulation.
Essentially, whenever a data controller asks whether they may process personal data, for example, to store, delete, or possibly disclose data to a third party, the answer lies in the existence of a legal basis for the processing of personal data. This is also related to compliance with one of the principles of the General Data Protection Regulation – the principle of legality.

Consent
Initially, it should be emphasized that consent does not take precedence over other legal bases but is an equivalent legal basis for the processing of personal data.
Moreover, an organization as a data controller should first check if there is another legal basis for processing data, for example, in the form of legal obligations from laws or contracts, and only in the absence of another basis should they rely on consent, precisely because it is necessary to allow data subjects to withdraw their consent at any time in a valid and simple manner, and it must be ensured that the data subject can withdraw consent at any time without affecting the lawfulness of processing prior to the withdrawal.
For the above reasons, we recommend that organizations (data controllers) carefully consider whether consent is the most appropriate legal basis for processing and, if so, whether the consent meets all the conditions prescribed by the General Data Protection Regulation.
The General Data Protection Regulation sets high standards for valid consent.
Namely, consent must be given by a clear affirmative action, must be voluntary, specific, and unambiguous, based on information that allows individuals to have a certain control over their personal data.
Consent must be a free choice, and the question of free choice must not depend on the existence of any negative consequences for the individual in case they do not agree to the processing.
For example, in the execution of a contract and the assessment of the legality of processing based on consent for data that is not necessary for the fulfillment of the contract, care must be taken to determine whether, among other things, the execution of the contract, including the provision of the service, is conditioned on consent to the processing of personal data that is not necessary for the execution of that contract.
On the other hand, voluntary consent may be questioned if there is a significant imbalance between the organization and the individual. For example, the processing of personal data in the context of an employment relationship where it is considered that the employee is in a "weaker position" compared to the employer because they are in a certain dependent, disadvantaged position.
Therefore, in most cases, it is not recommended to base the processing of employee data on consent, as it is not uncommon for an employee to consent to certain processing of personal data simply because they fear negative consequences, for example, fear of dismissal if they do not consent to certain data processing or concern about mistreatment by the employer in case of non-consent.
Consent must be specific, meaning that the processing and purpose for which it is given must be sufficiently specified to allow individuals greater control over their personal data. The data controller must pay attention to the granularity in obtaining consent and clearly separate information related to obtaining consent from any other important information.
We can say that the granularity of consent is important for the individual's ability to distinguish the specificity of the purpose of processing (the reasons for processing) for which they give their consent, and in this way, the data controller appropriately manages the reasonable expectations of the data subjects and indirectly meets the requirement for transparency of processing.
Greater control in separating consent (if there are multiple) and providing specific consent for specific purposes, in addition to informing about the processing for which consent is given, reflects the actual wishes of the data subjects.
The separation of processing purposes can be achieved by leaving a blank box in front of each processing purpose so that the individual can check the box if they wish.
Finally, the data controller must inform individuals about the processing. Some of the information that should be provided includes who the data controller is, the purposes of processing, information about the type of data, information about the right to withdraw consent, information about the use of data for automated decision-making, information about potential risks of data transfer, appropriate safeguards, etc. In this regard, the information must be provided in clear and simple language that any average individual can understand and must be easily accessible.
Regarding the consent of a child, we emphasize that in the Republic of Croatia it is stipulated that only a legally capable person can create legal effects with their own expressions of will and that a natural person acquires legal capacity upon reaching the age of majority.
**Legal Document Translation**

**Capacity**  
Instead of a person who lacks legal capacity, their legal representative or guardian will express their will.  
The Family Law stipulates that the content of parental care includes the right and duty to represent the child's personal and property rights and interests.  
Therefore, in the case of consent from a minor child, consent for the processing of their personal data is given by their parent or legal representative, unless it concerns the provision of information society services directly to the child.

**Contract**  
If there is a contractual relationship between an individual and the data controller or if there are actions preceding the conclusion of a contract (for example, a pre-contract, binding offer) and the processing of personal data is necessary for the execution of the contract, the legal basis for such processing is the contract.  
It is important to emphasize that data controllers must ensure the necessity and proportionality of contract execution. In principle, this means that data processing must be essential; therefore, if the data controller can reasonably process fewer data or use the data in a less intrusive manner, it will not be considered that the personal data is necessary for the conclusion/execution of the contract, and thus the data controller will not have a contractual basis for processing such personal data.  
For example, the data required for online purchases are name, surname, and address. If the data controller asks the respondent for information about the number of children, marital status, etc., in order to offer personalized products via a newsletter, that data is not necessary for the subject of the contract, and the legal basis for such processing could not be the contract.

**Legal Obligation**  
Here, it is necessary to emphasize that the data controller primarily has the duty to be familiar with the professional laws and subordinate regulations governing the activity they are engaged in.  
Namely, laws and subordinate regulations often specify the processing of personal data, and the content of such processing is essentially a legal obligation of the organization, and thus such processing is lawful as there is an adequate legal basis.  
For example, if it concerns a hotel (data controller engaged in tourism), the data controller is obliged to be familiar with the Law on Tourist Fees and the Regulation on the eVisitor System and to know that, for example, the processing of guests' personal data through the eVisitor system is grounded in the aforementioned regulations and is thus considered lawful from the perspective of personal data protection.  
It is also important to note that if personal data is processed based on a legal obligation, the individual does not have the right to erasure, the right to data portability, or the right to object.

**Legitimate Interest**  
Although there is a diverse spectrum of legitimate interests for which certain processing of personal data is necessary (for example, legitimate interest may include commercial interests, security interests, individual interests, or even broader social benefits), it is important to note that they will not always be lawful.  
There is no exhaustive list of legitimate interests for which it is necessary to process personal data, but some potential lawful legitimate interests could include, for example, marketing, fraud prevention, information exchange within the organization, IT security, protection of property and individuals, etc.  
The legitimate interest of the data controller or a third party is a legal basis for the processing of personal data that can be applied if the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of individuals do not prevail.  
For the use of legitimate interest as a legal basis for processing personal data, the processing must be necessary in terms of proportionality in achieving the goals of the data controller, which means that it should be kept in mind that if there is an alternative option that less affects rights and freedoms, processing for the purpose of achieving legitimate interest is likely not necessary, and thus not lawful.  
Furthermore, the existence of a legitimate interest requires careful assessment of whether individuals can reasonably expect certain processing for a specific purpose, with the expectation of individuals needing to be reasonable.  
For example, if the data controller wants to install a video surveillance system to achieve the goal of protecting property or possessions, it is not reasonable to expect the installation of the same in a changing room or sanitary facilities, while it is reasonable to expect the same in a hallway or in a room where valuable documents or items for the data controller are kept.  
Therefore, if the interests and fundamental rights of individuals outweigh the interests of the data controller, legitimate interest as a legal basis should not be applied, and this can be assessed in several steps before the processing begins by implementing a legitimate interest test, which you can find on the website of the Personal Data Protection Agency at https://azop.hr/obrasci-predlosci/ and at the link: https://arc-rec-project.eu/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/primjer-Test-razmjernosti-.docx  
The mentioned legitimate interest test is a key component for relying on legitimate interest as a legal basis for processing personal data, as it allows the data controller to establish a final balance by taking additional protective measures, determining whether there is a possibility of proving compliance, ensuring transparency, and considering how to proceed with the realization of individuals' rights, especially the right to object, which must be communicated to the individual no later than at the time of the first communication, presented clearly and separately from all other information.

**Vital Interests**  
Processing personal data for the protection of vital interests of an individual is a less frequently used legal basis, but it is still important to consider in certain specific situations when other legal bases are not appropriate. As with some other legal bases, data controllers should consider whether the processing is truly necessary to achieve the goal of protecting vital interests. Data controllers are most likely to rely on this legal basis when the processing of personal data is necessary to protect someone's life or to mitigate a serious threat to a person, for example, a child or a missing person.

**Tasks in the Public Interest**  
This legal basis is most relevant for public authorities. There does not need to be a specific legal authority...
for the processing of personal data attributed to that data controller, but their fundamental task, function, or authority must have a clear basis in EU law or national law, including customary law. Recital 41 of the GDPR clearly states that the law on which the task, function, or authority is based should be clear and precise, and its application should be predictable for those to whom it applies, in accordance with the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union and the European Court of Human Rights.
