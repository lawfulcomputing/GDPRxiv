Director General's Foreword  
2023 was a year when measures against organized crime and gun violence were in focus. The government's direction is clear; law enforcement must have access to more and better tools. Therefore, the government has initiated extensive investigative and legislative work on, for example, expanded camera surveillance, more and larger registers with biometric data, greater opportunities for law enforcement agencies to access information from other authorities and companies, as well as expanded possibilities to use covert coercive measures for preventive purposes. As a society, we need to both protect people's right to privacy and provide the police with effective tools in the fight against serious and organized crime. The benefits that surveillance entails in law enforcement must be proportionate to its intrusion into personal privacy. For IMY, it has been an important priority this year to contribute to this work. We have participated with experts in many investigations and have been a referral body in even more. We particularly highlight two things; first, we wish for a government investigation to be appointed with the task of conducting a comprehensive analysis of how the many proposals affect the right to privacy. Secondly, we point out the possibility of creating temporary laws that can be evaluated before they become permanent.  
2023 was also the year when the whole world talked about artificial intelligence (AI). For the past couple of years, IMY has been working specifically with guidance for innovation actors. Uncertainty hinders innovation, and we encounter many who find issues related to data protection and privacy particularly difficult and complex. The significant need for in-depth guidance was the background for us, as the first authority in Sweden, to start a regulatory sandbox where we have now conducted two projects, the first on federated machine learning of AI in healthcare and the second on sensors as an alternative to camera surveillance in efforts to create safety in public spaces. The sandbox is now a permanent part of our operations.  
2023 must also be described as a growth year for IMY. The government increased IMY's budget by nearly 40 percent starting in 2023. This enhancement was necessary and welcome. Since the middle of the year, when a large number of new employees have been recruited and introduced, we see clear results in the form of reduced processing times and case balances for all major case flows. During the year, we have also initiated over 200 supervisory cases, which is a significant increase from 120 the previous year, and decided on sanctions amounting to over 120 million kronor, compared to 10 million kronor in 2022.  
2023 is also a year we have celebrated. In May, we marked 50 years as an authority. In 1973, Sweden enacted the world's first national legislation on data protection, and the same year, the Data Inspection Board was established. We celebrated the anniversary with a well-attended conference in a hybrid format, with the Minister of Justice as a speaker and three panel discussions. We have also celebrated reaching the finals in the competition for Sweden's most modern authority.  
2023 has also been characterized by continued transformation of our work with complaints and supervision. At the end of the year, decisions from the Supreme Administrative Court confirmed that a person who submits a complaint to IMY has the right to appeal if IMY does not do what is requested in the complaint. A ruling from the Court of Justice of the European Union entails extensive requirements for the investigation of complaints. Next year, decisions are also expected regarding a new regulation with procedural rules for cross-border cases concerning complaints and supervision under the General Data Protection Regulation. Overall, these changes imply a need for comprehensive changes in our working methods and a significantly more extensive, resource-intensive handling of most of our cases.  
As we look ahead, we can note that there is intense work underway with new legal acts within the EU that will have extensive impacts on Swedish companies and the digitization of the public sector – and thus also for IMY's operations. In December, it was announced that the trilogue negotiations on the AI regulation have reached a conclusion, and a decision to adopt the regulation is expected within a few months. The negotiations on the regulation concerning the European Health Data Space (EHDS) are also in their final stages. Both of these regulations will have far-reaching consequences for the work with digitization, information security, and data protection and will give us new and additional tasks.  
The right to privacy is easy to take for granted, but it is challenged every day. It may seem obvious that there should always be protected zones for confidential conversations and that surveillance must always be justified. In a contemporary context where the technical possibilities for surveillance are endless and challenges are piling up, IMY's mission to ensure the correct handling of personal data is more important than ever. The right to privacy is a necessary foundation for many other fundamental rights, such as the right to freedom of expression and the right to demonstrate. In 2024, we will continue to work towards ensuring that technology is combined with strong privacy protection and for a sustainable digitized future, where even our children and grandchildren can have a private life.  
David Törngren  
Acting Director General

About the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection  
6
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY) works to protect personal data, such as information about health and finances, ensuring that it is handled correctly and does not fall into the wrong hands. We also work to ensure that video surveillance, credit reporting, and debt collection are conducted properly. At the turn of the year, debt collection matters were transferred to the Financial Supervisory Authority.

• Our mission affects the entire society. Sweden is in the midst of a digital transformation that impacts all individuals and sectors of society. We advocate for sustainable digitalization where privacy protection is a natural part of new goods and services. We are convinced that it is possible to safeguard citizens' security and societal safety without unwarranted tracking and surveillance.

• Our mission includes monitoring compliance with data protection regulations. We are the national supervisory authority and issue permits in the area of data protection, for video surveillance, as well as for credit reporting and, until 2023, debt collection. When we conduct supervision, we have strong powers, including the ability to impose administrative fines. We are also part of a comprehensive EU collaboration and contribute to a uniform application of data protection regulations within the EU.

• We provide guidance and support to individuals and organizations. Every day, we receive a large number of inquiries from authorities, companies, and organizations that need guidance in interpreting and applying data protection regulations in their work. We have a regulatory sandbox for data protection to promote privacy-friendly innovation and sustainable digitalization. Individuals can also contact us with tips or complaints if they believe there are inaccuracies in the processing of personal data or to ask questions. They can also whistleblow to us on issues related to data protection.

• We contribute our expertise in connection with investigative and legislative work and are tasked with monitoring, analyzing, and reporting to the government on developments concerning new technology and the right to privacy.

• Our mission is becoming increasingly important due to the digitalization of society, exponential technological development, and the growing need to protect organizations against cyberattacks. Data protection is closely linked to good information and cybersecurity, and we have an important role in this broader perspective.

We work based on a strategic direction with the goal that by 2025, personal privacy and data protection will have a clear focus at all levels of society. Our vision helps us to focus on effects and is based on those we exist for.  
By 2025, we want:  
• Individuals to have good knowledge of their rights  
• Private and public organizations to work more systematically with data protection and drive privacy-friendly digitalization

To achieve our vision for 2025, we need to have effective processes and set high goals. 1.2. Outlook on Privacy Protection  
Personal privacy and data protection are significantly affected by long-term and transformative changes in our environment, such as the pervasive digitalization of society and exponential technological development. In IMY's monitoring of the technological landscape, we have identified several trends with direct impacts on privacy protection.  
1. The development of AI is progressing at a very high speed. Recently, the breakthrough of large language models has made it clearer to the general public what potential AI has and how it can change, for example, how we work and seek information. The use of AI will constitute a larger part of digitalization in the coming years across all sectors of society.  
2. Data remains an important resource, both for businesses and the public sector. Data is needed as "raw material" in AI-based processes and is thus essential for the efficiencies that such processes can lead to. The ability to manage and transform data into utility is becoming increasingly important. Efforts to address the issue of more high-quality data are ongoing in both research and industry.  

3. Many believe that the need for security can be met through identification using various biometric identification solutions. However, the overuse of biometric data is risky. A password that falls into the wrong hands can be changed, but if someone unauthorized gains access to a fingerprint or facial biometrics, this cannot be replaced with another.
A growing area is data concerning "biohealth," a mix of biometric data and other health markers. The collection of data related to biohealth is taking place on a broad front, for example, in smartwatches, apps, pressure sensors, and heat meters. For an average user, there is information about the person's food preferences, sleep habits, and lifestyle combined with biometric data. Altogether, this creates an almost complete picture of the person and their personality. 

4. Virtual worlds are viewed as the future of the internet. Through virtual worlds, our experience of the internet will change. Some assess that the impact is as significant as when the internet first emerged. Virtual worlds enable massive data collection about individuals, primarily various biometric data or other health-related information.

5. The new generation of mobile networks, 5G, is being expanded, and in Sweden, the ambition was to reach 90 percent of the population by 2023. With 5G, the possibility for faster and more secure remote control of devices increases. 5G is considered a prerequisite for fully connected cities and homes.

6. The use of blockchain technology has increased in more sectors than just the financial one, and several government assignments are ongoing in this area. The connection of cryptocurrencies to the traditional financial system is increasing. 

The global situation in 2023 has otherwise been characterized by several security challenges; the security political situation with hybrid warfare, the high level of terrorist threats against Swedish targets, and the restructuring of Swedish criminal policy to address domestic organized crime and curb gun violence. There is a close connection between data protection and information security. Requirements for technical and organizational security measures can together work to strengthen robustness and resilience in society. Regarding criminal policy, the government is clear that law enforcement must have access to more and better tools. We can contribute to ensuring that effective law enforcement and the right to personal privacy are balanced satisfactorily, among other things, through guidance, participation in investigations, and responses to consultations.

1.3. The EU's Digital Decade
The decade of the 2020s is the EU's digital decade. This initiative aims to support Europe's digital transformation and primarily seeks to develop the EU's internal market and international competitiveness. One aspect of the digital decade is to develop new legal acts that provide the conditions for digital transformation while ensuring human rights, democracy, and the principles of the rule of law. The digital decade will create a new legal landscape for our digital present and future. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is one of the foundational elements for this transformation. The GDPR will not change in the digital decade but will protect personal data and the right to privacy in the application of the new legislation.

Since many of the regulations concern the handling of personal data, the digital decade will have a significant impact on privacy protection in society and on IMY. Two regulations are expected to have particularly large impacts:
• The AI Regulation is assessed to have a very significant impact on IMY and privacy protection. The EU has reached a trialogue agreement, and the regulation will be finalized in the spring of 2024. The AI Regulation will come into full effect two years after it enters into force.
• The regulation on the European Health Data Space (EHDS) has also progressed significantly in the negotiation process, and the Council's general orientation is now established. The assessment is that the regulation will be decided in 2024, but the implementation period may be longer than for the AI Regulation.

1.4. Our Organization  
IMY is a so-called single-authority agency with a transparency council. Organizationally, the agency is divided into departments based on the societal sectors of public operations and business, as well as a department for operational support.  
•  
In the business department, there are units that work with EU supervision, business supervision, security supervision, and our information service. From January 2024, there will also be a unit for national supervision.  
•  
In the public operations department, there are units that work with camera surveillance issues, the judiciary and defense, as well as supervision of and guidance to public operations.  
•  
The operational support department has an administrative unit, a finance and HR unit, a unit for IT, digitalization, and security, as well as a communications unit.  
•  
Additionally, there is a legal unit with an EU secretariat and a staff unit that supports the Director General. Finally, there are three central functions – data protection officer, information security officer, and security protection manager – that report directly to the Director General but have their organizational home in different units. The agency has office premises at Fleminggatan 14 in central Stockholm. In section 6.1, we describe in more detail how we have developed our organization over the year.

1.5. About IMY's Annual Report  
IMY's annual report for 2023 follows the same structure as the annual reports of recent years. Last year, our ambition was to make it shorter and clearer, and for 2023, the goal is to maintain that shorter and clearer approach. The operational statistics follow the structure of previous years. If a piece of information is missing, it is indicated as a dash in the table. In last year's report, there were several typographical errors in the comparison tables; these have been corrected and marked with a footnote for clarity. Several of the errors concerned figures from the year 2020, which have been omitted in this year's tables as they only cover the last three years. The statistics for the annual report are sourced from the operational systems on January 2 and reviewed by operational representatives on January 19, 2024.  
We report unit costs only for our volume flows (inquiries, complaints, and personal data incidents) as well as for supervision of data protection and permits for camera surveillance. By doing so, we cover approximately 83 percent of our operational case inflow as well as the central case categories that are the most resource-intensive.  
Following this introductory section, there is a brief summary of 2023 (section 2). This is followed by the reporting of the agency's tasks and results (sections 3–5) as well as a section on operational and organizational development during the year (section 6). Then, competence supply (section 7) and work environment, health, and sick leave (section 8) are described. The annual report concludes with an account of how the agency's appropriations have been utilized (sections 9–10).  
The results reporting is divided based on operational areas. Data protection is the largest part of our operations and is reported in sub-areas:  
• Data Protection (section 3)  
– outreach and guiding activities  
– complaints  
– supervision  
– work within the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) and other international cooperation  
– other tasks within data protection  
• Camera Surveillance (section 4)  
• Debt Collection and Credit Reporting (section 5)  
Camera surveillance is covered by provisions in several regulations. Fundamentally, data protection regulations are applicable, but they are supplemented for certain types of camera surveillance by the Camera Surveillance Act. Therefore, cases concerning camera surveillance are included both in the statistics for data protection and are separately reported in the section on tasks within the camera surveillance area.

1.5.1. About Our Overall Assessments
In the annual report, we make a number of overall assessments of our operations. In the assessment, we discuss how the operations function as a whole. The assessment is made on a three-point scale:
• Good; the operations are functioning well
• Satisfactory; the operations are functioning, but need improvement in certain areas
• Unsatisfactory; the operations do not have an acceptable result and improvement measures need to be implemented
The assessment is a weighting of quantitative criteria and qualitative reasoning. Quantitative measures of performance and result indicators do not exist for all parts of the operations. In these cases, the assessment is made solely based on qualitative reasoning. For the parts of the operations where we have quantitative measures, the assessment is based on three pillars:
• Our performances; the statistics regarding incoming and concluded cases, processing time, etc., that introduce the sections.
• Our result indicators; statistical indicators, which are found in connection with the result assessment.
• A qualitative reasoning; where we place the statistics in context and discuss what has influenced the outcome during the year, as well as how we have managed it.
IMY has no quantitative goals in the appropriation letter, but we have developed internal result indicators to measure the development of our operations. The result indicators have been formulated with different time specifications, mixing days and months. Some of the indicators harmonize with statutory deadlines, while others are those we have established ourselves. To facilitate understanding, the indicators in this year's annual report are stated in days as long as they are 30 days or fewer, and thereafter in months. This also applies in cases where the statutory deadlines are stated in days but exceed 30.

**Parts of our operations function satisfactorily but need improvement. The work on other tasks within data protection – which includes, among other things, work on personal data incidents, prior consultations, legality checks, and binding corporate rules – is one such category. Unfortunately, the processing time for all case types has increased, and for personal data incidents, it has doubled over the year. Regarding the work on camera surveillance, significant improvements have been made, but the handling of permits still has long processing times despite our prioritization of it during the year. The work on debt collection and credit reporting has also functioned satisfactorily and shows relatively comparable results to previous years, even though this work has had to take a back seat when we needed to prioritize complaints within data protection. The work on supervision has improved, and we now assess it as satisfactory. We initiate and conclude significantly more supervisions compared to previous years.**

**We assess that the work on complaints does not have acceptable results and needs to be improved with further measures. The balances have been very high during the year, although the year ended with fewer cases in balance than we had when the year began. We conclude a significantly higher number of less complex and national cases while international cases are based on an already high case balance. We have highlighted in various ways, including in previous annual reports and budget documents, that the work on complaints has fundamentally changed in a way that has been very challenging for our entire operation. During the year, several rulings from the Supreme Administrative Court and the Court of Justice of the European Union also imposed additional requirements on our work with complaints. This means that the significantly increased funding we received starting in 2023 risks not having a major effect on our balances. The work on complaints will continue to be one of our most prioritized issues in the coming years.**

The most common reasons for this include that there is no deficiency according to the Data Protection Regulation, that it is unclear what the complaint concerns, that the complainant does not want IMY to contact the data controller, that the issue should be handled by another authority such as the Swedish Post and Telecom Authority, that the complaint concerns publications on sites that have constitutional protection, or that the data controller has not had sufficient time to handle, for example, a request for access.

Approximately 80 percent of all cases are assessed and addressed without any in-depth review being conducted. Cases that require further action, cross-border cases, recurring cases where IMY has previously taken an initial action, and cases that are assessed to be relevant for supervision are sent to another operational unit for assessment.  
We are working to address the balances and reduce processing times in other complaint cases as well. A large part of these are cross-border cases. In these cases, we do not control all lead times, which affects the processing time.  
3.2.1. Significantly changed legal situation regarding complaints  
In the autumn of 2023, judgments from the Supreme Administrative Court (HFD) and the Court of Justice of the European Union have significantly changed the legal situation regarding the handling of complaints.  
1. HFD11 has, through two rulings in November, established that individuals who submit a complaint have the right to appeal the decision in the complaint case if the decision means that IMY will not do what the individual requests in their complaint.  

Previously, the practice has been that individuals who submitted complaints do not have the right to appeal our decisions in the complaint case or in a supervisory case initiated due to the complaint, nor do they have the status of a party in the supervisory case that is initiated. Due to HFD's new rulings, IMY now assesses that individuals who report complaints are parties in the cases that are initiated, both the complaint case and the supervisory case based on the complaint. This applies to both national and cross-border cases.  
2. The Court of Justice of the European Union12 has, through a new ruling in December, established more extensive requirements on the investigative duty of data protection authorities in complaint cases. This means more comprehensive investigations of complaint cases, that supervision due to a complaint is initiated more often, and that supervision cannot be limited to the same extent as before.

The size of the administrative fines can amount to a maximum of 20 million euros or four percent of a company's or group's annual turnover, whichever is higher, for private entities. For authorities and public entities, the fine can amount to 10 million kronor. Simplified, one can say that the amount is determined by the severity of the violation and the annual turnover of the supervisory subject. The EDPB has developed guidelines for how the administrative fines should be determined, but it is still a complicated task to calculate the fines since the practice is still limited. 

During 2023, eleven supervisory cases have led to administrative fines. IMY decided on fines totaling 120,700,000 kronor in these cases. The corresponding figure for 2022 was 9,720,000 kronor. Here are some of the decisions described. 
• Spotify. IMY found that the information provided by the company to individuals about how their personal data is processed was not sufficiently clear and decided on an administrative fine of 58,000,000 kronor.  
• Trygg-Hansa. IMY noted that Trygg-Hansa had security deficiencies that resulted in a large number of customer data being accessible on the internet and decided on an administrative fine of 35,000,000 kronor. 
• Bonnier. IMY found that the company had profiled its customers and website visitors without their consent and decided on an administrative fine of 13,000,000 kronor.
• Tele2 and CDON. IMY decided on administrative fines of 12,000,000 kronor and 300,000 kronor respectively for the companies transferring personal data to the USA via the analytics tool Google Analytics. 
• The Education Committee of Stockholm City. IMY decided on an administrative fine of 800,000 kronor because the camera surveillance at Aspuddens School had been too extensive.

• Region Skåne. IMY decided on an administrative fine of 200,000 kronor for inadequate security after the region stored sensitive personal data on an unencrypted USB drive that was subsequently lost.

3.3.3. Cross-border supervision
The cross-border cases involve businesses or individuals in several EU/EES countries. An individual can submit a complaint in their home country, but it is where the business is located that determines which European data protection authority is responsible.
**Supervisory Authority and Investigation of the Matter**  
Since Sweden has many large companies with international operations, we also have many cross-border supervisory matters. IMY has initiated 39 cross-border supervisory matters and concluded 17 in 2023. The work with cross-border supervision involves a more complicated process compared to a national matter. We assess that the processing time for a cross-border supervision is approximately 1.5 times longer than for a national supervision. According to the Data Protection Regulation, the responsible supervisory authority must cooperate and communicate with other relevant supervisory authorities during the processing. Investigation materials, drafts of decisions, and decisions are translated into English, shared, and commented on, usually in several rounds. The comments from the relevant supervisory authorities can be incompatible, which requires coordination by the responsible authority. Consensus must be reached before the responsible authority can make a decision in the matter. If the authorities do not agree, the matter goes to the EDPB for dispute resolution.  
IMY is also a relevant supervisory authority in many cross-border matters. As a relevant supervisory authority, we participate in the materials and drafts and can provide comments and objections. 3.5.1. Personal Data Incidents  
In 2023, nearly 6,000 personal data incidents were reported to IMY, which is on par with previous years. A personal data incident is a security incident involving personal data, such as data being destroyed, altered, lost, or accessed by unauthorized parties. When a personal data incident occurs, the responsible organization must assess the risk the incident may pose to the individuals whose personal data is involved. Risks may include identity theft, fraud, financial loss, discrimination, or harmful rumor spreading. If it is likely that the personal data incident poses a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the responsible organization is obligated to report the incident to IMY within 72 hours.  
The primary task of supervisory authorities in this regard is to provide advice and guidance to data controllers. During the year, IMY has produced a report on personal data incidents (read more about it in section 3.1.4.). Through the report, we provide advice and guidance on reporting personal data incidents. Incoming incident reports are an important knowledge source for IMY – we identify what is happening in various organizations and see new guidance needs.  
The work to develop and improve the authority's handling of reports of personal data incidents has continued throughout 2023. 3.5.2. Legality Checks  
Legality checks are a specific form of review that IMY conducts against law enforcement authorities to ensure that they process personal data about individuals correctly. 3.5.3. Prior Consultation  
When an organization plans a processing of personal data that is associated with high privacy risks, it must conduct a data protection impact assessment. If the organization determines that it is not possible to mitigate the risks to individuals' freedoms and rights with measures that are reasonable based on available technology and costs, it is obliged to request prior consultation with IMY. For law enforcement authorities, the requirements for data protection impact assessments and prior consultations are more extensive than for others. In 2023, we received 16 requests for prior consultation, which is an increase compared to the previous year. Of these, IMY has only provided opinions in five cases. The prior consultations where we have provided advice have included areas related to camera surveillance. We have also advised the Police Authority regarding a technical test of ANPR technology at the Øresund Bridge.

3.5.4. CSAM Regulation  
The temporary CSAM regulation is an EU regulation aimed at combating online sexual abuse of children (CSAM stands for 'child sexual abuse material'). It means that providers of certain types of communication services, such as email services and communication apps, under certain conditions, may access users' communications for the purpose of tracking material related to sexual abuse of children or searches for contact with children for sexual purposes. If a provider voluntarily wishes to start using technical solutions for tracking, it must first have conducted a prior consultation with IMY. By the end of the year, no prior consultation related to the temporary CSAM regulation had been submitted.

**3.5.6. BCR**  
Multinational corporations have the opportunity to apply for binding corporate rules (BCR) to be approved by a data protection authority within the EU. BCR are rules that corporations can develop to regulate their processing of personal data and ensure appropriate safeguards when transferring personal data within their own group, when parts of it are based in countries outside the EU/EEA. These matters have a long and complex processing time as they must be co-reviewed by other data protection authorities within the EU, and the EDPB must provide an opinion before a decision can be made by IMY.  
IMY is the responsible authority in five cases currently under processing but has been a co-reviewer in over 30. The processing time varies significantly depending on whether IMY is the responsible authority or merely provides comments in a case being handled by another data protection authority.

**3.5.7. Permission to Process Personal Data Regarding Legal Violations**  
According to the Data Protection Regulation, authorities may process personal data regarding legal violations. In Swedish law, there are supplementary regulations that allow others than authorities to process such personal data. The regulations provide an opportunity for those wishing to process such data to apply for permission from IMY.  
The number of applications for permission to process personal data regarding legal violations has decreased during 2023. The majority of cases concern companies in the financial sector that need to conduct checks, for example, customers against sanction lists to comply with legal requirements, such as requirements for measures against money laundering and financing of terrorism. During the year, several applications have also been received from companies conducting background checks and applying to verify whether employees and consultants have committed crimes on behalf of employers.  
IMY concluded significantly fewer cases compared to the previous year, which is related to several applications from member companies within associations that applied on the same grounds the previous year.  
The average processing time for these cases is long. To reduce processing time and facilitate for companies, IMY has developed proposals for regulations for the processing of personal data regarding legal violations through checks against sanction lists during the year. The purpose of this is that companies meeting the requirements in the proposed regulation should not need to apply for permission for such processing that the regulation targets. The regulations are proposed to come into effect on March 1, 2024.

IMY responds to inquiries, handles complaints, conducts supervision, and issues permits within the area of camera surveillance. Camera surveillance is governed by provisions in several different regulations, where both the General Data Protection Regulation and the Camera Surveillance Act are often applicable. It can be difficult to determine whether a surveillance is permitted or not, which creates a significant need for guidance. 

The majority of camera surveillance conducted in Sweden today does not require a permit from IMY. This may involve surveillance in stores and public transport or surveillance carried out by private companies. However, a permit for camera surveillance is required in certain cases. This applies if an authority or anyone else, in the performance of a task of public interest, such as a private school operation or private healthcare, surveils individuals in a location accessible to the public. Certain law enforcement agencies are completely exempt from the permit requirement for camera surveillance. 

Camera surveillance that does not require a permit from IMY is subject to the same rules in the General Data Protection Regulation and the Camera Surveillance Act as permitted camera surveillance, with the exception of the permit requirement itself. Camera surveillance conducted by a private individual that does not involve public spaces or anyone else's personal sphere, however, is not covered by data protection regulations. If a law enforcement agency conducts camera surveillance, the Crime Data Act applies instead of the General Data Protection Regulation, as well as specific data protection rules for each agency.

During the year, inquiries about camera surveillance accounted for approximately 20 percent of all questions received by the authority. There have been slightly fewer written inquiries about camera surveillance compared to previous years, which is a sign that the guidance we have on our website is being utilized. The inquiries often concern whether a permit is required and whether the surveillance is permitted.

As in previous years, many journalist inquiries to IMY have concerned camera surveillance. Many questions this year have related to camera surveillance in schools, but we have also answered questions about drones, dashcams, and camera surveillance in nightclubs. 

More complaints about camera surveillance have been received in 2023 compared to previous years. The majority of the incoming complaints concern camera surveillance conducted by private individuals. Often, it involves neighbors who have observed surveillance cameras on a private residence and suspect that the surveillance is too extensive and therefore not permitted. The increase in incoming complaints may be due to more private individuals conducting surveillance than before, as well as an increasing number of people becoming aware that they can report unlawful camera surveillance to IMY. Other recurring complaints involve employees who complain about their employer's camera surveillance because it is perceived to control the employees, or camera surveillance in common areas of multi-family buildings. IMY has also received complaints about surveillance in restaurants, gyms, and public places.

Just under 60 complaints have led to supervision of camera surveillance in 2023, which is a significant increase compared to previous years. The majority of the supervisions of camera surveillance initiated during the year concern private individuals' camera surveillance. As in the previous year, IMY has focused on cases involving private individuals' camera surveillance, as it is the most common basis for complaints regarding camera surveillance. IMY has also initiated several supervisions concerning surveillance in multi-family buildings. During the year, approximately 60 supervision cases were concluded, which is a marked increase compared to the previous year. Nine supervision decisions have been appealed to the court.

IMY is responsible for supervision and issuing licenses for companies engaged in debt collection or credit reporting. We receive complaints and respond to inquiries related to such activities. The purpose is to ensure that good practice is maintained in debt collection and credit reporting activities. 2023 was the last year we had responsibility for licenses and supervision under the Debt Collection Act.  
5.1. Debt Collection  
In the autumn of 2023, the government submitted a proposal to the parliament that the responsibility for licenses and supervision under the Debt Collection Act should be consolidated with the Financial Supervisory Authority starting in 2024. The supervisory responsibility has until now been divided between IMY and the Financial Supervisory Authority. The government argued that by consolidating the supervisory responsibility with one authority, it becomes easier for individuals to know where to turn with comments and complaints. Additionally, the risk of differences in assessments and practices in the field is reduced. IMY has welcomed the change.  
During the autumn, IMY has worked on preparations to ensure that the transition of the debt collection assignment goes as smoothly as possible. This has included the repeal of the regulations and general advice that IMY had in the debt collection area, communication with the public and license holders, contact with cooperating authorities in the debt collection area, adjustments in IMY's internal systems, and the practical details regarding the handover of completed and ongoing cases. The preparatory work has been conducted in close collaboration with the Financial Supervisory Authority.  
At the end of the year, there were just over 150 actors with IMY's license to conduct debt collection activities. In addition to these, organizations that only collect their own claims, and therefore do not need a license, have also been included in our supervisory responsibility. Among the debt collection companies licensed by IMY, there have been a handful of large companies, but most have been smaller actors such as law firms and accounting companies. Our supervisory and licensing responsibility has not included the debt collection companies that have been under the supervision of the Financial Supervisory Authority, which has included several large debt collection firms.  
IMY has maintained a basic level in the assignment throughout the year. In total, the authority has spent less than one percent of our total time on debt collection, which corresponds to less than one full-time equivalent. The average processing time for completed debt collection cases has increased during the year. At the same time, we have completed more inquiries and complaints compared to last year. Approximately 140 written inquiries regarding debt collection activities have been received during the year. Additionally, questions are posed to our guidance phone. Individuals wonder, for example, how to make objections to a debt collection claim, what deadlines apply, and what costs companies are allowed to charge. Businesses that conduct or plan to conduct debt collection activities ask questions, for example, about what is required to obtain a license or what constitutes good debt collection practice. To provide support, we have also conducted training on good debt collection practice for new debt collection case handlers at the industry organization Swedish Debt Collection's member companies on two occasions during the year.  
Nearly 230 complaints about debt collection activities were received by IMY in 2023, which is an increase compared to last year. Furthermore, almost 260 complaints were completed, which is also an increase. No supervision has been initiated. In the latter part of the year, IMY has strived to conclude cases, and at the end of 2023, IMY handed over 10 ongoing cases to the Financial Supervisory Authority.

5.2. Credit Reporting  
IMY conducts supervision and issues permits for companies engaged in credit reporting activities. We also receive complaints and respond to inquiries related to credit reporting activities. 6.1. New Organization – New Units and Management Forum  
To adapt the operations to the changed conditions that have arisen due to our significant growth in 2023, we made several organizational changes. Through this change, the management group has gained a distinct strategic responsibility for the operations.  

The number of units has increased, and they have fewer employees to enable close leadership in daily operations for all staff. The tasks have been streamlined and distributed to achieve scaling effects and learning among employees. We strive for greater mobility between units and more standardized working methods in our operational processes, to enable more people to support others when certain units are more burdened during periods. However, there is still a division of responsibilities for different tasks across various units.  

The departments coordinate and lead the operational work with support from departmental management groups. Additionally, there is a management forum, consisting of all managers in the authority. The management forum addresses cross-authority issues within the framework of the annual operational governance, such as directional decisions, operational planning, and in-depth operational follow-up.  

In September, our Director General Lena Lindgren Schelin left after more than 5 years at IMY. Since then, IMY has been led by an acting Director General.

Financial costs have increased significantly from the previous year due to the high interest rate environment but only constitute 0.2 percent of the authority's total costs.

**This is IMY**  
The Integrity Protection Authority (IMY) works to protect all citizens' personal data, such as health and financial information, ensuring that it is handled correctly and does not fall into the wrong hands. We are the ones who review that companies, authorities, and other actors comply with GDPR – the General Data Protection Regulation. We educate and guide those who process personal data. We also influence legislation. We want to see sustainable and privacy-friendly digitalization. We are convinced that it is possible to safeguard citizens' security and societal safety without unwarranted tracking and surveillance. Together with other data protection authorities in the EU, we work to ensure that citizens' personal data has the same protection throughout the union. We also work to ensure that credit reporting is conducted correctly. Our vision is a safe information society, where we together protect personal privacy.
