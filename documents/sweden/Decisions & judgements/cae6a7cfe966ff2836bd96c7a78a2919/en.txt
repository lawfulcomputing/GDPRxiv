Decision of the Swedish Data Protection Authority  
As the data controller healthcare provider under Chapter 2, Section 6 of the Patient Data Act (2008:355), PDL, MedHelp AB (MedHelp) has processed personal data about patients seeking care in violation of the General Data Protection Regulation1 in the following ways.  
a)  
MedHelp has, from May 25, 2018, to August 31, 2019, by disclosing personal data to the Thai company MediCall and allowing MediCall to collect personal data, processed personal data in violation of Articles 5.1 a, 6, and 9.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.  

b)  
MedHelp has, from an unknown date until February 18, 2019, exposed personal data in audio files with recorded phone calls to 11772 over the internet without protection against unauthorized disclosure or unauthorized access to the personal data in the storage server Voice NAS. MedHelp has thereby, in violation of Articles 5.1 f and 32.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, failed to take appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure an adequate level of security for the data.  

c)  
At the time of the inspection on March 20, 2019, MedHelp, in addition to a voice response message stating that the call is being recorded for patient safety and quality purposes, did not inform patients seeking care about the company's processing of personal data in connection with the collection of personal data during phone calls to 1177. MedHelp has thereby processed personal data in violation of Articles 5.1 a and 13 of the General Data Protection Regulation.  

d)  
At the time of the inspection on March 20, 2019, MedHelp had not backed up audio files with recorded calls to 1177 that were answered by MedHelp's nurses within MedHelp's telephony platform. MedHelp has thereby processed personal data in violation of Articles 5.1 f and 32.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

Legal Background  
National Regulations on Health and Medical Care  
The mission of health and medical care is regulated in, among other laws, the Health and Medical Care Act (2017:30), HSL.  
Measures to medically prevent, investigate, and treat diseases and injuries are defined as health and medical care, Chapter 2, Section 1 of HSL. The principal authority refers to the region or municipality that, according to the law, is responsible for providing health and medical care to the population in the region or municipality. Within a principal authority's geographical area, one or more healthcare providers may operate, Chapter 2, Section 2 of HSL. A healthcare provider refers to a government authority, region, municipality, another legal entity, or individual entrepreneur conducting health and medical care activities, Chapter 2, Section 3 of HSL. Regions and municipalities may, while retaining principal authority, enter into agreements with others to perform the tasks for which the county council or municipality is responsible, Chapter 15, Section 1 of HSL. Those who have a statutory responsibility for providing care are referred to as the principal authority.

The responsibility does not imply an obligation to conduct the operations oneself, but the operation can be carried out by someone else who is then referred to as the care provider (prop. 1981/82:97 p. 33 f.). The public responsibility as the principal does not entail decision-making authority over the care provider's daily operations, nor does it relieve the care provider of the responsibilities that come with the role of care provider (prop. 2016/17:43 p. 86).  

Anyone who belongs to or has belonged to the healthcare personnel within the private healthcare sector is prohibited, according to Chapter 6, Sections 12–15 of the Patient Safety Act (2010:659), (PSL), from unlawfully disclosing what he or she has learned about an individual's health condition or other personal circumstances in the course of their work. The Public Sector's operations are governed by the Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act (2009:400) (OSL).  

Data Controller and Data Processor  
According to Article 4.7 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), a data controller is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data. If the purposes and means of processing are determined by Union law or the national law of the Member States, the data controller or the specific criteria for how they are to be appointed may be prescribed in Union law or in the national law of the Member States.  

According to Chapter 2, Section 6 of the Data Protection Act (PDL), a care provider is the data controller for the processing of personal data that the care provider carries out in connection with individual-oriented care in its operations, for example regarding the obligation to maintain patient records.  

According to Article 24 of the GDPR, the data controller shall, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks, implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure – and be able to demonstrate – that the processing is performed in accordance with the GDPR.  

A data processor is defined, according to Article 4.8 of the GDPR, as a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller. The data processor may only process personal data on the instructions of the data controller, according to Article 29 of the GDPR.  

Fundamental Principles and Legal Basis  
Personal data shall, according to Article 5.1 a of the GDPR, be processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (the principle of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency). According to Article 5.1 f, personal data shall be processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, using appropriate technical or organizational measures (the principle of integrity and confidentiality). Data controllers shall, according to Article 5.2, be responsible for and be able to demonstrate compliance with the principles in Article 5.1 (the principle of accountability).  

For the processing of personal data to be lawful, it must be based on one of the legal grounds specified in Article 6.1 of the GDPR. In the context of processing for healthcare purposes, it is primarily Article 6.1 c (legal obligation) or 6.1 e (public interest or exercise of official authority) that may be applicable. According to Article 6.3, the basis for processing specified in Article 6.1 c and e must be established in accordance with Union law or the national law of a Member State that...

For activities under, among others, the Health and Medical Services Act (HSL), there are supplementary data protection provisions primarily in the Data Protection Act (PDL) and in HSLF-FS 2016:40, which among other things contain rules on information security and on the physical protection of information systems.

The data controller's obligation to voluntarily provide data subjects with information about the processing of personal data is stated in Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR. The information to be provided to data subjects is relatively extensive. In addition to what is stated in Articles 13 and 14, the healthcare provider that is the data controller according to Chapter 2, Section 6 of the PDL must provide additional information according to Chapter 8, Section 6 of the PDL to the data subject. The information according to Chapter 8, Section 6 of the PDL must include, among other things, what applies regarding search terms, direct access, and disclosure of data on media for automated processing.  

Security in connection with processing  
According to Article 32.1 of the GDPR, both the data controller and the data processor must take appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a suitable level of security to protect the data being processed. In assessing what technical and organizational measures are appropriate, the data controller and the data processor must consider the latest developments, the implementation costs, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. According to Article 32.1, appropriate protective measures, where appropriate, include a) pseudonymization and encryption of personal data, b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services, c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data in a timely manner in the event of a physical or technical incident, and d) a procedure for regularly testing, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
According to Article 32.2 of the GDPR, in assessing the appropriate level of security, special consideration must be given to the risks posed by the processing, in particular the risks of accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration, or unauthorized disclosure of or unauthorized access to the personal data that has been transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed.

The law contains provisions on how Swedish healthcare operations should be organized and conducted. Measures for the medical prevention, investigation, and treatment of diseases and injuries are defined as healthcare, Chapter 2, Section 1 of HSL. The term "principal" refers to the region or municipality that, according to the law, is responsible for providing healthcare to the population in the region or municipality. Within a principal's geographical area, one or more healthcare providers may operate, Chapter 2, Section 2 of HSL. The term "healthcare provider" refers to...

This responsibility does not imply an obligation to personally conduct the operations; rather,

nor does it relieve the care provider of the responsibilities that accompany the role of care provider (Prop. 2016/17:43, p. 86).  

MedHelp is the data controller care provider for the processing of personal data  
that occurs in connection with the medical advice that MedHelp has been commissioned by  
the regions to perform. This means that MedHelp, according to Article 5.2 of the  
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), must be able to demonstrate that personal data is processed in a manner that  
complies with the principles in Article 5.1.  

For the processing of personal data to be lawful, it must be supported by one  
of the legal bases specified in Article 6.1 of the GDPR. In the context of health and medical care,  
it is primarily Article 6.1 c (legal obligation) or 6.1 e (public interest or exercise of official authority) that may be  
applicable. According to Article 6.3, the basis for processing specified in Article 6.1 c  
and e must be established in accordance with Union law or the national law of a member state to which the data controller is subject.  
This means that if a care provider's processing of personal data is necessary to fulfill a legal obligation or  
perform a task in the public interest, it is required for the processing to be lawful that  
the legal obligation or the task in the public interest is regulated by national law  
(or by Union law).  

Within health and medical care, the GDPR is supplemented by the Patient Data Act (PDL), which  
contains provisions regarding, among other things, who is the data controller, the obligation to maintain patient records,  
and the permitted purposes for processing personal data. According to Chapter 1, Section 3 of the PDL,  
the scope of application of the PDL includes activities referred to in, among other things, the Health and Medical Services Act (2017:30), HSL.  
The determination in Chapter 2, Section 6 of the PDL that the care provider is the data controller is a national specification  
of Article 4.7 of the GDPR.  

A care provider needs to process data about health and sometimes also other special  
categories of personal data according to Article 9.1 of the GDPR, known as sensitive personal data.  
As a general rule, it is prohibited to process sensitive personal data according to the same article.  
The exceptions to the prohibition are outlined in Article 9.2. For health and medical care, there is an exception in Article 9.2 h that is applicable  
provided that there is a legally regulated duty of confidentiality according to Article 9.3.  

For Swedish health and medical care, it is primarily the regulations in HSL and PDL as well as  
the duty of confidentiality in the Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act (OSL) and in Chapter 6, Sections 12-15 of the Patient Safety Act (PSL) that provide the legal basis for  
the processing of personal data to be lawful in the manner intended in Articles 6.1 e, 6.2  
and 6.3 as well as 9.2 h and 9.3 of the GDPR (Prop. 2017/18:105, pp. 58 and 94  
and Prop. 2017/18:171, p. 105).

Healthcare according to the definition in Chapter 2, Section 1 of the HSL concerning 1177 through its measures to medically prevent, investigate, and treat diseases and injuries. The relationship is regulated through agreements and data processing agreements. MediCall has processed personal data in order to provide healthcare. This has occurred by MediCall accessing personal data concerning patients on a computer screen via MedHelp's journal system and by responding to calls to 1177. MediCall has therefore not been imposed national duties and responsibilities under the provisions of the HSL and is not a healthcare provider under Swedish law. Consequently, MediCall is also not covered by the scope of application of the PDL according to Chapter 1, Section 3 of the PDL, which states that the PDL applies to the processing of personal data by healthcare providers within the healthcare sector. This further implies that MediCall cannot be subject to the documentation obligation according to Chapter 3 of the PDL, as MedHelp has stated.  

The duty of confidentiality for private healthcare providers is regulated in Chapter 6, Sections 12–15 of the PSL. It is stated, as a main rule, that those who belong to or have belonged to healthcare personnel within private healthcare may not unlawfully disclose what they have learned about an individual's health condition or other personal circumstances in their activities. According to Chapter 1, Section 2 of the PSL, healthcare refers to activities covered by the HSL or other national regulations in the healthcare area, such as the Dental Care Act, the Act on Insurance Medical Investigations, etc. The activities of MediCall are not covered by the national provisions either in the HSL or in any of the other specified statutes, which means that Chapter 6 of the PSL is also not applicable. MediCall therefore lacks a confidentiality obligation regulated by Swedish law.  

The provisions in Chapter 6 of the PDL regarding consolidated record-keeping and in Chapter 25, Section 11, Paragraph 3 of the OSL mean that two or more Swedish healthcare providers can initiate a voluntary collaboration for healthcare purposes by following the provisions in Chapter 6 of the PDL (cf. Prop. 2007/08:126 p. 132 f regarding that private healthcare can seek guidance for assessments of confidentiality in secrecy provisions). If MediCall had been a Swedish healthcare provider – i.e., covered by the HSL, PSL, and PDL – MedHelp and MediCall could have initiated a voluntary collaboration for healthcare purposes by following the provisions in Chapter 6 of the PDL regarding consolidated record-keeping.  

MedHelp is a healthcare provider, and the legal basis for the processing of the personal data in question here is legal obligation or public interest according to Article 6.1 c or e of the General Data Protection Regulation, as established in Swedish law in statutes such as the PDL and HSL in accordance with Article 6.3 of the General Data Protection Regulation. Legal support for processing sensitive personal data despite the prohibition in Article 9.1 is found in Article 9.2 h and 9.3. Balancing of interests according to Article 6.1 f of the General Data Protection Regulation is not an applicable legal basis in this context.  

By engaging MediCall as a subcontractor for healthcare advice via telephone when individuals call 1177, MedHelp has conducted healthcare and has thereby allowed MediCall to process personal data without legal support in Swedish law and without a legally regulated confidentiality obligation in the manner required by Article 9.3 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

According to Article 83.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines in each individual case is effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. Infringements of Articles 5.1 a and f, 6, 9, and 13 of the General Data Protection Regulation are subject to the higher administrative fine according to Article 83.5. The fact that information is not provided regarding, among other things, that MedHelp is the data controller, the purposes and legal basis for the processing, and that care seekers can contact MedHelp to exercise their rights under the GDPR significantly limits the conditions for care seekers to safeguard their rights. MedHelp has also transferred personal data about patients to MediCall, a Thai company. It is aggravating that there has been a complete lack of information regarding the transfer of personal data to Thailand, which is a third country.  

In light of the seriousness of the violations and that the administrative fine must be effective, proportionate, and deterrent, IMY determines the administrative fine to be 500,000 kronor for this violation.

MedHelp receives approximately 3 million calls per year as part of 1177. The lack of backup at MedHelp concerns a large number of healthcare seekers. It is an aggravating circumstance that there is a complete absence of backup for healthcare documentation regarding a significant number of healthcare seekers. The loss of healthcare documentation can pose a high risk to the freedoms and rights of the healthcare seekers.  

In light of the seriousness of the violations and that the administrative fine must be effective, proportionate, and deterrent, IMY determines the administrative fine to be 500,000 Swedish kronor for the violation.  

Summary  
IMY has found that MedHelp must pay a total administrative fine of 12,000,000 Swedish kronor for the identified violations, of which 3,000,000 Swedish kronor pertains to the violation at point a), 8,000,000 Swedish kronor pertains to the violation at point b), 500,000 Swedish kronor pertains to the violation at point c), and 500,000 Swedish kronor pertains to the violation at point d).  

Orders  
MedHelp has not informed healthcare seekers in accordance with the requirements of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Chapter 8, Section 6 of the PDL. The lack of information regarding, among other things, who is the data controller and their contact details limits the healthcare seeker's ability to exercise the right to request access to personal data that MedHelp collects when healthcare seekers call 1177. MedHelp has not backed up audio files that constitute healthcare documentation according to the GDPR and Chapters 3, Sections 12-13 of HSLF-FS 2016:40.  

MedHelp receives approximately 3 million calls per year as part of 1177. This is a large number of healthcare seekers in need of medical advice who are affected by the deficiencies in information and backup.  

MedHelp is therefore ordered under Article 58.2 d of the GDPR to ensure, as soon as possible and no later than two months after the decision has become final, that processing is conducted in accordance with the GDPR and supplementary national law regarding information to healthcare seekers and backup.  

MedHelp has stated that it will suffer further harm if any orders regarding information to data subjects and backup conflict with MedHelp's agreements with Region Stockholm or with procurement law. MedHelp, as a healthcare provider and data controller, has the ultimate responsibility for lawful processing of personal data, which includes ensuring that healthcare seekers receive information about the processing of personal data and that measures for backup are taken.

How to appeal  
If you wish to appeal the decision, you should write to the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection. Indicate in the letter which decision you are appealing and the amendment you are requesting. The appeal must be received by the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection no later than three weeks from the day you received notice of the decision. If the appeal is submitted in a timely manner, the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection will forward it to the Administrative Court in Stockholm for examination.  

You may email the appeal to the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection if it does not contain any privacy-sensitive personal data or information that may be subject to confidentiality. The authority's contact details are provided on the first page of the decision.
