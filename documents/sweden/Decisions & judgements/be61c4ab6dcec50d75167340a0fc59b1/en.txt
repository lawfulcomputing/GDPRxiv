Health and Medical Care Board in Region Dalarna  
Decision following supervision under the  
Data Protection Regulation – Health and  
Medical Care Board in Region Dalarna  
Decision of the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY) finds that the Health and Medical Care Board in Region Dalarna (the Board) has processed personal data in violation of Article 32.1 of the Data Protection Regulation1, from May 6, 2021, to July 6, 2022, by failing to take appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a suitable level of protection in connection with the sending of physical summons for certain healthcare visits within Region Dalarna.  
IMY decides, based on Chapter 6, Section 2 of the Data Protection Act2 and Articles 58.2 and 83 of the Data Protection Regulation, that the Board shall pay an administrative fine of 200,000 (two hundred thousand) kronor.  
Account of the supervision case  
IMY initiated supervision of the Board following information that emerged from a complaint by a person who received a summons via mail for a healthcare visit within Region Dalarna on May 6, 2021. The complaint indicates that the summons was in a window envelope and that information indicating it was a summons, the complainant's name and address, as well as the healthcare facility to which the summons pertained, were fully visible through the window of the envelope. The purpose of IMY's supervision is to investigate whether the Board's processing of personal data in connection with the use of the relevant type of window envelope for summons to healthcare visits meets the security requirements for processing under Article 32 of the Data Protection Regulation.  
In the context of the supervision case, IMY has only reviewed the Board's sending of summons concerning the Child and Adolescent Medical Reception in Mora, the Child and Youth Counseling Reception in Falun, and the Sleep Laboratory in Avesta.  
The Board has mainly stated the following. The Board is the healthcare provider and data controller for the personal data processing that the supervision concerns. Summons from Region Dalarna are sent using the service eBREV from Postnord Strålfors AB (Strålfors). The summons are securely sent in digital format from Region Dalarna's medical records system to Strålfors, which mechanically prints, envelopes, and sends the summons to the recipient.

The Board identified the risk associated with using double windows when the service was procured and therefore purchased an additional service that entails using envelopes that contain only one window with the recipient's address. On these envelopes, Region Dalarna's postal address is printed where the sender's address is usually placed, and the name of the clinic/department that sent the summons is therefore not indicated. The Board's opinion is that the sending clinic should not be visible on the envelope.  
In a statement to IMY dated April 28, 2022, the Board stated that it had initiated an investigation together with Strålfors. In a statement dated June 16, 2022, the Board primarily stated the following. It is unclear when the Board began sending summons where the patient's name and the healthcare facility to which the summons pertains are visible in the envelope's window. The investigation shows that the additional service where the sender is concealed only applies to mailings of a maximum of five A4 sheets. If the mailing contains more A4 sheets, a larger envelope must be used, and there are no customer-specific envelopes in the assignment signed regarding eBREV; instead, standard envelopes with two windows are used. In the same statement, the Board indicated that the investigative work to review the flow regarding the sending of summons via eBREV and the risks associated with these that could generate a personal data incident is ongoing. Concurrently, discussions are being held with Strålfors about the need to renegotiate the customer assignment to ensure that the correct type of envelope, with a concealed sender, is used for all mailings.  
In a statement dated June 16, 2022, the Board stated that the processing subject to supervision had not ceased. In a supplement that the Board submitted to IMY on July 6, 2022, it was indicated that the Board, until an agreement is reached with Strålfors, will modify the template for summons for visits at the three units (Children's and Adolescent Medicine Clinic Mora, Children's and Youth Counseling Clinic Falun, and Sleep Laboratory in Avesta) that have been identified to send summons with attachments exceeding five A4 sheets. The Board has attached an image showing that the summons will be sent in an envelope with two windows, where Region Dalarna's postal address appears in one window and information that it concerns a visit via video link, along with the patient's name and address in the other.  
In a statement dated August 9, 2022, the Board has stated that previous responses have been based on summons sent through the IT system Take Care. This concerns approximately 2,500 summons per year, which corresponds to 0.5 percent of the total number of summons sent through the relevant system.  
Justification of the decision  
Applicable provisions  
Scope of the Data Protection Regulation  
Article 2.1 of the Data Protection Regulation states that the regulation shall apply to the processing of personal data that is wholly or partly carried out by automated means, as well as to other processing of personal data that is included in or will be included in a register.  
In doctrine, it is stated that since the Data Protection Regulation covers partially automated processing of personal data, the regulation is applicable to the disclosure on paper of personal data that exists in data format.3 Furthermore, the Chancellor of Justice has assessed that

The expression "fully or partially automated processing of personal data" in the now repealed Personal Data Act (1998:204) included the sending of documents by post when the underlying processing of personal data was automated.4  
Personal data is defined in Article 4.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person.  
Processing is defined in Article 4.2 of the GDPR as an operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment or combination, restriction, erasure or destruction.  

Data Controller and the Principle of Accountability  
According to Article 4.7 of the GDPR, a data controller is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data. If the purposes and means of the processing are determined by Union law or the national law of the Member States, the data controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided for in Union law or in the national law of the Member States.  
According to Chapter 2, Section 6 of the Patient Data Act (2008:355), PDL, a healthcare provider is the data controller for the processing of personal data that the healthcare provider carries out. In a region or municipality, each authority conducting healthcare is the data controller for the processing of personal data that the authority performs.  
The data controller shall, according to Article 5.2 of the GDPR, be responsible for and be able to demonstrate compliance with the principles in Article 5.1 (the principle of accountability).  
The data controller is responsible for implementing appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that processing is performed in accordance with the GDPR. The measures shall be implemented taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks, varying in probability and severity, to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. The measures shall be reviewed and updated as necessary. This is stated in Article 24.1 of the GDPR.  

Health Data  
Health data is defined in Article 4.15 of the GDPR as personal data concerning the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about the person's health status. Health data constitutes so-called sensitive personal data. It is prohibited to process such personal data according to Article 9.1 of the GDPR, unless the processing falls under one of the exceptions in Article 9.2 of the Regulation.

In Recital 35 of the General Data Protection Regulation, the following is stated. Personal data concerning health should include all information relating to the health status of a data subject that provides information about the data subject's past, present, or future physical or mental health condition. This includes data about the individual collected in connection with registration for or provision of health and medical services to the individual in accordance with European Parliament and Council Directive 2011/24/EU, a number, a symbol, or a characteristic assigned to the individual to identify them for health and medical purposes, data arising from tests or examinations of a body part or bodily substance, including genetic data and biological samples, and other information regarding, for example, illness, disability, disease risk, medical history, clinical treatment, or the data subject's physiological or biomedical condition, regardless of the source, such as from a doctor or other healthcare professional, a hospital, a medical device, or a diagnostic in vitro test.  
The Court of Justice of the European Union has established in the case of Lindqvist (C-101/01, EU:C:2003:596, para. 51) that information about a person having injured their foot and being partially sick-listed constitutes personal data concerning health according to the Data Protection Directive5 (the directive was repealed by the General Data Protection Regulation). The Court stated in the case that, considering the purpose of the Data Protection Directive, the term "data concerning health" should be given a broad interpretation and is to be understood as encompassing data relating to all aspects of a person's health, both physical and mental (see para. 50). Furthermore, in a later ruling, Vyriausioji tarnybinės etikos komisija (C-184/20, EU:C:2022:601), the Court found that the concept of sensitive personal data under Article 9.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation should be interpreted broadly and concluded that even personal data that indirectly reveals a natural person's sexual orientation constitutes sensitive personal data under the relevant provision.  
The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) has stated that the concept of health data under the General Data Protection Regulation must be interpreted broadly in light of, among other things, the Court of Justice's ruling in the case of Lindqvist and as it is evident from Recital 53 of the General Data Protection Regulation that health data deserves comprehensive protection6. IMY has, in a legal position statement, assessed that data regarding guardianship under the Parental Code constitutes health data (IMYRS 2022:3).  
Requirement for security in the processing of personal data  
According to Article 32.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, the data controller and the data processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk associated with the processing. This shall be done taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks, varying in likelihood and severity, to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.  
In assessing the appropriate level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks posed by the processing, especially from accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration, or unauthorized disclosure of or access to personal data that has been transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed. This is stated in Article 32.2 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

In Recital 75 of the General Data Protection Regulation, factors to be considered in assessing the risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons are outlined. Among other things, the loss of confidentiality concerning personal data subject to confidentiality obligations is mentioned, as well as whether the processing concerns data about health or sexual life. Furthermore, consideration should be given to whether the processing involves personal data about vulnerable individuals, particularly children, or whether the processing includes a large number of personal data and concerns a large number of data subjects.  
In Recital 76 of the General Data Protection Regulation, it is stated that the likelihood and severity of the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subject should be determined based on the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing. The risk should be assessed based on an objective evaluation, which determines whether the data processing involves a risk or a high risk.  
If the data controller engages a data processor to carry out processing, the data controller may only engage data processors that provide sufficient guarantees to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures. This should be done in such a way that the processing meets the requirements of the General Data Protection Regulation and that the rights of the data subject are protected. This is stated in Article 28.1 and Recital 81 of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
Assessment by the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection  
The investigation in the matter shows that the board has used a service for sending physical letters during the period from May 6, 2021, to July 6, 2022, which has resulted in certain summonses for medical visits being sent in windowed envelopes where information indicating that it is a summons, the patient's name and address, and the healthcare institution related to the visit were fully visible.  
The General Data Protection Regulation is applicable  
The information in the summonses that was visible through the windowed envelopes pertains to identified individuals. Thus, it concerns personal data. The board's processing of personal data consists of a series of actions where the board's disclosure of personal data by physical mail has been a part. The underlying part of the process, which includes sending summonses in digital format from Region Dalarna's medical records system, is automated. Therefore, IMY assesses that this constitutes a partially automated processing of personal data that falls within the scope of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
Data Controller  
The board has stated that it is the data controller for the processing of personal data related to summonses for medical visits within Region Dalarna that is subject to supervision, which is supported by the other investigation in the matter. IMY therefore assesses that the board is the data controller for the relevant processing.  
The processing has involved a high risk  
As the data controller, the board must, according to Article 32.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, take appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure an adequate level of security in relation to the risks associated with the processing of personal data. This also applies when personal data is processed by a data processor. IMY makes the following assessment of the risks associated with the relevant processing.

According to information from the board, summonses indicating the sending reception have been sent from the Child and Adolescent Medical Clinic in Mora, the Child and Adolescent Counseling Clinic in Falun, and the Sleep Laboratory in Avesta. The investigation in the matter shows that the clinics provide care for children and adolescents aged 0–17 years with illnesses and health issues that require more specialized knowledge and resources than what is normally available at a healthcare center, assistance for children and adolescents up to 17 years with mild to moderate mental health issues, and the investigation and treatment of various sleep disorders.  
IMY notes that the concept of health should be interpreted broadly and assesses that the care provided at the clinics in question is specific enough that a summons for a visit to any of these clinics can be considered to provide information about the individual's physical or mental health condition.  
In light of this, IMY assesses that the information regarding these healthcare facilities, which has been fully visible in the mailings, constitutes health data in the sense intended in Article 4.15 of the General Data Protection Regulation. The information is thus classified as sensitive personal data covered by the protection under Article 9.1 of the regulation.  
Furthermore, the information is protected within healthcare by confidentiality according to Chapter 25, Section 1 of the Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act (2009:400). Since two of the affected healthcare facilities only provide care for individuals who are 17 years or younger, it can also be noted that the information in certain cases has pertained to children, who are considered particularly worthy of protection under the General Data Protection Regulation. Additionally, this involves extensive processing that includes approximately 2,500 mailings from several different healthcare facilities.  
Against this background, IMY assesses that this is a processing that has involved a high risk and that therefore a strong protection has been required.  
The board has not taken sufficient security measures  
IMY notes that the mailings with summonses for visits to the Child and Adolescent Medical Clinic in Mora, the Child and Adolescent Counseling Clinic in Falun, and the Sleep Laboratory in Avesta have been conducted in such a way that the sensitive personal data has been fully visible to anyone who has come into contact with the letters. The information has been accessible to, for example, those who handle mail, those who share a household with the recipient, and those who have received a letter delivered to the wrong address.  
To achieve an appropriate level of security, the mailings should, according to IMY's assessment, have been conducted in such a way that the sensitive personal data was not visible. The board has thus failed to ensure the level of security required under Article 32.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
It can be noted that the board's view is indeed that it should not be apparent from the envelope which clinic a summons for a healthcare visit pertains to and that the board has therefore procured a service aimed at concealing the sending clinic. However, after IMY initiated supervision, the board conducted an investigation that showed that the relevant service only covered summonses of a maximum of five A4 pages and that other summonses were sent in envelopes that indicated which clinic the visit pertained to. IMY notes that it was the responsibility of the board as the data controller to ensure prior to the processing in question that the relevant supplementary service met the need to keep the information hidden from anyone other than the recipient of the letter.

Against this background, IMY assesses that the board has not taken sufficient measures to ensure a level of security that is appropriate in relation to the risk associated with the current processing. The board has therefore processed personal data in violation of Article 32.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).  

Choice of Intervention  
Applicable Provisions  
In the event of violations of the GDPR, IMY has a number of corrective powers available under Article 58.2 a–j of the GDPR, including reprimands, orders, and administrative fines.  

Article 83.2 of the GDPR states that IMY shall impose administrative fines in addition to or instead of the other measures referred to in Article 58.2, depending on the circumstances of the individual case. Member States may establish rules regarding whether and to what extent administrative fines may be imposed on public authorities. This is stated in Article 83.7 of the GDPR. According to Chapter 6, Section 2 of the Data Protection Act, IMY may impose fines on authorities for violations referred to in Articles 83.4, 83.5, and 83.6 of the GDPR, and that Articles 83.1, 83.2, and 83.3 of the Regulation shall then apply.  

Article 83.2 of the GDPR specifies the factors to be considered when deciding whether administrative fines should be imposed and when determining the amount of the fine. If it concerns a minor violation, IMY may issue a reprimand instead of imposing a fine, according to Recital 148 of the GDPR. The factors specified in Article 83.2 of the GDPR shall also be considered when determining the amount of the fine. Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines in each individual case is effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. This is stated in Article 83.1 of the GDPR.  

Fine to be Imposed  
IMY has concluded that the board has processed personal data in violation of Article 32.1 of the GDPR. IMY finds, upon a comprehensive assessment of the circumstances described under the heading "Amount of the Fine," that there are grounds to impose a fine on the board and that it is therefore not a minor violation that would warrant issuing a reprimand instead.  

Amount of the Fine  
For violations of, among other things, Article 32 of the GDPR, the fine for public authorities may amount to a maximum of 5,000,000 SEK. This is stated in Chapter 6, Section 2 of the Data Protection Act and Article 83.4 of the GDPR.  

In assessing the seriousness of the violation, IMY considers, in accordance with Article 83.2 g of the GDPR, that the processing has involved sensitive personal data concerning health and data about children, which are particularly worthy of protection under the GDPR.  

Furthermore, IMY considers what has emerged regarding the nature, severity, and duration of the violation based on what is stated in Article 83.2 a of the GDPR. It can be noted that the violation has lasted for a longer period of just over a year, from May 6, 2021, when the patient who filed the complaint that led to the supervision received their summons, until July 6, 2022, when the board took measures to conceal the sender's address on the envelope during the mailings. Given that the board has stated that it concerns approximately 2,500 summonses per year, it can also be noted that the violation affects a large number of registered individuals. Furthermore, the fact that the violation has occurred within healthcare – i.e., an operation where the registered patient is in a position of dependency and vulnerability in relation to the data controller – means that there are grounds to view the violation more seriously. However, IMY assesses that there are also factors that speak in the opposite direction when evaluating the seriousness of the violation. Firstly, it concerns physical handling and not digital handling, which would have posed a risk of greater and faster dissemination of the data. Distribution via regular mail is more limited and controlled than a transfer via the open network. Furthermore, it has emerged that the board identified the risk of exposing the relevant personal data during the mailings and therefore took certain measures to comply with the requirements and reduce the risks associated with the processing.  

Based on a comprehensive assessment of the circumstances in the case, IMY determines that the Health and Medical Care Board in Region Dalarna shall pay an administrative fine of 200,000 SEK.

How to Appeal  
If you wish to appeal the decision, you should write to the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection. Indicate in the letter which decision you are appealing and the change you are requesting. The appeal must be received by the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection no later than three weeks from the day the decision was announced. If the appeal has been submitted in a timely manner, the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection will forward it to the Administrative Court in Stockholm for consideration.  

You may email the appeal to the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection if it does not contain any privacy-sensitive personal data or information that may be subject to confidentiality. The authority's contact details are provided on the first page of the decision.
