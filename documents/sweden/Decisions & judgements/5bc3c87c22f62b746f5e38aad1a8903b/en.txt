Decision of the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection finds that the Municipal Executive Board of Kungälv Municipality is violating the permit requirement in Section 7 of the Camera Surveillance Act (2018:1200) by conducting camera surveillance without permission at the following locations during the specified time period.  
• Nordmannatorget from October 19, 2021, to May 15, 2023  
• Outdoor parking lot Vita fläcken from June 8, 2021, to May 15, 2023  
• Bandy rink Skarpe Nord from September 11, 2021, to May 15, 2023  
• Kongahälla Center from September 11, 2021, to May 15, 2023  
• Thorildskolan from August 1, 2018, to May 15, 2023  
• Outdoor swimming pool in Kungälv from August 1–November 7, 2018, May 13–September 1, 2019, June 10–August 29, 2020, May 21–September 4, 2021, and June 5–June 22, 2022.  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection issues a reprimand to the Municipal Executive Board of Kungälv Municipality according to Section 24 of the Camera Surveillance Act and Article 58.2 b of the General Data Protection Regulation for the violation.  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection prohibits the Municipal Executive Board of Kungälv Municipality under Section 24 of the Camera Surveillance Act and Article 58.2 f of the General Data Protection Regulation from conducting permit-required camera surveillance at Nordmannatorget, the outdoor parking lot Vita fläcken, the bandy rink Skarpe Nord, Kongahälla Center, Thorildskolan, and the outdoor swimming pool in Kungälv without a camera surveillance permit.  
Account of the case  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY) has received a complaint regarding camera surveillance in Kungälv Municipality. The complaint states that the municipality is conducting camera surveillance without permission at several locations accessible to the public.  
IMY has initiated supervision of the Municipal Executive Board of Kungälv Municipality (the Municipal Executive Board) to investigate whether the camera surveillance by the Municipal Executive Board at the public locations specified below is subject to a permit requirement according to Section 7 of the Camera Surveillance Act and – if a permit requirement is deemed to exist – whether the Municipal Executive Board has permission for the camera surveillance.  
The Camera Surveillance Act came into force on August 1, 2018. IMY's supervision concerns the period from August 1, 2018, to the date of this decision.  
What the Municipal Executive Board has stated  
The Municipal Executive Board is responsible for nine different camera systems at the public locations specified below in Kungälv. The Municipal Executive Board is the data controller for the surveillance. At three of these locations (places 1–3), surveillance is conducted without anonymization technology, and at six locations (places 4–9), surveillance is conducted with anonymization technology. Surveillance is conducted at the following locations.  
1. Parking garage Tre Kungar. Surveillance is conducted every day around the clock inside the parking garage.

Surveillance in Parking Garages and Art Hall (Locations 1–3)  
Regarding the surveillance at locations 1–3, the municipal council has stated the following. Surveillance is conducted without anonymization features. Since the surveillance in the parking garage Tre Kungar and the parking garage Palissaden is intended to prevent, deter, or detect criminal activity or to investigate or prosecute crimes, no permit is required. Regarding the surveillance in the Art Hall Mimers Hus, Kungälv Municipality has a valid permit for video surveillance from the County Administrative Board.  
Surveillance with Anonymization Technology (Locations 4–9)  
Regarding the surveillance at locations 4–9, the municipal council has stated that the surveillance is conducted using anonymization technology. The relevant technology – anonymization through pixelation – essentially means that foreground objects such as pedestrians, vehicles, and similar are replaced with a low-resolution version of the same object. The purpose of the surveillance is to determine whether individuals are present at a specific location for a certain period of time from anonymized video material and to automatically notify the alarm operator in undesirable situations. The alarm operator only accesses anonymized image material. The surveillance provides the alarm operator managing video alarms with information about the sequence of events in a particular situation so that they can determine whether security personnel need to be dispatched to the location. Anonymized material is saved and stored for three days. The original video is not recorded.  
Technical Description of "Anonymization through Pixelation"  
The technology replaces foreground objects with a number of larger pixels (square image elements) in a way that makes it impossible to identify the person in question. If the original video is in color, the color information of the foreground objects is eliminated and replaced with a grayscale version of the same object. The coarseness of the pixelation can be adjusted per camera.

The current cameras are set so that individuals' faces are covered by a maximum of 5x5 pixels, which is the default setting. All monitoring at locations 4–9 is conducted with the pixelation function activated. The cameras collect image material from the site, referred to as "original video." The low-resolution image is processed in software running on a server with a direct connection to the camera in a private network. Before the low-resolution image is processed, video data is only handled in the camera's RAM, network buffers, and similar, and only for a fraction of a second. No original video is stored on a hard drive, in a database, or any other more permanent storage location. Compared to other technologies, it is not the case that image capture with identifiable individuals is first saved and then anonymized – rather, the entire process of image capture and anonymization occurs simultaneously and concurrently. The software is a prerequisite for the camera to function as intended. It is not possible to capture image material through the camera without processing the original video via the software. The entire process takes between 0.02 to 0.1 seconds. Only after this process is the image capture available to humans. There is no technical possibility for an individual to access image capture that occurs before the process is completed. There is no way to restore the original video from the anonymized video.  

Assessment of the Municipal Executive Board regarding the identification of individuals  
The technology of anonymization through pixelation encompasses a variety of techniques to prevent the identification of individuals. These include reducing the resolution to a level so low that identification becomes impossible, removing color data to make large objects and clothing unrecognizable, and introducing "imprecision" in the image that makes precise measurements of object dimensions impossible. Therefore, individual persons cannot be identified based on video material where anonymization through pixelation has been applied, either manually or with technical methods. The Municipal Executive Board has, in this regard, referred to industry standards for video surveillance systems regarding the image resolution required to make a certain monitoring task, such as identification or recognition, possible.  

Assessment of the Municipal Executive Board regarding the obligation to obtain permission  
Camera surveillance conducted with the anonymization function activated does not constitute personal data processing according to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and does not constitute personal surveillance in the sense of the Camera Surveillance Act, as individual persons affected by the surveillance cannot be identified. Therefore, the GDPR and the Camera Surveillance Act are not applicable regarding the surveillance conducted at locations 4–9.  

The Municipal Executive Board has referred to a supervisory decision from the County Administrative Board of Västra Götaland, which according to the Municipal Executive Board and the alarm operator constitutes a precedent that surveillance with the anonymization technology does not constitute personal surveillance in the sense of the Camera Surveillance Act. The Municipal Executive Board states that the County Administrative Board's supervisory decision pertains to the same basic technology and settings for the anonymization function used for the surveillance at locations 4–9.

Justification of the Decision  
Applicable Provisions  
Article 4.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) states that any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person is considered personal data. According to Article 4.2 of the GDPR, processing refers to any operation or set of operations performed on personal data, such as collection, recording, storage, reading, and deletion.  

The term data controller, as defined in Article 4.7 of the GDPR, refers to a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data.  

The Camera Surveillance Act contains provisions regarding camera surveillance that complement the GDPR. The purpose of the Camera Surveillance Act is to meet the need for camera surveillance for legitimate purposes and to protect individuals against undue intrusion into their personal privacy during such surveillance (Section 2 of the Camera Surveillance Act).  

The concept of camera surveillance is defined in Section 3 of the Camera Surveillance Act. According to Section 3(1), camera surveillance refers to:  
- the use of a TV camera, another optical-electronic instrument, or comparable equipment,  
- the use of equipment that is operated without being manually controlled on-site,  
- the use of equipment in a manner that entails permanent or regularly repeated personal surveillance.  

Personal surveillance means that individuals can be identified through the surveillance. It is required that such characteristics can be observed that allow for distinguishing the observed individuals from others with a reasonable degree of certainty. This is the case if the entire person or the person's face is clearly visible. Distinctive clothing, specific body movements, or particular body constitution can also enable identification (Prop. 2017/18:231 pp. 41 and 137). Images from so-called thermal cameras can be sufficiently clear such that specific characteristics indicate that it may involve personal surveillance. Surveillance with still cameras can also constitute personal surveillance even if body movements cannot be observed (cf. the Administrative Court of Appeal in Stockholm's judgment on November 7, 2007, in case no. 6613-07).  

Permanent personal surveillance refers to, for example, surveillance with a camera that is placed at or directed towards a location where people normally gather for an extended period. Regularly repeated personal surveillance means personal surveillance occurring on multiple occasions.

The requirement for regularity implies that it primarily concerns occasions that are relatively close in time. However, monitoring at occasions that are more spread out can also be considered regular if it involves systematic use of monitoring equipment (Prop. 2017/18:231 p. 137).  
According to Section 7 of the Camera Surveillance Act, permission is required for camera surveillance of a location accessible to the public, if the surveillance is to be conducted by an authority, or by someone other than an authority in the performance of a task of public interest arising from law or other regulation, collective agreement, or decision made under the authority of law or other regulation.  
According to Section 8, third paragraph 2 of the Camera Surveillance Act, when assessing the individual's interest in not being monitored, particular consideration must be given to whether technology that promotes the protection of the individual's personal integrity will be used.  
According to Section 9, 8 of the Camera Surveillance Act, permission is not required for camera surveillance in a parking garage if the surveillance aims to prevent, hinder, or detect criminal activity or to investigate or prosecute crimes.  
Data Protection Responsibility  
The Municipal Executive Board has stated that it is the data controller for the processing of personal data that occurs through camera surveillance at locations 1–9 in Kungälv. What the Municipal Executive Board states is supported by the investigation in the matter. IMY therefore assesses that the Municipal Executive Board is the data controller for the relevant processing.  
Surveillance in Parking Garages and Art Hall (Locations 1–3)  
The surveillance in the art hall Mimers Hus, the parking garage Tre Kungar, and the parking garage Palissaden is conducted without anonymization technology. The investigation shows that the surveillance constitutes such camera surveillance as referred to in Section 3 of the Camera Surveillance Act. IMY further notes that since the Municipal Executive Board is an authority and the surveillance takes place in locations accessible to the public, permission is required as a general rule according to Section 7 of the Camera Surveillance Act.  
The surveillance in the parking garage Tre Kungar and the parking garage Palissaden (locations 1 and 2) occurs inside the parking garages and aims to prevent, hinder, or detect criminal activity or to investigate or prosecute crimes. Such surveillance is exempt from the requirement for permission according to Section 9, 8 of the Camera Surveillance Act. Surveillance at locations 1 and 2 is therefore not subject to permission.  
Regarding the surveillance in the art hall Mimers Hus (location 3), Kungälv Municipality has been granted permission for camera surveillance under earlier legislation. IMY notes that the permission according to the transitional provisions of the Camera Surveillance Act is still valid. The Municipal Executive Board therefore has permission for the surveillance at location 3.  
In light of this, IMY concludes that the Municipal Executive Board's camera surveillance at locations 1–3 either has permission or is exempt from the requirement for permission. There is therefore no reason to further investigate the camera surveillance of these locations.

Monitoring with Anonymization Technology (Locations 4–9)  
The Camera Surveillance Act is applicable to the monitoring  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY) notes that the investigation shows that the camera surveillance of locations 4–9 is conducted using equipment that falls under the definition of a television camera, other optical instruments, or comparable equipment according to Section 3, Subsection 1 of the Camera Surveillance Act. It is further indicated that the camera equipment is permanently installed at the relevant locations. The monitoring is conducted automatically, and the equipment is therefore not operated on-site.  
Crucial to the question of whether the municipal council's use of cameras constitutes camera surveillance in the legal sense is whether the cameras are used in a manner that implies permanent or regularly repeated monitoring of individuals. Individual monitoring refers to the ability to identify persons based on the surveillance.  

The municipal council has stated that the purpose of the monitoring is not to identify individual persons, but only to see if someone is present at a certain location and to notify the alarm operator in unwanted situations. IMY notes that the purpose of the use of the camera equipment is irrelevant to the question of the applicability of the Camera Surveillance Act. The law is applicable both in cases where cameras are used to monitor people and in cases where cameras are used for other purposes but where people simultaneously enter the camera's field of view for an extended period or somewhat regularly in a manner that makes them identifiable. Therefore, what the municipal council has stated regarding the purpose of the monitoring is of no significance for the assessment.  

Regarding the use of the camera equipment, the investigation shows that it occurs first through the recording of image data (the original video) and then through the transfer of the original video to a server. On the server, software processing takes place, resulting in processed image material (pixelated images). The pixelated images are then stored for three days.  

The municipal council believes that the assessment of whether the camera usage constitutes individual monitoring should be made based on the pixelated images and not on the original video. However, IMY notes that the recording of the original video, the transfer of the original video to the server, and the processing of the original video all constitute the use of equipment covered by the Camera Surveillance Act. The investigation indicates that the original video has such quality that it is possible to identify individual persons. While access to the original video is prevented through technical measures, this does enhance privacy protection but does not mean that the processing ceases to constitute personal data processing or individual monitoring. The processing of the original video is thus both personal data processing according to the General Data Protection Regulation and individual monitoring in the sense of the Camera Surveillance Act.  

Since the individual monitoring concerns locations where individuals normally reside and regularly enter the camera's field of view, the monitoring is permanent and regularly repeated. The municipal council's use of cameras through the recording of the original video, transfer to the server, and processing of the video on the server thus constitutes camera surveillance in the sense intended in Section 3 of the Camera Surveillance Act.

Regarding the assessment of whether the processing of the pixelated images also constitutes personal surveillance, IMY notes that the relevant software technology indeed complicates identification. The images, after pixelation, have a low resolution that reduces the ability to discern a person's facial features. Furthermore, color information about individuals in the images has been eliminated. However, this is not a complete masking where only outlines can be distinguished, as individuals in the images are presented in grayscale. Several factors are significant in assessing whether identification can occur, and a person may be identifiable based on one or more characteristics. The pixelated images can still provide information about, for example, the type of clothing the person is wearing or whether they are using aids such as a wheelchair, crutches, or a walker. The investigation shows that the cameras can observe at least such types of distinctive clothing, special body movements, or particular body constitution that allow for distinguishing the observed individuals from others with a high degree of certainty. IMY particularly emphasizes that the surveillance at Thorildskolan concerns a limited group of students and staff, which may facilitate identification. It can also be noted that the pixelated images are stored for three days, which means greater possibilities for identifying individuals compared to if the surveillance had only occurred in real-time. Overall, IMY assesses that the camera images, despite the use of the described software, are sufficiently clear for individuals passing by the cameras to potentially be identified. Thus, it is a matter of personal surveillance.  
Although not all individuals affected by the surveillance will be identifiable, IMY assesses that identification can occur regularly. Given that it concerns systematic use of camera equipment in places where many people gather, IMY assesses that the personal surveillance is enduring and regularly repeated. Against this background, IMY concludes that the pixelated image material also constitutes camera surveillance in the sense intended in Section 3 of the Camera Surveillance Act.  
The Camera Surveillance Act is thus applicable regarding the processing of pixelated image material.  
In summary, IMY assesses that both the recording and processing of the original video and the processing of the pixelated images fall under the Camera Surveillance Act.  
The technology used by the municipal council at locations 4–9 does not therefore mean that the surveillance falls outside the scope of the Camera Surveillance Act. However, the use of the technology means that the privacy risks associated with personal data processing and camera surveillance are reduced. It is thus a matter of such privacy-enhancing technology that, according to Section 8, third paragraph, 2 of the Camera Surveillance Act, should be particularly considered during a permit assessment.  
The municipal council lacks a permit for camera surveillance  
The municipal council has stated that, in light of the county administrative board's decision, information from IMY, and data from the alarm operator, it had reason to believe that the Camera Surveillance Act is not applicable when surveillance is conducted with the current anonymization technology.  
Whether a specific use of cameras constitutes permit-required camera surveillance or not is determined by IMY after an assessment in an individual case. Neither IMY nor the county administrative board

Choice of Intervention  
Applicable Provisions  
According to Section 24 of the Camera Surveillance Act, the provisions regarding the supervisory authority's powers in the General Data Protection Regulation shall also apply in the supervision of compliance with the Camera Surveillance Act.  
According to Section 25(1), the supervisory authority may impose an administrative fine on anyone conducting camera surveillance who violates the permit requirement in Section 7.  
When deciding on an administrative fine, according to Section 26 of the Camera Surveillance Act, Articles 83.1, 83.2, and 83.3 of the General Data Protection Regulation and Sections 4–7 of Chapter 6 of the Data Protection Act14 shall apply.  
Section 27 of the Camera Surveillance Act states that in cases of violations referred to in Sections 25(1)–(3), the amount of the fine shall be determined in accordance with Article 83.4 of the General Data Protection Regulation or, in the case of authorities, in accordance with the lower fee level in Section 2, second paragraph of Chapter 6 of the Data Protection Act.  
Articles 58.2 and 83.2 of the General Data Protection Regulation indicate that IMY has the authority to impose administrative fines in accordance with Article 83. Depending on the circumstances of the individual case, administrative fines shall be imposed in addition to or instead of the other measures referred to in Article

If it concerns a minor violation, IMY may, as stated in Recital 148, instead of imposing a fine, issue a reprimand according to Article 58.2 b. Consideration should be given to aggravating and mitigating circumstances in the case, such as the nature, severity, and duration of the violation, as well as any relevant prior violations.  
IMY's Assessment  
IMY has determined that the municipal council, by conducting video surveillance at locations 4–9, is in violation of the permit requirement in Section 7 of the Camera Surveillance Act. A violation of that provision may lead to an administrative fine.  
The video surveillance is conducted in areas accessible to the public and involves a large number of people from an indeterminate group, constituting an intrusion into their personal privacy. The surveillance has been ongoing for an extended period. In the supervisory matter, it has not emerged that video surveillance is no longer ongoing at all locations except for the outdoor swimming pool (location 9).  
The surveillance concerns various types of locations where the interest in privacy is differently pronounced. The intrusion into privacy from nighttime video surveillance in parking lots is generally considered limited, as few individuals are affected by the surveillance. However, in the case of schools and schoolyards, video surveillance often represents a significant intrusion into privacy. Children are, according to the General Data Protection Regulation, a particularly protected group. A schoolyard is a place for recreation where children and young people gather both during and after school hours. The school is also a workplace where students and teachers must be present. Video surveillance during operational hours is therefore particularly sensitive. Against this background, IMY assesses it as aggravating that the municipal council has conducted round-the-clock video surveillance at Thorildskolan for several years without permission.  
However, IMY assesses that the image processing software – anonymization through pixelation – used by the municipal council reduces the intrusion into privacy compared to if high-resolution images had been processed and stored throughout the entire process. The municipal council's use of privacy-enhancing technology is a mitigating circumstance.  
IMY assesses that the violation of Section 7 of the Camera Surveillance Act identified in this decision is of such a scope and severity that it would normally lead to an administrative fine.  
In this supervisory matter, however, there are special circumstances that argue against an administrative fine. The municipal council has referred to a supervisory decision from the County Administrative Board of Västra Götaland, which at that time was the competent supervisory authority, as support for the assertion that surveillance with anonymization technology does not constitute personal surveillance in the sense of the Camera Surveillance Act. The municipal council states that the County Administrative Board's supervisory decision pertains to the same basic technology and settings for the anonymization function used for surveillance at locations 4–9. IMY notes that the County Administrative Board's decision has given the municipal council some basis for the belief that the video surveillance was not subject to a permit requirement. This has resulted in uncertainty regarding how the regulation should be interpreted.  

The preparatory works for the Camera Surveillance Act indicate that there may be cases where it is unclear how the Camera Surveillance Act should be interpreted. In such cases, the party that has made an incorrect interpretation of the regulation should first be given the opportunity to rectify the violation through, for example, an order or a warning from the supervisory authority. Only if such a warning or order is not complied with should administrative fines be considered. IMY notes that this concerns the application of a provision on administrative fines that has been incorporated into national law (Section 26 of the Camera Surveillance Act) and that the preparatory statements are therefore guiding in the interpretation of the provision.  
IMY finds, upon a comprehensive assessment of the circumstances, that particularly considering what has been stated in the preparatory works for the Camera Surveillance Act, there is reason to refrain from imposing an administrative fine on the municipal council for the identified violation. The municipal council shall therefore, based on Section 24 of the Camera Surveillance Act and Article 58.2 b of the General Data Protection Regulation, instead be given a reprimand for the identified violation.  
The video surveillance constitutes an intrusion into the privacy of individuals affected by the surveillance, and it is imperative that the video surveillance ceases. IMY therefore assesses that there are grounds to prohibit the municipal council under Article 58.2 f of the General Data Protection Regulation and Section 24 of the Camera Surveillance Act from conducting permit-required video surveillance at Nordmannatorget, the outdoor parking area Vita fläcken, the bandy rink Skarpe Nord, Kongahälla Center, Thorildskolan, and the outdoor swimming pool in Kungälv without a video surveillance permit.

How to Appeal  
If you wish to appeal the decision, you must write to the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection. In your letter, specify which decision you are appealing and the change you are requesting. The appeal must be received by the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection no later than three weeks from the date the decision was communicated. If the appeal is submitted in a timely manner, the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection will forward it to the Administrative Court in Stockholm for consideration.  
You may email the appeal to the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection if it does not contain any sensitive personal data or information that may be subject to confidentiality. The authority's contact details are provided on the first page of the decision.
