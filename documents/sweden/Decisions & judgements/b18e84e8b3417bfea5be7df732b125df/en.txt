3. Justification of the Decision  
3.1 Which Legislation Applies to the Current Video Surveillance?  
Video surveillance typically involves the processing of personal data. The extent to which video surveillance is permitted is regulated by the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the Camera Surveillance Act.  
3.1.1 General Data Protection Regulation  
According to Article 2.1 of the GDPR, the regulation applies to the processing of personal data that is wholly or partly carried out by automated means. Article 4.1 of the GDPR states that any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person is considered personal data. According to Article 4.2, processing refers to any operation or combination of operations performed on personal data, such as collection, recording, storage, reading, and deletion.  
If a surveillance camera films an identifiable person, or any other personal data, processing of personal data occurs, and the rules of the GDPR must be followed. IMY notes that the company's surveillance cameras film identifiable individuals and that the provisions of the GDPR thus apply regarding the current processing of personal data.  
The data controller must identify the legal basis in Article 6 of the GDPR before initiating video surveillance and is responsible—both towards the data subjects and in relation to the supervisory authority—for ensuring compliance with the principles of personal data processing in Article 5 of the GDPR.  
The principle of accountability in Article 5.2 of the GDPR means that the data controller must be able to demonstrate compliance with Article 5.1, which implies that the application of the GDPR often needs to be documented.  
3.1.2 Camera Surveillance Act  
The Camera Surveillance Act (2018:1200) is a supplementary law in relation to the GDPR. Section 3, paragraph 1 of the Camera Surveillance Act states that video surveillance refers to a television camera, another optical-electronic instrument, or comparable equipment that, without being operated on-site, is used in such a way as to involve permanent or regularly repeated monitoring of individuals.  
The current video surveillance is not operated on-site and involves permanent monitoring of residents and other visitors in the property. IMY assesses that the Camera Surveillance Act is also applicable to the current surveillance.  
3.2 Is the Company the Data Controller?  
According to Article 4.7 of the GDPR, the data controller is the natural or legal person who, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data. The crucial factor in determining who is the data controller is who has decided why and how the processing takes place, as well as who exercises influence over which personal data is collected and processed, how long it is stored, and who has access. 3.3 Has the video surveillance had a legal basis under the GDPR?  
For the processing of personal data to be lawful, there must be support in at least one of the legal bases specified in Article 6.1 of the GDPR. The company has stated that the current video surveillance is supported by the legal basis specified in Article 6.1 f of the GDPR, which is the so-called balancing of interests. There are three conditions that must all be met for the video surveillance to be carried out with support from Article 6.1 f of the GDPR: The data controller must be able to demonstrate 1) that there is a legitimate interest, 2) that the current processing of personal data is necessary to achieve that interest, and 3) that the legitimate interest (the surveillance interest) outweighs the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subjects (the privacy interest) in the specific context.4  

3.3.1 Is there a legitimate interest?  
For the interest that the surveillance aims to protect to be considered legitimate, it generally needs to be supported by relevant national law or EU law. A housing cooperative that wants to protect the property, health, and lives of its members can be considered to have such a legitimate interest.5 Protecting property, health, and lives can constitute a legitimate interest for video surveillance, provided that it concerns a real interest and not an interest that is fictitious or speculative at the time of the personal data processing.6 This generally means that there must have been crimes or other incidents that create a concrete need for surveillance.  
The company has indicated that the purpose of the video surveillance is to increase the safety and security of the tenants, prevent and investigate crimes, and protect the property. The company has also stated that the surveillance is conducted for deterrent purposes. The surveillance complicates attempts at burglary in apartments, attics, and basements because individuals are monitored on their way out with potentially stolen goods. The investigation shows that since 2019, there have been incidents including car break-ins, theft, vandalism, graffiti, burglary, and attempted burglary. IMY assesses that the company has a legitimate interest in conducting video surveillance to increase the safety and security of the tenants, prevent and investigate crimes, and protect property.  

3.3.2 Is the video surveillance necessary to achieve the legitimate interest?  
For the video surveillance to be deemed necessary, the legitimate interest cannot reasonably be protected in an equally effective manner by other means that are less intrusive.

3.4 Has the company fulfilled the requirement for information and its obligation to inform?  
3.4.1 The Requirement for Information under the Camera Surveillance Act  
Section 15 of the Camera Surveillance Act establishes a requirement that information about camera surveillance must be provided through clear signage or in some other effective manner. The provision concerns the actual use of camera surveillance equipment and not the processing of personal data that the surveillance entails.12 The provision does not contain any requirements regarding what information must be provided, and the obligation to inform can thus be considered fulfilled if the entity conducting the surveillance clearly informs that camera surveillance is taking place at a certain location.  
The investigation in the case shows that there are signs on entrance doors, at the gate and door, as well as on the facade. The company has submitted a drawing of the basement level including the garage and a drawing of all stairwells (A, B, and C). The drawings indicate that signs are placed at all entrances to the property.  
IMY assesses that the company has provided information about the surveillance through clear signage and thereby fulfilled the information requirement in Section 15 of the Camera Surveillance Act.  
3.4.2 The Obligation to Inform under the General Data Protection Regulation  
IMY must also assess whether the company has met its obligations to inform about the processing of personal data according to Article 13 of the General Data Protection Regulation. Article 13 of the General Data Protection Regulation regulates what information must be provided when personal data is collected from the data subject. In the case of camera surveillance, personal data is considered to be collected from the data subject.13

The signs regarding camera surveillance have been updated during the supervision, as has the website with information about the camera surveillance. Even though the new signs and the website contain more information than before, IMY finds that there are still certain deficiencies in the first layer of information. The camera surveillance signs lack information about the identity of the data controller (the company) and contact details for the company, in the form of an email address or phone number. According to IMY, this information can be added to the existing signs with relatively simple means. IMY assesses that the information provided does not meet all the requirements under Articles 13.1 and 13.2 of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
IMY therefore concludes that the company has processed personal data in violation of Article 13 of the General Data Protection Regulation since November 1, 2022.  
3.5 Choice of Intervention  
IMY has a number of corrective powers in the event of violations of the General Data Protection Regulation, including reprimands, orders, and administrative fines. This follows from Article 58.2 a–j of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
IMY shall impose administrative fines in addition to or instead of other corrective measures referred to in Article 58.2 of the General Data Protection Regulation, depending on the circumstances of each individual case. If it concerns a minor violation, the supervisory authority may, according to Recital 148 of the General Data Protection Regulation, issue a reprimand instead of imposing an administrative fine. Consideration shall be given to aggravating and mitigating circumstances in the case, such as the nature, severity, and duration of the violation, as well as previous violations of relevance.  
Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines in each individual case is effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. This is stated in Article 83.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation. Article 83.2 specifies the factors that should be considered.

The rules for group liability in EU competition law revolve around the concept of an economic unit. A parent company and a subsidiary are considered part of the same economic unit when the parent company exerts decisive influence over the subsidiary. Decisive influence (i.e., control) can be achieved either through ownership or through agreement. Case law indicates that 100 percent or nearly 100 percent ownership creates a presumption that control is deemed to exist. However, this presumption can be rebutted if the undertaking provides sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the subsidiary operates independently in the market.16  
3.5.1 Fine to be imposed  
IMY has found that the company has violated Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation by conducting surveillance without legal basis on the company's property at Limhamnsvägen 22a in Malmö during the period from November 1, 2022, to December 11, 2024. The company has further processed personal data in violation of Article 13 of the General Data Protection Regulation by failing to meet the requirement to inform the data subjects regarding the surveillance.  
IMY notes that the processing of personal data through surveillance of individuals in direct connection with their homes has occurred without legal basis. Against this background, IMY assesses that this is not a case of minor infringements as referred to in Recital 148 of the General Data Protection Regulation. IMY thus finds that a fine cannot be replaced by a reprimand.

3.5.3 Order  
According to Article 58.2 d, the supervisory authority has the power to order a data controller to ensure that processing is carried out in accordance with the provisions of the data protection regulation and, if necessary, in a specific manner and within a specific period.  
Since the surveillance, as far as can be seen from the investigation, is still ongoing, it is important that the company ceases the surveillance that is not permitted. There are therefore grounds to order the company, pursuant to Article 58.2 d of the data protection regulation, to cease video surveillance of all locations in the property, except for the garage. According to IMY, it is also important that the company takes measures to ensure that the registered individuals receive correct and complete information regarding the personal data processing through video surveillance that occurs. IMY decides, based on Article 58.2 d of the data protection regulation, that the company shall be ordered to, no later than four weeks after this decision has become final, take measures to ensure that  
1. the company ceases video surveillance of all locations in the property except for the garage,  
2. the video surveillance signs contain information about the company's identity and contact details, in the form of an email address or phone number.  
The requirement for action to be taken no later than four weeks after this decision has become final means that if the decision is not appealed, action must be taken no later than four weeks after the appeal period has expired.

4. How to Appeal  
If you wish to appeal the decision, you must write to IMY. In your letter, specify which decision you are appealing and the change you are requesting. The appeal must be received by IMY no later than three weeks from the day you were informed of the decision. However, if you are a party representing the public interest, the appeal must be submitted within three weeks from the day the decision was announced.
