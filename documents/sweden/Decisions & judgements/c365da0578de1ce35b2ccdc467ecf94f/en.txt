Decision of the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection finds that Verifiera AB, during the period from April 6, 2022, to June 28, 2022, has processed sensitive personal data (data concerning health) in violation of Article 9 of the General Data Protection Regulation1 in its services on www.verifiera.se.  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection issues a reprimand to Verifiera AB pursuant to Article 58.2 b of the General Data Protection Regulation for the established violation.  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection orders Verifiera AB, pursuant to Article 58.2 d of the General Data Protection Regulation, to take measures so that it is no longer possible for users of the services to access decisions in cases under the Act (1991:1128) on Involuntary Psychiatric Care or the Act (1988:870) on Care of Substance Abusers in Certain Cases concerning the searched person when searching for individuals using any of the search parameters name, personal identification number, or address. The measures must be taken no later than eight weeks after this decision has become final.  

Report on the Supervision Case  
The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY) has received complaints regarding Verifiera AB's (Verifiera or the company) services. IMY has subsequently initiated supervision of Verifiera on its own initiative based on the description of its services (hereinafter "the Services") provided by the company on its website www.verifiera.se (hereinafter "the Website").2  
The purpose of the supervision was to investigate whether Verifiera, through the provision of the Services:  
• 
discloses sensitive personal data in a manner referred to in Chapter 1, Section 20 of the Freedom of the Press Act (YGL),  
• 
processes personal data in a manner consistent with the principles of legality, fairness, transparency, purpose limitation, and data minimization (Article 5 of the General Data Protection Regulation),  
• 
has support in any legal basis for the processing of personal data (Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation), and  
• 
processes health data, i.e., sensitive personal data as referred to in Article 9.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, and if so, whether any of the exceptions in Article 9.2 of the General Data Protection Regulation from the prohibition on processing such data is applicable.  
The supervision has been conducted through a review of the information that Verifiera has provided about the services on the Website and correspondence.

The wording of Chapter 1, Section 20 of the YGL indicates that the YGL does not prevent regulations from being issued in law prohibiting the disclosure of personal data. Purpose-interpretation of a constitutional law is not possible.

Justification of the decision  
Legal background  
EU law's regulation of the relationship between the right to protection of personal data and the right to freedom of expression and information  
The purpose of the General Data Protection Regulation is to protect personal integrity in the processing of personal data and to harmonize data protection regulations to enable the free flow of personal data within the EU.4 The General Data Protection Regulation specifies the fundamental right to the protection of personal data established in Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (hereinafter the Charter).5 According to Article 8.1 of the Charter, everyone has the right to the protection of personal data concerning him or her. According to Article 8.2, personal data must be processed lawfully for specified purposes and on the basis of the data subject's consent or another legitimate and lawful basis. Everyone has the right to access data collected concerning him or her and to have it rectified. Article 8.3 states that an independent authority shall monitor compliance with these rules. A right that is closely linked to the right to protection of personal data is the right to respect for private and family life, as established in Article 7 of the Charter.  

Article 11 of the Charter establishes the right to freedom of expression and information. It states that everyone has the right to freedom of expression. This right includes freedom to hold opinions and to receive and impart information and ideas without interference by public authority and regardless of frontiers.  
Neither the right to protection of personal data nor the right to freedom of expression and information are absolute rights. Article 52.1 of the Charter indicates that limitations on the exercise of the rights and freedoms recognized in the Charter must be provided for by law and must be compatible with the essential content of those rights and freedoms. Furthermore, it is stated that limitations, taking into account the principle of proportionality, may only be made if they are necessary and genuinely meet objectives of general interest recognized by the Union or the need to protect the rights and freedoms of others.  

The General Data Protection Regulation has been designed with consideration for other freedoms and rights than the right to protection of personal data, including freedom of expression and information.6 The task of reconciling these two rights has primarily been entrusted to the Member States within the framework of the regulation in Article 85.7 In Article 85.1

The General Data Protection Regulation stipulates an obligation for member states to reconcile the right to privacy under the data protection regulation with freedom of expression and information, including processing that occurs for journalistic purposes or for academic, artistic, or literary creation. According to Article 85.2, member states shall establish exceptions or deviations from Chapter II (principles), Chapter III (the rights of the data subject), Chapter IV (the data controller and the data processor), Chapter V (transfer of personal data to third countries or international organizations), Chapter VI (independent supervisory authorities), Chapter VII (cooperation and consistency), and Chapter IX (specific situations for the processing of personal data) for processing that occurs for journalistic purposes or for academic, artistic, or literary creation, if these are necessary to reconcile the right to privacy with freedom of expression and information. Article 52 of the Charter and Article 85 of the General Data Protection Regulation thus set limits on how member states may reconcile the right to the protection of personal data with the right to freedom of expression and information. The reconciliation of rights means that one of the rights should not be given general precedence over the other. Furthermore, exceptions to the right to the protection of personal data under the data protection regulation may only occur if the exceptions are necessary to reconcile the rights. The Court of Justice of the European Union has stated that to make a balanced assessment between fundamental rights, exceptions and limitations regarding the protection of personal data must not go beyond what is strictly necessary. This statement concerned Directive 95/46/EC, which was replaced by the General Data Protection Regulation, but is, according to IMY's assessment, relevant also in relation to the General Data Protection Regulation.  

The Court of Justice of the European Union has also established that to take into account the significance of freedom of expression in democratic societies, the concepts associated with it, including journalism, should be interpreted broadly. This means, among other things, that exceptions from and limitations of data protection regulation should not only apply to media companies but to all individuals engaged in journalistic activities. From the court's case law, it is further evident that "journalistic activity" is such activity aimed at disseminating information, opinions, or ideas to the public, regardless of the medium through which this occurs.  

The Swedish Data Protection Act's exceptions for freedom of expression and information  
In Swedish law, provisions based on Article 85 of the General Data Protection Regulation have been issued in Chapter 1, Section 7 of the Act (2018:218) with supplementary provisions to the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter the Data Protection Act).  

In Chapter 1, Section 7, first paragraph of the Data Protection Act, exceptions are made for such processing covered by the Freedom of the Press Act (TF) and the Fundamental Law on Freedom of Expression (YGL), hereinafter collectively referred to as the media fundamental laws. In the mentioned provision, it is stated that the General Data Protection Regulation and the Data Protection Act shall not apply to the extent that it would conflict with TF or YGL. According to the preparatory works, it is thereby clarified that the media fundamental laws take precedence over the provisions of the General Data Protection Regulation and the Data Protection Act.

The media fundamental laws provide extensive protection for freedom of expression and information. For example, according to the so-called instruction, those who are to judge cases of abuse of press and freedom of expression – or otherwise oversee compliance with the fundamental laws – must always keep in mind that press and freedom of expression are the foundations of a free society, always paying more attention to the subject and thought than to the legality of the expression, as well as the purpose more than the manner of presentation, and in doubtful cases, prefer to acquit rather than convict (Chapter 1, Section 10 of the Freedom of the Press Act (TF) and Chapter 1, Sections 15 of the Fundamental Law on Freedom of Expression (YGL)). Furthermore, an authority may not prohibit or hinder such provision based on its content, without support in the fundamental law, the so-called prohibition against hindrance (Chapter 1, Section 11 of the YGL). Additionally, an authority may not intervene against anyone for abuse of freedom of expression unless there is support for the intervention in the fundamental law (Chapter 1, Section 14 of the YGL).  
In Chapter 1, Section 7, second paragraph of the Data Protection Act, exceptions are made for freedom of expression and information outside the area protected by the fundamental law. The exception covers the processing of personal data that occurs for journalistic purposes or for academic, artistic, or literary creation. This may, for example, involve journalistic activities conducted in blog form but without a publishing certificate. The provision states that Articles 5–30 and 35–50 of the EU Data Protection Regulation and Chapters 2–5 of the Data Protection Act shall not apply to processing that occurs for, among other things, journalistic purposes. The exception means that the provisions of the Data Protection Regulation and the Data Protection Act regarding supervision, remedies, liability, and sanctions are practically applicable only concerning supervision over or violations of the provisions on the security of personal data.13  
Fundamental Law Regulation on Voluntary Publishing Certificates  
For the exception in Chapter 1, Section 7, first paragraph of the Data Protection Act to be applicable, it is required that the processing is covered by fundamental law protection according to the media fundamental laws. Publications on the internet generally fall outside the scope of application of the YGL.14 This means that the Data Protection Regulation is normally applicable to such processing. However, exceptions to the main rule are made through, among other things, the database rule (Chapter 1, Section 4 of the YGL). For the processing of personal data covered by the database rule, the Data Protection Regulation, according to Chapter 1, Section 7, first paragraph of the Data Protection Act, shall not apply to the extent that it would conflict with the Freedom of Expression Act.  
The database rule provides, under certain conditions, fundamental law protection for statements made through provisions to the public from databases. What is typically meant is provisions of stored information from websites upon request. For certain actors, the fundamental law protection applies automatically, that is, without any special action needing to be taken. This is the case for editorial offices of periodical publications and editorial offices for programs. Other traditional mass media companies, such as book publishers that publish printed books and news agencies, also have automatic fundamental law protection for their databases. Other actors have the opportunity to apply for a publishing certificate from the Swedish Authority for Press, Radio, and TV and thereby obtain a so-called voluntary fundamental law protection (cf. Chapter 1, Section 4, first paragraph 1 d and Section 5 of the YGL). When applying for a publishing certificate, there is no examination of the content or purpose of the database.

Interpretation of Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL and IMY's Authority  
According to Chapter 1, Section 20, first paragraph, item 2 of YGL, the provisions in YGL do not prevent regulations in law from being issued regarding prohibitions on the publication of personal data concerning, among other things, health. The first question that IMY must address is therefore whether the provisions of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) constitute such regulations regarding prohibitions issued in law as referred to in Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL.  

IMY assesses that the term "regulations in law" in Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL encompasses EU regulations and that the prohibitions referred to in Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL include not only outright prohibitions on the publication of personal data but also regulations that are less intrusive and that limit the possibility of publishing personal data. IMY makes this assessment for the following reasons.  

When the term "law" is used in Swedish legislation in the manner that occurs in Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL, it typically includes EU regulations without being explicitly stated in the text of the law.25 The preparatory works for Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL indicate that this provision should also be interpreted in this way. It is stated that EU regulations are equated with law, according to the practice developed in connection with the existing delegation provision.26 Furthermore, it is stated that the provision also includes a possibility to prescribe measures that are less intrusive than prohibitions.27 Similar statements are made in the legislative matter regarding consequential amendments in ordinary law to the changes in the media fundamental laws. It is indicated that the provisions of the GDPR and the Data Protection Act would regulate the collections of personal data that are covered by the proposed exceptions in the Freedom of the Press Act and the Fundamental Law on Freedom of Expression (i.e., Chapter 1, Section 13 of TF and Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL).28 In the bill prompted by the report from the 2018 Freedom of the Press and Freedom of Expression Committee, it is also stated that the provisions of the GDPR constitute such regulations in law as referred to in Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL.29 This means that the GDPR should be applied in cases where the conditions in Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL are met. In other words, the exception provision in Chapter 1, Section 7, first paragraph of the Data Protection Act is not applicable to such processing since an application of the GDPR would not conflict with YGL.  

IMY is the supervisory authority according to the GDPR and thus has the authority to supervise such processing that is exempted from constitutional protection according to Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL.30 This means that IMY has the authority to assess whether Chapter 1, Section 20 of YGL is applicable.

Personal data about health is disclosed.  
IMY first notes that the data included in the Services is disclosed in the manner referred to in Chapter 1, Section 20 of the YGL. The next question is whether the data provided by Verifiera through the Services contains such personal data as specified in Chapter 1, Section 20 of the YGL first paragraph. As mentioned above, IMY has limited its review to decisions in cases in the general administrative courts according to the Act on Compulsory Psychiatric Care (LPT), so-called psychiatric cases, and according to the Act on Care of Abusers in Certain Cases (LVM), so-called LVM cases. According to the preparatory works, the concept of personal data about health has been taken from Article 9.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation and should be given the same meaning as in the regulation.32  
Data about health is defined in Article 4.15 of the General Data Protection Regulation as personal data concerning the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which provides information about that person's health status.  
The Court of Justice of the European Union has established that the special categories of personal data specified in Article 9 of the General Data Protection Regulation, including data about health, should be given a broad interpretation. According to the Court of Justice, it is sufficient that the data indirectly reveals sensitive information to be covered by the protection in Article 9 of the General Data Protection Regulation.33 Furthermore, according to the Court of Justice, the concept of "data about health" encompasses all aspects of a person's health, both physical and mental.34 The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) has stated that the concept of "data about health" should be interpreted broadly and includes, among other things, information collected by healthcare providers in a patient record (e.g., medical history and results of examinations and treatments).35

The rulings of the administrative courts primarily concern the review of authorities' decisions following appeals by individuals. However, certain particularly intrusive decisions are reviewed or made there without the individual having appealed any decision, either after the authority that made the decision has submitted it for the court's review or has applied for the decision to be made. This applies, among other things, to psychiatric cases, where a patient subject to compulsory care can appeal certain decisions related to the care to the administrative courts (see Sections 32 and 33 of the LPT) while certain more intrusive decisions must be submitted for the administrative court's review (see, for example, Section 12 of the LPT) or made only after the authority's application to the administrative court (see, for example, Section 7 of the LPT) regardless of the individual's position. This also applies to LVM cases, where the court decides on the preparation of compulsory care under the LVM (see Section 5) or reviews decisions regarding immediate custody under the LVM after submission (see Sections 15 and 17).  
Cases in the administrative courts are decided by judgment or decision (collectively referred to as rulings). Such rulings must state the reasons that determined the outcome (Section 30, second paragraph of the Administrative Procedure Act [1971:291]). Furthermore, the ruling must specify the parties (i.e., among others, who has appealed or who is the subject of the application or the submitted decision), the matter in brief, and to the extent necessary, an account of the judgment or decision that has been appealed or submitted (Section 13 of the Regulation [2013:390] on cases in the administrative courts).  
Against this background, IMY notes that the rulings of the administrative courts in psychiatric cases and LVM cases typically contain health information about the person subject to compulsory care, that is, the person who has appealed the decision made under the respective law or who is the subject of the application or the submitted decision without appeal.  
Since such rulings in unchanged form are provided by Verifiera through the Services, the criterion in Chapter 1, Section 20, first paragraph of the YGL that sensitive personal data is disclosed is fulfilled.

According to IMY, the statements made in the preparatory works summarize the following. What the Constitutional Committee has stated in connection with the constitutional amendment means that, in assessing the risks of privacy infringements according to Chapter 1, Section 20 of the YGL, no significance should be attached to whether a search service is aimed at the general public or is available to a specific professional category. Processing is not for journalistic purposes  
Applicable Provisions, etc.  
Through Chapter 1, Section 7, second paragraph of the Data Protection Act, an exception is made from large parts of the data protection regulation for processing that occurs for, among other things, journalistic purposes. According to the exception, Articles 5–30 and 35–50 of the Data Protection Regulation as well as Chapters 2–5 of the Data Protection Act shall not apply to such processing.  

The Court of Justice of the European Union has assessed that in order to consider the importance of freedom of expression in democratic societies, the concepts associated with it, including journalism, should be interpreted broadly. This means, among other things, that exceptions from and limitations of data protection regulations should not only apply to media companies but to all individuals who are engaged in journalistic activities. Furthermore, the case law of the Court of Justice indicates that "journalistic activity" is such activity that aims to disseminate information, opinions, or ideas to the public, regardless of the medium through which this occurs.45  
At the same time, the Court of Justice has established that not all information made available on the internet that contains personal data falls under the concept of "journalistic activity."46 The Court of Justice has further found in the judgment Google Spain and Google that a search engine provider's processing through the provision of the search engine could not be considered to occur for journalistic purposes.47

A different arrangement would mean that privacy protection could easily be circumvented in a way that cannot be considered to correspond to the balanced weighing of privacy protection and freedom of expression as indicated by the Court of Justice of the European Union in the Satakunnan case.48  
In the bill "A Purposeful Protection for Freedom of the Press and Freedom of Expression" (prop. 2021/22:59, p. 54), the following was stated:  
"Against this background, it is difficult to imagine a situation where a collection of data provided in a constitutionally protected activity is exempt from constitutional protection due to the nature of the data collection but is covered by the journalistic exemption in the Data Protection Act. However, an assessment of every conceivable situation cannot, of course, be made generally in advance. Once the delegation provisions are applicable, it must be determined in the individual case which requirements in the data protection regulations the provider of the data collection needs to follow."  
Assessment by the Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection  
The question that IMY has to consider is whether the processing of personal data concerning health that Verifiera performs by including decisions in psychiatric cases and LVM cases in the Services is carried out for journalistic purposes in the sense referred to in Chapter 1, Section 7, second paragraph of the Data Protection Act and Article 85 of the Data Protection Regulation.49 It can initially be stated, in accordance with what is indicated in the legislative history statements above, that the scope for assessing that a data collection covered by Chapter 1, Section 20 of the Freedom of the Press Act is carried out for journalistic purposes must be considered to be very limited.  
IMY further notes that the fact that a website contains certain publications with a journalistic purpose does not mean that all publications on that website should be considered to have a journalistic purpose.50 The presence of independent articles and legal analyses in connection with a data collection of legal decisions does not automatically mean that the entire data collection has a journalistic purpose.

journalistic purpose. It should be required that the connection between the personal data and the editorial content is clear and relevant for the exception for journalistic activities to be invoked. It has not been established in this case that there is editorial content with a clear and relevant connection to the part of the data collection concerning decisions in psychiatric cases and LVM cases.  
The investigation shows that the Services aim, among other things, to provide personal data for background checks of individuals in connection with, for example, recruitment. According to IMY's assessment, such processing of personal data cannot be considered to have journalistic purposes. However, Verifiera has argued that the Services also have other purposes than background checks, e.g., research. As IMY notes above, the data collection provided by Verifiera is the result of extensive collection of legal decisions containing highly sensitive information. The collection occurs without assessing the relevance of the decision for, e.g., public debate or investigative journalism. There is also no processing of the decisions, e.g., to remove direct personal data such as names and personal identification numbers. The result is a data collection in which it is possible to search for anyone who has been subject to compulsory care due to mental illness or addiction since 2008. This data collection cannot be considered to primarily aim to disseminate information, opinions, or ideas to the public in the manner intended in Chapter 1, Section 7, second paragraph of the Data Protection Act and Article 85 of the Data Protection Regulation.  
In light of Verifiera's assertion that the operation is necessary for the general public's legitimate interest in accessing public documents, it is important to emphasize that the fact that the public may have a legitimate interest in accessing public documents in an easily accessible manner does not in itself mean that Verifiera has a journalistic purpose with its processing. The Court of Justice of the European Union stated in the judgment Google and Google Spain that the public could have a legitimate interest in accessing information through a search engine by searching for a person's name. At the same time, the court established that the search engine provider could not invoke the exception for journalistic purposes when processing personal data in such searches. This also applied according to the court in cases where the search result concerned a newspaper article and had journalistic purposes in itself. According to IMY's assessment, the activities that Verifiera conducts through the relevant part of the data collection, similarly to a search engine provider's activities, fall outside the exception for journalistic purposes.  
In summary, IMY assesses that Verifiera's publication of decisions in the relevant types of cases is not covered by the exception in Chapter 1, Section 7, second paragraph of the Data Protection Act.

As stated above, IMY assesses that the Data Protection Regulation is applicable to the processing in the Services.  
The data controller according to the Data Protection Regulation is the one who alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means for the processing of personal data (Article 4.7).

IMY notes that it is Verifiera that provides the Services for financial compensation to paying users. Processing Contravenes Article 9 of the Data Protection Regulation  
Applicable Provisions  
As stated above, IMY assesses that Verifiera processes sensitive personal data consisting of health-related information in the Services. According to Article 9.1 of the Data Protection Regulation, the processing of such data is generally prohibited. For the processing to be permissible, one of the exceptions specified in Article 9.2 of the Data Protection Regulation must apply to the processing.  
According to Article 9.2 a of the Data Protection Regulation, the prohibition in Article 9.1 shall not apply if the data subject has explicitly given consent to the processing.  
According to Article 9.2 g, the prohibition in Article 9.1 does not apply if the processing is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest, based on Union law or the national law of the Member States, which must be proportionate to the intended purpose, compatible with the essential content of the right to data protection, and include provisions for appropriate and specific measures to safeguard the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject.  

Consent  
The data collection subject to IMY's review consists of a large number of legal decisions in psychiatric and LVM cases. The data collection is used by Verifiera to provide the Services. One of these is referred to as the background check service.  
Verifiera has claimed that explicit consent, according to Article 9.2 a of the Data Protection Regulation, exists for the part of the processing related to this service. However, IMY notes that Verifiera obtains consent only in connection with a background check being ordered from Verifiera. Consent is thus given only after Verifiera has performed the processing to collect and organize the decisions for searchability in which sensitive personal data is processed. Consent obtained retrospectively is not valid. Therefore, there is no reason to assess whether the explicit consent that Verifiera claims to collect otherwise meets the conditions of the Data Protection Regulation.  

Article 9.2 g and Freedom of Expression and Information  
Regarding the processing in other respects, Verifiera has claimed that the processing "is necessary for purposes concerning Verifiera's legitimate interest in being able to conduct its constitutionally protected activities within the framework of its publishing license." According to Verifiera, the interests of the data subjects in this case do not outweigh the right to freedom of expression. Finally, Verifiera asserts that the documents in the data collection are available through several other sources and that the type of decision in question is likely irrelevant since courts must apply confidentiality to the extent that health information is involved.

IMY initially states that legitimate interest (also referred to as balancing of interests) is one of several legal grounds under Article 6.1 f of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) that is required for the processing of personal data to be lawful. However, this ground is not included in the enumeration of exceptions in Article 9.2 that can legitimize the processing of sensitive personal data as referred to in Article 9.1, which is the subject of this matter. As can be understood, Verifiera has claimed that the exception in Article 9.2 g constitutes legal support for the processing.  
IMY notes that the public's freedom of expression and information is an important public interest. This is also expressed in Article 85 of the GDPR, which prescribes an obligation for member states to legally reconcile the right to privacy in accordance with the GDPR with freedom of expression and information. According to Article 85.2 of the GDPR, member states may make exceptions to the provisions in, among others, Article 9 if necessary to reconcile the right to privacy with freedom of expression and information. This has been implemented in Swedish law through Chapter 1, Section 7 of the Data Protection Act, which, according to IMY's assessment in this decision, is not applicable to the part of Verifiera's data collection that is reviewed in this decision.  
The regulation in the GDPR can be interpreted as meaning that only Article 85, in accordance with the principle of lex specialis, should be used to regulate the relationship between the right to protection of personal data and the right to freedom of expression and information in national law. However, according to IMY, it cannot be ruled out that it is additionally possible to introduce national regulation of the kind referred to in Article 9.2 g of the GDPR to support the processing of sensitive personal data that is necessary to satisfy the public's freedom of expression and information. Such regulation would, among other things, need to include provisions on appropriate and specific measures to ensure the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject. However, no such regulation has been introduced in Swedish law. Thus, there is no possibility for Verifiera to apply Article 9.2 g of the GDPR by invoking the public's freedom of expression and information.  
Article 9.2 g and the Principle of Public Access  
Verifiera has also asserted that the processing in the data collection is necessary for the legitimate interest of the general public to, within the framework of Verifiera's constitutional protection, access public documents that are available in the Services. IMY therefore finds it necessary to assess whether the rules on the principle of public access in Chapter 2 of the Freedom of the Press Act (TF) can support Verifiera's processing.  
The relationship between the right to protection of personal data and the principle of public access is regulated in Article 86 of the GDPR. It states that personal data in public documents held by a public authority, a public body, or a private entity for the performance of a task of public interest may be disclosed by the authority or body in accordance with the national legislation of the member state to reconcile the public's right to access public documents with the right to protection of personal data in accordance with the regulation. Furthermore, Chapter 1, Section 7, first paragraph of the Data Protection Act has introduced regulation clarifying that the GDPR shall not apply to the extent that it would conflict with the rules on public access to documents in Chapter 2 of the TF.53  
The provisions on public access to documents in Chapter 2 of the TF grant everyone the right to access public documents that are not subject to confidentiality. For it to be a matter of

A public document shall be kept by an authority or another body that is covered by the principle of public access (Chapter 2, Sections 4 and 5 of the Freedom of the Press Act). The rules in Chapter 2 of the Freedom of the Press Act do not grant the public a right to access disclosed public documents held by an organization that, like Verifiera, is not subject to the obligation to disclose documents according to the principle of public access.  
The rules in Chapter 2 of the Freedom of the Press Act also do not grant the recipient of public documents the right to further disseminate or process them in any other way. The purpose of the principle of public access, as expressed in Chapter 2, Section 1 of the Freedom of the Press Act, is indeed to promote free exchange of ideas, free and diverse information, and free artistic creation. However, the further processing of disclosed public documents is not covered by the provisions of Chapter 2 of the Freedom of the Press Act but by other rules, particularly other parts of the media fundamental laws. The media fundamental laws provide, among other things, the right to publish public documents or information from such documents and to disseminate such publications (see, among others, Chapter 1, Section 1, second paragraph, and Chapter 6 of the Freedom of the Press Act, as well as Chapter 3 of the Fundamental Law on Freedom of Expression).  
It should be emphasized that Swedish data protection regulation creates significant room for further processing of personal data in public documents to meet the fundamental importance that freedom of the press and freedom of expression have for the Swedish constitutional order. This is achieved through the exception for media fundamental laws in Chapter 1, Section 7, first paragraph of the Data Protection Act and the exception for, among other things, journalistic purposes in Chapter 1, Section 7, second paragraph of the Data Protection Act. As IMY states in this decision, however, the personal data processing relevant to this case is not covered by these exceptions.  
Against this background, IMY concludes that Chapter 2 of the Freedom of the Press Act does not regulate Verifiera's disclosure of sensitive personal data and therefore cannot serve as a basis under Article 9.2(g) of the Data Protection Regulation for this processing. Furthermore, it can be noted that the rules in Chapter 2 of the Freedom of the Press Act do not contain any suitable and specific measures to protect the rights of the data subjects in the further processing of disclosed public documents.  
Conclusions  
Since nothing has emerged to suggest that any other exception in Article 9.2 is applicable, IMY finds that Verifiera, during the period from April 6, 2022, to June 28, 2022, has processed sensitive personal data (data concerning health) in violation of Article 9 of the Data Protection Regulation in its services on www.verifiera.se.  
Choice of Intervention  
Article 58.2 and Article 83.2 of the Data Protection Regulation indicate that IMY has the authority to impose administrative fines in accordance with Article 83. Depending on the circumstances of the individual case, administrative fines shall be imposed in addition to or instead of the other measures referred to in Article 58.2, such as orders and prohibitions. Furthermore, Article 83.2 outlines the factors to be considered when deciding whether administrative fines should be imposed and when determining the amount of the fine. If it concerns a minor infringement, IMY, as stated in Recital 148, may instead of imposing a fine issue a reprimand in accordance with Article 58.2(b). Consideration shall be given to aggravating and mitigating circumstances in the case, such as the nature, severity, and duration of the infringement, as well as previous relevant infringements.
