On the breach relating to the obligation to process data lawfully in accordance with Article 6 of the GDPR. 63. According to Article 6 of the GDPR, "processing shall be lawful only if and to the extent that at least one of the following applies: a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes; b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract; c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject; d) processing is necessary in order to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person; e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller; f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular where the data subject is a child." 64. The rapporteur notes that in the context of chat consultations, the company retains its clients' banking data beyond the strictly necessary time for the completion of the transaction, to facilitate subsequent payments, and this, without obtaining their prior consent, for example on the data collection form. 65. In defense, the company argues that the retention of its clients' banking data is for the purpose of purchasing credits and is based on the contract that binds it to its clients. It also considers that the retention of this data for the purposes of combating fraud is based on its legitimate interest. 66. The restricted panel recalls that the legal basis for processing banking data may vary, in particular depending on the purpose pursued. 67. In this case, firstly, regarding the purpose related to combating fraud, the restricted panel considers that the legal basis for the retention of banking data is, in accordance with what the company argues, its legitimate interest, which is not contested by the rapporteur. 68. In this regard, the Commission indicates, in its deliberation No. 2018-303 of September 6, 2018, adopting a recommendation concerning the processing of payment card data in the context of the sale of goods or the provision of services at a distance, that "the retention of payment card data beyond the completion of a transaction for the purposes of combating payment card fraud does not fall within the scope of the contract. It indeed considers that this processing falls under the legitimate interest of the data controller, provided that the interests or rights and freedoms of the individuals are not disregarded in accordance with Article 6-1-f of the GDPR, particularly by ensuring compliance with the principles of transparency and the effectiveness of the exercise of their rights by the data subjects." 69. Secondly, regarding the purpose related to re-crediting, the restricted panel considers that clients' banking data is retained to offer an additional service to clients, namely not having to re-enter their card number when purchasing additional credit, which goes beyond
execution of the concluded contract. 70. Consequently, it considers that the processing of clients' banking data for this purpose cannot rely on the contract concluded between the client and the company and requires the prior consent of the clients to be obtained. 71. The restricted formation recalls, by way of illustration, that the Commission estimates, in its aforementioned deliberation, that for the purposes of a one-time payment, "the credit card number can only be collected and processed to enable the execution of a transaction within the framework of the execution of the contract concluded by the data subject in accordance with Article 6-1-b of the GDPR (contractual execution). Thus, in the case of a contract involving a one-time payment, the Commission believes that the data should not be retained beyond the time of the commercial transaction." 72. Also by way of illustration, in the aforementioned deliberation, the Commission indicates that "the retention of the client's card number in order to facilitate any subsequent payments, and potentially to allow for a 'one-click' purchase on the merchant's site, goes beyond the execution of the concluded contract. It holds that this option constitutes an independent choice from the initial act that led to the collection of banking details and recalls that such processing requires the prior collection of the free, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent of the individuals, in accordance with Article 6-1-a of the GDPR." 73. The restricted formation also recalls that in its decision of December 10, 2020, No. 429571, the Council of State held that "the CNIL was right to consider that, in general, the retention of the credit card numbers of clients of online shopping sites to facilitate subsequent purchases should be subject to the explicit consent of the data subject. It follows that the argument based on the non-compliance by the disputed deliberation with the regulation of April 27, 2016 must be dismissed." 74. Consequently, the restricted formation considers that a breach of Article 6, paragraph 1, of the GDPR is constituted, as the company retains its clients' banking data beyond the execution of the transaction, in order to facilitate the purchase of additional credit, without obtaining their prior consent. D. On the breach relating to the obligation to obtain prior consent for the collection of special categories of data in accordance with Article 9 of the GDPR. 75. Under Article 9 of the GDPR, the processing of personal data revealing information concerning the health or sexual orientation of a natural person is prohibited unless it falls under one of the conditions set out in Article 9-2-a) to j) of the GDPR. 76. The rapporteur notes that the psychics have the possibility to write comments on the company's client files after a consultation. She notes that among these comments are information disclosed by clients regarding their health and sexual orientation. She observes that, at the time of creating the user account, the company does not obtain the agreement of the data subject regarding the use of this data. 77. In defense, the company argues that the mere fact of a person addressing a psychic and spontaneously communicating sensitive data during the exchange constitutes a clear positive act of transmitting certain data and thus consent. 78. Furthermore, the company asserts that the comments from the psychics do not, for the most part, allow for the identification of the data subject, as the data is pseudonymized. 79. The restricted formation notes, first of all, that during consultations, clients may communicate to the psychics data relating to their health status and sexual orientation. At the end of the consultations, the psychics make comments on their clients' files. These files are retained in their business tool.
restricted formation notes that, in the context of their comments, the observers mention information relating to the health status and sexual orientation of clients that they collected during the consultation. 80. The restricted formation considers that the measures taken by the company do not correspond to an anonymization of client data but rather to a simple pseudonymization, insofar as the identifier associated with the comment and the information it contains allow for the re-identification of the concerned client. 81. The restricted formation further notes that, in the absence of other conditions that could be invoked in the specific case under Article 9-2-b) to j) of the GDPR, such processing can only be implemented based on the explicit consent of the data subject for the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes, in accordance with Article 9-2-a) of the GDPR. The restricted formation recalls that the explicit nature of consent is assessed on a case-by-case basis and depends on the context of the processing of sensitive data. When the service requested by the user necessarily involves the processing of sensitive data, it is, however, essential that the user is fully aware that their sensitive data will be processed and sometimes retained by the data controller, which generally implies explicit information on this point at the time of obtaining consent. 82. The restricted formation recalls that according to Article 4, paragraph 11, of the GDPR, the notion of consent is understood as any manifestation of will, free, specific, informed, and unambiguous by which the data subject accepts, by a declaration or by a clear affirmative action, that personal data concerning them be subject to processing. 83. On the one hand, the restricted formation considers that the explicit nature of the consent provided for in Article 9, paragraph 2, a) of the GDPR implies allowing the data subject to express, through a positive action, their agreement to the processing of sensitive data, demonstrating the materiality of their consent. 84. For clarification, the restricted formation recalls that in its guidelines on consent under Regulation 2016/679 of April 10, 2018, the European Data Protection Board (hereinafter "EDPB") states that "the GDPR stipulates that a 'declaration or a clear affirmative action' is a sine qua non condition for 'standard' consent." Given that the requirements for 'standard' consent in the GDPR are already set at a higher level than those of Directive 95/46/EC, it is appropriate to specify what additional efforts a data controller should undertake to obtain the explicit consent of a data subject in accordance with the GDPR. The term explicit relates to how consent is expressed by the data subject. It implies that the data subject must make an express consent declaration. An obvious way to ensure that consent is explicit would be to confirm consent expressly in a written declaration. Where appropriate, the data controller could ensure that the written declaration is signed by the data subject to prevent any potential doubt and any potential lack of evidence in the future. However, such a signed declaration is not the only way to obtain explicit consent […]” (guidelines 2016/679 WP259 rev.01 of April 10, 2018, page 21). 85. The restricted formation emphasizes that it has, on several occasions, adopted corrective measures against data controllers who do not obtain the explicit consent of individuals to collect and process their "sensitive" data, notably in its deliberations No. 2016-405 of December 15, 2016, and No. 2016-406 of December 15, 2016, as well as in its
deliberation No. SAN-2017-006 of April 27, 2017, in which it considered that "the spontaneous provision of such data does not exempt the company from the obligation to obtain the express consent of the individuals who must be able to manifest their agreement to the processing of sensitive data through a positive action, thereby confirming that consent is given with full knowledge of the facts." 86. The restricted panel therefore notes that the mere desire to receive a fortune-telling service and the act of voluntarily providing sensitive information do not constitute explicit consent from the individuals concerned. 87. It considers that the company should have provided individuals from whom it collects particular categories of data with a means to ensure that they consent explicitly through a clear positive act. 88. Furthermore, the consent obtained under Article 9, paragraph 2, a) of the GDPR must be interpreted in light of the definition set out in Article 4, paragraph 11, of the GDPR, which implies that, in this case, for valid consent, the individual concerned must, beforehand, be fully informed about the particular nature of the data they communicate, notably in that such data may reveal their health status and sexual orientation, as well as the use that will be made of this data. 89. The restricted panel notes that the company does not provide specific information to the individuals concerned regarding the collection and processing of their health data and information related to their sexual orientation. 90. It considers that the company should have provided the individuals concerned with specific information, for example, at the time of creating their user account. 91. Consequently, the restricted panel considers that the company does not obtain the explicit consent of the individuals concerned, so it cannot invoke the exception to the prohibition on collecting and processing particular categories of data, as provided for in Article 9, paragraph 2, a), of the GDPR. 92. As a result, the restricted panel considers that in the absence of obtaining the prior and explicit consent of clients for the collection of their health data and information related to their sexual orientation, a breach of Article 9 of the GDPR is constituted. **FOR THESE REASONS**  
The restricted formation of the CNIL, after deliberation, decides to:  
- impose an administrative fine of 120,000 (one hundred twenty thousand) euros on the company KG COM for violations of Articles 5-1-c) and e), 6, 9, 12, 13, 28, 32, and 33 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of personal data and the free movement of such data, and an amount of 30,000 (thirty thousand) euros for the violation of Article 82 of the Data Protection Act;  
- make public, on the CNIL website and on the Légifrance website, its deliberation, which will no longer specifically identify the company KG COM after a period of two years from its publication.
