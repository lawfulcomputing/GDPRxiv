II. Reasons for the Decision 

A. On the breach of the obligation to process data lawfully 

19. According to Article 6 of the GDPR, "1. Processing shall be lawful only if and to the extent that at least one of the following applies: a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes; b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract; c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject; d) processing is necessary in order to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person; e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller; f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject."
that the interests or the freedoms and fundamental rights of the data subject prevail, which require protection of personal data, particularly when the data subject is a child. 

20. The restricted panel recalls that commercial prospecting actions by telephone can be carried out on the legal basis of the legitimate interest of the company (f) or on that of consent (a). 

21. In this case, the company indicated that it conducts commercial prospecting operations by telephone using prospect files purchased from several data providers, who collect said data through participation forms for online contests. 

22. The restricted panel notes that the company was unable, both in its written observations and in its oral observations during the session, to specify the legal basis on which it relied to carry out such processing. Under these conditions, the two legal bases that may be applicable in this case will be examined successively. 

1) On legitimate interest 

23. The rapporteur argues that, to justify its commercial prospecting operations by telephone, the company cannot invoke the legal basis of legitimate interest referred to in point f) of Article 6, paragraph 1 of the GDPR. She points out that, regarding the participation forms for online contests through which the company collects the data of prospects that it sells to the company FORIOU, the latter is not systematically mentioned in the list of partners likely to solicit the data subjects, and thus the latter cannot legitimately expect to receive commercial offers from this company. 

24. In defense, the company invokes the contractual commitments of the company [...], which stipulate that the company FORIOU must be mentioned among the recipients of the collected data. It argues that it cannot be held responsible for the shortcomings of its service provider and produces an example of a form implemented by the company [...] containing a URL link referring to a list of partners, among which is the company SFAM (a link to the privacy policy of the latter allowing access to the complete list of companies that are part of the same group as SFAM, including the company FORIOU). Finally, the company claims to implement regular checks regarding the compliance of the delivered files. 

25. The restricted panel recalls that, while commercial prospecting by non-electronic means can be carried out on the basis of the legitimate interest of the company, it must ensure that the processing does not infringe the rights and interests of the individuals whose data are processed, taking into account their reasonable expectations. 

26. In this regard, recital 47 of the GDPR states that: " […] the existence of a legitimate interest should be the subject of careful assessment, in particular to determine whether a data subject can reasonably expect, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that such data will be processed for a given purpose. The interests and fundamental rights of the person could, in particular, prevail over the interest of the data controller when personal data are processed in circumstances where the data subjects do not reasonably expect further processing […]." 

27. The restricted panel first notes that it follows from these provisions that, in its capacity as data controller, the company FORIOU is required to verify itself that the conditions allowing it to carry out commercial prospecting operations are met. In this regard, the responsibility of an organization has been retained by considering that a mere contractual commitment of...
the data broker to comply with the GDPR and the applicable rules regarding commercial prospecting was not a sufficient measure (CNIL, FR, November 24, 2022, Sanction, No. D-SAN-2022-021, published). 

28. Thus, concerning the contractual commitments of the company […] invoked by the company FORIOU, the restricted formation considers that the contractual obligations that can be imposed on suppliers cannot exempt the company FORIOU from its responsibility as a data controller, despite the possible existence of liability on the part of the suppliers. 

29. Furthermore, regarding the verifications that the company claims to carry out on the forms from which the data is collected, the restricted formation notes that it does not produce any evidence to attest to this, as the contractual commitments of its suppliers do not constitute a control measure as such. 

30. In this case, the restricted formation notes that certain contest entry forms from which the company […] collects prospect data that it transmits to the company FORIOU do not allow the data subjects to reasonably expect to receive commercial prospecting offers from this company. 

31. Thus, concerning the form accessible from the website […], the restricted formation observes that it contains a hyperlink referring to a nominative list of partners and not to categories of partners. Therefore, the data subjects can legitimately expect this list of partners to be exhaustive. However, the said list does not mention the company FORIOU. 

32. Regarding the forms present on the websites […] (this form referring to the site […]) and […], the restricted formation notes that they do not mention the list of partners or categories of partners to whom the data may be transmitted, and that they also do not contain any link allowing access to such a list. 

33. The restricted formation considers that under these conditions, the protection of the interests, freedoms, and fundamental rights of the data subjects takes precedence over the legitimate interests of the company, and that the latter cannot therefore rely on the legal basis mentioned in Article 6, paragraph 1, f) to justify its telephone commercial prospecting operations. 

2) On Consent 

34. The rapporteur considers that, to justify its telephone commercial prospecting operations, the company cannot rely on the legal basis of consent referred to in point a) of Article 6, paragraph 1, of the GDPR. She notes that the findings made by the delegation established that the data brokers from whom the company FORIOU sources its data collect said data through online contest participation forms, the design of which does not allow users to express their consent through a clear and unambiguous positive act, and strongly encourages them to accept the transmission of their data to the company's partners for prospecting purposes. 

35. In defense, the company invokes the terms of the contract entered into with the company […]. It acknowledges the documented findings but indicates that, if the shortcomings exist, they are neither representative of a willful disregard of its obligations nor of widespread practices. In this regard, it provides two examples of collection forms implemented by its suppliers, which it considers compliant. Finally, it reports controls carried out on the files following their provision by the service provider and emphasizes the impossibility, given the volume of these files, of implementing individual control. 

36. The restricted formation recalls that according to Article 4, paragraph 11, of the GDPR, "consent" of the data subject means "any manifestation of
will, free, specific, informed, and unequivocal by which the data subject accepts, through a declaration or a clear positive act, that personal data concerning them be subject to processing." 37. Regarding commercial prospecting operations, it emphasizes that when the data of prospects have not been collected directly from them by the organization conducting the prospecting, consent may have been obtained at the time of the initial data collection by the first collector, on behalf of the organization that will carry out subsequent prospecting operations. Failing this, it is the responsibility of the organization conducting the prospecting to obtain such consent before proceeding with prospecting actions (CNIL, FR, November 24, 2022, Sanction, No. SAN-2022-021, published) 38. Firstly, concerning the contractual commitments of the company […] invoked by the company FORIOU, the restricted panel refers to the elements developed in points 27 and 28. It further recalls that, while the intentional nature of the violation must be taken into account to decide whether to impose a fine and to determine its amount, it has no impact on the characterization of the breach, which may result from negligence. The same applies to whether the breach is generalized or not. 39. Secondly, the restricted panel recalls that the consent mentioned in the provisions of Article 6, paragraph 1, a) of the GDPR, which allows for the processing of personal data, can only result from the express consent of the user, given knowingly after adequate information about the use that will be made of their personal data. It is therefore necessary to ensure that the data subjects have given unequivocal, specific, free, and informed consent when their personal data is collected through participation forms for contests. 40.

The restricted panel notes in this regard that the work conducted on the practices implemented regarding cookies concerning consent collection banners can usefully serve to more generally assess the conditions for obtaining free, unequivocal, specific, and informed consent, and serve as a reference in commercial prospecting when it is based on the collection of consent. 41. Furthermore, regarding the same conditions of consent, the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter "CJEU") specified, in its decision Planet49 GmbH: "Article 7, under a) of Directive 95 provides that the consent of the data subject can make such processing lawful as long as this consent is 'undoubtedly' given by the data subject. However, only an active behavior on the part of this person to express their consent is capable of fulfilling this requirement" (CJEU, Grand Chamber, October 1, 2019, Planet49 GmbH, C-673/17, ECLI:EU:C:2019:801, §54). Therefore, it should be considered that in the absence of being given undoubtedly, consent must be regarded as lacking, which renders the processing illegal for lack of a legal basis. More specifically regarding the modalities of collection, the CJEU states that "the manifestation of will referred to in Article 2, under h), of Directive 95/46 must, in particular, be 'specific', in the sense that it must precisely relate to the processing of the relevant data and cannot be inferred from a manifestation of will having a distinct object. In this case, contrary to what Planet49 has argued, the fact that a user activates the participation button for the promotional game organized by this company cannot, therefore, be sufficient to consider that the user has validly given their consent to the placement of cookies" (Idem, §§ 58-59). 42. Furthermore, the Council of State has held that "free, specific, informed, and unequivocal consent can only be a
express consent of the user, given with full knowledge and after adequate information about the use that will be made of their personal data." (CE, 10th and 9th chambers combined, June 19, 2020, Google LLC, no. 430810, para. 21). 43. The restricted formation also notes, as an example, that the guidelines 5/2020 on consent, adopted on May 4, 2020, by the "Article 29" working group (now the European Data Protection Board, hereinafter "EDPB"), specify that the "free" nature of consent "implies a real choice and control for the data subjects. Generally speaking, the GDPR states that if the data subject is not truly able to exercise a choice, feels compelled to consent, or will suffer significant negative consequences if they do not give their consent, the consent is not valid […] In general terms, any pressure or inappropriate influence exerted on the data subject (which can manifest in various ways) preventing them from exercising their will will render the consent invalid." 44. By way of illustration and comparison, in its deliberation no. 2020-092 of September 17, 2020, adopting a recommendation proposing practical modalities for compliance in cases of the use of "cookies and other trackers," the Commission recommends that the concerned entities ensure "that users fully understand the options available to them, particularly through the chosen design and the information provided (§ 10) […] In order not to mislead users, the Commission recommends that data controllers ensure that the interfaces for collecting choices do not incorporate potentially misleading design practices that suggest to users that their consent is mandatory or that visually emphasize one choice over another. It is recommended to use buttons and a font of the same size, offering the same ease of reading, and highlighted in the same way" (§ 34). It adds that it is important "to ensure that the information accompanying each actionable item allowing for the expression of consent or refusal is easily understandable and does not require effort in concentration or interpretation on the part of the user. Thus, it is particularly recommended to ensure that it is not drafted in such a way that a quick or inattentive reading could lead to the belief that the selected option produces the opposite of what users thought they were choosing." (§ 23). Otherwise, the unambiguous nature of consent would not be characterized. 45. The restricted formation also recalls that studies conducted on the practices of digital interfaces, particularly regarding cookies, highlight the considerable impact of the appearance of consent collection banners on users' choices, which may lead them to make choices that do not reflect their preferences regarding data sharing. 46. The restricted panel recalls that it follows from the provisions of Article 32 of the GDPR that the data controller must implement appropriate measures to ensure the confidentiality of the data and to prevent it from being processed unlawfully by persons who do not need to know it (CNIL, FR, October 29, 2021, Sanction, No. SAN-2021-019, published). 67. This need to know may evolve depending on the data lifecycle and the purposes for which it is retained. Thus, during the phase of their current use, which corresponds to the necessary duration to achieve the determined purpose, the data is kept in an "active base" and accessible to all services responsible for implementing the processing. At the end of this phase, when the data is no longer used to achieve the set objective but still holds administrative interest for the organization (for example, for the management of potential litigation) or must be retained to comply with a legal obligation, it should only be accessible on a case-by-case basis and justified by specifically authorized individuals participating in the objective that justified this retention, subject to intermediate archiving. This intermediate archiving requires a separation from the active base, which can be physical (through a transfer of data to a dedicated archive base) or logical (through the implementation of technical and organizational measures ensuring that only those with a legitimate interest in processing the data due to their functions can access it). 68. The restricted formation emphasizes that the breach committed by the company relates to obligations concerning the fundamental principles of personal data protection. 74. Indeed, the restricted formation recalls that the consequence of failing to have a legal basis for processing the data of prospects in the context of telephone marketing is to render the targeted operations unlawful. 75. It emphasizes that if the company intends to base these operations on the legal basis of consent, the ecosystem of data resale from partner to partner requires particularly strong guarantees regarding the quality and validity of the consent obtained by the primary data collector, which partners rely on for commercial prospecting purposes. It underscores that, in this regard, the organization relying on such consent to conduct commercial prospecting operations bears essential responsibility, requiring it, as the data controller, to ensure that the conditions allowing it to carry out said operations are met, regardless of the potential liability of the data suppliers, the primary collectors. The restricted formation considers that these requirements should be particularly strengthened concerning the methods of obtaining consent from users of websites aimed at offering profit opportunities, as these individuals may not necessarily be aware of the scope of their agreement at the time of their registration. 76. The restricted formation also recalls the importance, in the absence of obtaining valid consent, of allowing affected individuals to assess the extent of the processing to which their data may be subject. 84. It also notes that this measure is intended to inform the individuals affected by the company's prospecting operations. This information will allow them, if necessary, to assert their rights. 

85. Finally, it considers that this measure is proportionate since the decision will no longer specifically identify the company after a period of two years from its publication. 

FOR THESE REASONS The restricted formation of the CNIL, after deliberation, decides to: • impose an administrative fine on the company FORIOU in the amount of three hundred ten thousand euros (€310,000) for violation of Article 6 of the GDPR; • make its deliberation public on the CNIL website and on the Légifrance website, which will no longer allow for the specific identification of the company after a period of two years from its publication. 

The President Alexandre LINDEN This decision may be subject to appeal before the Council of State within two months from its notification.
