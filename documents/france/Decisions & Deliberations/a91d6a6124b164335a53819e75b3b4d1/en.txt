Thirdly, the restricted formation recalls that the specific consent required by the provisions of Article L. 34-5 of the CPCE can only result from the express consent of the user, given knowingly after adequate information about the use that will be made of their personal data. The restricted formation notes in this regard that the work conducted on practices implemented regarding cookies concerning consent collection banners can usefully serve to assess more generally the conditions for obtaining free, unambiguous, specific, and informed consent, and serve as a reference in terms of commercial prospecting when it is based on the collection of consent. 49. Furthermore, regarding the same conditions of consent, the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter "CJEU") specified, in its decision Planet49 GmbH: "Article 7(a) of Directive 95 provides that the consent of the data subject can make such processing lawful provided that this consent is 'undoubtedly' given by the data subject. However, only an active behavior on the part of this person to express their consent is capable of fulfilling this requirement" (CJEU, Grand Chamber, October 1, 2019, Planet49 GmbH, C-673/17, ECLI:EU:C:2019:801, §54). Consequently, it should be considered that in the absence of undoubtedly given consent, consent must be regarded as lacking, which renders the processing illegal due to lack of legal basis. More specifically regarding the methods of obtaining consent, the CJEU states that "the manifestation of will referred to in Article 2(h) of Directive 95/46 must, in particular, be 'specific' in the sense that it must relate precisely to the processing of the relevant data and cannot be inferred from a manifestation of will having a distinct object. In this case, contrary to what Planet49 has argued, the fact that a user activates the participation button for the promotional game organized by this company cannot suffice to consider that the user has validly given their consent to the placement of cookies" (Idem, §§ 58-59). 50. Furthermore, the Council of State has held that "free, specific, informed, and unambiguous consent can only be express consent from the user, given knowingly and after adequate information about the use that will be made of their personal data" (CE, 10th and 9th chambers combined, June 19, 2020, Google LLC, n° 430810, pt. 21). 51. 1) On legitimate interest  
69. The reporter argues that, to justify its telemarketing operations, the company cannot rely on the legal basis of legitimate interest referred to in point f) of Article 6, paragraph 1 of the GDPR. She notes that, concerning the participation forms for online contests through which the company […] collects the data of prospects that it sells to the company HUBSIDE.STORE, the latter is not systematically mentioned in the list of partners likely to approach the data subjects, and thus the latter cannot legitimately expect to receive commercial offers from this company.  
70. In defense, the company invokes the contractual commitments of the company […], which stipulate that the company HUBSIDE.STORE must be mentioned among the recipients of the collected data. It considers that it cannot be held responsible for the shortcomings of its service provider and produces an example of a form implemented by the company […] containing a URL link referring to a list of partners, among which is the company […] (a link to the privacy policy of the latter allowing access to the complete list of companies that are part of the same group as […], including the company HUBSIDE.STORE). Finally, the company claims to implement regular checks regarding the compliance of the delivered files.  
71. The restricted panel recalls that, while non-electronic commercial prospecting can be carried out based on the legitimate interest of the company, it must ensure that the processing does not infringe upon the rights and interests of the individuals whose data is processed, taking into account their reasonable expectations.  
72. In this regard, Recital 47 of the GDPR states that: "… the existence of a legitimate interest should be subject to careful assessment, in particular to determine whether a data subject can reasonably expect, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that such data will be processed for a specific purpose. The interests and fundamental rights of the person may, in particular, prevail over the interest of the data controller when personal data is processed in circumstances where the data subjects do not reasonably expect further processing…".  
73. In this case, the restricted panel notes that certain contest forms from which the company […] collects prospect data that it transmits to the company HUBSIDE.STORE do not allow the data subjects to reasonably expect to receive commercial prospecting offers from this company.  
74. Thus, concerning the form accessible from the website […], the restricted panel observes that it contains a hyperlink referring to a nominative list of partners and not to categories of partners. Thus, the data subjects can legitimately expect this list of partners to be exhaustive. However, the said list does not mention the company HUBSIDE.STORE.

On the breach of the obligation of transparency and information of individuals 91. recalls that, insofar as the company has indicated that it conducts commercial prospecting by telephone using files transmitted by its partners, this constitutes a case of indirect collection, for which the information of individuals must be ensured under the conditions defined in Article 14 of the GDPR. It notes, for clarification, that the EDPS specifies in its guidelines on transparency under Regulation (EU) 2016/679 that while multi-level information is possible for greater clarity, it "recommends that the first level (in other words, the primary way of communicating for the first time with a data subject) generally communicates the most important information […] For example, when the first contact with a data subject is made by telephone, this information could be provided during the phone call while the other information required under Articles 13 and 14 could be provided later and by other means, including sending a copy of the privacy policy by email and/or sending the data subject a link to the online notice/statement of the data controller regarding privacy protection at different levels." **F. On the Breach of the Obligation to Ensure Data Security**

According to Article 32, paragraph 1 of the GDPR, "taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks, the likelihood and severity of which vary, for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk […]" and in particular "means to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services" and a "procedure for regularly testing, analyzing, and evaluating the effectiveness of technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing." The rapporteur notes that the company indicated it retains customer data from its points of sale in Europe, amounting to 104,391 individuals in November 2021, for a period of five years from the date of contract termination, in accordance with legal limitation periods, specifying that this data was retained in an active database, without any intermediate archiving mechanism being implemented. The rapporteur considers that these retention modalities do not allow for limiting access to the data to users who need to know it, as individuals with an interest in accessing this data during the contract period continue to have access.

This need to know may evolve depending on the data lifecycle and the purposes for which they are retained. Thus, during the phase of their current use, which corresponds to the duration necessary to achieve the determined purpose, the data is retained in "active base" and accessible to all services responsible for implementing the processing. At the end of this phase, when the data is no longer used to achieve the set objective but still presents an administrative interest for the organization (for example, for managing a potential dispute) or must be retained to comply with a legal obligation, it must be consultable only on a sporadic and justified basis by specifically authorized persons, participating in the objective that justified this retention, subject to intermediate archiving.
