Subject 
Decision to impose an administrative fine for violating the General Data Protection Regulation 
Dear members of the management, 

The Authority for Personal Data (AP) has decided to impose an administrative fine of €150,000 (one hundred fifty thousand euros) on International Card Services B.V. (ICS) for violating Article 35, paragraph 1, of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). This is because ICS failed to conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA). 

This decision explains the administrative fine. It will successively address the background and process, the established facts, the violation, and the amount of the fine. Finally, the ruling will follow.

1. Background of the Investigation  
1. ICS is a company based in Amsterdam and is active in offering financial products, particularly the issuance of so-called debit and credit cards.1  

2. The Customer Contact and Supervisory Research department of the AP initiated an investigation into a possible violation of the GDPR by ICS following signals and complaints from consumers about ICS. These complaints and signals were received by the AP after ICS began re-identifying its customers online in 2019 with the 'identification and verification process' (ID&V).  

3. The purpose of the investigation was to determine whether ICS complied with the rules set out in Article 35 of the GDPR when it did not conduct a DPIA in preparation for the ID&V process. 

2. Findings of the Research Report and Process  
4. The findings of the investigation are laid out in a report.2 It follows from this that the implementation of ID&V involved the processing of personal data that likely poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. ICS should have conducted a DPIA prior to the processing. The report concluded that ICS failed to conduct a DPIA. Thus, it acted in violation of Article 35, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, according to the report. 

5. The report was signed on June 23, 2022, and sent to ICS on July 7, 2022.3 ICS provided its comments on the report on September 14, 2022. On October 20, 2022, ICS orally explained its comments.  

6. The AP posed additional written questions to ICS on December 2, 2022, and February 13, 2023, to which ICS responded on December 21, 2022, and March 24, 2023, respectively. 

3. Legal Framework  
7. For better readability of this decision, the relevant legal framework is included in Annex 1. The legal framework is part of this decision.

4. Position of ICS  
8. ICS has provided the following position on the report.  

9. ICS has established an extensive risk process, called the Change Risk Assessment (CRA process). The CRA process identifies, mitigates, and monitors risks. A Privacy Impact Assessment is part of the CRA process, and a DPIA is conducted when warranted.  

10. ICS is a 100% subsidiary of ABN AMRO. Since 2015, ABN AMRO has been using an application from Mitek Systems B.V. (Mitek app) to verify the identity of its customers. ABN AMRO also uses the CRA process to analyze risks, and for the use of the Mitek app, ABN AMRO has conducted a comprehensive analysis of the use of the Mitek app. ICS has utilized the same technology to re-identify its customers. For ICS, there was no reason to conduct a new analysis, as ABN AMRO had already performed one.  

11. ICS further argues that it has investigated whether additional measures should be included in the CRA process for ID&V. A Privacy Officer was involved at an early stage, and the nature and purpose of ID&V were determined. It was also established which personal data is processed, who has access to this personal data, what retention periods apply, whether there is any transfer outside the EU, and the measures taken for such transfers.  

12. Moreover, ICS asserts that at the introduction of ID&V, there was no high risk of misuse of personal data. The personally identifiable information used in ID&V is not inherently sensitive and is necessary to comply with legal requirements. When processing the citizen service number, the highest level of security applies, which is also based on legislation from the financial sector. Furthermore, strict requirements are imposed on encryption, the use of secure connections, conducting penetration tests, and the audit requirements for the control of personal data when collaborating with third parties.  

13. ICS has acknowledged that the PIA triage of August 28, 2018, incorrectly concluded that there was no large-scale processing of personal data. The PIA form has been amended since 2020 to prevent such an error from occurring again.  

14. According to ICS, there is no power imbalance between its customers and ICS. ICS is legally required to identify its customers, who have the option to identify themselves non-digitally, and a credit card is not an essential payment service.

15. The Data Protection Officer (DPO) of ICS has been indirectly involved in data protection risks through the Privacy Officer. ICS has not clearly communicated this circumstance previously.  

16. The Decision regarding the list of processing activities for which a DPIA is mandatory provides a number of criteria, and according to ICS, only one criterion, namely large-scale processing, is applicable in this case. 5. Assessment  
5.1  
Data Controller and Competence of the AP  
17. It is established and undisputed that ICS is the data controller (Article 4, introductory part and under 7, GDPR) and that the AP is the competent supervisory authority (Article 56, first paragraph, GDPR).  

5.2  
Obligation to Conduct DPIA  

18. The GDPR states, in summary, that a DPIA must be conducted prior to processing when that processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons (Article 35, first paragraph, GDPR).  

19. The Guidelines WP 248 rev. 014 (hereinafter: the Guidelines) describe the criteria applicable to the question of whether a DPIA must be conducted. The AP has established the Decision regarding the list of processing activities of personal data (the Decision) considering the provisions in the GDPR (Article 35) and the aforementioned Guidelines. This decision states, among other things, that a DPIA is mandatory for the processing of biometric data.  

20. It is undisputed that ICS did not conduct a DPIA prior to the implementation of ID&V in 2018. The obligation to conduct a DPIA directly follows from Article 35, first paragraph, GDPR, read in conjunction with the aforementioned Guidelines. The fact that the Decision was only established and published by the AP on November 27, 2019, does not mean that ICS was not required to conduct a DPIA for that reason. The Decision contains requirements that are also mentioned in the GDPR and the Guidelines and stem from them.

21. The first question that must be answered in this case is whether there is a type of processing that likely poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. If this is the case, ICS has the obligation to conduct a DPIA prior to the implementation of ID&V.  

22. A processing operation is generally considered to likely pose a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons when two (of the nine) criteria listed in the Guidelines are met. In this case, three criteria listed in the Guidelines apply. These are: 1) sensitive data or data of a highly personal nature; 2) data processed on a large scale; and 3) data concerning vulnerable data subjects.  

23. It has been established that for ID&V, the first and last name, date of birth, place of birth, address details, email address, phone number, gender, BSN, ID document number, as well as the photo therein and a (liveness) photo of a data subject are processed.6 These personal data are, as follows from the Guidelines,7 to be regarded collectively as sensitive data and data of a highly personal nature.  

24. Furthermore, it has been established that ICS has approximately 1.5 million customers in the Netherlands who have had to re-identify themselves, and whose data ICS retains for as long as a data subject remains a customer of ICS.8 In the opinion of the AP, this means that there is also large-scale processing of personal data.  

25. Contrary to what is described in the research report, upon further examination of the facts, the AP is of the opinion that there are no vulnerable data subjects whose personal data are being processed in this case. Vulnerable data subjects can be children, but also employees or a segment of the population that requires special protection, as follows from the Guidelines.9 It concerns the fact that there is an imbalanced relationship between data subjects and the data controller. In this context, ICS has rightly argued that a credit card is not an essential financial product for the daily life of a data subject. A credit card does not serve the function of a bank account and is therefore not comparable. This means that there is no imbalanced relationship between ICS and its customers, and therefore customers of ICS cannot be classified as vulnerable data subjects as described in the Guidelines. Moreover, it is not excluded that other credit card providers may employ different methods for identifying their customers. Thus, data subjects interested in a credit card have the choice to use the services of other providers.

26. Now that at least two criteria from the Guidelines are applicable, there is a type of processing that likely poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. This means that conducting a DPIA by ICS was mandatory.  

27. According to ICS, it was not required to conduct a DPIA because the CRA process analyzed risks of misuse of personal data, and this process is, in ICS's view, comparable to a DPIA. The subsequent question that must be answered in light of this is whether ICS's CRA process can be equated with a DPIA.  

28. The AP rules as follows. The Guidelines provide criteria for an acceptable DPIA (not to be confused with criteria for assessing whether a DPIA must be conducted). It is then up to a data controller to choose a method that meets the established criteria. The data controller is obliged to comply with those (main and sub) criteria. Thus, it must:  

1. provide a systematic description of the processing;  
2. assess the necessity and proportionality of the processing;  
3. manage the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects;  
4. involve stakeholders (their representatives) and the data protection officer (DPO). For example, the advice of the DPO must be sought, or the opinions of data subjects must be requested.  

29. ICS has utilized the CRA process of ABN-AMRO. The CRA process of ABN-AMRO consists of 22 risks (threats) with an associated code. The vast majority of the described risks directly or indirectly relate to fraud prevention and compliance with the Act on the Prevention of Money Laundering and Financing of Terrorism (Wwft). Only three risks (risk key r007, r011, r025) pertain to the protection of personal data. Thus, ICS meets one of the main criteria from the Guidelines mentioned in paragraph 28, namely the third criterion: "management of risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects." The CRA process of ABN-AMRO was insufficiently focused on the protection of personal data concerning the other three criteria, so the CRA process is not comprehensive enough to comply with the GDPR.  

30. In addition to the CRA process of ABN-AMRO, ICS conducted its own CRA process in April 2020. This CRA process also does not provide a systematic description of the processing. Furthermore, it has not been demonstrated that the necessity and proportionality of the processing have been assessed, particularly the measures that contribute to the protection of the rights of data subjects. Moreover, it has not been shown that stakeholders, their representatives, or the data protection officer were involved in the processing. For instance, the DPO of ICS was unable to provide advice on conducting a DPIA. This means that ICS has not assessed three of the four (main) criteria for an acceptable DPIA.  

31. From the above, it follows that the criteria mentioned in the Guidelines for an acceptable DPIA have not been applied in ICS's CRA process. In the opinion of the AP, the CRA processes of ICS and ABN-AMRO cannot therefore be equated with a DPIA. The CRA processes of ICS and ABN-AMRO were primarily aimed at preventing and combating (identity) fraud and did not specifically focus on the protection of personal data.  

32. Moreover, the Privacy Impact Assessment triage of June 18, 2019, as part of the CRA process, did not result in a DPIA being conducted because it incorrectly did not recognize that it concerns large-scale processing.  

33. The AP is of the opinion that ICS should have conducted a DPIA. By failing to do so, ICS has violated Article 35, paragraph 1, GDPR. The AP sees reason to impose a fine.  

6. Administrative fine  
34. The AP is authorized to impose an administrative fine based on Article 58, paragraph 2, introductory phrase and under i, in conjunction with Article 83 GDPR and read in conjunction with Article 14, paragraph 3, UAVG.  

35. It has been concluded in paragraph 33 that ICS wrongfully did not conduct a DPIA and thereby violated Article 35, paragraph 1, GDPR. This means that there is one act for which a fine will be imposed.

36. This is in accordance with what is stated in the explanation of the Fine Policy Rules regarding the establishment of joint principles concerning the calculation of fines and the temporary nature of the AP's policy on this matter.

6.2  
Determining the starting amount based on the Penalty Policy Rules 2019  

39. As mentioned above, the starting point in this case is formed by the applicable range of the Penalty Policy Rules. The AP, when determining the amount of the penalty, takes into account the factors mentioned in Article 7 of the Penalty Policy Rules, without prejudice to Articles 3:4 and 5:46 of the General Administrative Law Act (Awb). These factors are also mentioned in Article 83, second paragraph, of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and in the Guidelines.  

40. For a violation of Article 35, first paragraph, of the GDPR, the AP can impose an administrative fine of up to €10,000,000. In the case of a company, a fine can be imposed of up to 2% of the total global annual turnover in the previous financial year, if this figure is higher. The AP determines that the total global annual turnover of the parent company of ICS in 2022 amounts to €7.841 billion, and therefore the legal maximum penalty amounts to €156.8 million.  

41. Under the Penalty Policy Rules, a violation is classified into a category based on the provision violated, ranging from category I to IV. It holds that the more important the provision is for the protection of personal data, the higher the category of violation. The Penalty Policy Rules state that violations of Article 35, first paragraph, of the GDPR fall into category II. The range of this category is from €120,000 to €500,000, with a base fine of €310,000. This amount will be taken as the starting point for the further calculation of the final penalty, after consideration of the relevant factors.

43. One of these factors is the severity of the violation. In determining this, account is taken of the nature, seriousness, and duration of the infringement. Other circumstances that are taken into account include the categories of personal data involved and whether the infringement is intentional or negligent in nature.  

44. The following is relevant in this regard. The obligation to carry out a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is intended to describe the process of processing personal data, so that not only the necessity and proportionality of the processing are mapped out, but also the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects in the processing of personal data. The failure to carry out a DPIA is therefore, in itself, already a violation of the GDPR, while it also increases the likelihood of new violations of the GDPR because risks of possible (other) infringements of the GDPR are not timely recognized.  

45. For determining the severity of the violation, it is further relevant that ICS processes personal data of a large number of data subjects, namely 1.5 million customers. This fact contributes to the severity of the violation. The Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) has classified the personal data processed by ICS as sensitive data and data of a very personal nature. At the same time, the AP takes into account the circumstance that ICS has initiated the process of re-identifying its customers based on obligations arising from the Anti-Money Laundering and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act (Wwft). In complying with this, the AP has not found that ICS intentionally failed to carry out the required DPIA. However, in the AP's opinion, negligence is present. The failure to carry out a DPIA is due to a misjudgment by ICS. They have used fraud prevention and compliance with the Wwft as guiding principles, but in that context, ICS should also have independently assessed compliance with the GDPR. The AP weighs the element of negligence in this case as "neutral," because it cannot be said that ICS was entirely non-compliant by not carrying out a DPIA, in which context the AP attaches significance to the circumstance that ICS does comply with one of the main criteria from the aforementioned Guidelines for an acceptable DPIA in the application of the CRA process, namely the management of risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects. To that extent, ICS has indeed paid (some) attention to the aforementioned risks that may arise in the processing of personal data.  

46. It has been established that ICS wrongly did not carry out a DPIA. In determining the severity of the violation, the AP does take into account the circumstance that ICS carried out the aforementioned CRA process at the start of re-identifying its customers. Part of that process is, as ICS has indicated in its opinion, a Privacy Impact Assessment, in which a Privacy Officer is involved. In that assessment, it has been established which personal data are processed, who has access to this personal data, what retention periods apply, and whether there is any transfer outside the EU and the measures that have been taken for this transfer. As the AP has also considered earlier, ICS has been aware of the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects in the processing of personal data, but has insufficiently recognized that this should have led to the carrying out of a DPIA.

6.4  
Consideration of the circumstances based on the Guidelines  

50. The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) established the final text of the Guidelines on May 24, 2023. As mentioned above, the EDPB has established joint principles regarding the calculation of fines for violations of the GDPR.  

51. The Guidelines describe a methodology in which the following steps are sequentially considered:  

1. Which and how many actions and violations are under assessment;  
2. What starting amount serves as the basis for calculating the fine for this;  
3. Whether there are mitigating or aggravating circumstances that necessitate an adjustment of the amount from step 2;  
4. What maximum amounts apply for the violations and whether any increases from the previous step do not exceed this amount;  
5. Whether the final amount of the calculated fine meets the requirements of effectiveness, deterrence, and proportionality, and if necessary, is adjusted accordingly.  

52. The number of actions that resulted in violations of the GDPR and the starting amount for fine calculation have already been qualified under paragraph 6.2.  

53. Like the Fine Policy Rules, the Guidelines stipulate that the AP examines whether there are mitigating or aggravating circumstances that may lead to an adjustment in the qualification of the infringement. This should be done based on the circumstances mentioned in Article 83, second paragraph, introductory phrase and under a to k, GDPR.

54. First of all, attention must be paid to the severity of the infringement.17 This takes into account the nature, seriousness, and duration of the infringement, as well as the intentional or negligent nature of the infringement and the categories of personal data processed. These factors have already been discussed in paragraphs 43 to 46. This has led to the conclusion in paragraph 49 that the seriousness of the infringement is classified as not low.  

55. The Guidelines stipulate that, from the perspective of fairness, the size of the company must be taken into account when calculating the amount of the fine. The size of the company is determined based on revenue. According to the case law18 of the Court of Justice of the European Union, the revenue of the entire group must be used to determine the upper limit of the fine. ICS is a wholly-owned subsidiary of ABN-AMRO. Therefore, the size of the company will be determined based on the global revenue of ABN-AMRO.19 ABN-AMRO achieved a revenue of €7.841 billion in 2022. Since ABN-AMRO's revenue exceeds €156.8 million, the GDPR prescribes a maximum fine of 2% of the total global annual revenue.20  

56. Subsequently, the Guidelines prescribe that the other circumstances referred to in Article 83 GDPR are taken into account. As already mentioned, sections c to f and sections h to j of Article 7 of the Fine Policy Rules were found to be irrelevant in the case of ICS. These sections correspond to the prescribed elements that must be considered under the Guidelines and are therefore not relevant in the case of ICS.

This provision corresponds to Article 83, paragraph 2, introductory part and under k, GDPR. 60. Finally, it must be assessed whether the fine is effective, proportionate, and deterrent. Based on Article 49 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU, the administrative fine, considering the circumstances of the specific case, must not lead to a disproportionate outcome. This is also laid down in Articles 3:4 and 5:46, second paragraph, of the General Administrative Law Act (Awb).  

61. Based on Article 83, paragraph 5, introductory part and under b, GDPR, the AP may impose an administrative fine for the violations described above. The purpose of imposing an administrative fine may lie in both punishing unlawful conduct and promoting compliance with applicable regulations.  

62. Considering the nature, seriousness, and duration of the infringement, as well as the other factors from Article 83, paragraph 2, GDPR as assessed in this chapter, imposing an administrative fine under these circumstances has an effective and deterrent effect. Furthermore, it has not been shown that the infringement cannot be attributed to ICS.  

63. Given all the circumstances mentioned above, the AP concludes that a fine of €150,000 for the infringement of not conducting a DPIA (Article 35, paragraph 1, GDPR) is appropriate and warranted in this case.  

7. Decision  
64. The AP imposes an administrative fine of €150,000 (say: one hundred fifty thousand euros) on International Card Services B.V. for violating Article 35, paragraph 1, GDPR.21

Article 83  
General conditions for the imposition of administrative fines  
1. Each supervisory authority ensures that the administrative fines imposed under this article for the infringements mentioned in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 of this regulation are effective, proportionate, and dissuasive in each case.  
2. Administrative fines are imposed, depending on the circumstances of the specific case, in addition to or instead of the measures referred to in Article 58, paragraph 2, points a) to h) and j). In deciding whether to impose an administrative fine and determining its amount, the following shall be duly considered for each specific case:  
a) the nature, seriousness, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing in question as well as the number of affected data subjects and the extent of the damage suffered by them;  
b) the intentional or negligent nature of the infringement;  
c) the measures taken by the data controller or the processor to mitigate the damage suffered by data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the data controller or the processor, considering the technical and organizational measures they have implemented in accordance with Articles 25 and 32;  
e) previous relevant infringements by the data controller or the processor;  
f) the degree to which the data controller or the processor has cooperated with the supervisory authority to remedy the infringement and mitigate its possible negative effects;  
g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement;  
h) the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, in particular whether, and if so to what extent, the data controller or the processor reported the infringement;  
i) compliance with the measures referred to in Article 58, paragraph 2, insofar as they have previously been taken against the data controller or the processor in question regarding the same matter;  
j) adherence to approved codes of conduct in accordance with Article 40 or approved certification mechanisms in accordance with Article 42; and  
k) any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as financial gains made, or losses avoided, whether or not directly resulting from the infringement.  
3. If a data controller or a processor intentionally or negligently infringes multiple provisions of this regulation concerning the same or related processing activities, the total fine shall not exceed that for the most serious infringement.
