Dear Mr. Bruins, 

The Central Archive for Special Jurisdiction (CABR) is the most consulted and largest archive regarding World War II in the Netherlands. This archive contains approximately 485,000 files of individuals suspected of collaboration with the occupier and/or prosecuted for this. The files contain many different types of documents such as reports, indictments, witness statements, records of court sessions, judgments, membership cards of National Socialist organizations, (personal) letters, photographs, and other materials that were used as evidence at the time. These documents contain (very) sensitive information, including information about living individuals. For example, personal data of a criminal nature of suspects of collaboration. But also special personal data of suspects and third parties, such as ethnicity, religious beliefs, political preferences, and sexual orientation. The National Archive is in the process of digitizing the CABR. It intends to gradually publish the CABR online for everyone starting January 2, 2025, and to make it fully text-searchable. This is part of the project War for the Judge. 

With this letter, the Authority for Personal Data (AP) expresses its serious concerns to you regarding the intended manner of disclosure. These concerns are fundamentally: 
- that it is highly likely that the intended manner of disclosure also pertains to ordinary personal data, special personal data, and personal data of a criminal nature of living individuals;  
- that publishing ordinary personal data, special personal data, and personal data of a criminal nature in the intended manner is unlawful unless a specific legal exception has been made for this; 
- that no such legal exception has been made on which this intended manner of disclosure can be based;       
- that the publicly accessible online publication with digital full-text searchability could lead to significant privacy violations for living individuals involved; 
- that there are other, less intrusive ways to achieve the accessibility of the CABR; 
- that the measure allowing individuals to report the unlawful processing of their personal data to the National Archive does not eliminate the unlawfulness of the processing. 

If the disclosure of the CABR is carried out in the intended manner, you will not comply with the requirements of the GDPR. Therefore, the AP issues a formal warning. The AP limits itself in this letter and with the warning to the intended manner of making the CABR digitally accessible. The AP refrains from making a judgment on the disclosure itself but focuses on the manner in which this is being implemented with the project War for the Judge.    

1. Background 

The National Archive manages the largest collection of archives in the Netherlands. The archives of the National Archive are generally public. However, there are restrictions on the public availability of certain archival documents. These restrictions are regulated in Article 15, paragraph 1, of the Archives Act (Aw). In order to respect the privacy of living individuals, restrictions were placed on the public availability of the CABR when it was transferred to the National Archive in 2000. These apply retroactively for a period of 75 years and will expire in 2025. The National Archive has decided that from that moment on, the archive will not only be publicly available for request in the reading room of the National Archive but will also be digitized and made available online for everyone and fully text-searchable. 

The AP sends you this letter fully aware that making the CABR accessible is a highly sensitive issue with significant societal implications. The AP is well aware that expectations have been raised among all stakeholders and the broader public regarding the accessibility of this archive after the expiration of the public availability restriction in 2025. In this context, the AP points out that the privacy risks addressed in this letter are not new facts for the National Archive. The National Archive has been warned about these risks multiple times and could have anticipated and prevented this intervention by the AP. 

The National Archive has followed a lengthy deliberation process regarding the intended decision to publish the CABR online for everyone and to make it fully text-searchable. In this process,

3. Intended publication likely unlawful  

Article 14 of the Archives Act (Aw) stipulates that archival documents located in an archive repository that are no longer subject to restrictions on public access are public. Everyone is entitled, subject to the restrictions arising from Articles 15, 16, and 17 of the Aw, to consult these archival documents free of charge and to make or have made images, copies, extracts, and adaptations thereof at their own expense. This provision of the Aw can be seen as a basis in the sense of Article 6, first paragraph, introductory phrase and under e, of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) to make personal data in the CABR available to everyone. However, it is a condition that the processing of personal data based on that basis is necessary.  
The necessity requirement means that the principles of proportionality and subsidiarity must be met. The principle of proportionality implies that the infringement on the interests of the individuals involved in the processing of personal data must not be disproportionate in relation to the purpose served by the processing. According to the principle of subsidiarity, the purpose for which the processing is carried out must be necessary.

The publicly accessible online publication and full-text digital searchability of the CABR goes beyond what is necessary to comply with Article 14 of the Archives Act (Aw). After all, this article only stipulates that archival documents for which there are no longer any restrictions on public access must be made public. Article 14 Aw does not prescribe that this public access must be fulfilled in the manner of the intended processing: making the CABR available online in a full-text digitally searchable format to everyone, where everyone has barrier-free access to the files in the CABR. This extensive form of availability is not standardly applied in other public archives. If this level of availability were necessary for the proper execution of the statutory task, then the National Archive would be failing in all those other cases.  
This online disclosure of the CABR significantly increases the infringement on the protection of personal data compared to, for example, digital availability in the reading room of the National Archive. When personal data is published online, an unlimited number of internet users can consult, store, and disseminate this personal data. This infringement on the protection of personal data is even more pronounced when the documents available online are also full-text searchable, ultimately even for search engines. This method of making information public is considered a serious infringement on the protection of personal data of the individuals involved. It is important to note that the CABR contains a lot of sensitive information, which can provoke significant reactions and, in certain cases, pose a risk of stigmatization for those involved.  
The processing of personal data in the execution of the statutory task of the National Archive is only lawful when the necessity requirement is met. Necessity is, after all, part of this processing basis under Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). When the necessity requirement is not met, the processing of personal data cannot be based on this legal basis. According to the opinion of the Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP), the intended processing likely goes further than what is necessary based on Article 14 Aw. Access to the documents can also be achieved in ways that make a less significant infringement on the fundamental rights of the individuals involved. This implies that the intended processing likely does not meet the necessity requirement and is therefore in violation of Article 6, paragraph 1, under e, GDPR. This applies to both the processing of ordinary personal data and the processing of special personal data and personal data of a criminal nature. The requirements of Article 6 GDPR must also be met for these last two categories.

personal data within the meaning of the GDPR.4 The protection of natural persons in the processing of their personal data is a fundamental right.5 Special categories of personal data receive additional protection under the GDPR. These are personal data that are so sensitive to privacy that their processing can have a significant impact on an individual. Special categories of personal data include, for example, data about a person's ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, sexual life, or health. The processing of special categories of personal data is prohibited unless an exception applies.6 The exceptions are regulated in Article 9, paragraph 2, of the GDPR. In this case, the exception based on overriding public interest may be applicable. This occurs when the processing is necessary for reasons of overriding public interest, based on Union law or national law, ensuring proportionality with the pursued objective, respecting the essential content of the right to the protection of personal data, and implementing appropriate and specific measures to protect the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject.7 This means that the processing prohibition in the GDPR is overridden if there is national law that complies with Article 9, paragraph 2, under g, of the GDPR, and that - among other things - provides for appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests of the data subjects.8 Currently, there is no such legal provision for making special categories of personal data available online to everyone in the CABR.9 Moreover, Article 2a, under d, of the Aw prohibits making special categories of personal data and criminal data available for consultation or use.10  
This means that as long as and to the extent that special categories of personal data and criminal data are present in the CABR, it is unlawful to publish the CABR online for everyone and to make it fully text-searchable.

Personal data of a criminal nature  

The processing of personal data concerning criminal convictions and criminal offenses is also subject to extra strict rules under the GDPR. These data may only be processed under government supervision or, if the processing is permitted, under Union law or national provisions that provide appropriate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. Comprehensive registers of criminal convictions may only be maintained under government supervision. Articles 32 and 33 of the UAVG contain national legal exceptions, allowing for the processing of personal data of a criminal nature in certain situations. For example, if the data subject has given explicit consent for this, or if the processing relates to personal data that has evidently been made public by the data subject. There is also a separate provision for scientific or historical research and statistical purposes. However, there is no national legal regulation based on which, in accordance with Article 10 of the GDPR, personal data of a criminal nature in the CABR files can be made accessible online and fully searchable in digital form for everyone.  

Scope of personal data of a criminal nature  

In the meeting between the AP, the National Archive, and the Ministry of Education, Culture and Science on October 21, it was discussed the scope of the concept of ‘personal data of a criminal nature’. In particular, the question was raised whether in a criminal file of a suspect, every personal data of a third party mentioned therein should also be regarded as personal data of a criminal nature of that third party. The AP is pleased to clarify the scope of the concept of ‘personal data of a criminal nature’.  
It is not the case that every person mentioned in a criminal file is considered to have personal data of a criminal nature. The concept of ‘personal data concerning criminal convictions and criminal offenses’ in the sense of Article 10 of the GDPR is a Union law concept that must be interpreted autonomously. The GDPR does not allow member states to provide their own, broader interpretation of this concept. The data must be sufficiently established to be considered personal data of a criminal nature. Established case law states that there must be “such concrete facts and circumstances that they can support a conviction qualifying as a criminal offense - in the sense of Article 350 of the Code of Criminal Procedure.” A mere isolated suspicion or

Special personal data and criminal personal data of other parties involved  

The protection of the GDPR only applies to living natural persons. The stricter rules of the GDPR for the processing of special personal data and personal data of a criminal nature also only apply if these data can be traced back to living natural persons. The National Archive has indicated that it will only make files of deceased natural persons available online to everyone. However, the AP understands that the files often also contain information about others. From the DPIA conducted by Eiffel, the additional risk assessment, the conversation on October 21, 2024, and the website about the CABR of the National Archive, it emerges that the files regularly contain special and criminal data about other parties involved than the one for whom the file was created. If searches have been conducted at the homes of suspects for whom a file has been created, various documents may have been seized and added to the file. For example, diaries or letters may be present in the files, or statements from doctors. The files may contain information about third parties, such as information about NSB membership, or there may be witness statements in the file describing that third parties have committed criminal offenses. If these third parties are still alive, and there are special personal data or personal data of a criminal nature in the CABR concerning them, then the provisions of the GDPR relating to these categories of personal data also apply to these third parties. It is therefore insufficient to merely establish that the person for whom the file was compiled has passed away. This does not sufficiently address an infringement on the protection of personal data of other parties involved than the one for whom a file has been compiled.  

Conclusion on special personal data and data of a criminal nature  

In light of the above, a processing prohibition applies in this case for special personal data. Furthermore, the intended manner of disclosure of personal data of a criminal nature is not in line with Article 10 GDPR. It is important to note that the National Archive does not intend to request explicit consent from any living parties involved for the publication of special personal data and personal data of a criminal nature concerning them, nor to ascertain on a case-by-case basis whether the information in the CABR has previously been made public by a (still living) party involved in question.

**5. Statistical Basis and Identifiability**  

In the decision of the General State Archivist and in the underlying documents, reference is made at certain points to the statistical probability that the individuals involved are still alive and the statistical probability that their personal data appear in the files. The AP notes that this statistical consideration does not align well with the GDPR and cannot be presented as a valid reason to legitimize an infringement on the protection of personal data of still living individuals. Relevant from a GDPR perspective is solely the observation that a portion of the people appearing in the files are still alive. The GDPR indeed provides protection to every natural person who is still living and whose personal data is being processed.  

Furthermore, it emerged from the documents and during the conversation with the National Archive that the National Archive assumes that the GDPR would not apply to still living persons whose date of birth is missing from the archive. According to the National Archive, these individuals would then not be identifiable. The AP points out that this interpretation of the concept of personal data is incorrect. Identifiability can indeed occur in many ways. A date of birth is not always necessary to identify someone. For example, consider individuals with a rare surname or individuals from families that have lived for generations in a small community. Information from the CABR can be traceable to individuals who are named in the documents, but even to people whose names are not mentioned, but from whose context it can be inferred who they are.

7. Absence of DPO Advice and Prior Consultation  

The AP has noted that advice from the Data Protection Officer (DPO) regarding the intended processing is missing. Under the GDPR, a data controller is required to seek DPO advice when conducting a DPIA. Given the volume and complexity of the GDPR issues concerning (the disclosure of) archives, the AP advises the Minister of OCW to provide more capacity and resources to carry out the DPO tasks for the National Archive. Under Article 38, paragraph 2, GDPR, the Minister is obliged to provide the necessary resources for fulfilling the DPO tasks.  

Furthermore, the National Archive has not submitted a request for Prior Consultation to the AP. If there is an intended processing for which the high residual risks cannot be mitigated by measures, a Prior Consultation with the AP is mandatory.  

8. Imposing a Formal Warning  

The AP has the authority to warn a data controller or processor that an intended processing is likely to infringe provisions of the GDPR. The website of the National Archive announces that the digitized CABR will be made available online in phases, starting January 2, 2025. A lengthy decision-making process has taken place during which legal advice was provided to the National Archive, highlighting the same risks that now prompt the AP to issue a formal warning. The AP observes that these advices have not deterred the National Archive from the intended method of disclosure. This indicates that it is likely that (parts of) the CABR will indeed be made fully text-searchable and available online to everyone if the AP does not exercise its powers. This circumstance, combined with the assessment that this method of publication is likely to lead to a breach of the GDPR, legitimizes the use of the authority to impose a formal warning. It is also relevant that the potential consequences for the individuals involved could be very significant. For this reason, the AP issues a formal warning to the Minister of OCW, as the data controller, not to proceed with the online publication and full-text digital searchability of the CABR.  
The AP notes that if the intended processing is nonetheless pursued, the AP may initiate a supervisory investigation and proceed to enforcement. The AP also points out that in the case of unlawful online publication, the Ministry of OCW may be held liable for material or immaterial damages suffered by living individuals due to that publication.  

This imposed warning is not a decision under the General Administrative Law Act. Filing an objection against the warning is not possible.  

The AP is always willing to further explain the content of this letter in a conversation. The AP requests that you inform them shortly about how this warning will be followed up, especially considering the intended date of online publication of the CABR.
