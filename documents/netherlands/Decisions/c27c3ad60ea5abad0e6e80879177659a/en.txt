Subject  
Decision to impose an administrative fine on Uber  
Dear    ,  

The Dutch Data Protection Authority (hereinafter: AP) is of the opinion that Uber Technologies Inc. and Uber B.V. (hereinafter collectively: Uber) have committed several violations of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) concerning transparency. These include the exclusive provision of guidance notes to drivers in the English language and the failure to provide information in a readily accessible form in response to a request for access, which is in violation of Article 12, paragraph 1, of the GDPR. Additionally, the digital form through which drivers can exercise their right of access and data portability is not easily accessible enough in the driver app, which contravenes Article 12, paragraph 2, of the GDPR. Furthermore, the information regarding retention periods in the privacy statement is not sufficiently specific (Article 13, paragraph 2, introductory part and under a, and Article 15, paragraph 1, under d, of the GDPR), and the information regarding data transfers is not complete and meaningful enough (Article 13, paragraph 1, introductory part and under f, of the GDPR). Finally, Uber has not explicitly mentioned the right to data portability in the privacy statement (Article 13, paragraph 2, under b, of the GDPR).  

For these violations, the AP imposes a total administrative fine of €10,000,000.00 on Uber Technologies Inc. and Uber B.V. jointly.

1. Background of the investigation  

Uber is an internationally operating company that acts as an intermediary between taxi drivers and passengers. Passengers use the general Uber App (for mobile phones) or possibly a browser, while drivers use the Uber Driver App (hereinafter: driver app).  

Creating an account for drivers is mandatory for using the driver app. Drivers are rated by their customers after a ride and are paid by Uber for the services provided.  

Uber has established a digital form, accessible via the Uber website and the driver app, which drivers can use to submit requests for access and data portability regarding their personal data. Uber has also prepared a privacy statement, which provides insight into the processing of personal data by Uber.  

On June 12, 2020, the Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL) received a complaint from the French non-governmental organization Ligue Des Droits De L’homme Et Du Citoyen (LDH) on behalf of 21 Uber drivers. Over time, an additional 151 Uber drivers joined the complaint, resulting in a submission on behalf of 172 complainants. On September 29, 2020, the LDH submitted a supplementary complaint to the CNIL. The complaints were forwarded by the CNIL to the AP, after which the International Research department of the AP initiated an investigation and prepared a report.  

2. Findings of the investigation report  

The report identifies 5 violations concerning transparency, namely:  

1) The digital form through which a request for access can be made is not easily accessible enough in the driver app (Article 12, paragraph 2, GDPR).  
2) In response to a request for access, Uber provides a copy of the personal data in a form that is not easily accessible, and the guidance notes provided are not in a language understandable to (French) drivers (English) (Article 12, paragraph 1, GDPR).  
3) Uber does not provide sufficiently specific information about retention periods in the privacy statement (Article 13, paragraph 2, a, GDPR and Article 15, paragraph 1, a and d GDPR).  
4) Uber does not specifically mention the names of the countries to which data transfers occur in the privacy statement, nor the specific protective measures (Article 13, paragraph 1, f, GDPR and Article 15, paragraph 2, GDPR).  
5) Uber does not explicitly mention the right to data portability in its privacy statement (Article 13, paragraph 2, b, GDPR).

4. Assessment  

4.1 Data Controller and Competence of the AP  

Uber B.V. is the Dutch establishment of Uber, while Uber Technologies Inc. is based in the United States and is the parent company of, among others, Uber B.V. Together, Uber B.V. and Uber Technologies Inc. determine the purpose and means for the processing of personal data of Uber drivers in the European Economic Area (EEA). The (French) drivers have an agreement with Uber B.V. For requests regarding the rights of data subjects, the rough division is that Uber B.V. is responsible for assessing such requests and that Uber Technologies Inc. provides the technical means and the data. Uber Technologies Inc. is also the publisher of the driver app.  

In Uber's privacy statement, Uber B.V. and Uber Technologies Inc. are also designated as joint data controllers. The joint data controller status has not been contested by Uber.  

Uber offers its services in multiple EU member states and processes personal data for these services. This means that data subjects in more than one member state experience significant consequences from the processing of personal data by Uber. This constitutes cross-border processing (Article 4, introductory phrase and under 23, sub a and b, GDPR). Since Uber's central administration in the EEA is located at Uber B.V., Uber B.V. is designated as the main establishment within the meaning of Article 4, introductory phrase and under 16, GDPR. The AP is competent to act as the lead supervisory authority (Article 56, first paragraph, GDPR).

4.2 Accessibility of the Request for Access Form  

The research report established that the digital form through which drivers can exercise their right to access and data portability is not easily accessible enough in the driver app because too many steps must be taken and the wording of the steps does not intuitively lead to the form. As a result, Uber does not sufficiently facilitate the exercise of the aforementioned rights of data subjects and violates Article 12, second paragraph, GDPR.  

Uber's Comments  
Uber states that neither the GDPR, legislative history, case law, applicable guidelines, nor authoritative commentary specifies how many actions a data subject may take to make a request for access or transfer. This also applies to the AP's argument that a data subject should be able to reach a page intuitively. Uber has a certain degree of discretion under Article 12, second paragraph, GDPR, to determine how it facilitates the right to access and data portability. The AP further introduces a new standard without justification by stating that the form must be "sufficiently easy" to find, according to Uber.  

In the guidelines, the EDPB explains the transparency obligations in such a way that they can be met by employing a "layered information structure." Drivers are therefore expected to go through multiple steps. This is also the standard in consumer law. Drivers use the menu in the app every day, and a reasonable and average driver should be able to find the form without any difficulty, even if it requires six clicks. Additionally, Uber points out that the driver app is designed for a smartphone with a small screen. Uber aims to prevent drivers from having to scroll through long menus when using the app and has therefore opted for menu paths.  

Uber then states that the AP does recognize that Uber offers multiple ways to submit a request for access or transfer, but the AP has incorrectly only considered the methods available in the driver app. The AP has also incorrectly disregarded the route that redirects through the driver app to the privacy statement on the website. Uber disputes the number of steps counted by the AP to reach the form: Uber uses a different count and arrives at a lower number of steps.  

Regarding the wording of the various steps, Uber states that "more obvious" is not an existing criterion in the GDPR and that it is subjective which route is the most obvious. Elevating the form under a heading "privacy" would detract from other menu options. Since February 2022, Uber has made adjustments to the routes in the driver app to the form, but these adjustments were not included in the research report.

In the version of the driver app assessed in the report, the following steps must be followed, schematically represented, to reach the form to submit a request for access or data portability: Menu > Help > Account and app issues > Legal concerns > Request your personal Uber data > Submit a privacy inquiry > log in via “Sign in to get help” or “Submit a privacy inquiry without an Uber account,” after which the driver arrives at the form (route 1). The investigation has shown that the primary interaction between Uber and the drivers takes place via the driver app. For this reason, the AP is of the opinion that at least in the driver app, drivers must be facilitated in exercising their rights in the sense of Article 12, paragraph 2, GDPR. In accordance with Recital 59 of the GDPR, arrangements must be in place to enable the data subject to exercise their rights more easily. This entails that the arrangements must align with the ways in which interactions with the data subjects occur. That Uber also provides other means for drivers to submit a request for access or data portability does not detract from the fact that drivers must be facilitated in exercising their rights at least in the driver app. The AP is of the opinion that route 2 to the request form in the research report has been wrongly disregarded, as the privacy statement on the website is automatically opened via the driver app and this does not detract from facilitating the submission of a request. 4.3 Response to access request: form and language  

The AP has determined that Uber provides information in response to an access request in a CSV file, without Uber providing information on how the information from such a file can be structured for presentation. Because Uber does not provide the information in an easily accessible form, it violates Article 12, paragraph 1, of the GDPR. Additionally, the AP has determined that Uber violates Article 12, paragraph 1, of the GDPR by providing the guidance notes, in which Uber gives further explanation about the values in the CSV files, only in English. This violation affects not only French drivers but nearly all Uber drivers in Europe.  

Form: Uber's viewpoint  
Uber states that the GDPR does not specifically prescribe which form meets the standard of 'easily accessible' or on what criteria this can be determined. The explanation in the Guidelines also makes the standard vague, and the AP cannot base a violation of the GDPR solely on the guidelines. According to Uber, the AP acknowledges on its website that the GDPR does not prescribe how information should be provided. CSV is indeed a very suitable file format for the

Uber also states that the standard should be the 'reasonable and average data subject', and according to this standard, the CSV files provided by Uber are easily accessible. Uber argues that CSV is a universal file format and will generally be automatically opened in software that can display tables by Windows and Apple operating systems. Even if that is not the case, Uber meets the 'easily accessible' standard because the data subject can simply adjust the display of the file, possibly after consulting a search engine for instructions. Form: assessment  
The AP states at the outset that it does not believe that providing information in a CSV file cannot meet the 'easily accessible' standard from Article 12, paragraph 1, GDPR. The Guidelines6 indicate the following regarding 'easily accessible': 'The element “easily accessible” means that the data subject does not have to figure out the information themselves; it must be immediately clear to the data subject where and how this information can be found.' In the information provided in response to a request for access, this relates, for example, to the structure in which the information is presented (such as the use of paragraphs).  

In a CSV file, values from a table are stored as lines of text. The values are separated by punctuation marks. When the information from a CSV file is displayed as lines of text and thus appears unstructured, the form in which the information is presented is not easily accessible. Data subjects cannot directly abstract the information from this. By using a spreadsheet program, the information from a CSV file can be displayed in table form. A CSV file can be automatically opened in table form in a spreadsheet program, but this is not always the case, depending on settings, for example. The files that Uber has provided to complainants contain a comma as a delimiter between the values. This is not automatically usable as a list delimiter in all cases, for example in countries or regions where a comma is also used as a decimal separator (such as the Netherlands and France). Uber does not provide any information on how the information from the CSV files can be structured if the file is not automatically opened in table form: the data subject must figure this out themselves7. Therefore, Uber has not provided the information in an easily accessible form and has violated Article 12, paragraph 1, GDPR.

Language: Uber's Position  
By assessing whether the guidance notes are provided in understandable language, the AP (Dutch Data Protection Authority) is, according to Uber, not adhering to the correct standard. According to Article 12, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, the language must be 'clear and simple.' Uber contends that the guidance notes are provided in clear and simple language. A reasonable command of English, rather than a good one, is sufficient to understand the guidance notes. Furthermore, Uber may assume that French drivers have a good command of English, as French taxi drivers (and thus Uber drivers) are required to have a VTC registration. This requires passing an exam that tests, among other things, English at A2 level.  

Language: Assessment  
Although the research report incorrectly applied the criterion of 'understandable' exclusively, this does not alter the outcome of the assessment. According to Article 12, paragraph 1, GDPR, information must be provided in clear and simple language. This means that when the data controller addresses individuals who speak a different language, a translation in that language must be provided. The requirement to use clear and simple language is closely related to understandability. Uber should not have assumed that French drivers possess sufficient knowledge of English to understand the English guidance notes. The fact that French Uber drivers must pass an exam for their VTC registration, which tests English at A2 level, does not change this, as a poor score in the English component can be compensated by a good score in other exam components. Therefore, passing the exam for a VTC registration does not indicate proficiency in English. Even if this were the case, the AP believes that proficiency in English at A2 level is insufficient to understand the English guidance notes. According to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), the following standard applies for reading and understanding a text at A2 level: “I can read very short, simple texts. I can find specific, predictable information in simple, everyday texts such as advertisements, brochures, menus, and timetables, and I can understand short, simple personal letters.” The guidance notes consist of a document of 26 pages explaining various very specific table values, such as telematics data and various device data. This exceeds the level of a very short and simple text as indicated by the CEFR. In this context, the AP also points to the fine imposed by the Swedish supervisory authority (IMY) on Spotify, among other reasons, for not providing technical log files in the language of the individuals concerned (but solely in English).

4.4 Privacy Statement: Retention Periods  

In its response to a request for access, Uber refers to the privacy statement for the retention periods. The investigation report has established that the information provided by Uber in the privacy statement regarding retention periods is too general, thereby violating Article 13, paragraph 2, under a, and Article 15, paragraph 1, under d, of the GDPR.  

Uber's Position  
Uber indicates that the explanation from the guidelines that it is insufficient to state that personal data will be retained as long as necessary for the legitimate purpose of processing goes beyond what the GDPR prescribes. This contradicts Article 5, paragraph 1, sub e, of the GDPR, which formulates the criterion for determining the retention period in exactly that way, and the AP cannot base a violation solely on the Guidelines. Furthermore, information must be provided concisely and understandably in accordance with Article 12, paragraph 1, of the GDPR. In the case of Uber as a multinational, there are different retention periods per processing per country, in certain countries per city, per category of data subjects, and those periods are subject to change. Mentioning specific retention periods would lead to an expansion of the privacy statement by dozens to hundreds of pages. Article 13, paragraph 2, under a, of the GDPR also only prescribes that retention periods must be mentioned if possible, which is not the case for Uber for the aforementioned reason. Uber also states that the AP cannot conclude a violation of Article 15, paragraph 1, of the GDPR based on the investigation report, because drivers are provided with information in various ways in response to a request for access, and this has not been included in the report. Additionally, the guidelines against which the AP assesses are not relevant to Article 15 of the GDPR. In the assessment of the version of the privacy statement dated October 15, 2020, the AP incorrectly did not take into account a passage that, according to Uber, meets the requirements against which the AP assesses.

Assessment  
In the version of the privacy statement dated November 12, 2019, Uber states that personal data (of drivers) will be retained as long as the user has an account. Additionally, Uber may retain information to the extent necessary for safety, security, and fraud prevention purposes, followed by an example. In the version dated September 1, 2021, the information regarding retention periods is summarized to indicate that Uber retains personal data as long as necessary for various purposes, followed by the mention that users (including drivers) can request the deletion of their account, after which the data will be deleted unless it needs to be retained for safety, security, and fraud prevention purposes or account-related matters for a longer period. Since Uber is bound by laws and regulations concerning (among others) drivers, this generally means a retention period of at least seven years for both the account and the data after a request for account deletion, according to Uber's privacy statement.  

Pursuant to Article 13, paragraph 2, introductory phrase and under a, GDPR, the data controller must inform the data subject at the time of obtaining personal data about the period for which the personal data will be stored, or, if that is not possible, the criteria for determining that period. The AP observes that Uber does not mention any (concrete) retention periods in either version of the privacy statement. Although Uber mentions a retention period of seven years in the privacy statement dated September 1, 2021, this period is not sufficiently concretely formulated (it only concerns a minimum retention period and applies 'in general,' without clarity on the circumstances under which this period is or is not applicable) and only relates to those cases where a request for account deletion has been made. Uber has indicated in its views, among other things, that there are indeed many different retention periods. The AP notes that these various retention periods are not mentioned in the privacy statement.  

In its views, Uber further indicated that, given the many different retention periods, it is not possible to name all concrete retention periods and that therefore it may suffice to mention the criteria for determining the periods. Naming all concrete retention periods would, according to Uber, lead to a privacy statement several pages long, which is contrary to Article 12, paragraph 1, GDPR. The AP agrees that Uber may suffice with mentioning the criteria for determining the retention periods, given the circumstances mentioned, but notes that these are also not sufficiently specified in the privacy statement. Merely stating in general terms that personal data will be retained as long as necessary for certain purposes (as Uber does) cannot be equated with naming criteria for determining the retention period. The obligation to name criteria for determining the retention period, in the opinion of the AP, cannot be interpreted otherwise than that data subjects must be able to determine the retention periods for their personal data, and the information provided by Uber is, in that regard, too general in nature. The AP concludes that Uber has violated Article 13, paragraph 2, introductory phrase and under a, GDPR. The AP finds, contrary to what is stated in the investigation report, insufficient grounds in the findings of the investigation to establish a violation of Article 15, paragraph 1, introductory phrase and under d, GDPR.

4.5 Privacy Statement: Transfer  

In its response to a request for access, Uber refers to the privacy statement for information regarding transfers. The investigation report has established that the privacy statement does not mention the countries outside the EEA to which personal data is transferred and what specific measures have been taken for this, resulting in Uber violating Article 13, paragraph 1, introductory phrase and under f, and Article 15, paragraph 2, GDPR.  

Uber's Position  
Uber argues that Article 13, paragraph 1, introductory phrase and under f, GDPR does not require the naming of transfer countries. The interpretation of the EDPB in the guidelines that, in accordance with the principle of propriety, information about transfers must be as meaningful as possible and that this generally means that the third country must be named, is incorrect. The principle of propriety is already filled in, given the introductory phrase, in Article 13, paragraph 2, GDPR (as understood by the AP: exhaustively filled in) and therefore not applicable to the first paragraph of Article 13 GDPR. The EDPB's interpretation contradicts the text and systematics of Article 13, paragraphs 1 and 2, GDPR. Furthermore, the AP cannot base a violation solely on the guidelines. The guidelines also only require that, in general, third countries must be named, and it is impossible for Uber to determine in advance to which specific countries the personal data will be transferred. Uber would then have to include all 72 countries in which it offers its app in the privacy statement, which would not be meaningful or understandable. The same applies to naming all protective measures per country. Uber also believes that, given the shared controller responsibility of Uber B.V. and Uber Technologies Inc., there is no transfer of personal data to third countries in this context, as the processing, according to Article 3, paragraph 1, GDPR, falls within the scope of the GDPR.  

Article 15, paragraph 2, GDPR also does not require the naming of transfer countries, and the explanation from the guidelines is not applicable to Article 15. Uber further states that the AP cannot conclude a violation of Article 15, paragraph 2, GDPR based on the investigation report, as drivers are provided with information in various ways in response to a request for access, and this has not been included in the report. The versions of the privacy statement assessed by the AP fully meet the requirements set by Article 13, paragraph 1, introductory phrase and under f, GDPR, particularly because in all versions it was possible to click on 'Standard contractual clauses', after which the standard provisions could be downloaded from a webpage of the European Commission.  

Finally, Uber asserts that the AP incorrectly did not include the privacy statement of June 13, 2022, in its assessment.

The data controller intends to transfer personal data to a third country. The AP observes that Uber, in the various versions of the privacy statement, only mentions that Uber transfers personal data to a third country or third countries. Regarding the applicable safeguards, Article 13, paragraph 1, introductory part and under f, GDPR requires that it must be indicated whether or not there is an adequacy decision from the Commission, or what other appropriate safeguards apply and how a copy of these can be obtained or where they can be consulted. The requirements for information provision set out in this provision are so specific that data subjects must be given access to detailed information about the safeguards used to protect their personal data during transfer. Regarding Uber's response to a request for access, the AP, unlike what is stated in the investigation report, sees insufficient grounds in the findings of the investigation to establish a violation of Article 15, paragraph 2, GDPR.

Uber's Position  
According to Uber, Article 20, paragraph 1, GDPR indicates that the right to data portability consists of two actions, namely obtaining (from Uber) and transferring (by the data subject). Uber states that the right to data portability is mentioned in the privacy statement, specifically as ‘receiving data’. Uber also explains in its privacy statement what the right to data portability entails, deliberately avoiding complex terms (such as data portability). The guidelines22 (which state that a distinction must be made between the right to data portability and other rights) provide only an explanation, and the AP cannot base a violation solely on this. Assessment  
Article 13, paragraph 2, introductory phrase and under b, GDPR obliges the data controller to inform the data subject about the right to data portability when providing personal data. The AP observes that Uber does not explicitly mention the right to data portability in the various versions of the privacy statement. The AP does not follow Uber's position that informing about the right to data portability is covered by ‘receiving data’, as receiving the personal data processed by Uber can also be understood as fulfilling the right of access under Article 15 GDPR. The obligation under Article 13, paragraph 2, introductory phrase and under b, GDPR to inform about the right to data portability naturally implies that this right must be named separately and explicitly. This is also outlined in the guidelines.24 By not naming the right to data portability separately and explicitly, Uber has violated Article 13, paragraph 2, introductory phrase and under b, GDPR.  

As of November 3, 2022, Uber does explicitly mention the right to data portability in its privacy statement. The violation has thus been rectified.

5.2  Systematics for determining the fine amount  

The EDPB approved the adoption of the Guidelines 04/2022 on the calculation of administrative fines under the GDPR (hereinafter: the Guidelines) during its plenary meeting on May 24, 2023.29 The Guidelines are directly applicable, as they do not provide for transitional provisions for procedures that were already ongoing at the time of approval of the Guidelines. The AP will therefore apply these Guidelines to this case.30  

The Guidelines describe a methodology in which the following steps are sequentially considered:  

1. which and how many actions and violations are under assessment;  
2. what starting amount serves as the basis for calculating the fine for this;  
3. whether there are mitigating or aggravating circumstances that necessitate an adjustment of the amount from step 2;  
4. what maximum amounts apply for the violations and whether any increases from the previous step do not exceed this amount;  
5. whether the final amount of the calculated fine meets the requirements of effectiveness, deterrence, and proportionality, and if necessary is adjusted accordingly.  

These steps will be sequentially addressed below.  

5.3  Calculation of the starting amount

The AP has also established that Uber did not provide the information about the data in the CSV file (the guidance notes) in the language of the data subjects, but only in English. Determining the starting amount  

According to Article 83, paragraph five, of the GDPR, the maximum fine is 4% of the annual revenue. The annual revenue amounts, as stated, to €29.750 billion, so the maximum fine for each of the violations is €1.19 billion.  

Considering what has been discussed under i) and ii), the AP is of the opinion that the level of seriousness of the violations should be classified as "low." According to the Guidelines, for violations with a low level of seriousness, the starting amount should be determined at a point between 0 and 10% of the maximum fine. The general rule is that the more serious the violation is within its own category, the higher the starting amount will be.  
The AP believes that, given the described circumstances, the violations in question are of minor seriousness in themselves. Therefore, the starting point for calculating the fine should be relatively low compared to the starting amount. Taking all of the above into account, the AP sets the starting amount in this case at €5 million for each of the violations (€10 million in total). This corresponds to 0.42% of the applicable maximum fine.  

5.4 Assessment of mitigating or aggravating circumstances  

5.4.1 Step 3: Assessing relevant circumstances  

As stated in the Guidelines, it must then be considered whether the circumstances of the case warrant setting the fine higher or lower than the previously determined starting amount. The circumstances to be taken into account are listed in Article 83, paragraph two, introductory part, and under a to k, of the GDPR. The circumstances mentioned in that provision must each be considered only once. In the previous step, account has already been taken of the nature, gravity, and duration of the violation (part a), the intentional or negligent nature of the infringement (part b), and the categories of personal data (part g). This leaves parts c to f and h to k.  

The only applicable circumstance is the manner in which the AP became aware of the violation, specifically whether, and if so to what extent, the data controller reported the violation (part h). In this case, Uber did not report the violations itself, but they came to the attention of the AP through complaints. However, according to the Guidelines, this is assessed as "neutral" and therefore has no impact on the amount of the fine to be imposed.  

The other circumstances are not applicable in this case because the circumstances to which they refer do not occur in this instance.  

5.5 Determining the amount of the fine  

In paragraph 5.3.2, a starting amount of €5 million has been determined for each of the violations. In paragraph 5.4.1, it has been concluded that the only circumstance that can be further considered must be assessed as neutral. The conclusion is therefore that the fine should be set at €5 million for each of the two violations (€10 million in total).  

5.5.1 Step 4: Checking the maximum amounts applicable to the violations  

As stated, a maximum fine of 4% of the global annual revenue of the company applies to the identified violations, also considering Uber's revenue. Given Uber's revenue (€29.750 billion), the legal maximum of the fine to be imposed is therefore €1.19 billion per violation.

5.5.2 Step 5: Assessment of the requirements of effectiveness, proportionality, and deterrence  

Finally, the AP assesses whether the fine is effective, proportional, and deterrent. Based on Article 49, third paragraph, of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU and Articles 3:4 and 5:46, second paragraph, of the General Administrative Law Act (Awb), the administrative fine, given the circumstances of the specific case, must not lead to an disproportionate outcome.  

According to Article 83, fifth paragraph, introductory phrase and under b, of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the AP may impose an administrative fine for the violations described above. As outlined in the Guidelines, the imposition of a fine can be considered effective if it achieves the purpose for which it was imposed. That purpose may lie in, on the one hand, punishing unlawful conduct and, on the other hand, promoting compliance with applicable regulations. Given the nature, severity, and duration of the infringement, as well as the other factors from Article 83, second paragraph, GDPR, as assessed in paragraphs 5.3.2 and 5.4 of this decision, the AP is of the opinion that the imposition of an administrative fine under these circumstances achieves both goals and is therefore effective and deterrent. The amount of the administrative fine, which has also been determined based on Uber's revenue, is also considered effective and deterrent by the AP.  

The AP considers the fine to be proportional in light of the seriousness of the violation and the size of the company. Uber has indicated in its opinion that the imposition of a measure leads to disproportionate consequences for Uber, as Uber is constantly working to improve its services and has always shown a willingness to cooperate with the AP. The AP sees no reason to consider the fine disproportionate in this regard. Compliance with the provisions of the GDPR and cooperation with the AP in exercising its powers are, after all, legally required.
