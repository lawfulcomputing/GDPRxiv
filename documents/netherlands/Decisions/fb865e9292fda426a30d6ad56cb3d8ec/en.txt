Dear [confidential], 

The Authority for Personal Data (AP) has decided to impose an administrative fine of € 290,000,000 on Uber Technologies Inc. and Uber B.V. (hereinafter collectively referred to as Uber). The AP is of the opinion that Uber has violated Article 44 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), as Uber allowed the transfer of personal data to the United States without providing appropriate safeguards as stipulated in Chapter V of the GDPR. 

The AP believes that imposing an administrative fine on Uber is not only appropriate but also necessary. The AP has determined that the intended purpose of Article 44 GDPR, namely the continuity of the high level of protection of the GDPR in the transfer of personal data to third countries, has not been guaranteed by Uber. The AP considers this serious and has therefore proceeded to enforcement against Uber. 

This decision explains the administrative fine. It will successively address the reasons for the investigation, the facts, the established violation, and the amount of the fine. Finally, the dictum will follow.

1. Reason for investigation 
1. 
Uber is an internationally operating company that acts as an intermediary between taxi drivers and passengers. Passengers use the general Uber App (for mobile phones) or possibly a browser to book a ride. Drivers use the Uber Driver App (hereinafter: driver app) to offer rides. 
 
2. 
Creating an account for drivers is mandatory for the use of the driver app. Drivers are rated by their customers after a ride and are paid by Uber for the services rendered. 
 
3. 
On June 12, 2020, the Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés (CNIL) received a complaint from the French non-governmental organization Ligue Des Droits De L’homme Et Du Citoyen (LDH, hereinafter: complainant) on behalf of 21 Uber drivers. Subsequently, an additional 151 Uber drivers joined the complaint, bringing the total to 172 complainants. LDH then submitted a supplementary complaint to the CNIL on September 29, 2020, which was forwarded to the AP on January 11, 2021. 
 
4. 
In this supplementary complaint, the complainant asserts that Uber's legal position is unclear following the so-called SCHREMS II ruling of the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter: CJEU). In the SCHREMS II ruling, the CJEU states that there is no equivalent level of protection in the United States for the transfer of personal data from the European Union (hereinafter also: EU) to the United States. As a result, the adequacy decision with the United States (“Privacy Shield”) was no longer valid as an instrument for the transfer of personal data. The CJEU indicated that it was still possible to transfer data to the United States using “Standard Contractual Clauses” (SCC), provided that sufficient additional measures are taken to ensure an equivalent level of protection.1 
 
5. 
On April 16, 2021, the AP formally notified Uber in writing that it had initiated an investigation following the complaints filed by French Uber drivers. The investigation focused on whether Uber complies with the requirements set out in Chapter V of the GDPR for the transfer of personal data of drivers from the EU to the United States. 
 
6. 
The AP subsequently established a violation of Article 44 of the GDPR in its investigation, as Uber allowed transfers of personal data to take place without providing appropriate safeguards as stipulated in Chapter V of the GDPR. The investigation report was sent to Uber by letter dated April 13, 2023. Uber provided its comments on the investigation report in a letter dated June 9, 2023. On July 5, 2023, Uber elaborated on its comments during a hearing at the AP's office.

Introduction  

7.  
Uber is the name of an electronic platform developed by Uber Technologies Incorporated (hereinafter UTI). The main headquarters of UTI is located in San Francisco, United States. The Uber platform is represented in the European Economic Area (hereinafter: EEA) by Uber B.V. (hereinafter UBV). In the cities where Uber operates, Uber enables passengers to order transportation services via the Uber platform. One can request a ride with Uber in various European cities and countries, and Uber has drivers who work in those territories.2  

8.  
According to Uber's privacy statement, UBV and UTI are joint controllers for the processing of personal data of Uber drivers in the EEA.3 Their responsibilities regarding compliance with the obligations under the GDPR are described in an agreement.4  

9.  
UBV has several data processing agreements with other subsidiaries of Uber in EEA countries. For example, with respect to Uber France SAS, Uber's subsidiary in France, UBV is designated as the data controller and Uber France SAS as the processor.5 The data processing agreement states that within this mutual relationship, UBV is the entity that provides the personal data of Uber drivers to other subsidiaries of Uber in the EEA. However, in an explanation, Uber has indicated that while UBV is responsible for the personal data of Uber drivers in the EEA, technically these are made accessible by UTI.6  

10.  
Uber drivers in the EEA must enter into a contract with UBV to become a driver for Uber.7

The processing activities  

16.  
Uber has a centralized IT infrastructure on the platform and the servers of UTI located in the United States. The personal data of Uber drivers located in the EEA is therefore processed in the United States. The AP has identified the following two relevant situations in its investigation, in which processing occurs that qualifies as transfers in the sense of the GDPR.  

17.

18.  
The second situation concerns the exercise of GDPR rights by data subjects. Firstly, personal data of Uber drivers, which are processed under the responsibility of UBV within the EEA, end up on the servers of UTI in the United States via the driver app and email (from the device in the EEA). 3. Uber's Position  
46.  
The AP is of the opinion that Uber has violated Article 44 of the GDPR, as Uber allowed transfers of personal data to the United States from August 6, 2021, to November 27, 2023, while there was no valid adequacy decision and no appropriate safeguards were provided as stipulated in Chapter V of the GDPR. Article 44 of the GDPR states that “personal data that is processed or intended to be processed after transfer to a third country or an international organization may only be transferred if, without prejudice to the other provisions of this regulation, the controller and the processor have complied with the conditions laid down in this chapter; this also applies to further transfers of personal data from the third country or international organization to another third country or another international organization. The GDPR does not provide a definition of 'transfer.' However, in its Guidelines, the EDPB has established three cumulative criteria that a transfer must meet:  

1. A controller or a processor (“exporter”) falls under the GDPR for the specific processing activity.  
2. Personal data that is the subject of this processing is provided by the exporter through forwarding or made available in another way to another controller, joint controller, or processor (“importer”).49

However, in the opinion of the AP, this does not mean that the forwarding of data by Uber, as it occurred and still occurs according to the description of situations 1 and 2, does not involve transfers of personal data from UBV to UTI within the meaning of the GDPR.63 Uber uses the driver app as a technical tool to transmit personal data from the EEA to the United States. Therefore, UBV bears joint responsibility and has (technical) control over the transfer of personal data from the EEA to the United States.  

89.  
Furthermore, the AP is of the opinion that the question of whether there is an international transfer should not only be assessed based on the determination that it is the driver who operates the driver app via his private device. It must also be taken into consideration that Uber exerts a significant degree of influence over the context in which those actions and the will of the driver are established. This context consists of various elements set forth by Uber that effectively leave the driver with no other choice but to enter the data into the Uber app. The following will explain what these elements are and how they determine the context in which the data transfers occur. It will become clear that attributing the transfer to the driver by Uber (who is merely a minor link in the entire process and context of the processing) undermines the protection that the GDPR offers for the processing of personal data.

For the above reasons, the AP considers the impact of this aspect to increase the overall severity of the infringement.  

Ad iii) Turnover of the company  

158.  
The Guidelines stipulate that from the perspective of fairness, the starting amount of the fine must be related to the size of the company. The size of the company is determined based on its turnover. For a small company, with a turnover of up to €2 million, the starting amount is generally limited to 0.2% to 0.4% of the actual starting amount, and the starting amount increases as the company's turnover increases. If a company has a turnover of more than €500 million, the amount of the fine is determined as a percentage of the company's annual turnover.122 This means that the size and turnover of the company are already factored into the amount of the fine, so the starting amount does not need to be adjusted on that basis.

159.  
As stated in recitation 150 of the GDPR, when imposing a fine on a company, the "company" must be regarded as a company in accordance with Articles 101 and 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. Established case law from the Court of Justice of the European Union indicates that a company is any entity that carries out an economic activity, regardless of its legal form and the manner in which it is financed. Thus, it concerns the economic unit of the company and not the legal entities within it. Different companies or entities within the same economic unit can therefore together constitute a company in the sense of the aforementioned provisions.  

160.  
Uber B.V. is indirectly a wholly-owned subsidiary of Uber Technologies Inc. Therefore, for the application of Article 83 of the GDPR, they must be considered as the same company.  

161.  
As mentioned in the Guidelines, revenue can be determined based on the annual accounts of the company for the previous financial year. According to Article 83, paragraphs four to six, of the GDPR, the global revenue for the previous financial year is taken into account.  

162.  
Uber Technologies Inc. has published its annual accounts for 2023 on its website.123 On page 75, a consolidated overview of the company is included. It can be inferred from this that the global revenue of the company for 2023 amounts to $37.281 billion. This corresponds to €34.235 billion.124  

Determine starting amount  

163.  
According to Article 83, paragraph five, of the GDPR, the maximum fine is 4% of the annual revenue. The annual revenue amounts to €34.235 billion, so the maximum fine for the infringement is €1.369 billion.  

164.  
The assessment of the above circumstances and factors determines the overall seriousness of the infringement committed by Uber. This involves a thorough evaluation of the specific circumstances of the case in which all circumstances must be considered in conjunction.  

165.  
In light of what has been considered under i) and ii), the AP is of the opinion that the level of severity of the infringement should be classified as "high." According to the Guidelines, for infringements with a high level of severity, the starting amount should be determined at a point between 20% and 100% of the maximum fine, which in this case is €1.369 billion. This corresponds to an amount between €273.881 million and €1.369 billion. As a general rule, the more serious the infringement is within its own category, the higher the starting amount will be.

The AP is of the opinion that, given the described circumstances, the infringement is serious. In this regard, the AP has taken into account, among other factors, the number of affected individuals and the fact that the violation has been terminated.

Based on the categorization of the infringement, the severity of the infringement, and the turnover of the company, the AP sets the starting amount for the violation of Article 44 GDPR in this case at €290 million.  

5.3.3  
Step 3: Assessing other relevant circumstances  

168.  
As stated in the Guidelines, it must then be considered whether the circumstances of the case warrant setting the fine higher or lower than the aforementioned starting amount. The circumstances to be taken into account are listed in Article 83, second paragraph, introductory phrase and subparagraphs a through k of the GDPR. The circumstances mentioned in that provision must each be considered only once. In the previous step, account has already been taken of the nature, seriousness, and duration of the violation (subparagraph a), the intentional or negligent nature of the infringement (subparagraph b), and the categories of personal data (subparagraph g). This leaves subparagraphs c through f and h through k.  

169.  
The only applicable circumstance is the manner in which the AP became aware of the infringement, specifically whether, and if so to what extent, the data controller reported the infringement (subparagraph h). In this case, Uber did not report the infringements itself, but they came to the attention of the AP through complaints. However, according to the Guidelines, this is assessed as "neutral" and therefore has no consequences for the amount of the fine to be imposed.  

5.3.4  
Step 4: Checking the maximum amounts applicable to the infringements  

170.  
As mentioned, a maximum fine of 4% of the global annual turnover of the company applies to the identified infringement, considering Uber's turnover. The annual turnover amounts to €34.235 billion, so the maximum fine for the infringement is €1.369 billion.  

171.  
Based on the above considerations, the AP sets the fine amount for the identified infringement at €290 million. This is below the legal maximum, so there is no exceeding of that limit.  

5.3.5  
Step 5: Assessment of the requirements of effectiveness, proportionality, and deterrence  

172.  
Finally, it must be assessed whether the fine is effective, proportionate, and deterrent. Furthermore, the administrative fine, given the circumstances of the case, should not lead to an disproportionate outcome.  

173.  
The Guidelines stipulate that the imposition of an administrative fine can be considered effective if it achieves the purpose for which it was imposed. The purpose may be to punish unlawful behavior, as well as to promote compliance with applicable regulations. Given the above considerations regarding the nature, seriousness, and duration of the infringement, as well as the aggravating and mitigating circumstances of Article 83, second paragraph, GDPR, the AP is of the opinion that the present administrative fine achieves both objectives and is therefore effective and deterrent.  

174.  
The AP further believes that the imposition of the fine and its amount, due to the seriousness of the infringement and the size of the company, is not disproportionate. Uber has indicated in its views that the imposition of an administrative fine would lead to disproportionate consequences for Uber, as any notification about the fine to the New York Stock Exchange could have significant consequences for the share price of Uber. However, the AP does not see this as a reason to consider the fine disproportionate. While it cannot be ruled out that the fine may have some influence on the share price of Uber, it has not been demonstrated that this influence is such that the fine must be considered disproportionate. It is also important that the amount of the fine must be sufficiently deterrent and must reflect the nature and seriousness of the infringement. In the opinion of the AP, no other special circumstances have occurred in this regard that would render the fine disproportionate.  

175.  
The AP further notes the following regarding the AP Fine Policy Guidelines 2019. If the AP had to impose a fine based on this policy, it would have seen reason to apply Article 8.4 of the AP Fine Policy Guidelines 2019, given Uber's global turnover and thus the large scale of its status as an offender. The determination of fine amounts within the fine bandwidth of the AP Fine Policy Guidelines 2019 would not lead to an appropriate punishment that is effective, proportionate, and deterrent. The conclusion is that the AP would have imposed a fine of the same amount under the AP Fine Policy Guidelines 2019.
