Dear Mr. Kuipers,

In your letter dated January 27, 2023, the Authority for Personal Data (AP) was consulted based on the provisions of Article 36, fourth paragraph, of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), regarding the draft amendment of several laws in the area of the Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport to strengthen the legal basis for data processing (hereinafter: the draft). The request for advice was supplemented on March 2, 2023.

Scope of the draft
The draft concerns a consolidation act consisting of thirteen separate parts aimed at amending various laws in the healthcare sector.1 The draft assigns and amends powers and obligations regarding the processing of personal data, including health data, for various actors within and outside the healthcare sector. The AP observes that this again concerns the resolution of a few specific situations, but that a more overarching and coherent approach to the problems surrounding the legal bases for the processing of health data is still awaited. Bottlenecks in the processing of health data for various purposes (such as healthcare provision, but also quality monitoring, collaboration, policy and planning, scientific research) have already been signaled and recognized multiple times from various perspectives. In this regard, the AP advises you to proceed with the necessary urgency in the project you have initiated regarding the 'reassessment of legal bases'2 to achieve more clarifications and concrete results.

Advice  
1. National Contact Point for eHealth  

Within MyHealth@EU3, a European digital infrastructure consisting of a network of national contact points for eHealth (NCPeH), each member state can establish a national NCPeH that connects with other European contact points.4 An NCPeH is a national hub that serves as a communication gateway between the healthcare information systems of EU member states, thereby making healthcare data digitally available and receiving it for the provision of care.5 6  

Article 15k of the Act on Additional Provisions for the Processing of Personal Data in Healthcare (hereinafter Wabvpz) designates the Minister of VWS as the Dutch NCPeH. The Minister is authorized to receive healthcare data of patients from other EU member states from Dutch healthcare providers and to provide that data to the NCPeH of the relevant member state.7 8 Additionally, the Minister forwards the healthcare data originating from another member state to the Dutch healthcare provider that requests it.9  
The legislative proposal goes further than it seems  
The establishment of an NCPeH effectively means participation in the European exchange of personal data.10 This exchange will be further expanded when the EU proposal for a regulation on a European Health Data Space (EHDS)11 is adopted.12  

This is a far-reaching step, which is also being critically monitored by the House of Representatives.13 Not only is the structural exchange of patient data "across borders" a novelty, but similar issues arise as those previously encountered with the (Dutch) National Switch Point, such as the delineation of access to patient data (how is it prevented that data is requested from a person who is not receiving treatment from the requesting healthcare provider?).14 The National Switch Point, as is known, had a turbulent history.15  

Another point that may be sensitive – and has no precedent – is the role of the government in these processes. In the case of the Dutch National Switch Point, it was specifically chosen to have the sector itself16 be the data controller.  

The explanatory memorandum does not address these more fundamental issues.  

The directive for regulation 6.4 states the following regarding the admissibility of omnibus laws:  

"An amendment law that regulates multiple substantive topics will only be pursued in principle if:  

a. the different components are interconnected;  
b. the different components are not of a scope and complexity that justifies a separate legislative proposal; and  
c. it is not expected in advance that one of the components is so politically controversial that a proper parliamentary treatment of the other components is jeopardized."  

Given the above, it is possible that this component of the draft does not meet condition c.  

The AP advises that the explanatory memorandum pay more attention to the position of the draft in relation to the EHDS, the described history, the role of the government, and the introduction of structural exchange of patient data across borders. The AP also advises considering whether this component should not be regulated in a separate legislative proposal. The explanatory memorandum should then be expanded in such a way that a well-founded assessment can be made regarding the intended European exchange of patient data.

**3. Contact Persons CIZ**  

Article 3.2.3, first paragraph, of the Long-Term Care Act (Wlz) assigns the Center for Indication of Care (CIZ) the task of issuing an indication decision upon request from an insured person, in which the right to care for the insured person is established. According to the explanatory memorandum, there are cases where competent clients prefer that someone else acts as a contact person for the purpose of an application.  

In Article 9.1.1a, first and second paragraphs, of the Wlz, grounds are included for the CIZ to process the data about the contact person and to share this data with the care office. The third paragraph also provides a basis for the care office to discuss data about (the health of) the client with the contact person, if necessary, to determine which care provider can offer appropriate care. With the proposed Article 9.1.1a, fourth paragraph, of the Wlz, the processing in Article 9.1.1a, first and third paragraphs, of the Wlz is exempted from the prohibition in Article 9, second paragraph, part h, of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).  

In the opinion of the AP (Dutch Data Protection Authority), it is not necessary to include a separate basis for the processing of the contact person's data. Everyone can be assisted in dealings with administrative bodies or be represented by an authorized representative in the interest of their affairs, see Article 2:1, first paragraph, of the General Administrative Law Act (Awb). This also applies to a client who designates someone to act as a contact person on their behalf for the purpose of an application for an indication decision. The AP is of the opinion that this designation sufficiently addresses the processing of contact details of that contact person, that these details are shared with the care office to find an appropriate care provider, and that medical data of the client are discussed with the contact person.  

The AP advises that the proposed provision be deleted in its entirety.

**Proportionality**  
Firstly, the AP considers it insufficiently plausible that the legal obligation is suitable to achieve the desired goal, as it is unclear how the municipality can reach a homeless or roofless person after receiving a report. This is while, according to the explanatory memorandum, homeless and roofless individuals constitute a large part of the target group.40  

Secondly, the necessity of the legal obligation is also based on the task that the municipality would have to encourage wrongly uninsured individuals to insure themselves. However, under Articles 9a-9d of the Health Insurance Act (Zvw), the CAK can already take various measures aimed at insuring wrongly uninsured individuals.41 Moreover, the CAK knows exactly which individuals are wrongly uninsured through file comparison.42 Moreover, Article 7b.1, third paragraph, of the Health Insurance Regulation already contains a basis for the CAK to provide data about wrongly uninsured individuals against whom measures have been taken to the municipality. The explanatory memorandum does not clarify why, in addition to the existing possibilities for the CAK, healthcare providers must now also provide data to the municipality.  

Thirdly, the AP finds it insufficiently plausible that the wrongful lack of health insurance should be linked in this way to, for example, participation in a debt assistance program or (labor) participation. The processing of the fact that medically necessary care has been provided is thus linked to purposes that, in principle, do not have much to do with that fact.  

In light of all these objections, the AP advises against continuing this draft if these issues regarding proportionality cannot be resolved. The AP recommends that the draft be amended so that the municipality learns which individuals are wrongly uninsured not through the healthcare provider, but through the CAK. A basis must first be created that meets the requirements of proportionality, with the aim of enabling the municipality to offer (social) assistance.

9. Data Exchange RIVM Prevention Programs  

The RIVM is tasked with ensuring the management and coordination of the execution, registration, monitoring, and evaluation of population screenings designated by general administrative order.47 Additionally, the RIVM is responsible for the national steering and guidance of prevention programs established by general administrative order.48  

If the result of a population screening or screening is positive, the patient is referred to a healthcare provider or laboratory for follow-up diagnostics or treatment. According to the explanatory memorandum, it is necessary for the quality of a prevention program that the RIVM has access to the (follow-up) diagnostic data of individuals who have been referred after a positive result, or in whom an anomaly has been detected after a negative result. These feedbacks from healthcare providers and laboratories to the RIVM are currently based on the legal tasks of the RIVM. However, according to the explanatory memorandum, healthcare providers have become more reserved in this regard since the introduction of the GDPR.49  

This draft introduces legal obligations for healthcare providers to provide (follow-up) diagnostic data about their patients to the executors50 of prevention programs, and for executors to provide that data to the RIVM.51 Additionally, a legal basis is provided for executors to process the data and the BSN so that they can link the (follow-up) diagnostic data to the data available to them.52  

Scope of Obligation  
The AP objects to the scope of the legal obligation for healthcare providers to share certain health data with the executor of a population screening or screening. The situation where withholding consent by the individual concerned could lead to a distorted view of the effectiveness of a prevention program will only occur in a limited number of rare conditions. After all, few participants are referred and diagnosed for those conditions. It is advisable to limit the legal obligation to that limited scope.

11. Involuntary admission to Wzd care institution  

Article 2.3, second paragraph, of the Forensic Care Act (Wfz) gives the criminal court the authority to issue a judicial authorization for involuntary admission and residence in a Wzd55 care institution. The judge may issue that authorization if he believes that the criteria in Article 24 of the Wzd56 are met.  

The implementation of these authorizations is laid down in Articles 28a to 28c of the Wzd. Since the entry into force of the Wzd (2020), there have been placement issues, and executing parties often fail to find a suitable care place in a timely manner.57  

To find a place on time, it is important that the parties involved - Public Prosecution Service, Wlz executor58, the municipality where the client resides, the health insurer, the CIZ, the care provider, and the Judicial Institutions Service - can exchange the necessary data early in the procedure of Article 2.3, second paragraph, Wfz, which is needed to find a suitable place.59  

The proposed Article 28a, sixth paragraph, Wzd makes this possible. The data may only be exchanged to the extent that it is necessary for the preparation or execution of a judicial authorization granted under Article 2.3, second paragraph, of the Wfz.  

In the proposed Article 28a, sixth paragraph, Wzd, it is stated that health data60 and data of a criminal nature61 will also be provided in connection with the preparation or execution of a judicial authorization for involuntary admission and residence in a Wzd care institution.  

In the explanatory memorandum, a detailed assessment against Articles 9 and 10 of the GDPR is lacking in this regard.  

The AP advises that the explanatory memorandum should clarify which considerations have been made in the context of Articles 9 and 10 of the GDPR.  

13. Supervision by IGJ on (parts of) Wabvpz  

To date, the supervision of the Act on Additional Provisions for the Processing of Personal Data in Healthcare (Wabvpz) by IGJ has been shaped in relation to the concept of "good care" from Article 2 of the Act on Quality, Complaints, and Disputes in Healthcare (Wkkgz). This means that IGJ can only take enforcement action for violations of the Wabvpz if this also results in a violation of Article 2 Wkkgz.62 For example, when inadequate information security leads to a deterioration in the quality of care provided.  

In the new Chapter 3b of the Wabvpz, IGJ will receive the following enforcement instruments in the context of supervision of certain provisions of the Wabvpz:  

a. written instruction;  
b. written order; and  
c. penalty under coercive fine.  

This concerns Articles 5 through 12, 15d, 15e, and 15j of the Wabvpz63.  

The draft also stipulates that IGJ and the AP will provide each other with the information and data necessary for the performance of their duties, as far as reasonably necessary for the fulfillment of that duty. This does not include data from medical files.64

In the draft, IGJ is granted enforcement powers regarding Articles 5 through 12, 15d, 15e, and 15j of the Wabvpz65, even if – unlike now – there is no violation of Article 2 of the Wkkgz.66  

The background for this is:  

“To effectively supervise, supervisory and enforcement options are necessary […] The text of the Wabvpz does not explicitly prescribe which supervisory authority oversees the articles from the law that describe obligations for care providers […] Due to the increasing importance of digitization and electronic data exchange, the importance of good information security in healthcare is also increasing. It is desirable that IGJ has the explicit authority to supervise this, even when there is no direct relationship to the quality of care provided […] The policy objective is that the enforceability of the Wabvpz is adequately secured. This objective is achieved by explicitly regulating supervision and enforcement of the Wabvpz in the Wabvpz.”67  

The AP emphasizes that the Wabvpz does not need to explicitly prescribe which supervisory authority oversees the articles from the Wabvpz that describe obligations for care providers. The AP is responsible for both the supervision of the application of the GDPR and for the processing of personal data in national legislation such as the Wabvpz.68 The IGJ can, insofar as “good care” is at stake or threatened, also – whether or not together with the AP69 – supervise the Wabvpz. No explicit indication in the law is needed for this.  

The draft creates a duplication in the supervisory field of the AP and the IGJ. Both are authorized to supervise without a relationship to the quality of care provided. The potential risks of this duplication can be mitigated by adjusting the Cooperation Protocol AP-IGJ. Nevertheless, the question remains whether the IGJ already has sufficient instruments to effectively supervise the provisions of the Wabvpz.  

The AP advises that the explanatory memorandum should better substantiate why the current instruments of the AP and the IGJ are not sufficient to supervise the provisions of the Wabvpz. Should this lead to the conclusion that the enforceability of the Wabvpz is already sufficiently secured, the AP advises not to continue this part of the draft.
