Subject 
Advice on the draft bill DIAZ 
Dear Mr. Kuipers,

In a letter dated July 14, 2023, the Authority for Personal Data (AP) was consulted pursuant to the provisions of Article 36, fourth paragraph, of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) regarding the draft amendment to the Act on Additional Provisions for the Processing of Personal Data in Healthcare (Wabvpz) in relation to digital identification and authentication in healthcare (hereinafter: the draft).

The AP objects to the draft concerning the proposed Articles 14 and 15 and advises that the procedure should not continue unless the objection is removed (paragraph 1). Furthermore, the draft gives the AP reason to make several comments (paragraphs 2 to 5).

Main points of the advice

- The draft aims to encourage healthcare providers and healthcare workers to use login tools that meet a high level of reliability for access to electronic exchange systems and healthcare information systems. Although the AP welcomes this initiative, it believes that stronger government action is warranted.
- Firstly, because in practice there are still insufficient suitable login tools available at a high level of reliability. The AP is not convinced that these will emerge voluntarily from the field in the near future. Secondly, because the draft does not mandate the use of login tools at a high level of reliability, except for access to SBV-Z.1

Scope of the draft  

Currently, there are separate UZI registers for care providers, indication bodies, and health insurers in which they can be registered upon request. Once registered, they can apply for a UZI card or a UZI server certificate (together: UZI means) that allows access to SBV-Z. Additionally, with the UZI means, access can be obtained to the National Switch Point (LSP) and several services such as the National Implant Register and the communication node VECOZO. The possession of a UZI card alone is not sufficient to gain access to care information without further authorization. Moreover, simply offering the card is not enough; a PIN code must also be entered.  

Primarily, the explanatory memorandum mentions three problems regarding current practice. First, there is currently no basis for the use of the UZI registers and UZI means for services other than SBV-Z. Second, practice experiences various problems.

Advice  

1. Stronger government action required  
Pursuant to Article 32 GDPR, data controllers and processors are required to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk. Since health data is considered special personal data32, stricter requirements are imposed on the security of that data than on other personal data. In cases involving data subject to the medical confidentiality of the healthcare provider, the AP has regularly indicated in the context of patient authentication for access to patient portals that the ‘highest level of reliability’ is required.33 In the terminology of the eIDAS Regulation, a login means must meet the reliability level ‘high’.

The draft also emphasizes the use of a high level of reliability for the authentication of healthcare providers and healthcare staff.34 The Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) endorses the importance of this and strongly supports the government's initiative to guide the sector towards that goal.  

For the sake of completeness, the AP notes that the use of a login credential with a high level of reliability does not, in itself, mean that an organization has taken all the necessary appropriate technical and organizational measures. However, the use of such a login credential can be part of the measures that need to be implemented. The AP therefore emphasizes that, in addition to the requirements set by the eIDAS Regulation for a high level of reliability, there may also be other measures that data controllers and processors must take under Article 32 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).  

The AP states that it is primarily the responsibility of the data controller to take appropriate technical and organizational measures to meet the required level of security under Article 32, paragraph 1, of the GDPR. However, the explanatory memorandum indicates that the healthcare sector still uses "many different login methods that often do not meet the required high level of reliability."35 This provides sufficient reason to conclude that stronger government action is warranted. In the opinion of the AP, the current proposals fall short in this regard.  

This is fundamentally related to the fact that, as indicated by additional information provided by the ministry to the AP, there are still insufficient suitable login credentials available at a high level of reliability in practice, and it does not appear that such credentials will be available in the near future. The sector has been under the obligation for years to use login credentials that meet the highest level of reliability but has, as it appears, failed to do so adequately. This draft does not change that; healthcare providers are still allowed to choose whether or not to use an approved login credential, and the UZI credentials that do meet the high level of reliability will also be phased out. Thus, the healthcare sector will lose an important login credential that, although not ideal, works, is secure, and is currently the standard for access to the LSP, the National Implant Register, and VECOZO. Whether and when a suitable login credential will replace it remains a significant question mark. The AP considers this undesirable.  

Furthermore, in the AP's opinion, the draft is too noncommittal. The approval of login credentials is not made mandatory, except for access to SBV-Z. However, inquiries with the ministry reveal that access to SBV-Z is often obtained via server certificates, not by logging in with, for example, a card or wallet. There is a risk that the draft will become an empty shell. Additionally, the costs that healthcare providers must incur to certify, adjust, or acquire new login credentials make it unattractive for them to take that step. This is especially true for medium-sized and small healthcare providers.  

Given the seriousness of the current practice and the importance of reliable authentication for access to health data, the AP deems it necessary for the minister to take further measures to

2. Counterproductive measures

2.1 Circumvention of higher reliability level  
Logging in with stronger authentication is estimated by the ministry to take a few seconds longer. To compensate for this, the ministry proposes that authentication only needs to occur once a day, and can also be requested outside of the healthcare process. Additionally, it is suggested to reduce the frequency of logging into various healthcare applications.36

The AP warns of the security risks that such measures may entail. If logging in with a high level of reliability only needs to occur once a day, it remains possible to give that tool to someone else during the day. That other person would then apparently not need to provide a username, password, or anything similar.

The AP advises removing passages from the MvT that may suggest that secure login could be circumvented for the sake of user convenience.

2.2 Use of private phone  
Registered individuals can choose, in addition to a card, for a digital wallet on a smartphone or tablet. If a work phone is used for this purpose, various security measures may already be in place. However, inquiries with the ministry reveal that a private phone can also be used. This need not be a problem, provided that the correct guidelines are followed to mitigate the risks that may arise from its use. However, monitoring compliance with those guidelines may prove more difficult than with a work phone, as an employer cannot simply monitor which other apps are being used.

3. Registration of Care Workers  

In addition to care providers, indication bodies, and health insurers, based on the new Article 14, first paragraph, introductory clause, of the draft, care workers can now also register in the UZI register. This group of natural persons is broader than the group that falls under the definition of care providers in Article 1 of the Act on Quality, Complaints, and Disputes in Healthcare (Wkkgz): “an institution or a solo-working care provider.” The new definition of care workers includes not only care providers but also administrative staff, students, interns, or supervisors. In short, everyone who works or wants to work in the healthcare sector. Inquiry with the ministry reveals that there is no verification of this: “For natural persons (care workers, not being care providers), there are no grounds for refusal. A revocation of a registration in the register is [however] possible.” The consequence of this is that there are no limits on who can register in the UZI register. Even though registration without authorization from an affiliated institution is in principle useless, it may open a door for abuse. This can be prevented by requiring that someone must demonstrate that they are working in the healthcare sector or will be doing so in the foreseeable future. For example, by requiring that someone provides an email address with a domain name of an institution affiliated with the register.  

The AP advises that the register should be structured so that only natural persons who can demonstrably show they are working in the healthcare sector, or will be doing so in the foreseeable future, can be registered.

One example is the National Implant Register. 4.2 HR Systems  
According to the MvT, "it is made possible to establish a connection with an HR system for automated registration in the register."40 The background of this, as revealed by inquiries with the ministry, is that large healthcare institutions with many healthcare employees will spend a lot of time manually reporting initial registrations and changes. With a connection between the care provider's HR system and the UZI register, entries and exits will be immediately reported, making unauthorized access more difficult.  

The AP advises that this explanation should also be included in the MvT. Additionally, the AP recommends addressing the security risks that may be associated with such a connection, and the requirements that such HR systems must meet.  

5. Other  

5.1 Excessive Emphasis on Data Exchange  
In Article 14, first paragraph, under b of the draft, 'electronic data exchange' is mentioned. Also in the MvT, the emphasis is often on the exchange of data,41 while it equally concerns, perhaps even more often, access to one's own care information systems.  

The AP advises to amend Article 14, first paragraph, under b, of the draft so that, in accordance with Article 14a, second paragraph, of the draft, it not only refers to 'electronic data exchange in healthcare' but also to 'access to care information systems'.  

5.2 Expansion of IGJ Powers  
In the draft, the new Articles 14 and 15 Wabvpz are categorized under the scope of the supervisory and enforcement powers assigned to the IGJ in the legislative proposal for the Data Processing Collection Act VWS II. The AP has advised in its opinion on that earlier legislative proposal to better substantiate why the current instruments of the AP and the IGJ are not sufficient to ensure the enforceability of the Wabvpz.42  

The AP advises to take into account the implementation of the earlier advice from the AP in this draft.
