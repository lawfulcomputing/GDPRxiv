Disproportionate is unlawful  
In your first four questions, you essentially request further clarification regarding the AP's assessment that the legislative proposal is unlawful and explicitly ask about the article(s) on which the AP's position is based. In this regard, the AP notes the following.  

Legal context  
In the advice dated March 12, 2020, it is explained why the AP believes that the legislative proposal does not comply with the principle of proportionality, including the requirements of subsidiarity and proportionality, as derived from Article 52, paragraph 1, of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (EU Charter). Among other things, there must be a reasonable relationship between the objective of the measure, in this case, preventing and combating money laundering and terrorist financing, and the limitation of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, as enshrined in Article 8 of the EU Charter. Article 6, paragraph 3, of the GDPR indicates that the requirements of the principle of proportionality from the Charter also apply in 'national law' if that law provides a basis for the processing of personal data within the meaning of Article 6, paragraph 1, introductory phrase and point c, GDPR (legal obligation), as is the case in this legislative proposal.  
The principle of subsidiarity implies that, from the perspective of the protection of personal data, there are no less far-reaching alternatives available to achieve the objective. If this is met, the proportionality of a measure must still be assessed. For this, the effectiveness and severity of the measure are important, particularly in light of the accumulation with other existing measures and the existence of appropriate conditions and safeguards. In the event that a legislative measure does not comply with the principle of proportionality, it is, of course, in conflict with the Charter and the GDPR and thus with higher law. The establishment of legislation that proves to be non-binding due to conflict with higher law is (also) unlawful.

In the context of the effectiveness of the measures, it is also important to note that the Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) is not clear on how effective compliance with the current Anti-Money Laundering and Anti-Terrorist Financing Act (Wwft) rules actually is. Thorough research into and about the extent of money laundering issues and terrorist financing within the context of electronic payment transactions is insufficiently available, while this is required for a robust justification of these measures. The processing of almost all transactions of citizens does not align with the risk-based nature of the European anti-money laundering directives. Potentially every deviation from a standard pattern is, after all, investigated further solely based on the deviation. The utility and necessity of new measures are questionable due to a lack of knowledge about the effectiveness of the current system.  

The above leads to the conclusion that there is no reasonable relationship between the objective of the measure and the limitation of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data - even after the implemented changes. This means that if the legislative proposal is actually enacted, certain aspects will not comply with the principle of proportionality, thus conflicting with the EU Charter and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), and the relevant provisions of the law will have to remain inapplicable.  

In this regard, I would like to point out that the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) has recently expressed critical views on the proposal for a European Regulation aimed at combating money laundering and terrorist financing; the so-called AML/CFT legislative package. The EDPB advises against including the data-sharing powers set forth therein in the relevant regulation due to the far-reaching infringements on the protection of personal data and the serious concerns regarding the legality, necessity (insufficient insight into effectiveness), and proportionality of the proposed measures. The EDPB explicitly refers to Articles 7, 8, and 52, paragraph 1, of the EU Charter.

Irreparable  
In questions 6 to 8, you request clarification on the qualification 'irreparable.' The AP notes the following on this matter. The term 'irreparable' was used during the oral explanation of the AP at the round table meeting of the House of Representatives when the so-called €100 limit was discussed. The AP wanted to make it clear that setting a limit of €100—a safeguard to mitigate the infringement of the protection of personal data—does not eliminate the AP's objection that the legislative proposal does not meet the requirements of the principle of proportionality. Even with this €100 limit, the AP believes that there is still a scale of processing that is so extensive that it remains disproportionate, even though supportive, mitigating measures have been taken. Against that background—and within the context of the oral proceedings—the present legislative proposal has been labeled as 'irreparable' in this respect. This does not mean, however, that the AP believes that the objection regarding proportionality (point 3 of the assessment framework in the annex) could not be addressed by any limit. However, it is not for the AP to set that limit. Moreover, even a substantially higher limit would not resolve the other stated objections.

Relationship with the AP's supervisory task  
Your last questions (9 and 10) concern the enforcement possibilities of the AP. During the roundtable discussion on January 26 in the House of Representatives, it was not so much a matter of the AP 'suspending' legislation. The AP does not have that authority. However, it is (not disputed) understood that national legislation that conflicts with Union law must be set aside.  

The AP has the tasks and powers listed in Articles 57 and 58 of the GDPR, including investigating and enforcing the application of the GDPR, whether or not in response to a complaint. In that context, the AP can, of course, also take enforcement action, for example, by imposing a processing ban.23  

The obligation to set aside national legislation that conflicts with Union law rests not only on the national court but on all government bodies, including administrative bodies.24 This may also include the obligation to take all measures to facilitate the full effect of Union law.25 Specifically regarding supervision, the Court considered in the so-called Cif case that insofar as a supervisor is tasked with ensuring compliance with Union law, the useful effect of Union law would be limited if the supervisor could not establish that a national rule is in conflict with that Union law and could not set aside this rule in the context of an investigation. It is 'of little relevance' that this cannot be blamed on the involved companies (which comply with the conflicting national law); the obligation of the Member State remains unchanged, so the national supervisor remains obliged to set aside the relevant national rule. The GDPR stipulates that processing requires a lawful basis. This basis can be found in national legislation, but the relevant legislation must 'serve a legitimate objective of general interest and be proportionate to the pursued legitimate aim.'27 As previously outlined, it follows that the assessment framework from the Charter, as it applies to Union law that infringes fundamental rights, must also be applied to national legislation on subjects or policy areas that do not fall under Union law. To the extent that such legislation provides a basis for the processing of personal data, it concerns a subject that is indeed fully governed by Union law (and thus by the Charter). If the requirements of the principle of proportionality are not met in accordance with the Charter, the relevant provisions are therefore non-binding due to conflict with Union law and cannot, of course, serve as a lawful basis for the processing of personal data. The AP can take action against processing without a lawful basis. The question of the binding nature of the relevant national legislation in such a case, and thus whether the AP has rightly acted against a processing that the national legislator intended, is (of course) ultimately for the court, in the highest instance the Court of Justice of the EU.  

For completeness, I would like to note that if legislation is enacted that is in conflict with or appears to be in conflict with the principle of proportionality, any affected party – in this legislative proposal a substantial part of the population – can, in principle, challenge the legality of its application in a civil procedure, whether or not referring to unaddressed points from an opinion of the AP or the outcome of a complaint submitted to the AP.  

Of course, it must be emphasized that the legislator must do everything possible to prevent the enactment of legislation that conflicts with higher law. This quickly leads to significant damage in terms of legal certainty and breached trust, but also, depending on the circumstances, to concrete disadvantages, for example, financially. In line with this, the GDPR imposes an obligation on the legislator to consult the supervisory authority during the drafting phase if the legislation relates to the processing of personal data.28 This is intended to ensure that the processing intended by the legislator is in line with the GDPR and that the risks for the affected parties are minimized.29 The opinion of the AP thus aims to protect the legislator from this danger.

ANNEX  

Assessment Framework  

The AP assesses whether the draft concerning the processing of personal data is in accordance with the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (EU), the general legal principles of the EU, the GDPR, and other relevant law, whereby it must be sufficiently demonstrated that the principle of proportionality is met, by:  
•  
the draft being suitable to achieve the pursued objective of general interest or the protection of the rights and freedoms of others (1. suitability);  
•  
the objective cannot reasonably be achieved as effectively by other means that are less intrusive to the fundamental rights of the affected parties (2. subsidiarity);  
•  
the interference is not disproportionate to that objective, which particularly implies a weighing of the importance of the objective and the severity of the interference (3. proportionality);  
•  
the draft is sufficiently clear and precise regarding the scope and is predictable in application (4. legal certainty);  
•  
the draft sufficiently indicates under which circumstances and conditions personal data may be processed, thereby ensuring that the interference is limited to what is strictly necessary (5. substantive and procedural safeguards); and  
•  
the draft is binding under national law (6. binding nature under national law).
