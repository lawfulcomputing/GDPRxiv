**1. Effective, proportionate, and deterrent enforcement and penalties**  

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) contains the general rules for the protection of personal data. The Directive on Data Protection in Criminal Justice (DPD) contains the rules applicable to the processing of personal data by 'competent authorities for the purpose of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses or the enforcement of penalties, including the protection against and prevention of threats to public safety.' The DPD therefore applies not only to processing carried out by, for example, police and judiciary but also to all other bodies/entities 'authorized under national law to exercise public authority and public powers for the purpose of preventing, investigating, detecting, and prosecuting criminal offenses or the enforcement of penalties, including the protection against and prevention of threats to public safety.'  

The Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) has been designated as the supervisory authority for the application of both the GDPR and the DPD. Regarding the enforcement of EU law, it generally applies that enforcement authorities must (be able to) enforce and penalize effectively, proportionately, and deterrently. This is well regulated in the (directly applicable) GDPR. Furthermore, the DPD includes a clear provision in Article 57: 'Member States shall establish the rules regarding the penalties applicable to infringements of this directive and take all necessary measures to ensure that they are implemented. The penalties established must be effective, proportionate, and deterrent.'  

However, directives do not have direct effect and must be transposed into national legislation. In the Netherlands, the existing Police Data Act (Wpg) and the Judicial and Criminal Procedure Data Act (Wjsg) were 'utilized' for the transposition of the DPD. The intention at that time was that this 'utilization' would be temporary and that a completely new (and better) transposition law would be created. However, that project has been halted, as the AP has understood. The AP has previously pointed out that the use of both existing laws for the transposition of an entirely new directive has led to necessary shortcomings. Although part of those shortcomings is, in a sense, materially remedied because provisions in directives can directly apply if a directive has not been properly transposed. This particularly applies when provisions in a directive are 'sufficiently clear and precise' and 'unconditional' and confer rights on citizens. Whether this is the case must, however, be assessed on a provision-by-provision basis, but this often leads to necessary legal uncertainty. For example, in this case concerning organizations that are not part of the police or judiciary but are tasked with – in short – the enforcement of provisions that can be penalized with administrative fines. In carrying out those tasks, the provisions in the DPD must be complied with. Facts that are penalized with an administrative fine qualify (then) as a 'criminal offense' – which is (here) an autonomous EU legal concept – while the Wpg and

2. Compensation  

Fundamental rights protect the rights and freedoms of citizens. Enforcement should primarily aim to end unlawful interferences. Furthermore, it is important that legal relationships are restored, that unlawfully obtained benefits are removed – after all, violating the fundamental rights of others should never be rewarding – and that there is (additionally) punishment where it is appropriate and warranted from a preventive and/or repressive perspective.  

Enforcement actions by the AP should, ideally, lead to the cessation of violations of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and often also to the cessation of violations of other fundamental rights.

3. Combating unfair competition: private and public  

The AP (Dutch Data Protection Authority) experiences that a high level of protection of personal data is increasingly becoming a unique selling point for companies and welcomes this development. Unfortunately, the AP also observes in practice that companies experience unfair competition because competitors do not comply with the GDPR and can thus gain an advantage. According to the letter of the GDPR, these adversely affected companies cannot complain to the AP due to the circumstance that they do not meet the complainant definition from the GDPR.2 Of course, the AP can conduct an official investigation upon receiving such a 'complaint' and based on that, can start enforcement and/or the AP may declare enforcement requests admissible in relevant cases, and judges may be inclined (if necessary) to create some leeway by (somewhat) 'softening'/correcting the relativity requirement through what has come to be known in the literature as the Widdershoven correction, but the outcome of such procedures is still uncertain (which is not good for legal certainty and does not promote compliance with the GDPR).  

However, companies that suffer disadvantages in this way should also be able to initiate enforcement actions through the courts. The AP finds it important that entrepreneurs have a proper legal avenue in such situations. However, a judge recently decided3 that – in short – the standards of the GDPR do not extend to protection against what is perceived as unfair competition. There is a difference of opinion regarding the correctness of this ruling.4 Therefore, the AP requests the legislator to explicitly address this issue. If the legislator believes that this route to the civil court should also be open in such cases, then the relevant legislation can either be tightened/clarified now or this intention can be announced now should it turn out that the court's approach stands up to the highest instance.

4. Obligation to Disclose for the AP  

In its advice on the GDPR, the AP advocated for an obligation to disclose sanctions in the GDPR. Other supervisory authorities such as the ACM (§ 4 Establishment Act of the Authority for Consumers and Markets), AFM, and DNB (for example, Article 1:97 of the Financial Supervision Act et seq.) have specific disclosure obligations for sanctions. In the explanatory memorandum on the GDPR, the minister indicated that, given the policy-neutral nature of the GDPR at that time, it was not obvious to include this in the GDPR, and it was also reasonable to reconsider this topic once there was clarity about the progress of the legislative proposal for the Open Government Act (hereinafter: Woo). That clarity is now present. The Woo came into effect on May 1, 2022. The consideration of the desirability of amending the GDPR in this regard is therefore also timely. This is why the AP has reiterated its request to include an obligation to disclose sanctions for the AP in the law.  

5. Commissioning, Instigation, and Complicity  

Violations of the GDPR often involve unlawful interference with the fundamental rights of citizens. Not only with the fundamental right to the protection of personal data but often also with the fundamental right to respect for private life. Furthermore, there are also regular violations of other fundamental rights protected by the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU (CFR), such as the non-discrimination provisions. In this sense, data protection law does not fundamentally differ (much) from (the core of) so-called classical criminal law. Violent crimes, crimes against life, and sexual offenses are indeed infringements of the fundamental rights to bodily integrity and/or private life of the victims, while property crimes generally infringe upon the free enjoyment of property, discrimination violates the non-discrimination provisions, etc.  

In cases of unlawful interference with fundamental rights that are criminalized in 'ordinary' criminal law, enforcement action can generally also be taken against all so-called participants in those criminal acts: perpetrators, instigators, accomplices, and (if the act is committed by a legal entity) clients and de facto managers, where applicable and necessary. This means that if there is (with exceptions) at least (conditional) intent among those participants. Complicity is only not punishable if the (main) perpetrators do not have intent.  

Unlawful interferences with the fundamental right to the protection of personal data (whether or not accompanied by unlawful interferences with other fundamental rights such as respect for private life or non-discrimination provisions) are now always punishable under the GDPR, and sometimes these violations are also committed intentionally. In several cases, this is also punishable under 'ordinary' criminal law.

Enforcement and punishment can be directed against both private law and public law legal entities, as well as against the clients or the actual leaders within those legal entities. 6. Legal protection and enforcement against companies not established in the EU  

Article 3 of the GDPR states that the GDPR also applies "to the processing of personal data of data subjects who are in the Union, by a controller or processor not established in the Union, when the processing is related to: a) the offering of goods or services to these data subjects in the Union, regardless of whether payment is required from the data subjects; or b) the monitoring of their behavior, insofar as this behavior takes place in the Union." The exceptions to this are not relevant for the point that the AP wants to make here.  

That the GDPR also applies in such cases is logical, as that "offering" or "monitoring" indeed takes place in the Union. However, in the case where the controller or processor has no establishment in the Union, it is particularly important that they are also accountable within the Union; for, among others, data subjects and for the AP. This is also provided for in the GDPR. The GDPR states that the controller or processor must designate a representative in the EU for such cases. Exceptions to this can be omitted here – for the point to be made. The GDPR further considers this – insofar as relevant here: "The representative shall act on behalf of the controller or the processor and may be approached by any supervisory authority. The representative must be expressly designated by a written mandate from the controller or the processor to act on their behalf regarding their obligations under this regulation." Enforcement thus seems to be guaranteed as the GDPR also considers: "In the event of non-compliance by the controller or the processor, the designated representative shall be subject to enforcement procedures." This can and will increase legal certainty for citizens who wish to accept an offer from a provider not established in the EU.  

Many companies, by the way, choose to open an establishment in the EU as soon as they become active within the EU or target citizens within the EU (from a non-EU country). However, various companies also choose not to establish themselves in the EU even then, but still comply with the obligation to designate a representative.

The Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) can, of course, take enforcement action regarding both violations of the GDPR in general and regarding non-compliance with the designation obligation. The country from which the business operates can often be determined with some certainty. It is not excluded that there is often intent involved in remaining anonymous. 7. Transparency regarding the use of algorithms  

Citizens have the right under the GDPR ‘not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning them or similarly significantly affects them’. This prohibition does not apply if these forms of automated processing are explicitly ‘permitted by a provision of Union law or national law applicable to the data controller and which also provides for appropriate measures to safeguard the rights and freedoms and legitimate interests of the data subject’.  

The GDPR includes a number of rights for data subjects concerning transparency and information. And it must always be clear – even if there is a so-called ‘human-in-the-loop’ – which processes and what logic and/or criteria in algorithms and/or AI have led to, for example, (additional) checks or (thereafter) to a rejection or other negative decision. A ‘human-in-the-loop’ must also be able to answer these questions and reasonably guarantee that, for example, an algorithm and/or AI (whether or not self-learning) has not unlawfully discriminated. Being unable to answer such questions makes scrutiny and effective contestation – whether or not in court – of such processes and decisions practically illusory and will mean that a legality assessment will not easily withstand scrutiny for that reason alone.  

The AP will reflect more generally at a later stage on how data controllers should and are fulfilling these obligations, especially in relation to processes that use algorithms.  

But at this moment, it is urgent that government institutions actively inform citizens about processes in which algorithms are used to make choices when selecting citizens for, for example, (additional) checks. And if an (additional)

Based on the GHV (General Data Protection Regulation) and the AVG (General Data Protection Regulation), citizens – in short – have the possibility to request operators of search engines to remove search results, the so-called

right to be forgotten. The operator must always weigh, on the one hand, the fundamental rights to respect for private life and to the protection of personal data, and on the other hand, the fundamental right to freedom of expression and information. If such a request is not honored, citizens can file a complaint with the AP or take legal action. For a long time, it was unclear whether an operator—if a request or complaint had to be honored—needed to remove only (summarily) the search results within the EU or also globally all search results. The Court of Justice of the EU (CJEU) has since clarified this: if such a removal request must be honored, it only needs to be honored concerning search queries originating from EU countries, such as on all European extensions (.nl, etc.). The reason for this is—briefly put—that not all countries outside the EU recognize this right to be forgotten and that the outcome of a balancing of different (fundamental) rights can vary by country, and the GDPR does not provide a framework for such a balancing. Regarding countries outside the EU, that balancing can therefore differ from country to country. As a result, the effect of honored requests and enforcement is only noticeable within the EU.

Thus, the practical effect of the GDPR on this point deviates from Article 2, which states that companies established outside the EU are also bound by the GDPR in cases where they offer services to citizens or monitor the behavior of citizens within the EU. This gives the GDPR a certain extraterritorial effect for those activities.

The consequence of this is that EU citizens who wish to have their personal data removed concerning the visibility of search results outside the EU have less legal protection than within the EU. While that information often originates from sites that make that information public within the EU. And that information—which has been lawfully made public within the EU by being posted on the internet and is only visible outside the EU for that reason—has not been transferred outside the EU to a third country based on, for example, an adequacy decision or another form of 'transfer' that has been accompanied by various due diligence requirements. This leads to a gap in the legal protection of EU citizens. However, the CJEU also explicitly considered in this ruling, which provided clarity on the territorial effect of the GDPR, the following: "Finally, it should be emphasized that Union law, in its current state, does not provide for the obligation to remove links for all versions of the search engine in question when a request for removal is granted, but it does not prohibit it either. Consequently, a supervisory authority or a judicial body of a Member State is still competent to weigh, in light of national standards for the protection of fundamental rights, the right of the person concerned to respect for his private life and to the protection of his personal data on the one hand, and the freedom of information on the other hand, and to do so after this."

9. Data of deceased persons  

The GDPR states: ‘This regulation does not apply to the personal data of deceased persons. Member States may establish rules regarding the processing of personal data of deceased persons.’ This is therefore a power and not an obligation.  

The Dutch legislator has so far chosen not to make use of this possibility to allow the GDPR to remain applicable to the personal data of an individual for several years after their death.  

The consequence of this is that at the moment of death, the protective effect of the GDPR immediately ceases concerning all personal data of the deceased. If third parties unlawfully use the personal data of a deceased person, recourse will have to be made to, for example, tort law. This occasionally leads to discussions about the applicable protective law. For instance, when taking photos or videos at traffic accidents. Regarding photos and videos of living victims, one can invoke the protective effect of the GDPR if those photos and/or videos are used for purposes other than solely for domestic use or, for example, journalistic purposes. However, surviving relatives can no longer invoke this once the individual has passed away. This occasionally leads to distressing situations, which you will be familiar with. Normative discussions regarding, among other things, the applicable protective law can easily be prevented and resolved by including in the GDPR that it remains applicable to the processing of personal data of deceased persons, for example, for up to five years after death.

10. Limitation of the rights of data subjects  
Article 23 of the GDPR provides national legislators with the possibility to limit the rights of data subjects under certain circumstances – briefly summarized. In the UAVG, this possibility has been utilized in Article 41, but the manner in which this has been done does not align with the requirements set forth by Article 23 of the GDPR. As it is currently stipulated, it is up to the relevant administrative body to make the assessments in a specific case that the legislator should have made in general beforehand. However, if limitations are imposed on the rights of data subjects, that legislation must always comply with the requirements set by the GHV. This means that such legislation must also meet, among other things, the strict standards of the principle of proportionality.  
The AP has already warned several times about this incorrect, overly general use of the possibilities of Article 23 of the GDPR. The CJEU has also commented on the (im)possibilities of Article 23 of the GDPR and on how it can be implemented. This confirms, in the opinion of the AP, the aforementioned position of the AP. The CJEU has considered – in Quadratur 9 – that Article 23 allows member states to limit the scope of the obligations and rights mentioned therein through legislative measures, ‘provided that such limitation does not affect the essence of the fundamental rights and freedoms and is a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society to safeguard’ the pursued objective and that ‘any legislative measure established on that basis must in particular comply with the specific requirements set out in Article 23, paragraph 2, of the GDPR.’ The CJEU further stated ‘that the power granted to member states by Article 23, paragraph 1, of the GDPR can only be exercised in accordance with the requirement of proportionality, which requires that exceptions to the protection of personal data and limitations thereof remain within the limits of what is strictly necessary.’ This means, among other things, that such legislation must be sufficiently precise and accurate. Article 41 of the UAVG does not meet those requirements.  
In the explanatory memorandum, the minister now considers – in short – that, after weighing everything, it is (still) undesirable to repeal Article 41 UAVG because the consequences of repeal are currently insufficiently foreseeable. That may be the case, but it does not mean that data controllers can invoke that article if they wish to limit the rights of a data subject. This creates significant legal uncertainty for both data controllers and citizens, as the AP will not honor a data controller's invocation of Article 41 UAVG in the event of complaints, expecting that judges will follow suit. In other words: repealing the practically unusable Article 41 UAVG actually greatly promotes legal certainty regarding this issue.

11. Some remarks on the Explanatory Memorandum regarding biometrics and consent  
After the AP advised on the earlier version of the amendment to the UAVG, a passage was added in the explanatory memorandum regarding Article 29 of the UAVG with examples of companies and areas that meet the requirement of a compelling general interest for processing biometric data. The AP's concern regarding this addition is that by naming these examples, the impression is created that biometric data can be processed generically and unconditionally at these companies and locations. The AP emphasizes that even at these companies and in these locations, a concrete assessment based on the dual necessity test must be made.  
Furthermore, after the AP had issued its advice, an additional passage was added in the explanatory memorandum about the possibility that an employee freely gives consent to their employer for processing biometric data if there are alternative access options and the employee does not feel pressured. The explanatory memorandum thus further elaborates on the autonomous European legal concept of ‘explicit consent’ that must be freely given. This concept is interpreted in the same way across Europe. There is already a clear normative framework for this concept, which is supported at the European level. Therefore, in the opinion of the AP, this passage in the explanatory memorandum is redundant (and without legal relevance) and raises concerns that it may cause unnecessary confusion about the circumstances under which (explicit) consent is freely given in an employer-employee relationship.
