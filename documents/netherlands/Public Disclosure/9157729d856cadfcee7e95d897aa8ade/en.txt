Introductory considerations  

Reason for investigation  
3. [CONFIDENTIAL] is an (international) company specialized in [CONFIDENTIAL].1 Following a report received by the AP on July 5, 2018, regarding the collection of fingerprint scans by [CONFIDENTIAL] from its staff, the AP initiated an investigation. In this context, company visits were made to [CONFIDENTIAL] on November 6, 2018, and March 18, 2019.2 This investigation resulted in a research report and the imposition of an administrative fine. [CONFIDENTIAL] has lodged an objection against the fine decision, as noted earlier. The objection primarily concerns the imposition of the administrative fine on the grounds that there was no violation of the GDPR and, alternatively, against the amount of the fine.  

Prohibition on processing biometric data  
4. Following the investigation by the AP, it has been established that [CONFIDENTIAL] processed biometric data of its employees by recording and storing so-called fingerprint scans. [CONFIDENTIAL] has explicitly acknowledged this in its objection.3 For a further elaboration of the method of processing, the AP suffices in the context of the objection procedure with a reference to paragraph 3.6 (‘Recording of fingerprints’) and paragraph 3.7 (‘Storage of fingerprint templates’) of the final research report dated September 4, 2019 (hereinafter: the research report), as well as to the report of the technical investigation that is part of the research report as Appendix 4. [CONFIDENTIAL] is to be regarded as the data controller as referred to in Article 4, part 7, of the GDPR, which [CONFIDENTIAL] does not dispute.4  

5. The fingerprint scans processed by [CONFIDENTIAL] are biometric (personal) data as referred to in Article 4, part 14, of the GDPR. Such data is considered a special category of personal data under the GDPR.5 They are sensitive because they provide unique information about an individual's physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics. Unlike, for example, personal passwords, which can be changed periodically to prevent misuse if it is found that unauthorized persons have gained access, biometric data, such as fingerprints6, are immutable identifiers. Due to the sensitivity of this type of data, a prohibition on processing applies unless an exception is applicable.7

Grounds for objection  
7. In its initial objection letter as well as in its two pleadings, one of which was sent before the objection hearing and the other submitted during the hearing, [CONFIDENTIAL] raised various objections. These are summarized below and responded to by the AP.  

Objection 1: No violation, employees gave consent  

8. In its objection letter, [CONFIDENTIAL] argues that - with one exception - all employees, after being informed, gave explicit (and voluntary) consent for the taking of fingerprint scans. The fingerprint scanning system was used alongside the so-called drop system. There was always an alternative to the fingerprint scan. According to [CONFIDENTIAL], this constitutes a lawful exception to the processing prohibition and therefore does not constitute a violation of Article 9 GDPR. The AP does not agree with [CONFIDENTIAL] on this point and explains this below.  

(Explicit) Consent according to the GDPR  
9. The general prohibition on the processing of special personal data does not apply if the data subject gives explicit consent for the processing.9 The GDPR sets out a number of requirements for (explicit) consent. According to Article 4, second paragraph, under 11, GDPR, consent is only present if there is 1) a free, 2) specific, 3) informed, and 4) unambiguous expression of will 5) by which the data subject accepts the processing of personal data concerning him or her through a statement or a clear affirmative action. Furthermore, Article 7 GDPR imposes additional conditions on consent. For instance, the data controller must be able to demonstrate that the data subject has given consent.10  

10. In addition to the aforementioned requirements, the consent that may apply as an exception to the processing prohibition of special personal data under Article 9 GDPR must also be given explicitly. The EDPB guidelines on consent11 provide further clarification on the term ‘explicit consent’ in recitals 91-102. According to recital 93, ‘explicit consent’ refers to the manner in which consent is expressed by the data subject. This may include written consent, signing (possibly with an electronic signature), the data subject sending an email, or, for example, consent with two-factor authentication. Although

In that case, it cannot be demonstrated that consent was given as required by Article 7, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, and the accountability obligation under Article 5, paragraph 2, of the GDPR is also not fulfilled.  

Consent not established or demonstrated  
11. In the present case, it has not been established, and [CONFIDENTIAL] has also not demonstrated, that all its employees were asked for consent. And insofar as consent may have been given, it has not been demonstrated that the consent was free, specific, and/or informed.  

12. In this regard, the AP first notes that the director of [CONFIDENTIAL] stated during the hearing regarding the objection that no written consent was requested. This corresponds with the findings of the AP during the investigation phase. The AP did not find any documentation of policies or procedures evidencing the granting or refusal of consent as a result of its investigation. The AP notes that "indicating via the employee handbook that clocking in will take place in the future using fingerprints" cannot be considered as asking—let alone obtaining—(explicit) consent based on prior information provided about this intended data processing. The objection raised by [CONFIDENTIAL] in this regard, which amounts to the assertion that valid consent can be obtained this way, is therefore deemed unfounded by the AP. Even if this could be considered consent, which the AP expressly disputes, it has not been demonstrated that there was free, specific, and informed consent as required by the GDPR.  

13. The absence of consent (in the sense of the GDPR) is confirmed by the findings of the AP's supervisors. In the reports they prepared from conversations with employees of [CONFIDENTIAL], it appears that some employees stated that either no consent was requested, or it was not given, or they could not remember whether consent was given. It can be concluded from this that there was no clear process in place whereby consent was requested if consent was indeed requested, and there was no evidence of prior information provided to employees about the specific processing of personal data, and it has not been demonstrated that consent was given. Therefore, in those cases, consent as such has not been established.  

14. Although two employees stated that they gave oral consent and [CONFIDENTIAL] also refers to this in its objection, this consent cannot be regarded as valid consent. Indeed, it has not been demonstrated that the specific conditions of the GDPR for consent, namely free and informed consent, were met.

An employee could no longer remember what information was provided regarding the use of the fingerprint scan, while another stated that they had not received any information.16 Thus, it has not been demonstrated that the employees were informed prior to giving consent about the purpose of the processing, the retention period, and the security of the data, and therefore there is no informed consent.  

15. Furthermore, it has not been demonstrated for any employee of [CONFIDENTIAL] that there was ‘free’ consent. In this context, it is important to note that this would involve consent given in the context of an employment relationship. This creates a dependency relationship in which an equal position between employer and employee is lacking.17 In such a case, it is assumed that free consent is not present, or at least it is unlikely that an individual can freely give consent.18 Not giving consent could indeed have negative consequences for the employment relationship and/or further career opportunities. The AP refers for further substantiation of this position to the extensively reasoned considerations in paragraph 3.3.3.1 (pp. 12-15) of the contested decision. This means that if (oral) consent was given by some employees of [CONFIDENTIAL], this consent, given the dependency relationship between the employees and their employer [CONFIDENTIAL], is deemed not to have been freely given. [CONFIDENTIAL] has not made the contrary plausible. Therefore, it may be assumed that free consent is not present.  

Conclusion on consent  
16. In light of the above, the AP concludes that the contested decision rightly established that [CONFIDENTIAL] processed biometric data of its employees without being able to invoke an exception. There is no evidence of (explicit) consent from the individuals for the processing of their fingerprints. It has not been demonstrated that the employees of [CONFIDENTIAL] were asked for and granted consent, and insofar as there were employees who gave (oral) consent, it has not been demonstrated and there is also no informed and free consent due to the employee-employer relationship. This means that [CONFIDENTIAL], because it cannot invoke an exception, has acted in violation of the processing prohibition as referred to in Article 9, paragraph 1, of the GDPR. In light of Article 58, paragraph 2, introductory phrase and under (i), in conjunction with Article 83, paragraph 5, introductory phrase and under (a), GDPR and Article 14, paragraph 3, UAVG, the AP is authorized to impose a fine for this violation.

Consent cannot be inferred from the behavior of employees  
18. During the hearing on the objection, [CONFIDENTIAL] stated that employees were informed in advance, during the New Year's speech of 2017 and via the staff magazine, about the use of fingerprint scans, and that consent can be inferred from the behavior of the employees because they allowed their fingerprints to be scanned and (voluntarily) used time registration through the fingerprint.  

19. The AP does not follow [CONFIDENTIAL] in its objection and notes that the fact that employees allowed their fingerprints to be scanned for the purpose of time registration is insufficient to conclude that consent can be inferred from this. Thus, there is no unequivocal expression of will by which the data subject accepts the relevant processing through a statement or an unequivocal active action, as required by the GDPR. Inferring consent from certain behaviors (of employees) does not, in the opinion of the AP, align with the specific requirements for consent set by the GDPR legislator.  
The same applies to the argument of [CONFIDENTIAL] that employees were (unilaterally) informed during the New Year's speech and via the staff magazine that a fingerprint scan would be used. Even in that case, there is no unequivocal expression of will. On the contrary, it implies that there was a unilateral decision by the employer and thus rather an obligation.  

Alternative to fingerprint scan and the statement of the system administrator/security officer [CONFIDENTIAL]  
20. [CONFIDENTIAL] argues that an alternative, the so-called 'drop', could also be used. In this context, [CONFIDENTIAL] also referred to a written statement from its system administrator/security officer. It is stated therein that there was voluntariness in the use of the fingerprint. A clear indication of this, according to the system administrator/security officer, lies in the circumstance that an employee stated that both the drop and the fingerprint scan could be used.  

21. The AP cannot follow [CONFIDENTIAL] in its argument. The mere fact that an alternative could also be used does not mean that there is consent for processing a fingerprint scan of an employee. This 'derived consent' also does not, by its nature, align with the specific requirements for consent set by the GDPR legislator, including the requirement for an unequivocal expression of will.

Biometric Data  
Article 9, paragraph 1, of the GDPR defines special personal data as follows, insofar as relevant here:  
“[...] personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a person, or data concerning health, or data related to a person's sexual behavior or sexual orientation […]”  

According to Article 4, paragraph 14, of the GDPR, biometric data are personal data resulting from specific technical processing related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person, on the basis of which unique identification of that natural person is possible or confirmed, such as facial images or fingerprint data.  

According to Article 9, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, the processing of biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a person is prohibited.  

Exceptions to the prohibition on processing special personal data are listed in Article 9, paragraph 2, of the GDPR, insofar as relevant here:  
“Paragraph 1 shall not apply where one of the following conditions is met:  
a) the data subject has given explicit consent to the processing of those personal data for one or more specified purposes, except where Union law or Member State law provides that the prohibition referred to in paragraph 1 may not be lifted by the data subject;  
[…]

Administrative Fine  
According to Article 58, paragraph 2, introductory phrase and under (i), in conjunction with Article 83, paragraph 5, introductory phrase and under (b), of the GDPR and Article 14, paragraph 3, of the UAVG, the AP is authorized to impose an administrative fine for violations of the GDPR.  

According to Article 83, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, each supervisory authority shall ensure that the administrative fines imposed pursuant to this article for the infringements mentioned in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 of this regulation are effective, proportionate, and dissuasive in each case. According to paragraph 2, administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of the specific case, in addition to or instead of the measures referred to in Article 58, paragraph 2, under (a) to (h) and under (j).  

In deciding whether to impose an administrative fine and the amount thereof, due regard shall be given to the following for each specific case:  

a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing in question as well as the number of data subjects affected and the level of damage suffered by them;  
b) the intentional or negligent nature of the infringement;  
c) the measures taken by the data controller or processor to mitigate the damage suffered by data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the data controller or processor, taking into account the technical and organizational measures they have implemented in accordance with Articles 25 and 32;  
e) previous relevant infringements by the data controller or processor;  
f) the degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority to remedy the infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects thereof;  
g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement;  
h) the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, in particular whether, and if so to what extent, the data controller or processor notified the infringement;  
i) compliance with the measures referred to in Article 58, paragraph 2, insofar as they have previously been taken against the data controller or processor in question regarding the same matter;  
j) adherence to approved codes of conduct in accordance with Article 40 or approved certification mechanisms in accordance with Article 42; and
