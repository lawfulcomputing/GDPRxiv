DECISION 16/2024

The Hellenic Data Protection Authority convened, following an invitation from its President, in a session at its headquarters on May 17, 2024, May 18, 2024, and May 27, 2024, in order to examine the case referred to in the background of this document. Present at the session were the President of the Authority, Konstantinos Menoudakos, the regular members Charalampos Anthopoulos, Spyridon Vlachopoulos, and Konstantinos Lamprinoudakis, as rapporteurs, and the regular members Aikaterini Iliadou, Christos Kalloniatis, and Grigorios Tsolias. Present, without voting rights, were the expert scientists - auditors, Georgia Panagopoulou, Georgios Rousopoulos, and Charis Simeonidou, as assistants to the rapporteurs, and Irini Papageorgopoulou, an employee of the administrative affairs department of the Authority, as secretary.  
The Authority took into account the following:

I. BACKGROUND

From March 1, 2024, to April 16, 2024, the Authority received 236 complaints and requests from Greeks abroad against MEP Anna Michel Asimakopoulou for unsolicited political communication via email to their personal email addresses, which all complainants had used for their registration in the special electoral rolls for Greeks abroad in 2023 through the platform of the Ministry of Interior apodimoi.gov.gr. The email from the accused, titled "100 days before the European elections," was sent on the same day and in close temporal proximity to an informational message from the Ministry of Interior to Greeks abroad regarding postal voting. Several complainants reported, providing relevant screenshots, that when attempting to unsubscribe from the message recipient list, the information appeared that the list was named "APODIMOS." In some cases, the complainants stated that the email address to which they received the MEP's message is used exclusively for their communications with the Greek Public. At the same time, during the same period, 66 complaints were submitted to the Authority by Greeks abroad against the Ministry of Interior for violating their personal data, due to the alleged leakage of their email address from the Ministry to the aforementioned MEP.

I.A DESCRIPTION OF ACTIONS AND FINDINGS  
Following the large number of complaints and inquiries submitted and having taken note of related protests published in the press and on social media, the Authority sent a letter numbered G/EX/754/04-01-2024 to Ms. Asimakopoulou. In its letter, the Authority informed the MEP that it is exercising its investigative powers under Article 58 of the GDPR and Article 13 paragraph 1 item h of Law 4624/2019 and invited her to respond within 10 days of receipt, to the following questions, providing the relevant documents: a) What was the source of the email addresses belonging to the recipient list of the message titled "100 days before the European elections," and what was the source specifically for the list of email addresses named "APODIMOS," which is mentioned during the attempt to unsubscribe by the complainants, b) What was the legal basis for processing the email addresses of the recipients of the aforementioned message, c) How were the data subjects informed about the processing of their data as mentioned above, in accordance with the principle of transparency of processing, d) Full and accurate details (proof of sending, recipient lists, etc.) must be provided from which the total number of recipients of the above message as well as the number of email addresses on the "APODIMOS" list can be determined.

On 15-03-2024, the resignation of the Secretary General of Local Government and Decentralization (formerly Secretary General of Interior and Organization) of the Ministry of Interior, as well as the Secretary of Expatriates of New Democracy, was made public, as they are alleged to be involved in the leak of personal data related to the case, following relevant announcements from the Government, and as a result of a related investigation that was conducted. There was no specific notification to the Authority; the Authority became aware of these developments through the media. On 18-03-2024, the Authority received notification number G/EIS/2484/18-3-2024 regarding a Personal Data Breach Incident from the Ministry of Interior. In the said notification, the start and end time of the incident is stated to be under investigation and is approximately May 2023. The time the entity became aware of the incident is stated as 15/3/2024, 14:00, and the manner in which it became aware of the incident is through the media. The type of data mentioned includes name and e-mail address. It states that an investigation is ongoing by the Ministry's internal audit service, the number of subjects is not known (only a maximum of 26,905 can be considered, which is also mentioned in the lists submitted by the Ministry to the Authority with document number G/EIS/2445/15-03-2024) and that the subjects have not yet been informed, as the internal investigation has not been completed.

Given this notification, the data from the examination of the lists of Ms. Asimakopoulou and the Ministry of Interior, as well as the public announcements of the resignations, the order number G/EXE/903/19-03-2024 for an audit was issued by the President of the Authority with the audited entity being the Ministry of Interior, in order to provide clarifications and answers and to find relevant data. The on-site audit was conducted on 20-03-2024, at the offices of the Ministry of Interior, Evangelistrias 2, 1st floor, Athens. On behalf of the Ministry, the following individuals were present: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I. The audit team of the Authority consisted of Konstantinos Lamprinoudakis, regular member, and Georgia Panagopoulou and Charis Symeonidou, auditors. The on-site audit plan was followed, which included questions related to the content of the submitted notification of the breach incident as well as the search for data following the documents submitted by the Ministry and the conclusions that had emerged. Based on the notification of the incident, the attendees were asked about what is known regarding the incident, the time and manner in which it occurred, as well as how the Ministry became aware of it. The response given was that the Ministry's employees learned about the leak from the media, subsequently the same information was circulated by the Minister's Office, and after the acknowledgment of the incident, they submitted, as they were obliged, the Notification to the Authority, following a relevant indication from the Data Protection Officer, while the related investigation by the Internal Audit Unit is ongoing. During the audit, the manner in which the examination of the registration applications of expatriates in the special electoral rolls for the parliamentary elections of 2023 was conducted by authorized employees through the OSYED system was described, in order to verify compliance with the legal requirements and to decide on the approval or rejection of the applications. The method of assigning roles in the system and the safeguards that prevent mass extraction of data were mentioned (assignment of control of specific cases to employees, only authorized persons can execute queries from which a file with mass data of voters is extracted). During the audit, data that substantiate the conclusion publicly announced by the Ministry were sought, but no evidence was submitted and no specific answer was given. The attendees reserved the right to inform the Authority accordingly when the report of the Internal Audit Unit is issued.

As "processing" of personal data according to Article 4 paragraph 2 of the GDPR is understood any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment or combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.

3. As the "data controller," according to Article 4(7) of the GDPR, is defined the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data; when the purposes and means of such processing are determined by Union law or the law of a Member State, the data controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be provided for by Union law or the law of a Member State. As the "processor" (Article 4(8) of the GDPR) is defined the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller, while as a "third party" (Article 4(10) of the GDPR) is understood any natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or body, except for the data subject, the data controller, the processor, and the persons who, under the direct authority of the data controller or the processor, are authorized to process personal data. Furthermore, according to Article 29 of the GDPR, "The processor and any person acting under the authority of the data controller or of the processor, who has access to personal data, shall process those data only on instructions from the data controller, unless required to do so by Union law or the law of a Member State." Consequently, in the case of processing personal data by a natural person on behalf of the data controller, that is, in the exercise of their duties within the framework of the activities and objectives pursued by the data controller, the actions of the natural person are attributed to the legal entity. As noted in the EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on the concepts of the data controller and the processor in the GDPR - Version 2.0 (July 7, 2021)2, "In principle, any processing of personal data carried out by employees in the context of an organization’s activities can be considered as being carried out under the control of that organization (since employees who have access to personal data within an organization are generally not considered 'data controllers' or 'processors,' but rather 'persons acting under the authority of the data controller or the processor' within the meaning of Article 29 of the GDPR). In exceptional circumstances, however, an employee may decide to use personal data for their own purposes and, as a result, unlawfully exceed the powers assigned to them (e.g., to establish their own company or for a similar purpose). Consequently, as the data controller, the organization has a duty to ensure that appropriate technical and organizational measures are implemented, including training and informing employees, which will ensure compliance with the GDPR." (§19). The same EDPB Guidelines also clarify: "In practice, the processing of personal data involving multiple actors can be broken down into several smaller processing activities for which it could be considered that each actor determines the purpose and means of processing independently. Moreover, a sequence or series of processing operations involving multiple actors may also be carried out for the same purpose(s), so it is likely that the processing may involve one or more joint data controllers. In other words, at the 'micro-level,' various individual processing actions may appear to be disconnected from one another, giving the impression that each individual action may serve a different purpose. However, it is necessary to thoroughly examine whether at the 'macro-level' these processing actions should not be considered as a 'series of operations' aimed at a common purpose using jointly determined means." (§43). Anyone deciding to process data must consider whether such processing involves personal data and, if so, what obligations they have under the GDPR. An actor is considered a "data controller" even if they do not intentionally target the personal data itself or have mistakenly assessed that they are not processing personal data." (§44). The EDPB clarified, finally, that access to the filing system is not a prerequisite for being designated as a data controller, nor is "ownership" of the filing system or supervision over it, as long as they determine the purpose of processing even in "foreign" files: "The data controller does not need to actually have access to the data being processed. Anyone assigning processing activities to an external provider while exercising critical influence over the purpose and (essential) means of processing (e.g., by adjusting service parameters in such a way as to influence the selection of the individuals whose personal data are being processed) is considered...

"data controller, even if it has no actual access to the data" (§45). As has been determined by the CJEU, any natural or legal person who influences the processing of personal data for their own purposes and thus participates in determining the objectives and means of the processing can be considered a data controller. The Union legislator did not distinguish between natural and legal persons for the establishment of liability under the GDPR, as this liability depends solely on the condition that these persons, alone or jointly with others, determine the purposes and means of processing personal data. Therefore, any person who meets this condition—regardless of whether they are a natural or legal person, a public authority, service, or other entity—is liable, among other things, for any violation referred to in Article 83 paragraphs 4 and 6, which is committed by them or on their behalf, while the imposition of sanctions on a legal entity as a data controller does not depend on the prior identification that the violation was committed by an identified natural person. As determined by the CJEU, for the exemption of a legal entity data controller from liability for damages under Article 82 of the GDPR, it is not sufficient to claim that the damage in question was caused by the fault of a person acting under its supervision, within the meaning of Article 29 of the GDPR. Specifically, Article 32 paragraph 4 of the GDPR, regarding the security of processing, provides that the data controller takes measures to ensure that any natural person acting under its supervision and having access to such data processes it only on the instructions of the data controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law. Given that the employees of the legal entity data controller act under its supervision, it is the responsibility of that controller to ensure that its instructions are properly followed by them, and therefore, the legal entity cannot be exempted from its liability simply by claiming negligence or omission of its employee. Furthermore, the Authority has also accepted that a natural person—an employee of the data controller—can be considered a data controller if they use data for their own purposes outside the scope and potential control of the activities of the legal entity.

Political communication is understood as communication carried out by political parties, members of parliament, members of the European Parliament, factions, and holders of elected positions in local government or candidates in parliamentary elections, European Parliament elections, and local government elections at any time, whether pre-election or not, for the promotion of political ideas, action programs, or other activities aimed at supporting them and shaping political behavior. Following the decisions No. 1343-5/2022 of the Council of State, which ruled that the activity of political communication, as not aimed at commercial promotion, does not fall under Law 3471/2006, the Authority re-examined, within the scope of its competence, the issues of legality arising in relation to political communication in order to determine the framework for the lawful processing of personal data for this purpose based on the provisions of the GDPR, and with Decision 9/202310 decided to issue Guidelines 1/2023 for the processing of personal data for the purpose of political communication. With these Guidelines (hereinafter referred to as “GCG 1/2023”), it is clarified, among other things, the following: According to the democratic principle, the activity of political communication is protected by the Constitution, particularly within the framework of the general right to participate in the political life of the country (Article 5, paragraph 1 of the Constitution), the right of citizens to information (Article 5A, paragraph 1 of the Constitution), and the fulfillment of the constitutional mission of political parties (Article 29, paragraph 1 of the Constitution). However, when methods are used that require or consist of the processing of personal data, such as names, postal addresses, telephone numbers, email addresses of individuals, etc., political communication must be conducted in a manner that ensures respect for Article 9A of the Constitution and national and European legislation for the protection of personal data (ad hoc Council of State 1343-5/2022, para. 14). In the above cases, the entities and individuals conducting political communication become data controllers, according to the definition of Article 4, paragraph 7 of the GDPR, to the extent that they determine the purpose and means of processing personal data. For example, when a member of parliament or a parliamentary candidate receives data from the political party they belong to and processes it for their personal political communication, they become the data controller themselves. In this capacity, according to the fundamental principle of accountability in the GDPR (Article 5, paragraph 2), the aforementioned individuals must be able to demonstrate compliance with the basic principles of data processing, as provided in Article 5, paragraph 1 of the GDPR. Regarding the legal basis for processing personal data in the case of political communication, the GCG 1/2023 clarified that such processing may be based either on the prior consent of the data subjects (Article 6, paragraph 1, subparagraph a of the GDPR) or on a legitimate interest that may arise (Article 6, paragraph 1, subparagraph f of the GDPR). Specifically, political communication may rely on the legal basis of the legitimate interest of the data controller, provided that it is demonstrated that the processing is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest of the data controller in promoting their political positions, and that this interest does not override the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subjects, taking into account their legitimate expectations based on their relationship with the data controller (See Recital 47 of the GDPR). The processing of personal data for the purpose of political communication is also permitted under Article 6, paragraph 4 of the GDPR, provided that the purpose of political communication is deemed compatible with the purposes for which the personal data were initially collected by the data controller. To ascertain whether the purpose of political communication is compatible with the purpose of the initial collection of personal data, provided that all requirements for the legality of the initial processing are met, the data controller must take into account, among other things:
- Any relationship between the initial purposes and the purposes of the intended further processing;
- The context in which the personal data were collected, particularly the reasonable expectations of the data subject based on their relationship with the data controller regarding their further use;
- The nature of the personal data;
- The consequences of the intended further processing for the data subjects;
48

- and the existence of appropriate guarantees for both the initial and the intended further processing activities (See Recital 50 GDPR).  
The KGs 1/2023 also emphasize that in the case of collecting data for the purpose of political communication based on the legal ground of legitimate interest (Article 6(1)(f) GDPR), according to the principle of accountability under Article 5(2) GDPR, the data controller must be able to adequately document the balancing test that must be conducted prior to the commencement of processing, and based on which it assessed that the interests of the data subjects do not outweigh the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the subjects - recipients of the political communication. Similarly, in the case of further use of the data for the purpose of political communication, the data controller must be able to demonstrate compliance with the criteria of paragraph 4 of Article 6 GDPR regarding the compatibility of the purpose. The KGs 1/2023 provide illustrative examples of cases in which personal data cannot be used for the purpose of political communication, as well as cases in which, based on the assessment of the specific circumstances, the legality of processing personal data for the purpose of political communication can be substantiated. From the examples of these latter cases, it follows that the collection of data from a lawful source is in every case a necessary condition for legitimate processing.  
Finally, the KGs 1/2023 remind of the information that must be provided in every electronic communication for the purpose of political communication, which includes the source from which the contact details of the subject have been collected, provided that it is not the subject itself (Article 14(2)(f)), while it is emphasized that in the event of exercising the right to object, the data controller must, in principle, cease processing the subject's data for the purpose of political communication (Article 21(1)(b) GDPR).

A breach of personal data (Article 4 paragraph 12 GDPR) is understood as "a security breach that leads to accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of, or access to personal data that has been transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed." According to the Guidelines 18/2018 of the Article 29 Working Party of Directive 95/46/EC (now European Data Protection Board) on Personal Data Breach Notification (“Guidelines on Personal data breach notification under Regulation 2016/679” WP 250 rev. 1) dated 06-02-2018, one type of personal data breach is categorized based on the security principle of "confidentiality," when unauthorized access to personal data is identified (“confidentiality breach”). A breach may potentially have various significant adverse consequences for individuals, which can lead to physical, material, or moral harm. The GDPR explains that this harm may include loss of control over their personal data, restriction of their rights, discrimination, identity theft or misuse, financial loss, unlawful reversal of pseudonymization, damage to reputation, and loss of confidentiality of personal data protected by professional secrecy, etc. (see also recitals 85 and 75). Data breach incidents must be reported to the Authority within 72 hours from the moment the data controller becomes aware of them, according to Article 33 paragraph 1 GDPR: "1. In the event of a personal data breach, the data controller shall notify without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of the personal data breach to the supervisory authority competent pursuant to Article 55, unless the personal data breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. When the notification to the supervisory authority is not made within 72 hours, it shall be accompanied by reasons for the delay." The notification must contain at least the minimum information referred to in paragraph 3 of Article 33 GDPR, while according to paragraph 5 of the same article "The data controller documents any personal data breach,
52

which consist of the factual circumstances regarding the breach of personal data, the consequences, and the corrective measures taken. This documentation allows the supervisory authority to verify compliance with the current article." If it is not possible to provide all the required information at once, paragraph 4 of Article 33 GDPR stipulates that it may be provided to the Authority "in phases, without undue delay." Regarding the time of knowledge of the incident by the Data Controller, the aforementioned WP29 Guidelines (wp 250) state the following: "As detailed above, the GDPR requires, in the event of a breach, the data controller to notify the breach without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of the fact. This may raise the question of when a data controller can be considered to have acquired 'knowledge' of a breach. WP29 considers that a data controller should be deemed to have acquired 'knowledge' when the data controller has a reasonable degree of certainty that a security incident has occurred that results in personal data being compromised. However, as previously mentioned, the GDPR requires the data controller to implement all appropriate technical and organizational measures for the immediate detection of any breach and the prompt notification of the supervisory authority and the data subjects. It also states that it should be established that the notification was made without undue delay, taking into account in particular the nature and severity of the data breach, as well as the consequences and adverse effects for the data subject. In this way, the data controller is obliged to ensure that it acquires 'knowledge' of any breaches in a timely manner so that it can take appropriate actions. The exact point in time when a data controller can be considered to have acquired 'knowledge' of a specific breach will depend on the circumstances of the particular breach. In some cases, it will become relatively clear from the outset that a breach has occurred, while in others, it may take some time to ascertain whether personal data has been compromised. However, emphasis should be placed on the timely action to investigate an incident to determine whether personal data has been breached and, in such cases, to take corrective measures and notify, if required." Furthermore, the breach must also be communicated to the data subject, as applicable and in accordance with the provisions of Article 34 paragraphs 1 and 2 GDPR: "1. When a personal data breach is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall notify the personal data breach to the data subject without undue delay. 2. The notification to the data subject referred to in paragraph 1 of this Article shall describe in clear and plain language the nature of the personal data breach and shall contain at least the information and measures referred to in Article 33 paragraph 3 points b), c), and d)." In assessing the risk, according to recitals 75 and 76 GDPR, the likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects should be taken into account, and the risk should be assessed based on an objective evaluation. According to Guidelines 18/2018 of WP29, in assessing the risk to individuals as a result of a breach, the data controller should consider the specific circumstances of the breach, including the severity of the potential impact and the likelihood of its occurrence, taking into account a range of criteria: the type of breach, the nature, sensitivity, and volume of data, the ease of identifying individuals, the severity of the consequences for individuals, the specific characteristics of the individual, the specific characteristics of the data controller, and the number of affected individuals (wp 250 rev.01, pp. 28-31). Therefore, Article 33 GDPR not only imposes the obligation to notify breach incidents to the supervisory authority but also the obligation to actively investigate any potential incident, provided that the data controller becomes aware of relevant indications. Furthermore, from paragraph 3 of Article 33 GDPR, which specifies the elements that must be included at a minimum in the notification, it follows that the data controller has the obligation to promptly investigate the information referred to in this provision so that it can include it in the notification to the Authority (nature of the breach, categories and number of affected subjects, number of affected records, potential consequences of the breach, measures taken or proposed to be taken to address the breach, measures to mitigate potential adverse consequences), as well as to assess the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subject, in order to decide whether notification is required under Article 34 GDPR, while paragraph 5 of Article 33 GDPR explicitly states the obligation to maintain documentation for all the above processes (see also Decision DPA 35/2023).

It was also noted that an investigation is ongoing by the internal audit service of the Ministry, that the number of subjects is unknown (only a maximum of 26,905 can be considered, as mentioned in the lists submitted by the Ministry to the Authority with the document No. Γ/ΕΙΣ/2445/15-03-2024) and that the data subjects have not yet been informed, as the internal investigation has not been completed.  
l) Referring to the case in question, in an interview on 16/3/2024, the Minister of Interior explained that the leak "occurred during the period between the two electoral contests of the parliamentary elections held in May and June 2023, when the Ministry of Interior was under caretaker leadership. During this period, an associate of the then responsible for the elections, General Secretary Michalis Stavrianoudakis, exported email addresses of expatriates to the Secretary of Overseas Hellenism of New Democracy, Nikos Theodoropoulos. He then forwarded them to Ms. Asimakopoulou."  
m) It also does not appear that the Ministry of Interior conducted an exhaustive investigation and search for information in order to facilitate the identification of the time and subject of the illegal transmission of electoral roll data. During the Authority's inquiries during on-site inspections, no relevant information was provided nor was an authorized legal representative of the Ministry present. Therefore, there is no evidence that substantiates the fact and time of the data leak from the Ministry to the Secretary of Overseas Greeks of New Democracy or any other recipient outside the Ministry. The records mention the Ministry's employees as executors, while the processing contractor company, which was indeed mentioned in the submitted incident notification of data breach, is not included. There is also insufficient reference to security measures, the duration of data retention is not specified, and there is a reference to general legislation rather than a specific reference to the electoral legislation.

A. From the above points a), b), c), it follows that a record containing personal data of all registered voters abroad for the June 2023 elections, for which the Ministry of Interior is responsible for processing, and for which the applicable legislation does not provide for any case of transmission outside the Ministry, was circulated outside the Ministry. Although it was not possible to ascertain how this transmission occurred, this fact undoubtedly constitutes an incident of breach of confidentiality of personal data and thus a breach of personal data, according to the definition of Article 4, paragraph 12 of the GDPR, as it is certain that the existing measures were violated and there was unauthorized disclosure of personal data that was transmitted, which was subsequently subjected to further processing.

B. From the above points d) to g), it follows that the policies and procedures followed by the Ministry for the mass export and internal circulation of data of voters abroad did not contain specific provisions (specific security measures during the circulation of the records) and restrictions (regarding the recipients of the records), nor provisions for the recording and documentation of actions as well as the approval of the purpose of export. Furthermore, the designed procedures do not include any measures to mitigate risks caused by the human factor (which, according to the Ministry, proved to be decisive and critical). It should be noted that the risk from the human factor is referred to by the authorities' data as a key factor causing incidents of personal data breaches, and it had been specifically highlighted by the Authority since 2018. It also emerged that the implemented measures were not reassessed for the specific needs of processing the lists of voters abroad.

C. From the above points h) to i), deficiencies are identified in the procedures and policies for the protection of data (as mentioned in the context of accountability in Article 24 of the GDPR), meaning that the data controller did not effectively implement appropriate technical measures for recording user actions and organizational measures for the application of data protection principles at the time of determining the means of processing, as provided in Article 25, paragraph 1 for data protection by design.

D. From the above points j), k), l), it follows that the submitted Notification of Personal Data Breach does not include information that was publicly announced and had been mentioned in an interview by the Minister prior to the submission of the notification, while it is not included in the submitted Notification of Personal Data Breach, even though it had been mentioned in an interview by the Minister prior to the submission of the notification. With this handling of the incident, the obligations of Article 33, paragraphs 3, 4, and 5 of the GDPR regarding the notification of personal data breaches to the supervisory authority were not fulfilled.

E. From the above point m), deficiencies and inaccuracies are observed in the content of the maintained activity records concerning the OSYED system, as required by Article 30 of the GDPR. 

Therefore, it follows that the Ministry of Interior violated i. Articles 5, paragraph 1, subparagraph f and Article 32 of the GDPR, as analyzed in the above points A and B, ii. Article 25.1 of the GDPR, as analyzed in point C, iii. Articles 33, paragraphs 3, 4, and 5 of the GDPR, as analyzed in point D, and iv. Article 30 of the GDPR as analyzed in point E.

Based on the above, the Authority considers that there is a case to exercise its corrective powers under Article 58, paragraph 2 of the GDPR concerning the identified violations and that, based on the circumstances identified, a penalty should be imposed, according to the provisions of the GDPR.

Furthermore, the Authority took into account the criteria for the assessment of the fine as defined in Article 39(2) of Law 4624/2019 applicable to the present case, the Guidelines for the application and determination of administrative fines for the purposes of Regulation 2016/679 issued on 03-10-2017 by the Article 29 Working Party (WP 253), and the Guidelines 04/2022 of the European Data Protection Board for the calculation of administrative fines within the framework of the General Regulation, as well as the factual data of the examined case, based on which the severity of the violation is deemed significant, and taking into account in particular:

o that the nature of the violations concerns the obligations of accountability and the adoption of technical measures for recording user actions and organizational measures of the data controller,
o the processing concerns the main activities of the data controller responsible for organizing and conducting the electoral process, which must not be called into question nor should the trust of citizens in it be undermined,
o the violations do not directly result in serious consequences for the data subjects,

THE AUTHORITY

For the violations of Articles 5(1)(a) in conjunction with Article 6(1) and Article 14 of the GDPR, as analyzed in Chapter B1 of the reasoning of this decision, i) imposes pursuant to Article 58(2)(h) GDPR on Anna Michelle Asimakopoulou, as data controller, a monetary fine of forty thousand (40,000) euros, and ii) orders, pursuant to Article 58(2)(g) of the GDPR, Anna Michelle Asimakopoulou, as data controller, to delete all data of the overseas voters.
66

B. For the violations of Articles 5(1)(f) and 32 of the GDPR, Article 25(1), and Article 33(3), (4), (5) of the GDPR, as well as Article 30 of the GDPR, as analyzed in Chapter B4 of the reasoning of this document, i) imposes pursuant to Article 58(2)(h) of the GDPR on the Ministry of Interior, as the data controller, a monetary fine of four hundred thousand (400,000) euros, and ii) orders, pursuant to Article 58(2)(d) of the GDPR, the Ministry of Interior, as the data controller, to take the following actions:  
In light of this incident, the Ministry must document in approved policies, review and revise the procedures and measures it implements regarding the protection of personal data during the processing of personal data of voters, including those data retained in the OSYED system as well as in any other related system. The above actions must ensure compliance with the principles of Article 5(1) of the GDPR, particularly confidentiality throughout the election process, and provide for a procedure for periodic evaluation of the measures.  
The Ministry must, within three months from the notification of this decision, prepare a relevant timeline, which will specify the procedures for the preparation, implementation, supervision, and updating of the above, as well as the timeframe for their execution, promptly notify the Authority of the timeline, and update it quarterly on its implementation.

As specific measures for the prevention, detection, and investigation of personal data breach incidents, the following must be provided and implemented without delay:

1. Systematic analysis of the institutional framework for conducting elections, identification of the procedures that require the processing of personal data, documentation for each procedure of the flows of personal data and the recipients of each type of data.  
As part of the analysis, the possibility of actions being executed on datasets by natural persons (e.g., employees) should be minimized to only what is absolutely necessary. Repetitive processes should be carried out with minimal human intervention and without data extraction. Specifically, it is proposed:

a) All functions requiring mass processing of voters' personal data, especially contact data, should be integrated into applications, so that lawful processing occurs within the application environment (e.g., sending informational messages to voters), with full documentation of actions.  
b) The possibility of using DLP software should be examined, especially for the Ministry units involved in file exports.  
c) Prohibition of the transmission via email or other internal communication methods of files containing voters' personal data. Exceptionally, if truly necessary, such transmission may only occur in encrypted form. Each message must state the purpose of processing and the purpose of transmission, as well as indicate the retention period of the file. The code must be communicated through a different medium. This transmission must be approved by a competent senior official, and the relevant approval must be kept on file (audit trail) for a sufficient period.  
d) Review of the process for logging actions of the type of queries (SELECT) in the OSYED database tables or in other systems that process personal data and take measures for the automated, proactive, control of the log files.

2. Security Policy and Security Plan – other organizational measures  
Given that no approved security policy (or individual security plans) was submitted to the Authority, the Ministry must update the Security Policy it applies and the accompanying individual security plans concerning the processing of voters' personal data so that:  
a) The systems for managing voters' data are included, and it is decided which of the proposed rules will be implemented (or it is recorded which have been implemented).  
b) Appropriate provisions are included for updating contracts with processors regarding the changes that will arise.  
c) Documentation, review, and updating of the procedure for handling personal data breach incidents, with emphasis on the content of the notification to the Authority, the informing of data subjects, and the appropriate procedures and bodies for internal investigation.

68

d) Regular checks by an independent organization or body, at least annually, of the security of systems and procedures, including the assessment of the security measures applied.  
e) Periodic checks by the Ministry, at least annually, of any processors regarding the adoption of appropriate security measures.

3. Measures for informing and raising awareness among staff  
A program of awareness and training actions should be designed for both leadership and staff at all levels of the Ministry regarding personal data protection issues, and specifically regarding the obligations of the data controller, the manner of cooperation with the Authority, and the role of the DPO. The actions of the program should include activities that keep the Ministry's staff alert.
