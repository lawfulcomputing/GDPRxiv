The Authority took into account the following:  

The Authority conducted an extraordinary on-site inspection on 18/11/2019 at the Athens Urban Transport Organization (OASA) S.A. (hereinafter "data controller" or OASA) regarding the protection of personal data processed within the framework of the Automatic Fare Collection System (hereinafter AFC), a system also referred to as "electronic ticketing," in light of what the Authority had outlined in its Opinion No. 4/2017. Specifically, the Authority, in Opinion 4/2017, had expressed its opinion on the processing that OASA would carry out within the framework of the then-upcoming electronic ticketing system. Already in its previous Opinion No. 1/2017, the Authority had set forth all the conditions that must be met for the processing of personal data within an electronic system for movements made using public transport (which data also includes special category data, such as those related to the movements of persons with disabilities) to comply with the requirements of the relevant legal framework and not to infringe upon the fundamental rights and freedoms of individuals. The Authority, in its latest Opinion 4/2017, concluded that the new form of the said processing, as described by OASA, had been satisfactorily harmonized with the conditions set by the Authority in Opinion 1/2017. However, the Authority, in Opinion 4/2017, determined that OASA, as the data controller, should make certain modifications, the effectiveness of which must be substantiated by a data protection impact assessment (hereinafter DPIA) that the data controller is obliged to prepare, which should be documented in a document approved by the management of OASA and made available by 25 May 2018 – the date on which the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter GDPR) would come into effect.

CONSIDERED IN ACCORDANCE WITH THE LAW  

1. According to the provisions of Articles 51 and 55 of the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (hereinafter, GDPR) and Article 9 of Law 4624/2019 (Government Gazette A' 137), the Authority has the competence to supervise the application of the provisions of the GDPR, this law, and other regulations concerning the protection of individuals from the processing of personal data.  
2. According to Article 4, point 1 of the GDPR, "personal data" means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ("data subject"), whereas an identifiable natural person is one whose identity can be ascertained, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, identification number, location data, online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, psychological, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.  
3. "Pseudonymization" is defined, according to Article 4, point 5 of the GDPR, as the processing of personal data in such a way that the data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such additional information is kept separately and is subject to technical and organizational measures to ensure that it cannot be attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person.  
In this regard, Recital 26 of the GDPR states that "personal data that have been pseudonymized, which could be attributed to a natural person by the use of supplementary information, should be considered information relating to an identifiable natural person. In order to determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means that could reasonably be used, such as the separation of the data from the data controller or a third party for the direct or indirect identification of the natural person, should be taken into account. To ascertain whether any means are reasonably likely to be used to identify the natural person, all objective factors should be considered, such as the costs and time required for identification, taking into account the technology available at the time of processing and technological developments."  
4. Furthermore, according to Article 4, point 7 of the GDPR, the data controller is defined as "the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data."

5. According to Article 5(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation, personal data:  
a) shall be processed lawfully and fairly in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject ("lawfulness, fairness, and transparency"),  
b) are collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and shall not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes ("purpose limitation"),  
c) are adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the purposes for which they are processed ("data minimization"),  
d) are accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable steps must be taken to ensure that personal data that are inaccurate, with respect to the purposes of processing, are erased or rectified without delay ("accuracy"),  
e) are kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of processing personal data; personal data may be stored for longer periods, provided that the personal data will be processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89(1) of the GDPR and provided that appropriate technical and organizational measures are applied to safeguard the rights and freedoms of the data subject ("storage limitation"),  
f) shall be processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, using appropriate technical or organizational measures ("integrity and confidentiality").  
6. Furthermore, according to paragraph 2 of the same article, "the controller shall be responsible for, and be able to demonstrate compliance with, paragraph 1 ("accountability")." The introduction of the principle of accountability shifts the "burden of proof" regarding the lawfulness of processing and compliance with the GDPR from the data protection authorities to the controllers or processors themselves. The GDPR provides controllers/processors with a range of regulatory methods and tools for this purpose, such as -among others- maintaining records of processing activities, implementing security measures, and conducting data protection impact assessments.  
7. Moreover, for the processing of personal data to be lawful, it must have a legal basis as described in Article 6 of the GDPR. According to the provision of Article 6(1), "processing shall be lawful only if and to the extent that at least one of the following applies (…) f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, especially if the data subject is a child." However, as defined in the next paragraph of the same provision, this legal basis does not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks. As clarified in Recital 47 of the GDPR, the justification for the prohibition of applying this legal basis to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their duties lies in the principle of legality, meaning that, with respect to public authorities, it is up to the legislator to provide by law the legal basis for the processing they undertake.  
8. Article 4 of Law 4624/2019 provides that "public authority" means public authorities, independent and regulatory administrative authorities, legal entities under public law, local government authorities of the first and second degree, and their legal entities and enterprises, state or public enterprises and organizations, legal entities under private law that are owned by the state or are subsidized by at least 50% of their annual budget or whose management is appointed by it."

9. 10. The GDPR does not define what constitutes a "public authority or public body." The Article 29 Working Party in its Guidelines on Data Protection Officers (WP 243), which have been adopted by the European Data Protection Board, considers that this concept should be defined by national laws, but believes that the fulfillment of a public duty and the exercise of public authority can occur not only by public authorities or public bodies but also by other natural or legal persons of public or private law, in various sectors arising from the national legislation of each Member State, such as public transport services, water supply and energy provision, road infrastructure, public broadcasting, the construction of workers' housing, or disciplinary bodies for legislatively protected professions. In these cases, data subjects are likely to find themselves in a situation very similar to the processing of their data by a public authority or public body. Specifically, data processing for similar purposes is possible, and likewise, individuals usually have little or no choice regarding whether and how their data will be processed. This view of the Article 29 Working Party reflects the prevailing perspective of Greek theory and case law, according to which public enterprises providing vital services for the social whole, regardless of their form as legal entities of private law, pursue the public purpose of ensuring for the social whole those vital goods and services without which the normal conditions for a decent human living according to modern criteria and the free development of personality and activity guaranteed by the Constitution cannot exist. In order to ensure the safeguarding of the constitutional guarantees surrounding the vital services provided (e.g., public transport, water supply, sewage, etc.), the State, through the regulatory supervision and ownership control it exercises over public enterprises, participating with a majority in their share capital, uses tools of administrative law that essentially constitute the exercise of public authority.  
11. Regarding the principle of transparency of processing, the GDPR imposes specific obligations on data controllers concerning the information they must provide to data subjects. Specifically, according to Article 12 paragraph 1 of the GDPR, the data controller takes appropriate measures to provide the data subject with all information referred to in Article 13 – which specifically states that "when personal data relating to a data subject are collected from the data subject, the data controller, at the time of collecting the personal data, provides the data subject with all of the following information: a) the identity and contact details of the data controller and, where applicable, of the representative of the data controller, b) the contact details of the data protection officer, where applicable, c) the purposes of the processing for which the personal data are intended, as well as the legal basis for the processing, d) if the processing is based on Article 6 paragraph 1 point f), the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, e) the recipients or categories of recipients of the personal data, if any (…)” (see paragraph 1 of Article 13 of the GDPR). Furthermore, in paragraph 2 of the same article, it is provided that "in addition to the information referred to in paragraph 1, the data controller, at the time of collecting the data…".
15. Personal data provides the data subject with the following additional information necessary to ensure fair and transparent processing: a) the period for which the personal data will be stored or, when that is not possible, the criteria used to determine that period (...).

12. Article 25(1) of the GDPR establishes the principle of data protection by design as follows: "Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons posed by the processing, the data controller implements effective, both at the time of determining the means of processing and at the time of processing, appropriate technical and organizational measures, such as pseudonymization, designed to implement the data protection principles, such as data minimization, and to integrate the necessary safeguards into the processing in such a way that the requirements of this Regulation are met and the rights of data subjects are protected." Specifically regarding the security of processing, Article 32(1) of the GDPR states that "taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller and the processor implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, including, among others, as appropriate: a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data (...)."

13. Regarding the data protection impact assessment, Article 35 of the GDPR states that when a type of processing, particularly using new technologies and taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall carry out, prior to the processing, an assessment of the impact of the intended processing operations on the protection of personal data. Furthermore, the DPIA is a key tool for fulfilling the principle of data protection by design, as outlined in Article 25(1) of the GDPR (see Decision 50/2021 of the Authority).

14. The Authority has established, based on Article 35(4) of the GDPR, a list of types of processing operations that are subject to the requirement for conducting a DPIA (see Decision 65/2018 of the Authority). According to this list, a data protection impact assessment is required in cases, among others, of "systematic and large-scale processing for the monitoring, observation, or control of natural persons using data collected through video surveillance systems or through networks or by any other means in public spaces, publicly accessible spaces, or private spaces accessible to an unlimited number of persons. This includes monitoring the movements or location/geographical position in real-time or non-real-time of identified or identifiable natural persons. Relevant examples include the use of cameras in a shopping mall or at public transport stations, or the processing of location data of passengers at an airport or on public transport."

15. In this specific case, regarding the processing of data through the ASSK, OASA is the data controller within the meaning of Article 4(7) of the GDPR. It is noted that, for personalized tickets, the data processing carried out by the ASSK concerning the movements of the passenger public constitutes the processing of personal and not anonymous data, even though the names of passengers are not retained. This is because the database of the ASSK stores the "digital fingerprint" ("hashed value") resulting from the combination of the passenger's AMKA (or passport number or other official identification document) and the 8-digit code (PIN), as well as the month and year of birth and the specific category of the beneficiary. Therefore, the AMKA and the 8-digit code chosen by each user are the pieces of information that allow for the matching of the AMKA with the digital fingerprint – and thus, the identification (via the AMKA) of the passenger holding a card with a specific digital fingerprint.

16. Regarding the implementation of the DPIA, OASA cites, among other reasons for the delay in its preparation, the difficulty of gathering information about the system from the contractors. However, the above claims, as presented, do not constitute a justification for this delay. It is noted that according to Article 28 of the GDPR, "processing by a processor shall be governed by a contract or other legal act under Union or Member State law, which binds the processor to the controller and sets out the subject matter and duration of the processing, the nature and purpose of the processing, the type of personal data and categories of data subjects, and the obligations and rights of the controller. The said contract or other legal act specifically provides that the processor: (…) (e) assists the controller in ensuring compliance with the obligations arising from Articles 32 to 36, taking into account the nature of the processing and the information available to the processor (…)". Therefore, the data controller should have taken appropriate measures to ensure the timely preparation of the impact assessment regarding personal data, as well as the implementation of any corrective measures that the results of the study might indicate, without this obligation being lifted due to difficulties arising from the collection of data from the processors.

18. Regarding the data retention periods for the various processing purposes, OASA, which had not specified data retention periods at the time of the on-site inspection and, after the inspection, set a retention period of 20 years for all data, regardless of the processing purposes, has now proceeded to specify the retention periods for the various data processing purposes—without the twenty-year period applying universally to all purposes. However, ultimately, it appears that movement data continues to be retained for twenty years, on the grounds that their retention is justified for tax purposes, “given—as OASA states in its memorandum—the twenty-year statute of limitations provided for cases of tax evasion (Article 36, paragraph 3 of Law 4174/2013).” The examples mentioned do not relate to the tax obligations of the Organization but to requests that data subjects may submit. Specifically, as noted in the updated EAPD, “a user is likely to request an analysis of their movement history—of their transactions. For example, if a passenger complains about an excessive charge when validating their card at a station or vehicle, checking the movement history is necessary to confirm or deny the passenger's claims and ultimately resolve the issue. Moreover, the user may consider that they are entitled to dispute any zero balance on their card or any charge-transaction, which is, in any case, interesting from a tax perspective.” As noted in the background of this document, OASA did not respond to a more recent document from the Authority to provide further clarifications on this matter, explaining, among other things, why the twenty-year retention period was chosen for the aforementioned data by invoking a twenty-year statute of limitations in cases of tax evasion without citing a specific provision, while the provision of Article 36, paragraph 3 of Law 4174/2013, according to which it was possible to issue a tax determination act in cases of tax evasion within twenty years and which OASA invoked, has been replaced by Article 32, paragraph 2 of Law 4646/2019, which sets the deadline for issuing the tax determination act at ten years.  
19. OASA, for various processing purposes, cites as the legal basis for processing the provision of Article 6, paragraph 1, point (f) (this applies both in the updated EAPD and in the updated processing activities record submitted to the Authority following the findings of the Authority's audit report that was communicated to it). However, for the reasons outlined above, this legal basis cannot be used by OASA, which is a public utility enterprise, supervised and controlled in ownership by the state. Moreover, OASA's invocation for certain processing purposes of the provision of Article 6, paragraph 1, point (e) (processing necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller) reinforces the view that OASA, as a public utility enterprise, falls within the concept of “public authorities,” as defined in the last sentence of Article 6, paragraph 1, in addition to the contradiction that arises from the simultaneous invocation of Article 6, paragraph 1, point (f) as a legal basis.  
Furthermore, it should be noted that the recognition and selection of the appropriate legal basis from those provided in Article 6, paragraph 1 of the GDPR is closely linked to the principle of lawful or fair processing as well as to the principle of purpose limitation, and the data controller is obliged not only to select the appropriate legal basis prior to the commencement of processing but also to inform, pursuant to Article 13, paragraph 1, point (c) and Article 14, paragraph 1, point (c) of the GDPR, the data subject about its use, as the choice of each legal basis has a legal impact on the application of the

Rights of the Data Subjects.8  
Furthermore, the expression of the principle of legality of processing is also the principle of transparency.9 Pursuant to Article 5(1)(a) of the GDPR, in addition to the requirement that data be processed lawfully and fairly, transparency is expressly included as a fundamental aspect of these principles, and is inherently linked both to legality and to the principle of accountability, according to which the data controller must always be able to demonstrate that personal data is processed transparently in relation to the data subjects.10  
In this context, while it cannot be excluded that in specific cases the possibility of simultaneous application of different legal bases for the processing of personal data may arise, this is only permissible if they are used in the correct context and particularly if they serve different processing purposes. For the same processing purpose, the data controller must initially choose a specific legal basis, the most appropriate in relation to the specific purpose. In this way, the data subjects know precisely, without any doubt, the correct legal basis for each processing operation, while the data controller, in this regard, complies with the principle of accountability under Article 5(2) of the GDPR, since, according to this provision, as previously mentioned, they bear the burden of invoking and proving the legality of the processing, which does not arise when two different legal bases are presented simultaneously for the same processing purpose.

However, for the following purposes of processing: a) to enable the study of anonymized statistical data by OASA and third parties, in such a way that they cannot identify the data subject, b) to enable the management of websites and all forms of communication, reference is made, regarding the legal bases for processing, to three legal bases, thereby preventing the easy identification of the correct one for each purpose, according to what is stated above in Recital 19 - while one of the legal bases is the legitimate interest of OASA, which, as previously mentioned, cannot apply in this specific case.

a. Violation of Article 5(1)(e) of the GDPR regarding the principle of storage limitation (see above Recital 18 of this document, as well as Finding 3 of the audit report).  
b. Violation of Article 35(1) of the GDPR regarding the preparation of a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), as it had not been prepared in a timely manner (it was not available at the time of the on-site audit - see Finding 1 of the audit report), and even after its preparation, as well as after various revisions, it appears that it has not been prepared in such a way as to fully document the handling of all data protection risks (see above Recital 17). Violation of Article 25(1) of the GDPR regarding data protection by design, since, although measures have indeed been taken by design to address various risks, it appears that there is room for additional measures, for which the necessity of their implementation is not documented (see above Recital 17, point b of this).
