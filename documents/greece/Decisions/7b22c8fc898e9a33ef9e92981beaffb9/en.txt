The Hellenic Data Protection Authority convened, upon the invitation of its President, in a meeting via teleconference on 30-04-2024, following a postponement on 26-04-2024, in order to examine the case referred to in the background of this document. Present at the meeting were the President of the Authority, Konstantinos Menoudakos, the regular members Charalampos Anthopoulos, Spyridon Vlachopoulos, Aikaterini Iliadou, Christos Kalloniatis, Konstantinos Lamprinoudakis, and Grigoris Tsolias, as the rapporteur. The Authority took into account the following:

With complaint No. G/EIS/9855/03-09-2022 against the Athens-Macedonian News Agency (AMNA S.A., hereinafter referred to as “AMNA”) and the Hellenic Film Center (“HFC”), A alleges unlawful processing of her personal data. Specifically, according to the complaint, on … the complainant was informed via email from the Independent Authority for Public Revenue (IAPR) that on … her details (her VAT number) were searched through the IAPR's online service “Search for Basic Business Registry Data” by AMNA, with which, as she states, she has no professional or economic relationship. Following this, the complainant states that she contacted AMNA requesting information regarding the aforementioned processing of her data, from which it appears that the said entity received basic data from her Tax Registry, without, as she claims, being provided with adequate explanations. Specifically, as detailed in the said complaint, the Legal Service of AMNA informed her that the search of her data was conducted “by mistake,” which, according to the complainant, could not have happened, since the search process includes a security safeguard, an additional step after entering the VAT number, during which the full name and patronymic corresponding to it appear, and the user is required to confirm the search in order to proceed with obtaining the tax registry information. Furthermore, the complainant stated that after her relevant complaint, she was informed that the person responsible for the search was … of AMNA, B, whose duties include access to taxisnet and the verification of VAT numbers related to contracts of AMNA. The same individual is also employed at the Hellenic Film Center (HFC) and, according to AMNA's response, has related responsibilities for investigating VAT numbers at HFC. However, the complainant argues that at the time of the search, only she held the taxisnet credentials of HFC, as Director …, while she had not disclosed her VAT number to any employee of HFC.

CONSIDERED IN ACCORDANCE WITH THE LAW

1. From the provisions of Articles 51 and 55 and Article 9 of Law 4624/2019 (Government Gazette A' 137), it follows that the Authority has the competence to supervise the application of the provisions of the GDPR, this law, and other regulations concerning the protection of individuals from the processing of personal data. According to the above findings and from the provisions of Articles 57 paragraph 1 subparagraph (f) of the GDPR and 13 paragraph 1 subparagraph (z) of Law 4624/2019, it follows that the Authority has the competence to address the complaint of A regarding the unlawful processing of her personal data and to exercise, correspondingly, the powers conferred upon it by the provisions of Articles 58 of the GDPR and 15 of Law 4624/2019.
2. With Article 5 paragraph 1 of the General Regulation (EU) 2016/679 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data (hereinafter GDPR), the principles that must govern a processing are established. According to Article 5 paragraph 1 (a) and (f) GDPR, “1. Personal data: (a) shall be processed lawfully and fairly in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject (‘lawfulness, fairness, and transparency’), […] (f) shall be processed in a manner that ensures the
13

appropriate security of personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and accidental loss, destruction, or damage, by using appropriate technical or organizational measures (“integrity and confidentiality”), while as noted in the Preamble of the Regulation, “Personal data should be processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security and confidentiality of personal data, including to prevent unauthorized access to such personal data and to the equipment used for processing them or the use of such personal data and that equipment” (Recital 39 in fine). Furthermore, according to the accountability principle expressly defined in the second paragraph of the same article and which constitutes a cornerstone of the GDPR, the data controller “is responsible for and is able to demonstrate compliance with paragraph 1 (“accountability”)”. This principle entails the obligation of the data controller to be able to demonstrate compliance with the principles of Article 5 paragraph 1.

3. According to Article 6 paragraph 1 of the GDPR “1. Processing shall be lawful only if and to the extent that at least one of the following applies: a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes, b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract, c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject, d) processing is necessary in order to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person, e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller, f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, especially if the data subject is a child.”

4. Regarding the adherence to the principle of transparency, Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR specifically define the information that must be provided to the data subject by the data controller, both in cases where the data is collected from the data subject (Article 13) and in cases where the data has not been collected from the data subject (Article 14). According to Article 14 paragraphs 1 and 2 of the GDPR, “1. When personal data have not been obtained from the data subject, the data controller shall provide the data subject with the following information: a) the identity and contact details of the data controller and, where applicable, of the representative of the data controller, b) the contact details of the data protection officer, where applicable, c) the purposes of the processing for which the personal data are intended, as well as the legal basis for the processing, d) the relevant categories of personal data, e) the recipients or categories of recipients of the personal data, if any, f) where applicable, that the data controller intends to transfer personal data to a recipient in a third country or an international organization and the existence or absence of an adequacy decision by the Commission or, when it comes to the transfers referred to in Article 46 or 47 or in Article 49 paragraph 1 second subparagraph, reference to the appropriate or suitable safeguards and the means to obtain a copy thereof or where they have been made available. 2. In addition to the information referred to in paragraph 1, the data controller shall provide the data subject with the following information that is necessary to ensure fair and transparent processing in relation to the data subject: a) the period for which the personal data will be stored or, if that is not possible, the criteria used to determine that period, b) whether the processing is based on Article 6 paragraph 1.

item (f), the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, (g) the existence of the right to request access to and rectification or erasure of personal data or restriction of processing concerning the data subject and the right to object to processing, as well as the right to data portability, (h) when processing is based on Article 6(1)(a) or Article 9(2)(a), the existence of the right to withdraw consent at any time, without affecting the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal, (i) the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority, (j) the source from which the personal data originate and, where applicable, whether the data originated from publicly accessible sources, (k) the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling, as provided in Article 22(1) and (4) and, at least in those cases, significant information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences of such processing for the data subject." According to paragraph 3 of the same article, "The data controller shall provide the information referred to in paragraphs 1 and 2: (a) within a reasonable period after the collection of personal data, but at the latest within one month, taking into account the specific circumstances under which the personal data are processed, […]", while according to paragraph 4 of this article, "When the data controller intends to further process personal data for a purpose other than that for which the personal data were collected, the data controller shall provide the data subject, prior to such further processing, information on that purpose and any other necessary information, as referred to in paragraph 2." Finally, paragraph 5 of Article 14 of the GDPR provides for exceptions to the obligation to inform the data subject, including in cases where "...(b) the provision of such information proves impossible or would involve a disproportionate effort […] or if the obligation referred to in paragraph 1 of this article is likely to make it impossible or seriously impair the achievement of the purposes of that processing. In such cases, the data controller shall take appropriate measures to protect the rights and freedoms and the legitimate interests of the data subject […]".

5. To ensure the principle of transparency of processing, Article 12(1) of the GDPR states: "The data controller shall take appropriate measures to provide the data subject with all information referred to in Articles 13 and 14 and any communication in the context of Articles 15 to 22 and Article 34 regarding processing in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language, particularly when the information is addressed specifically to children. The information shall be provided in writing or by other means, including, where appropriate, electronically. When requested by the data subject, the information may be provided orally, provided that the identity of the data subject is proven by other means." Furthermore, according to the Guidelines on Transparency (WP260 rev.01), when informing data subjects in accordance with Articles 13-14 of the GDPR, the information provided should be specific and definitive. As noted in §56 of those Guidelines: "In accordance with the principle of accountability, data controllers must demonstrate (and document) the information that the data subject already has, how and when it was received, and that no changes have occurred since then to that information that would render it outdated."

In Opinion 1/2020 of the Authority regarding the provisions of Law 4624/2019, the following is noted: "Articles 24-25-26 According to the explanatory report of the law, Articles 24, 25, and 26 provide or create a 'national legal basis' for the processing of personal data for a purpose different from that for which the data was originally collected. In order to justify this choice, the national legislator invokes for Article 24 the provision of Article 6 paragraph 3 GDPR, for Article 25 the provision of Article 6 paragraph 4 GDPR, and for Article 26 the 'explicit legal provision.' The subject matter of the provisions of Articles 24, 25, and 26 of the law is the further processing of personal data, that is, the case of processing for a purpose other than that for which the data was initially collected, only if the subsequent processing is compatible with the purposes of the initial collection, in accordance with the principle of purpose limitation under Article 5 paragraph 1 point b) GDPR. In this case, a separate legal basis from that which allowed the collection of the data is not required (see Article 6 paragraph 4 and Recital 50 GDPR). Furthermore, from the provisions of Article 6 paragraphs 2 and 3 GDPR, it follows that the national legislator is given the possibility to maintain or establish more specific provisions for the adaptation of the application of the rules regarding processing to comply with cases c) (legal obligation of the data controller) and e) (fulfillment of a duty to the public interest or in the exercise of public authority entrusted to the data controller) of paragraph 1 of Article 6 GDPR, specifying more precisely special requirements for processing and other measures to ensure lawful and fair processing. From the above, it follows that the GDPR does not authorize the national legislator to establish new 'national legal bases,' as mentioned in the explanatory report, but only to specify the legal bases of Article 6 paragraph 1 points c) and e) GDPR, under the conditions and guarantees provided by the provisions of paragraphs 2 and 4 of Article 6."

Furthermore, from the provision of Article 6(4) GDPR regarding further processing for purposes that are different but compatible with the purposes of the initial collection, it follows that the national legislator is not obliged to take implementing measures for further processing since the GDPR itself establishes the criteria under a) to e) of that paragraph 4. However, even if it is considered that the national legislator was authorized by the GDPR to establish "national legal bases" for the further processing of data under Article 6(4) GDPR, these should only constitute a specification of the legal bases of Article 6(1)(c) and (e) GDPR, must respect the principles of legality of Article 5(1) GDPR and particularly the purpose limitation principle under Article 5(1)(b) GDPR, must be a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society to ensure the purposes referred to in Article 23 with the provision of relevant substantive and procedural guarantees, must be legislatively formulated with clarity and precision, and their application must be predictable for individuals subject to them according to the case law of the CJEU and the ECHR (recital 41), and finally, according to recital 50(b) GDPR, in the event of highlighting potential criminal acts or threats to public safety by the data controller and the transmission of the relevant data to the competent authority, this transmission or further processing of personal data must be prohibited if the processing is not compatible with a legal, professional, or other binding obligation of confidentiality. The provisions of Articles 24, 25, and 26 of the law do not meet any of the above substantive and procedural prerequisites and guarantees, in accordance with what has been previously stated regarding Articles 5 and 22 of the law. It is noted that the provisions of Article 25, which provide for the processing of personal data by private entities as long as it is necessary, among other things, for the prevention of threats to national security or public safety (paragraph a) as well as for the prosecution of criminal offenses (paragraph b), despite their wording, from which interpretative doubts arise, cannot be considered as assigning to private entities responsibilities related to the above purposes, given that these responsibilities belong to the core of state authority and it is not constitutionally permissible to assign them to private individuals. It should be emphasized that similar conclusions were reached by the Portuguese DPA with its decision No. 494/2019 regarding the corresponding article of the national law 58/2019, finding a violation of the GDPR, while the Independent Authority for Public Revenue (AADE) in its letter dated 30-9-2019 to the Authority expresses the opinion that the restrictions imposed by Articles 24 and 26 of the law do not find support in the GDPR, that in this way it is deprived of exercising its powers, and that these provisions will also hinder the implementation of the objectives of Law 4623/2019 for digital governance.

In this case, from the examination of the file's elements and after the hearing process, the following were established: The EKK assigned the consultant, B, as the processor, the task of checking the adequacy of the files of the Directors of the EKK. In this context, the manner of conducting the audit was left to the discretion of the processor, and thus, the use of the AADE application for checking basic business registry elements was done on his own initiative, as a "means of processing of minor importance" according to the aforementioned Guidelines. The EKK, as the controller, determined the purpose (checking the adequacy of the Directors' files) as well as the essential means of processing, and therefore bears the responsibility for maintaining the fundamental principle of legality, objectivity, and transparency of the processing concerning this processing. However, in violation of the accountability principle (Article 5(2) of the GDPR), the EKK did not sufficiently document: a) the necessity of the processing, as it did not emerge that the complainant was asked for information which she refused to provide, while the Board of Directors' decision cited by the EKK was made on …, that is, at a time subsequent to the search for the complainant's data through the AADE Platform (…), b) the legal basis for the processing, given that, in response to a related question from the Authority, the EKK invoked multiple legal bases from Article 6(1) of the GDPR, while subsequently, although clarification was requested, it did not specify the legal basis for processing, and c) the transparency of the processing, as it did not emerge that the complainant was informed with all the necessary information required by Article 13 of the GDPR, nor was it established that any case of application of an exception from the notification based on the provisions of Law 4624/2019 cited by the EKK existed. Specifically, the EKK did not substantiate the claim that informing the complainant would hinder the establishment, exercise, or support of its claims or would jeopardize the proper performance of its duties. Based on the above, the controller did not sufficiently document the legality, fairness, and transparency of the processing, as it was obliged to do, resulting in a violation of the provisions set forth in Article 5(1)(a) in conjunction with Article 6(1) of the GDPR. Considering that the violation concerns one (1) data subject and simple personal data, to which the controller had the right of access as the employer of the subject, the Authority assesses the violation as minor (see relevant Recital 148 of the GDPR). For this violation, the Authority deems it appropriate to issue, pursuant to Article 58(2)(b) of the GDPR, a reprimand to the EKK, as the data controller.

Furthermore, from the examination of the case, it emerged that in the context of conducting the completeness check of the files following a relevant order from the EKK as the data controller, the processor, B, mistakenly used the credentials of a third party on the AADE platform, specifically that of the AMPE-MPE with which he was also collaborating. This fact did not result in the transmission of the complainant's data to AMPE-MPE, as no extraction of information concerning her was made in the systems of AMPE-MPE, nor did the information generated by the search remain available in the AMPE-MPE account on the platform. It is therefore established that the processor executed the processing inadequately, using the credentials of a third legal entity with which the subject is not connected, resulting in the creation of...
