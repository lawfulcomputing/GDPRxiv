CONSIDERED IN ACCORDANCE WITH THE LAW

1. From the provisions of Articles 51 and 55 of the GDPR and Article 9 of Law 4624/2019 (Government Gazette A' 137), it follows that the Authority has the competence to supervise the application of the provisions of the GDPR, Law 4624/2019, and other regulations concerning the protection of individuals from the processing of personal data.
2. According to Article 15 paragraphs 1 and 3 of the GDPR: “1. The data subject has the right to obtain from the data controller confirmation as to whether or not personal data concerning them is being processed and, if that is the case, the right of access to the personal data and to the following information: a) the purposes of the processing, b) the relevant categories of personal data, c) the recipients or categories of recipients to whom the personal data have been or will be disclosed, in particular, recipients in third countries or international organizations, d) where possible, the period for which the personal data will be stored or, if not possible, the criteria used to determine that period, [..], g) where the personal data are not collected from the data subject, any available information as to their source, h) the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling, as referred to in Article 22 paragraphs 1 and 4 and, at least in those cases, significant information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences of such processing for the data subject. 2. When personal data are transferred to a third country or to an international organization, the data subject has the right to be informed of the appropriate safeguards pursuant to Article 46 regarding the transfer. 3. The data controller shall provide a copy of the personal data undergoing processing. […] If the data subject submits the request by electronic means and unless the data subject requests otherwise, the information shall be provided in a commonly used electronic format.” 4. The right to obtain a copy referred to in paragraph 3 shall not adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others.”

Furthermore, according to Article 12 of the GDPR: “1. The data controller shall take appropriate measures to provide the data subject […] with any communication in the context of Articles 15 […] 2. The data controller facilitates the exercise of the rights of data subjects as provided in Articles 15 […]. 

According to the EDPB Guidelines 1/2022 on the Right of Access, in the context of a request for access under Article 15 of the GDPR, any information related to the processing being carried out that is available to the data controller may need to be tailored and individualized concerning the specific processing activities carried out regarding the data subject exercising the right of access at that time. Therefore, a reference to the wording of the Privacy Policy does not always, and without exception, constitute a sufficient means of providing information in accordance with the requirements of Article 15 paragraph 1 points a) to h) and paragraph 2 of the GDPR. 

The CJEU recently reached the same conclusion, ruling that, unlike Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR, which establish an obligation for the data controller to provide the data subject with information related to the categories of recipients or the specific recipients of the data concerning them, whenever that data is collected from the data subject or not collected from them, Article 15 of the GDPR establishes a genuine right of access.
13

In favor of the data subject, resulting in the latter needing to have the ability to obtain from the data controller information regarding the specific recipients to whom the data has been disclosed or is to be disclosed, or, alternatively, to choose to request information only regarding the categories of recipients. Taking the above into account, it emerges that the right of access of the data subject to the personal data concerning them is established, primarily aimed at ensuring the data subject of the accuracy and lawful nature of the processing of their data and enabling them to exercise the remaining rights guaranteed by the GDPR for the benefit of the data subjects (Articles 16 to 19, 21, 79, and 82 of the GDPR). Therefore, the information provided to the data subject based on the right of access must be as accurate as possible. In the case under consideration, regarding the personal data held by the data controller and which were the subject of the exercised right of access, the data controller informed the complainant about the retained identification data, registry details, mobile phone connection details, device data, and information regarding the consents provided by them for the use of their personal data for promotional purposes. For more detailed information regarding the purposes of processing, the recipients of their personal data, specific categories of retained data, and personal data collected during the use of the "My Vodafone" application, they were referred to the posted Privacy and Personal Data Protection Policy on the website and in the "My Vodafone" application, where, however, the detailed information requested by the complainant is not provided with the required specificity. Consequently, based on the above, it emerges that regarding the said information, the data controller did not adequately satisfy the complainant's right of access under Article 15, paragraph 1, 3 in conjunction with Article 12, paragraph 2 of the GDPR.

3. The Constitution states in Article 19, paragraph 1 that "the confidentiality of letters and free correspondence or communication in any other way is absolutely inviolable. Law defines the guarantees under which the judicial authority is not bound by confidentiality for reasons of national security or for the investigation of particularly serious crimes." It is consistently accepted by the case law of the CJEU, the ECtHR, and the Council of State that the concept of confidentiality encompasses the entirety of the communication event, that is, both the content and the external elements of the communication, namely, in relation to electronic communications, and the related generated traffic and location data or metadata. The protection of the external elements of communication is also provided by Directive 2002/58/EC and the declared invalid by the CJEU Directive 2006/24, which have been incorporated into Greek law (by Laws 3471/2006 and 3917/2011 respectively). Moreover, there is no violation of the confidentiality of communication by the communicating party, given that confidentiality does not exist against the entity it protects; rather, the prohibition of violations is directed at third parties. Consequently, there is no impediment, based on Article 19 of the Constitution, for the party called upon to request the disclosure of the external elements of the communication of which they are a part.

Law 3917/2011, the provisions of which are invoked by the respondent, incorporated into the Greek legal order Directive 2006/24/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council on the retention of data generated or processed in connection with the provision of publicly available electronic communications services or public communications networks (known as the Data Retention Directive). This Directive aimed at the retention and access to traffic and location data of communications of all users and subscribers of fixed and mobile telephony for the purposes of investigating serious crimes and combating terrorism and organized crime. 

However, the CJEU, in its decision on the case Digital Rights Ireland Ltd. et al., declared Directive 2006/24/EC invalid, ruling that a general interest purpose, such as combating serious crime and particularly organized crime and terrorism, however fundamental it may be, cannot, by itself, be proposed as a justification for the preventive, mandatory retention and non-deletion by providers of communication data for all citizens of the EU, without the existence of some element that directly or indirectly links them to suspicions of committing criminal acts, as this would violate the principle of proportionality in light of Articles 7, 8, and 52(1) of the Charter, which respectively guarantee the protection of private and family life and personal data and set the conditions for the lawful restriction of rights protected by the Charter. This decision resulted in several EU countries having subsequently abolished their corresponding national legislation, making preventive surveillance of electronic communications impossible. In Greece, however, Law 3917/2011 continues to be in force, while the relevant legislative committee of the Ministry of Justice, which was established in 2014 to repeal or amend the existing national legislation in light of the CJEU's decision in Digital Rights Ltd. et al., has suspended its work.

Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council concerning the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (known as the e-Privacy Directive), which was incorporated into the Greek legal order by Law 3471/2006, aims to ensure full respect for the rights guaranteed in Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter and for this purpose establishes in Article 5(1) the principle of confidentiality of electronic communications and related traffic data and entails the prohibition of storage or any other form of monitoring or surveillance of the aforementioned data by third parties (especially public Authorities) other than the users, without their consent. Regarding specifically the storage of traffic data by providers, Article 6 of Directive 2002/58 states that this is permitted only to the extent and for as long as necessary for the commercial promotion of services, their billing, and the provision of value-added services. Upon the expiration of this period, the stored data must be deleted or rendered anonymous. A similar regulation applies to the provisions of Article 9 of the aforementioned Directive concerning location data.

The conditions for the lawful restriction of the rights of data subjects protected under the GDPR are defined in Article 23 of the GDPR, which provides for the possibility of restricting these rights by legislative measure for the purpose of serving important objectives of general public interest mentioned in the same article, provided that the imposed restrictions respect the essence of fundamental rights and freedoms and constitute a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society. This condition set forth in the above article corresponds to the criteria established by the case law of the CJEU and the ECtHR for the restriction of the rights protected by the GDPR, including the right of access. Regarding restrictions on the right of access, Article 33(1)(b) of Law 4624/2019 provides that “[...] the right of access of the data subject, according to Article 15 of the GDPR, does not apply when [...] b) the data aa) were recorded solely because they cannot be deleted due to legal or regulatory provisions requiring their retention or bb) serve exclusively purposes of data protection or control, and providing information would require a disproportionate effort and the necessary technical and organizational measures make processing for other purposes impossible.”

Regarding the access of the data subjects/subscribers to their incoming calls, the entity in question claims, as a prerequisite that must be met in order to provide the requested data subject access to the incoming calls they have received, the technical capability of its information system to distinguish between calls made with caller line identification suppression and those made with the caller line identification displayed, which essentially refers to the application of the provision of Article 15 para. 4 of the GDPR, according to which "the right to obtain a copy referred to in paragraph 3 shall not adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others." The reservation contained in this provision regarding the safeguarding of the rights and freedoms of third parties does not prevent the provision of any information to the data subject (see Recital 63 GDPR), but the fulfillment of the conditions for application must be assessed on a case-by-case basis (in concreto) by the data controller (see Recital 63 GDPR), who is obliged, pursuant to Article 12 para. 1 in conjunction with Article 24 para. 1 GDPR, to take appropriate measures to provide the data subject with all possible information within the framework of Articles 15 to 22 and Article 34 of the GDPR. Consequently, in order to restrict the right to obtain a copy of the data subject by invoking the provision of Article 15 para. 4 GDPR, it is not sufficient to have a general concern that the rights and freedoms of others may be affected by the response to the access request, but the data controller must be able to demonstrate that the satisfaction of the right in the specific case would adversely affect the rights and freedoms of a specific other person. 

In particular, when the assessment under Article 15 para. 4 GDPR demonstrates that the response to the request has negative implications for the rights and freedoms of other parties involved, their interests must be weighed considering the specific circumstances of the case and, in particular, the likelihood and severity of the risks posed by the disclosure of the data. The data controller should seek to reconcile the conflicting rights, for example, by implementing appropriate measures to mitigate the risk to the rights and freedoms of others. As emphasized in Recital 63 of the GDPR, the protection of the rights and freedoms of others under Article 15 para. 4 GDPR should not result in the denial of the provision of any information to the data subject. This could mean, in the case of applying the restriction, that information concerning others should be rendered unreadable to the extent possible rather than a total denial of providing a copy of the personal data. However, if it is impossible to find a solution for reconciling the relevant rights, the data controller must decide which of the conflicting rights and freedoms prevail.
