EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This document aims to guide data controllers in complying with their notification obligations to the competent Control Authorities regarding personal data breaches and the communication to individuals affected by such breaches. This guide updates the one previously published by the AEPD in June 2018, simultaneously with the entry into force of the GDPR, which aimed to provide a tool to assist controllers in fulfilling their obligations concerning personal data breaches. This new version includes the experience gathered in the first years of applying the obligations set forth in Articles 33 and 34 of the GDPR, both at the national level and in relation to the criteria established by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB). The main purpose of this update is to enable effective and efficient compliance with the ultimate objectives of notifying personal data breaches. These are: the effective protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, the creation of a more resilient environment based on knowledge of vulnerabilities in processing, and the assurance of legal certainty by providing controllers with a means to demonstrate due diligence. This guide is intended to provide general guidelines on the notification of personal data breaches and communication to data subjects, specifying deadlines and specific aspects regarding the procedure for notification and the content of notifications. The information provided allows the controller to accurately understand the scope of their obligations and facilitate compliance. The guide focuses on cases where the breach has or may have an impact within the scope of the GDPR, that is, in those cases where the personal data breach may affect the fundamental rights and freedoms of individuals. The final section highlights specific issues regarding the notification of personal data breaches in accordance with the General Telecommunications Law.

Keywords: GDPR, LOPDGDD, notification, communication, affected parties, breach, headquarters, form, applicant, DPO, controller, breach, security.

I. INTRODUCTION  
Regulation (EU) 2016/679, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) establishes in its Article 33 the obligation to notify personal data breaches that may pose a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons to the competent Supervisory Authority. In the case of Spain, the Supervisory Authority to which notification must be made is the Spanish Data Protection Agency (AEPD), both for the private and public sectors, with the exception of public bodies1 of the Autonomous Communities where there is an Autonomous Control Authority.  
Likewise, Article 34 of the GDPR establishes the obligation of the data controller to communicate personal data breaches to the affected individuals when it is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms.  
In the original English version of the GDPR, as well as in the guidelines of the European Data Protection Board, the expression used is "personal data breach"; however, the Spanish version uses "violación de la seguridad de los datos personales." Throughout this guide, the expression "brecha de datos personales" will be primarily used and occasionally simply "brecha"2. Nevertheless, the expressions "violación de la seguridad de los datos personales," "brecha de seguridad de los datos personales," "brecha de seguridad," "quiebra de seguridad," and "quiebra de seguridad de los datos personales" used in other texts and procedures, some prior to the GDPR, should be understood with the same meaning.  
In June 2018, the AEPD published the "Guide for the Management and Notification of Security Breaches" developed in collaboration with various institutions. It was a pioneering instrument in Europe aimed at assisting controllers and processors in complying with their new obligations regarding personal data breaches. This guide quickly became a useful reference among controllers and processors throughout the European Union.  
After several years of applying the GDPR, it is advisable to leverage the experience gained by the AEPD, other Supervisory Authorities, and the European Data Protection Board to renew the guide and thus provide controllers and processors with more precise guidelines that further facilitate and simplify compliance with the obligations established in Articles 33 and 34, related to the management and notification of personal data breaches.  
In this update, it is also intended to specify some deadlines that the GDPR leaves open, such as the deadlines for notifying a personal data breach gradually to the Supervisory Authority, deadlines for communicating a personal data breach to the affected individuals, or deadlines for processors to inform controllers about a breach. The text will specify the scope, content, and deadlines for notifications by the Supervisory Authority, which will allow optimizing the resources that data controllers must dedicate to those notifications.  
The main purpose of updating this guide is to enable more effective and efficient compliance with the ultimate objectives of notifying personal data breaches. These are: the effective protection of the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals through the

Articles 33 and 34 of the GDPR outline the necessity for organizations to integrate within their information policies a process for managing personal data breaches that specifies how the organization will comply with its obligations regarding breaches. This breach management process would complement the organization's incident management process.  
In this way, the breach management process adds to the existing information policies within an organization and is a necessary part of maintaining the activity of any entity. This process constitutes one of the most important organizational measures when it comes to safeguarding the rights and freedoms of data subjects through security measures in processing.  
Any organization that processes personal data is exposed to suffering a personal data breach that may impact the rights and freedoms of individuals, and therefore is obliged to anticipate and manage them appropriately.  
Analogous to the GDPR, Directive (EU) 2016/680 regarding the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses or executing criminal sanctions, and the free movement of such data, establishes in its Articles 30 and 31 the conditions for notifying a personal data breach to the Supervisory Authority and to the affected individuals.  
Notifications of personal data breaches to the Supervisory Authority are part of the proactive responsibility of controllers, or processors in their case, demonstrating diligence in data processing. The notification of breaches made in accordance with the GDPR does not necessarily imply the imposition of a sanction. On the contrary, a timely and proper notification and communication, in the event that the Supervisory Authority initiates preliminary investigation actions, is evidence of the organization's diligence in effectively executing the proactive responsibility obligation required by the GDPR. However, failure to comply with the obligations of notification and communication to the affected individuals is classified as an infringement.  
The examples included in this guide are limited to the specific circumstances and situations described in each case, and should be understood in that sense as examples to clarify specific concepts and should be considered as such. These examples do not constitute, in any case, rules of general application that can be used in any circumstance.  
In no case does a notification of a personal data breach serve as a channel for filing claims against a natural or legal person, nor will they be considered complaints. The notification of personal data breaches is a task and an obligation of the data controller.

II. PERSONAL DATA BREACHES  
A.  
WHAT IS A PERSONAL DATA BREACH? WHAT IS NOT?  
The GDPR broadly defines "personal data breaches" as "all security violations that result in the destruction, loss, or accidental or unlawful alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data."  
Incidents that will not be considered personal data breaches subject to Articles 33 and 34 of the GDPR are those that:  
•  
Do not affect personal data, that is, data that does not pertain to identified or identifiable natural persons.  
•  
Do not affect personal data processing carried out by a controller or a processor.  
•  
Occur in processing carried out by an individual in a domestic context.  
Therefore, not all security incidents are necessarily personal data breaches, and not only cyber incidents can be personal data breaches. Furthermore, not every action that constitutes a violation of data protection regulations can be considered a personal data breach.  
For example, merely receiving emails with malware or suspected malware without executing it, detecting a system infected with a virus, or experiencing an attempted cyberattack that does not materialize cannot be considered a personal data breach in itself when it cannot produce consequences for the rights and freedoms of individuals. However, they must be managed as security incidents, including the need to determine whether they have affected personal data. Based on the principle of proactive responsibility, in the event of any occurrence that may have consequences for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, the data controller must react and mitigate such consequences.  
In the Guidelines 01/2021 on examples related to the notification of personal data breaches adopted by the European Data Protection Board on January 14, 2021, some examples of personal data breaches can be found.  

A security incident that has not affected personal data or personal data processing is not a personal data breach, as it could not cause harm to the rights and freedoms of the natural persons whose data is subject to processing, regardless of other harm it may cause to the controller or processor of the data.  
B.  
INCIDENT MANAGEMENT PROCESS  
In any processing, the risk that a personal data breach may pose to the rights and freedoms must be determined, that is, an illegitimate or accidental processing of the data. This is a task that must be carried out prior to the materialization of a
Page: 9 of 48  
personal data breach, and is part of the organization's preparation to address any breaches that may occur.  
Once the level of risk has been established, and even if it is low, measures must be put in place to minimize that risk, as outlined in Articles 24 (e.g., data protection policies), 25 (data protection measures by default and by design), 32 (security measures), and 35 (impact assessments for data protection), among others. The GDPR provides for both preventive measures to avoid or reduce risk and corrective measures to respond to the realization of the risk.  
In particular, Article 32.1 specifically enumerates a non-exhaustive set of security measures that could be considered to manage risk through security measures in processing, such as:  
• Measures aimed at ensuring confidentiality, integrity, and availability  
• Measures to ensure the resilience of processing systems and services, and to provide the capacity to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident  
• Pseudonymization and encryption of personal data  
• Regular verification, evaluation, and assessment processes of security measures.  
From this set of measures, the need to assess the impact of an incident on personal data arises, regardless of whether the processing is carried out automatically or manually, or whether the incidents are accidental, either human or associated with natural events.  
Furthermore, reference is made to the need to manage potential errors, weaknesses, vulnerabilities, or attacks that may arise from the various technical and organizational measures that implement data protection strategies by default, by design, or other guarantees (the systems of guarantees) such as:  
• The pseudonymization and encryption of personal data already mentioned  
• Anonymization processes  
• Data unlinking processes  
• Execution of data deletion  
• Federated processing  
• Preference panels  
Incident management is a process that, to a greater or lesser extent of maturity, should already be part of the culture of those responsible for and in charge of processing. This incident management must be updated, if it is not already, and incorporate the procedures to respond to the obligations arising from the GDPR. Specifically, for the case of this guide, from Articles 33 and 34 regarding the notification of the breach to the Supervisory Authority and communication to the affected individuals.  
The data controller must be diligent in implementing measures for the detection of an incident and its classification as a personal data breach. These measures could include procedures, resources, and means of detection and management, whether internal or through third parties, as well as guarantees that the aforementioned operate correctly. The measures must allow for a prompt response to the personal data breach and assess the risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. Data processors must promptly inform the controllers of any breaches they experience so that they can assess the risk and fulfill their obligations.  
Once the personal data breach has been detected and assessed, the resolution process must document all the information being collected. This documentation will be attached to the incident register that the data controllers must maintain. Information regarding the decisions made about notifying the competent authority and communicating with the affected individuals (including a copy of the communication if it occurs) must also be recorded in this register in detail.  
There is no standard model for incident registration. Each organization must use the one it deems most appropriate and that integrates into its management systems. In any case, through the FACILITA-EMPRENDE tool, available on the website of this Agency, a model incident register for companies within the scope of the tool can be obtained, which can be extended to other organizations.  
As part of the incident management process, a procedure for notifying personal data breaches must be incorporated that specifies all the fundamental aspects necessary for the correct application of the GDPR. For example, it must define which Supervisory Authority should be notified, what events will trigger the execution of the procedure, who should make the notification to the Supervisory Authority, provide the necessary technical or other means for notification, ensure compliance with deadlines, and, if applicable, establish the authorization procedure required for notification according to the instructions of the data controller.  
Similarly, a procedure must be established for communication to the affected individuals that specifies aspects such as who will carry out the communication, how the affected individuals will be informed, the channels and means through which the communication will be made, and generally the details that allow for effective communication.

C.  
PARTIES INVOLVED  
Once a personal data breach is detected in the organization, and for the purpose of proper and effective management, the collaboration and action of different parties will be necessary. For each of the involved individuals to act effectively, the procedures and necessary means must have been established in advance.  
Below, the functions and responsibilities of the involved parties are briefly outlined:  
Data Controller6: it is their responsibility to apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the GDPR. If applicable, they must ensure that the personal data breach is reported to the competent authority without undue delay, and also that the personal data breach is communicated to the affected individuals when necessary.  
The data controller must seek the advice of the data protection officer when one has been appointed, or, in their absence, may rely on the advice of internal or external teams with expertise in data protection.  
They may also seek the advice of security experts, such as the CISO7 of the organization, or the IT services they have in-house or may have subcontracted. Likewise, they may delegate the management of the personal data breach to data processors, such as external IT services.  
The controller may delegate to the processor the management of the personal data breach, both regarding the response and the notification, documenting such delegation of functions in the context of the established contractual relationship. However, the controller must ensure that appropriate response, notification, and communication actions are being taken, as the delegation of functions does not imply delegation of responsibility.  
Data Processor8: it is their responsibility to inform the data controller without undue delay of personal data breaches affecting the processing entrusted to them, without prejudice to any additional obligations they may have acquired under the data processing agreement.  
Although the GDPR does not specify a specific timeframe for processors to inform controllers, it does indicate that the information must be sent without undue delay.  
The data processor has the obligation to assist the controller in ensuring compliance with the obligations established in the GDPR, including the management, notification, and communication of personal data breaches.

Data Protection Officer (DPO)9:  
In cases where a DPO has been appointed (either because required by the GDPR or decided by the controller), they will play a very relevant role in the breach management process. The GDPR assigns the DPO the function of informing and advising the controller or processor on the obligations that pertain to them, including those related to the management and notification of personal data breaches, as well as cooperating with the Supervisory Authority and acting as a point of contact for the Supervisory Authority on matters related to processing.  
The DPO must therefore inform and advise the controller/processor regarding:  
• the implementation of a personal data breach management process within the organization,  
• the assessment of the risk and the consequences that a personal data breach may pose to the rights and freedoms of individuals,  
• the appropriate actions that should be taken to mitigate the effects of the personal data breach on the affected individuals,  
• the necessity to notify the personal data breach to the Supervisory Authority and, if applicable, to the affected individuals,  
• in the case of processors, the necessity to notify the personal data breach to the controller.  
The DPO will act as a point of contact with the Supervisory Authority in the notification process by the controller of personal data breaches, as well as the responses to requests made by that Authority regarding the same, always in accordance with the breach management process implemented in the organization.  
The data controller, and the processor if applicable, must provide the DPO with the necessary means and information for the exercise of their functions.  
However, the responsibility inevitably lies with the controller and processor regarding the obligations of each of them.

IV.  
NOTIFICATION TO THE SUPERVISORY AUTHORITY  
Regardless of the need to notify the Supervisory Authority about a personal data breach, Article 33.5 of the GDPR establishes the obligation for the data controller to document any breach, including the facts related to the breach, its effects, and the corrective measures taken.  
A.  
WHEN TO NOTIFY  
According to Article 33 of the GDPR, as soon as the data controller becomes aware that a personal data breach has occurred, they must notify the competent Supervisory Authority, when it is likely that the breach poses a risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. If applicable, this must be done without undue delay and no later than 72 hours following the breach, including hours that occur during weekends and holidays.  
The criterion for determining whether an incident has resulted in "a personal data breach" is outlined in the GDPR itself: "a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration of, or unauthorized disclosure of, or access to, personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed."  
It is not mandatory to notify all personal data breaches, as the GDPR provides an exception to this obligation when, in accordance with the principle of proactive responsibility, the controller can ensure that it is unlikely that the personal data breach poses a risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals.  

In Annex B of the WP250 guidelines, some examples can be found regarding the assessment of the need to notify the Supervisory Authority. The Guidelines 01/2021 on examples related to the notification of personal data breaches provide a very comprehensive collection of examples.

If the personal data breach is detected by the data processor, they must send the data controller all the necessary information so that the controller can fulfill their obligations in a timely manner. The controller must document the breach and assess both the need to notify the Supervisory Authority and the need to communicate with the affected individuals. The processor may carry out the notification of the personal data breach on behalf of the involved controllers when stipulated in a contract or legal link.  
When the personal data breach poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals, in addition to notifying the Supervisory Authority, the breach must be communicated to the affected individuals without undue delay, except in certain cases, outlined and specified in this guide. The language used will be clear and simple, in a concise and transparent manner. More details on this obligation can be found in section VI “Communication of a personal data breach to the data subjects.”  
The notification of a personal data breach to the Supervisory Authority in accordance with Article 33 of the GDPR is not only an obligation but also an exercise of proactive responsibility. Conversely, if the intention is to report or claim a possible violation of data protection regulations by a third party, employee, former employee, or others, or to communicate a personal data breach of which one has been aware or affected, the channel to be used is the complaint submission form on the Electronic Headquarters of the Agency.  

The determining parameter for notifying a personal data breach to the Supervisory Authority or communicating it to the affected individuals is the level of risk. Not just any type of risk or a risk to the organization, but specifically the risk to the rights and freedoms of the natural persons affected by the breach.  
B.  
DEADLINES FOR NOTIFICATION  
The GDPR establishes that the data controller will notify the Supervisory Authority of personal data breaches without undue delay and no later than 72 hours after becoming aware of the personal data breach.  
The 72-hour period begins to be calculated from the moment the data controller becomes aware that the security incident has affected personal data, including the hours that pass during weekends and public holidays. It is the responsibility of the data processor to notify the data controller without undue delay of the personal data breaches of which they are aware. For the notification from the processor to the controller, the GDPR does not establish a specific time frame and merely indicates that it must be done without undue delay.  
To ensure that there is no undue delay in the notification from the processor to the controller, the procedures for managing personal data breaches must be in place.

C.

• When their only establishment is located in Spain.

• If they do not have a main establishment in the European Union, only if they have designated a representative in Spain.  
• If they have no establishment or representative in the European Union, in the case that the personal data breach affects individuals in Spain.

V.  
COMMUNICATION TO AFFECTED PARTIES  
The affected parties are the individuals whose personal data has been impacted by a breach compromising the confidentiality, integrity, and/or availability of that data, and who may suffer the consequences.  
In any case, the personal data breach management process established within the organization must include a procedure for communicating the personal data breach to the affected parties, specifying the information contained in the following sections, including establishing appropriate specific deadlines.  
A.  
WHEN TO COMMUNICATE  
Article 34 of the GDPR states that when it is likely that the personal data breach poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals, the data controller shall communicate the personal data breach to the affected parties without undue delay.  
Therefore, as soon as the data controller becomes aware of the personal data breach, they must assess the risk to the affected individuals and determine the necessity of communicating the breach to the affected parties. If the risk is determined to be high, the communication to the affected parties must be made as soon as possible.  
There are various factors to consider when deciding whether to communicate to the affected individuals:  
• What are the legal and contractual obligations.  
• What risks the loss of confidentiality, integrity, or availability of their personal data, the services associated with such personal data, as well as the compromise of the identity or identification of the affected parties poses to the rights and freedoms of individuals. In particular, the harm to their fundamental rights, physical damage, reputational damage, fraud, etc.  
• To what extent the damages incurred will be irreversible, whether immediate damages can be avoided or mitigated, and the potential subsequent harm.  
Communication to the affected parties will not be necessary when:  
• The controller has taken appropriate technical and organizational measures that prevent the aforementioned risks, minimize harm to rights and freedoms, and/or make them reversible.  
• The controller has taken protective measures after the personal data breach that mitigate, in whole or in part, the possible impact on the affected parties and ensure that there is no longer a possibility of the high risk to their rights and freedoms materializing. For example, by identifying and immediately implementing measures such as the revocation, cancellation, or blocking of compromised access credentials or digital certificates, or by restoring services and data backups in such a way that other personal data cannot be compromised.  
The Communicate-Breach GDPR tool provides assistance to data controllers in decision-making regarding the obligation to communicate a data breach.

The GDPR does not establish a specific deadline for communication to the affected individuals, but it does indicate that it must be done without undue delay.  
Any delay in communication to the affected individuals reduces its effectiveness, so a late communication may have the same effect as a communication that was not made. Therefore, any delay in the immediate communication to the interested parties when necessary must be justified.  
In particular, if after the corresponding analysis it is concluded that it is necessary to communicate to the interested parties, but it is anticipated that the communication to the interested parties may compromise the outcome of an ongoing investigation, the communication may be postponed under the supervision of the Supervisory Authority.  
When the communication to the affected individuals occurs as a result of an order issued by the Spanish Data Protection Agency, the communication to the affected individuals must be carried out without undue delay, and confirmation of having executed the order must be communicated within 30 days, unless a different deadline is indicated in the order.

An incomplete communication (lacking the minimum content), difficult to access, or made to the wrong individuals is not effective, so a communication under these conditions could be considered as not having been made.

D.  
TYPOLOGY  
One of the most important parameters when assessing the risk level of a personal data breach is to accurately determine its typology, that is, to determine which dimension(s) of personal data security have been affected by the breach. These dimensions are confidentiality, availability, and integrity. It is important to consider that a single personal data breach can affect more than one dimension depending on the particular circumstances in each case.  
Confidentiality: A breach affects confidentiality when personal data from a processing activity has been accessed by third parties without permission, including when data is exfiltrated. This includes, for example, cases of intrusion into an information system with access to and/or exfiltration of personal data, the erroneous sending of personal data, the loss of devices or documentation containing personal data, ransomware malware with data exfiltration, etc.  
It is important to know whether the affected personal data was (totally or partially) securely encrypted, anonymized, or protected in such a way that it is unintelligible to anyone who has accessed that data or may access it in the future. If so, the consequences of the confidentiality breach are largely mitigated, reducing or even nullifying the risks arising from the incident.  
Example: In confidentiality breaches caused by the loss or theft of mobile devices whose storage elements are encrypted with a non-compromised algorithm and access to the device is protected by a strong and hard-to-guess password, it can be considered that the risks associated with the loss of confidentiality of the data are appropriately mitigated.  
Example: In confidentiality breaches caused by the exfiltration of a user database file containing username, password, contact data, and address.  
• If the users' passwords are protected with a cryptographically secure hash algorithm, making them unintelligible to anyone who has accessed the database, the risk would be partially mitigated. If the hash algorithm is not considered cryptographically secure (md5, sha1, …), the risk mitigation is not effective.  
• If the exfiltrated database file was fully encrypted using a cryptographically secure algorithm and the encryption key is not compromised, the risk is mitigated to the extent that in some cases it can be considered practically null.  
Availability: A breach affects the availability of personal data when it has been temporarily or permanently inaccessible to those who legitimately need to process or access it. This situation can occur due to events affecting the personal data themselves or also due to events affecting the systems used.

for its processing. For example, it includes cases of encryption of personal data or information systems caused by ransomware-type malware, loss of paper documentation containing personal data, or the inability to access data storage (physical or logical access).  
For the data controller, it is important to determine whether availability has been restored or is in the process of being restored, as recovering data and processing systems is the way to mitigate the damage that such personal data breaches can cause. To this end, data controllers must establish strategies and procedures for recovery in such situations, including backup procedures, incident recovery, and data governance strategies.  
Example: In availability breaches caused by ransomware-type malware where the data controller can confidently rule out data exfiltration and personal data and processing means can be restored without significantly affecting the services provided, it can be considered that the risk has been adequately mitigated. In the event that the recovery of data and/or processing takes a prolonged period affecting the services provided significantly, for example, due to the absence or malfunction of data backup systems and processes, it can be concluded that the risk has not only not been mitigated but is materializing and causing various harms to the affected individuals.  
Example: In availability breaches caused by the accidental loss or destruction of personal data, the risk will be considered mitigated when there is a recovery plan that includes an updated and recoverable copy of the data and the service can be restored without causing harm to the affected individuals.  
Integrity: A breach affects integrity when personal data has been unlawfully altered and the processing of that personal data can cause harm to the affected individuals. For example, a third party has modified the information related to employees' banking data in the organization's database that is used for payroll payments, or a student modifies grades in the database of an educational institution.  
When breaches of personal data integrity occur, the controller must determine whether the processing of the unlawfully altered data can cause or has caused any harm to the affected individuals and, if applicable, whether the harm can be reversed.  
Example: To mitigate integrity breaches caused by file modifications, the controller can implement integrity control tools for the files that are based on calculating the hash of each monitored file, and when it is modified, even if it is just one bit of any of these files, the system periodically recalculates the hash of each one and, by comparing it, will detect the modification and issue a warning.  
Example: Controllers can mitigate the risk of an integrity breach in databases by having access controls, alerts, and logs for modifications. Additionally, by implementing systems that continuously audit read and write access to these databases.

If it is determined that the risk of a personal data breach is, for example, high for employees but low for clients, the data controller may choose to communicate the personal data breach in accordance with Article 34 of the GDPR only to its employees, as they are the ones who may suffer the consequences with high severity.  
Example: suppose there is a cyber incident in which the access credentials of an employee have been compromised. This has allowed, in addition to access to the employee's data, the deletion of basic information of a dozen clients which, however, is recoverable from a backup copy. The controller is faced with a personal data breach that affects the availability29 of basic data of clients and the confidentiality of the employee's data. The risks are different for each of these profiles and a different response must be given to each of them.

The data controller must determine, at least approximately, the number of individuals whose personal data has been affected by the personal data breach. It is necessary to indicate a number greater than 0.  

The number of affected individuals refers to the number of natural persons whose rights or freedoms could be harmed as a result of a personal data breach, for example, due to unlawful or unauthorized processing of their personal data, the inability to access a service, or ultimately the loss of control over their personal data.  
Legal entities (or other organizations) that may also have been affected do not count, as the concept of personal data pertains exclusively to natural persons.  
If you are certain that the personal data of a processing activity may have been affected, but do not know the exact number of affected individuals, you should indicate approximately or, as a last resort, the total number of individuals whose data is being processed.  
F.  
CONSEQUENCES  
Recital 85 of the GDPR indicates that personal data breaches may entail physical, material, or immaterial harm to natural persons, such as loss of control over their personal data or restriction of their rights, discrimination, identity theft, financial losses, unauthorized reversal of pseudonymization, damage to reputation, loss of confidentiality of data subject to professional secrecy, or any other significant economic or social detriment to the individual in question.  
When a personal data breach occurs, it is necessary for the data controller to rigorously determine what the possible consequences are and how they may affect the individuals involved.

With this data, the controller will be able to determine the level of risk31 to the rights and freedoms of individuals and, based on the risk, take appropriate actions to protect them.  
It is important to emphasize that the aim is to determine the level of risk for the individuals whose data has been affected by the personal data breach, and it should not be confused with other types of risks or the risk to the data controller or any of its data processors.  
To determine all these factors, the data controller must inevitably rely on the prior risk management work of the processing activities that have been affected by the personal data breach.

Regarding probability, it is not about determining the likelihood that the personal data breach will materialize, because obviously, in this situation, the breach would have already occurred, but rather determining whether there is a possibility that the consequences will materialize with a high or very high level of severity. To determine this, the technical and organizational measures applied before the breach occurred and the actions taken afterward to prevent the damage from materializing must be taken into account.  
It may happen that the data controller is already aware that specific damage has materialized concerning an affected individual, in which case it would no longer be necessary to determine a probability level because there is certainty that it has already occurred.  
If the damage has not materialized, this probability must be estimated. It will be "unlikely" when the controller can guarantee that the damage cannot materialize; and graded as low, high, and very high when there is some probability of the damage materializing.

VII.  
SANCTIONING REGIME RELATING TO THE OBLIGATIONS OF  
ARTICLE 33 AND 34  
Notifications of personal data breaches to the Control Authority are part of the proactive responsibility of the data controllers, or processors in their case, demonstrating diligence in data processing. The notification of a breach does not imply the imposition of a sanction. On the contrary, a notification, and where applicable communication, made in a timely and proper manner is evidence of the organization's diligence in effectively executing the proactive responsibility obligation under the GDPR. However, failure to comply with the obligations of notification and communication to the data subjects is classified as an infringement.  
Article 58 of the GDPR establishes the investigative, corrective, and authorization and advisory powers of the competent Control Authority.  
Regarding personal data breaches, the following corrective powers established in Article 58.2 of the GDPR should be highlighted:  
•  
“e) Order the data controller to communicate the data security breaches to the data subject;”  
•  
“i) Impose an administrative fine in accordance with Article 83, in addition to or instead of the measures mentioned in this paragraph, depending on the circumstances of each particular case;”  
Article 83 of the GDPR provides for administrative fines of up to €10,000,000 or up to 2% of the total global annual turnover of the previous financial year for controllers and processors for infringements of the obligations established, among others, in Articles 32 (Security of processing), 33 (Notification to the Control Authority), and 34 (Communication to the data subject) of the GDPR.  
Furthermore, non-compliance with the resolutions of the Control Authority under Article 58 of the GDPR, such as an order to communicate a personal data breach to the data subjects, may result in fines of up to €20,000,000 or up to 4% of the total global annual turnover of the previous financial year.  
Title IX of the LOPDGDD specifies the sanctioning regime established in the GDPR. Article 70 establishes that in addition to controllers and processors, when they are not established in the territory of the European Union, their representatives will also be subject to the sanctioning regime of the GDPR.  
The same Article 70 of the LOPDGDD excludes Data Protection Officers from the application of the sanctioning regime.  
Specifically in relation to personal data breaches, Article 73 of the LOPDGDD establishes as serious infringements, among others:  
•  
“q) The failure of the data processor to notify the data controller of the security breaches of which it became aware.”  
•  
“r) The failure to notify the data protection authority of a security breach of personal data in accordance with the provisions of Article 33 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”  
•  
“s) The failure to communicate to the affected party a security breach of personal data in accordance with the provisions of Article 34 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 if the data controller has been required by the data protection authority to carry out such notification.”  
•  
“f) The failure to adopt those technical and organizational measures that are appropriate to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk of processing, as required by Article 32.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”  
•  
“g) The breach, as a result of a lack of due diligence, of the technical and organizational measures that should have been implemented in accordance with the requirements of Article 32.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”  
Finally, Article 74 of the LOPDGDD establishes as minor infringements:  
•  
“m) The incomplete, late, or defective notification to the data protection authority of information related to a security breach of personal data in accordance with the provisions of Article 33 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”  
•  
“n) The failure to document any security breach, as required by Article 33.5 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”  
•  
“ñ) The failure to communicate to the affected party a security breach of personal data that poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals, as required by Article 34 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, unless the provisions of Article 73 s) of this organic law apply.”

VIII. SPECIFICITIES OF OBLIGATED SUBJECTS IN THE LGT  
Other obligated subjects to notify security incidents to the designated computer security incident response teams (CSIRT) are operators of essential services and providers of digital services. Additionally, providers of Information Society services may voluntarily notify competent computer emergency response teams (CERT), and in any case, are required to cooperate with them for the resolution of cybersecurity incidents that have significant effects on the continuity of the services they provide.  
However, the obligation applicable to operators of publicly available electronic telecommunications services or those operating public electronic communications networks continues to be governed by the provisions of Article 41 and concordant articles of Law 9/2014, of May 9, General Telecommunications Law (LGT).  
Indeed, Article 95 of the GDPR establishes that the GDPR does not impose additional obligations within the framework of the provision of public electronic communications services of public telecommunications networks of the Union in areas where such services are subject to specific obligations with the same objective established in Directive 2002/58/EC.  
Therefore, it must be interpreted that the obligations set forth in the LGT, as a transposition norm of the aforementioned Directive, remain in force.  
Although the regulation of the LGT and the GDPR presents common elements, it also includes differential elements such as the following:  
• Absence of the maximum 72-hour notification period in the LGT.  
• Does not contemplate the obligation of the data processor to notify personal data breaches to the controller in the LGT.  
• Differences in the minimum content of the notification (omission of categories and approximate number of affected data subjects and records or personal data in the LGT).  
• The classification of violations of the notification obligation as serious and minor in the LGT.  
• The sanctioning regime (fines of up to 50,000 euros or up to 2,000,000 euros for minor or serious violations, respectively, in the LGT).  
• The competence to declare violations in case of non-compliance with the obligation to notify the telecommunications administration and not the AEPD.
