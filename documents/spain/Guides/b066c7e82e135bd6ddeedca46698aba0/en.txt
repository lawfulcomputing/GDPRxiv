Wi-Fi tracking technologies: guidelines for data controllers  
Wi-Fi tracking is a technology that allows for the identification and tracking of mobile devices through the Wi-Fi signals they emit, to detect the presence of the device in a specific area and to identify movement patterns. It is used, for example, in estimating occupancy, analyzing flows of people, or measuring time spent in a location.  
Practical applications can be found in shopping centers, museums, places of special interest, workplaces, public areas, public transport, or large public events. However, this practice poses serious privacy risks, as it can allow for the tracking of individuals' movements without their action or knowledge and without an appropriate legal basis.  
It is crucial to be aware that many of these uses of Wi-Fi tracking involve the collection and other processing of personal data and, therefore, must comply with the set of principles, rights of individuals, and obligations for data controllers established in the GDPR.  
The guidelines analyze both the technical and legal implications of using this technology, identify the main risks associated with it, and offer a series of concrete recommendations for responsible use that is compliant with data protection regulations.  
These guidelines have been jointly developed by the Spanish Data Protection Agency, the Catalan Data Protection Authority, the Basque Data Protection Authority, and the Transparency and Data Protection Council of Andalusia, resulting from the collaboration of the four supervisory authorities in light of the impact that inappropriate use of Wi-Fi tracking technology can have on the privacy and data protection of individuals.

Wi-Fi Tracking Technologies: Guidelines for Data Controllers  
Currently, the mobile phone is a ubiquitous personal device equipped with various wireless technologies such as Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, in addition to supporting current and past generations of mobile network technologies (i.e., 2G–5G).  
To carry out communications, all these technologies rely on the exchange of messages between these devices and other network equipment such as base stations and access points.  
In particular, Wi-Fi technology is a wireless technology based on standardized communication protocols (IEEE802.11 protocol family), characterized by a set of terminals (mobile or not) that connect to an “Access Point” (AP). A set of APs managed under the same entity constitutes a Wi-Fi network.  
Wi-Fi communication occurs through messages called “frames,” a set of bytes that always contains a header including the identifier of the originating device known as the MAC address (Media Access Control). Some of these frames are unencrypted and are emitted from the device periodically, even when it is not connected to any Wi-Fi network.  
The data contained in these frames, using various technologies currently available, can be captured, analyzed, and processed to determine an identifier that allows for the singularization of the terminal originating these frames. Just as in the world of the Internet, this set of technologies is known as “device fingerprinting,” or simply “fingerprinting,” in Spanish, huella digital del dispositivo or huella del dispositivo. Throughout the text, these terms will be used interchangeably.  
The information used to determine this digital fingerprint is sent by the device without any action or knowledge on the part of the person carrying it, which makes these technologies particularly sensitive from the perspective of privacy and data protection.  
Through the construction, storage, and analysis of this fingerprint, it is possible to detect the presence of the device in a specific area and identify its movement patterns and therefore, the person carrying it.  
These types of technologies that collect data from the Wi-Fi messages exchanged between terminals and APs for subsequent processing and analysis are referred to as “Wi-Fi tracking.”  
The two main types of analytics offered by these technologies are presence analytics and location analytics. Presence analytics focuses on studying the existence of terminals in a specific area and their duration in the same, while location analytics aims to trace the path followed by the terminal within a study area.  
Among its main uses, one can mention the estimation of occupancy, analysis of people flows, calculation of attendance statistics and average times of stay in specific locations or waiting in a queue, determination of the most common routes, or calculation of the visit repeat rate.

A. THE USE OF FIXED AND RANDOM MAC ADDRESSES  
Description of the Theoretical Framework  
DESCRIPTION OF THE  
TECHNOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK  
2.  
The MAC address is a generally fixed and unique identifier for every device, used in communications between the various elements of a network.  
When a device connects to an Access Point (AP) of a Wi-Fi network, all its messages start by identifying the MAC address of the device itself. In this way, the AP can read the content of the frames and, through the MAC address, uniquely identify the terminal.  
The coverage of Wi-Fi networks is provided by the APs that make them up. These APs can be located in open public spaces or inside buildings or facilities.  
However, most mobile terminals that enter the coverage area of Wi-Fi networks do not connect to them. Nevertheless, devices with Wi-Fi capability periodically search for networks that may be available within their coverage area. This search is conducted by periodically sending frames called "Probe Requests" that the device transmits even if it is not connected to a Wi-Fi network and sometimes even if the Wi-Fi functionality is not activated.  
The main purpose of this type of frame is to send a "probe request" to the various Wi-Fi networks that may exist in the area. The APs are prepared to respond to these messages, sending the terminal information that will allow it to connect to the network if the user chooses to do so.  
A few years ago, in the Probe Request frames, in addition to certain technical information, the fixed and unique MAC address of the terminal was sent. Therefore, by collecting these frames over time along with location technologies, it was possible to uniquely identify the terminal using that identifier and record its path within the coverage area of the Wi-Fi network even without being connected to it.  
Due to the privacy issues that this situation posed, mobile terminal manufacturers widely incorporated the use of random MAC addresses. This process began with Apple iOS8 and was followed and extended by Android.  
The random MAC address is a "virtual" address used in Probe Request frames. In this way, different messages sent from a terminal do not share the same unique identifier (fixed MAC address), and therefore it is no longer possible to know the real MAC address of the device by capturing it through the analysis of Probe Request frames. On the other hand, since the random MAC address is frequently modified and the frames themselves are sent randomly, it is also not easy to identify the device using the random MAC addresses directly.

B. DATA USED IN WI-FI TRACKING  

◤ It is estimated that currently between 5% and 10% of devices do not use random MAC addresses.  

◤ There are numerous techniques capable of uniquely identifying, in a high percentage of cases, mobile devices even when they use a changing random MAC address. These are the current techniques employed in Wi-Fi tracking, based on the diverse information contained (or deduced from) the Probe Request frames combined with the detection of statistical patterns of random MAC addresses. The most advanced techniques for linking information from different frames to the same device employ machine learning algorithms and data analytics based on Big Data.  
The various Wi-Fi tracking techniques aim to uniquely and accurately identify and track devices in Wi-Fi environments. This method relies on the use of a wide variety of parameters and physical characteristics of both the devices and the transmission conditions themselves to generate an individualized digital fingerprint for each device.  
The use of pattern recognition techniques and data analytics in general, combined with the continuous evolution of the techniques employed by device manufacturers in defense of privacy, leads to a situation of constant change. As a result, some techniques that were fully effective a few years ago have lost their utility today. The main methods currently employed are described below.  
The "Probe Request" frame is a type of management frame contemplated by the Wi-Fi standard and is used when the device (for example, a smartphone) is not connected to a Wi-Fi AP. The device, on each of the available Wi-Fi channels, polls by "asking" for available APs within its coverage area to which it can connect.  
When a specific AP receives the Probe Request frame, it responds with a frame called "Probe Response." With this frame, the device becomes aware of the existence of that AP and its characteristics, should it wish to connect.  
This type of frame is emitted by all devices in Wi-Fi communications automatically, without user control and unencrypted, so that it can be received and decoded not only by any AP but also by any low-cost device listening to the Wi-Fi channel.

Wi-Fi tracking technologies leverage these characteristics of Probe Request frames (sent by all devices, sent unencrypted, and containing a large amount of data) to generate a unique digital footprint that allows for device identification.  
In addition to the information directly sent in the Probe Request frames, it is possible to obtain information through indirect measurements based on combinations of these or through heuristic techniques.  
In practice, any measure or data that assists in the identification of the device can be used. Among the physical measures or additional data that can employ this type of technology are the received signal strength (RSSI, which stands for Received Signal Strength Indicator), which can also be used to determine the approximate location of the device, the time interval between the sending of the Probe Request frames, the statistical distribution of both the sequence number of the frames and the random MAC addresses, or the inherent deviations of the device clocks. In summary, all of these can ultimately allow for the generation of a digital footprint.  
The objective of collecting and analyzing data as previously described (and others depending on the specific solution of the manufacturer) is to generate a unique digital footprint that allows for the identification of each device.  
The process of generating the digital footprint of mobile terminals in a Wi-Fi network generally involves the following phases:  

◤Capture of Probe Request frames: Probe Request frames received at the various APs of the observed Wi-Fi network are collected en masse. In addition to the information from the frames, data related to the physical conditions of the transmission, such as RSSI and others, can be collected. To carry out this capture, specialized equipment or even conventional Wi-Fi network equipment that provides these additional capabilities can be used.  

◤Extraction and sending of information: Relevant information is extracted from the captured frames and the physical characteristics of the transmission. This information is sent to a centralized server for processing and analysis. The specific data sent will depend on the Wi-Fi tracking model implemented in the network.  

◤Pattern analysis and generation of the digital footprint: After extracting the information, a pattern analysis of the collected data is carried out. The objective is to combine specific characteristics of the collected data that allow distinguishing one device from another. This analysis will vary according to the system used and is fundamental to achieving a unique identification of mobile terminals in the monitored area, with the use of advanced techniques, such as machine learning and probabilistic modeling, already being common.  

Machine learning algorithms are capable of learning patterns and establishing correlations between the different parameters of the frames to determine the probability that a frame belongs to a previously identified device or is from a new one. 3.  
Wi-Fi tracking technology has evolved significantly, allowing for the collection and analysis of multiple characteristics of devices. This capability is not limited to identification through the MAC address but also includes the creation of unique fingerprints. These fingerprints, created by combining multiple characteristics, enable continuous identification of devices, surpassing anonymization measures such as MAC address randomization. In turn, the mobile phone, which has become an everyday and inseparable element, acts as a direct link to its user, generating both direct and indirect identifiability. These devices are part of the private sphere of users, and therefore must be protected in accordance with the European Convention on Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms and the regulations governing the protection of personal data. Thus, in this technological context, it is crucial to analyze the possible existence of personal data processing, as well as, where applicable, the content thereof.  

A. SCOPE OF THE TERM "PERSONAL DATA"  
The concept of "personal data" as established in the GDPR has a very broad scope. Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines "personal data" as "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person." The reference to "any information" in this definition highlights the legislator's intention to give this concept a very broad meaning, which is not limited to confidential data or data related to privacy but can encompass all kinds of information, both objective and subjective, as long as it is "about" the person in question. For this purpose, it is sufficient that the information is related to a specific person due to its content, purpose, or effects.  

Regarding the concept of "identifiable," the same Article 4.1 states that a natural person shall be considered identifiable if "their identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, particularly by means of an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that person." Therefore, to qualify information as personal data, it is not necessary for that information to allow, by itself, the identification of the data subject.

Moreover, a natural person is identifiable from the moment the data holder is able to distinguish and treat them differently, even when that data alone is not sufficient to do so.  

In this regard, it is useful to establish a differentiation between unique identifying data that allow for unequivocal identification of the person and "quasi-identifiers." The latter, at first glance, do not allow for the identification of a specific person. However, when combined with each other or with other data, they can identify the person due to "unique combinations." This phenomenon, known as the "mosaic effect," illustrates how the accumulation of "quasi-identifying" data can lead to the identification of a person, a process facilitated by big data analytics technologies. It is perfectly possible to speak of the existence of personal data even in cases where there is no direct or express identification of the data subject.  

Example:  
Information is available about a mobile phone that has visited (knowing the date, time, and duration of stay) the store A on two occasions, the establishment B on three occasions, has stayed at the hotel C for 16 days, and is classified as a tourist with a stay of more than 15 days and less than 1 month. This information alone is so detailed that with very little additional information, the person carrying that mobile device could be identified.  

Regarding the capacity to be "identifiable" as a person, Recital 26 of the GDPR emphasizes that "all means, such as singling out, that could reasonably be used by the data controller or any other person to identify the natural person directly or indirectly must be considered." In other words, it is not necessary for all the information that allows for the identification of the data subject to be held by a single person or within a single processing operation. What must be analyzed is whether there is a reasonable possibility that using additional means, the person could be identified.  

On the other hand, this is a dynamic analysis, so the degree of technological advancement at the time of processing and its possible development during the period in which the data will be processed must be taken into account. Ultimately, this identifiability is also related to the fact that it does not require disproportionate efforts, and, as just indicated, the constant technological evolution facilitates this.

B. WI-FI TRACKING AND “FINGERPRINTING” AS PERSONAL DATA  
This broad definition of the concept of personal data established by the GDPR is particularly relevant in the context of Wi-Fi tracking. This technology affects all devices with Wi-Fi functionality, regardless of whether they are connected to any specific network, and sometimes even when that functionality is not activated. These devices include mobile phones, tablets, laptops, desktop computers, routers, printers, household appliances, toys, wearable devices (the so-called “wearables,” including pacemakers, portable oxygen systems, devices for diabetics, neural implants, etc.), and even automobiles.  

The GDPR, in its Recital 30, warns of the ability to identify individuals through device fingerprints:  
“Natural persons can be associated with online identifiers provided by their devices,[...] This can leave traces that, in particular, when combined with unique identifiers and other data received by servers, can be used to create profiles of natural persons and identify them.”  
According to the above, the data transmitted by Wi-Fi signals used in such processes can be considered personal data insofar as they are related to identifiable individuals and are capable of being used for their direct or indirect identification. In particular, “fingerprinting” in the context of Wi-Fi tracking may constitute, according to what is established in the GDPR, a processing of personal data.  
The concept of “fingerprinting” is defined as “a set of information elements that identifies a device or application instance,” thus encompassing any information that can be used to individualize, link, or infer a user or device over time.  
This may include, among others, data derived from:  

◤ the configuration of a user agent/device;  

◤ or data exposed by the use of network communication protocols.  
Therefore, fingerprinting provides the ability to distinguish one device from another and could be used to track a user's location or behavior over time, even if there is no direct or explicit identification of the person.  
Some supervisory authorities, such as the Unabhängige Landeszentrum für Datenschutz (Schleswig Holstein, Germany), have expressed concern about the application of these technologies to automobiles that incorporate Wi-Fi access points, allowing for the tracking of individuals. D. PROCESSING OF PERSONAL DATA  
The position of a device can be known either approximately by "presence" (proximity to the sensor) or more precisely through triangulation. By maintaining the identification or individualization of the device and determining its position over time, it is possible to establish a trajectory during the time the device is within the coverage area of the sensors.  
This location data represents a type of high-risk personal data.  
Wi-Fi tracking technology itself is not a processing of personal data, but it could be part of one in certain cases. In fact, when deciding how to develop a processing operation or achieve a purpose, the controller may choose solutions other than Wi-Fi Tracking or options that do not involve the processing of personal data.  
Wi-Fi tracking technology may appear in operations of personal data processing derived from two main types of analytics: presence analytics and location analytics. Presence analytics focuses on studying the existence of terminals in a specific area and their duration in the same, while location analytics aims to trace the path followed by the terminal within a specific study area, even for an indefinite period.  
In many cases, its purpose is to detect and analyze collective behaviors; however, it should not be forgotten that it starts from the detection of individual data.  
For the privacy of individuals, as detailed in the section on risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, when the spatial and temporal scope of the data collected through Wi-Fi tracking is maintained, these may be sufficient on their own or in combination with others to allow the identification of individuals, and the data controller should consider this type of data as personal data.  
By way of illustration, some processing operations in which Wi-Fi tracking technologies have been employed are noted:  

◤ Device geolocation service, with or without the user's consent.  

◤ Tracking of individuals in workplaces.  

◤ Emergency services, through search or location as part of providing vital assistance to individuals. This is a mechanism by which emergency call centers can automatically receive information about the caller's location, enriched by Wi-Fi location data.  

◤ Surveillance of individuals, using Wi-Fi tracking to detect if there are people in certain premises or locations, and individual control of access to these areas.

LEGITIMIZING BASES FOR THE PROCESSING OF PERSONAL DATA  
4.  
Any processing of personal data must comply with the principles established in Article 5 of the GDPR and meet one of the lawful conditions listed in Article 6 of the GDPR, which applies to Wi-Fi tracking in cases where the data controller opts for a technological option that enables such processing.  
The processing must be fair and transparent, and it must be entirely clear to individuals what data is being processed and how it is being processed through Wi-Fi tracking, providing this information in an easily accessible and understandable manner, regardless of the technical or practical difficulties that Wi-Fi tracking may pose for the data controller in complying with these principles.  
The purposes of the processing through Wi-Fi tracking must be explicit, meaning they must be clearly stated, must be legitimate, and must be communicated to the individuals concerned, at the latest, at the time of collection. Additionally, the data collected for a specific purpose through Wi-Fi tracking may not be used for a subsequent purpose that is incompatible. To this end, it is essential to ensure that the subsequent processing does not deviate from the purposes already established for the processing, and of which the individuals concerned must be informed. Article 24.1 of the GDPR establishes the obligation to manage the risk that the processing of personal data poses to the rights and freedoms of individuals, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing. Therefore, any organization that decides to initiate the processing of personal data must manage these risks.  
This section presents the main risks that could be associated with the processing of personal data implemented using Wi-Fi tracking technology. It is not an exhaustive list of risks, but rather an overview of the main risks to consider, and in any case, data controllers must determine, based on the specifics of the particular processing, which risks are applicable in their case and identify the existence of other possible risks not identified in this guide.  
The management of risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals is distinct from the management of compliance risks concerning the principles established in the GDPR and applicable data protection regulations. Compliance risk management, like other risk management for different objectives (legal, financial, business, fraud, project, etc.), may be necessary to achieve certain organizational goals, but it does not address the obligations of risk management for the rights and freedoms of natural persons imposed by the GDPR. If the processing intended to be initiated does not comply with the principles of the GDPR, for example, due to lacking an adequate legal basis or failing to meet the principles of necessity and proportionality, the processing would be unlawful and prohibited. The use of Wi-Fi tracking will fall under the processing of personal data, and the organization implementing it is obliged to comply with the requirements and obligations established in the GDPR, including managing the risks for individuals who will be affected by the processing as a whole.  
Example:  
In order to preserve the safety of individuals at access points to a mass event, it may be necessary to determine if some of the access routes are becoming congested. To measure this congestion, the use of Wi-Fi tracking technology could be proposed to conduct an approximate count, and it will be a means to implement one of the processing operations, the measurement of congestion. Merely measuring congestion will not achieve the purpose of preserving the safety of individuals, as the processing must include other operations, such as the decision-making process to reduce congestion at a given moment, the ability to do so effectively, and actions to ensure that this occurs efficiently and in an orderly manner. If these operations that give meaning to the ultimate purpose of the processing are not correctly implemented, the processing is not fulfilling a purpose that has a legal basis. Likewise, if there are already means by which these objectives are being achieved (video surveillance, people counters, etc.), the processing would also not be necessary; at the same time, it could prove unsuitable since this measure entails an additional risk as there are less intrusive means to achieve the possible purpose of this system.

G. RISKS ASSOCIATED WITH LOCATION DATA  
It is essential to emphasize the particular difficulty of anonymizing datasets when they include various location data of the same person or trajectory data due to the ease of re-identification they present.  
Example:  
The trips of 173 million taxis in New York were published, supposedly anonymizing the license number of each taxi with a hash. The data included the hash of the license number, start, end, duration, time, cost, and tip. In a very short time, the license number of the taxis was re-identified, and with Google searches, images of publicly relevant individuals taking the re-identified taxis were obtained.  

The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) and previously the Article 29 Working Party have warned on several occasions about the particularly sensitive nature of location data. Movements of a person can provide revealing information, such as their workplace, residence, and centers of interest, including places of worship or activities related to their sexual orientation, which could allow for the creation of a detailed profile of their behaviors.  
The identification capability of location data is well known, and often only a few spatial points are needed to uniquely identify a person within a population with high precision, considering usual mobility patterns. This means that even when unique identifiers such as the MAC address are removed, as the device is singularized, the location data can lead to the identification of a person. Logically, the greater the temporal and spatial scope of the location, the more feasible identification will be.  
A data pattern that contains a person's location over time cannot be completely anonymized, even if the precision of the recorded geographic coordinates is reduced or specific details of the itinerary are removed.  
Moreover, this also applies to incompletely aggregated location data. That is, simply anonymizing data does not guarantee privacy protection, as if the mobility patterns are unique enough, external information can be used to re-link the data to a specific individual.  
Thus, specific circumstances may arise, such as the existence of sparsely populated areas at certain times where it would be easy to identify the person and their behaviors or even combine the capture of an indicator with images from a surveillance system leading to the automatic identification of the person.

OBLIGATION TO CONDUCT A DATA PROTECTION IMPACT ASSESSMENT  
6.  
The GDPR establishes the obligations related to the data protection impact assessment (DPIA) in Articles 35 and 36. It does not require a DPIA for every processing of personal data, but it does for those where it is likely to pose a high risk.  
The existence of a reasonable degree of presumption that the processing may entail a high risk makes the conduct of a DPIA essential.  
The DPIA is a process of evaluating a processing operation that extends over time, throughout its entire lifecycle, and must be continuously reviewed, at least when there is a change in the risk posed by the processing operations. In no case should it be considered a mere documentary formality.  
In general terms, a DPIA:  

◤ Is required when there may be a high risk to rights and freedoms.  

◤ Is a specific obligation of the controller.  

◤ Requires surpassing an assessment of the suitability, necessity, and proportionality of the processing in relation to its purposes.  

◤ Requires that the assessment determines that the residual risk has been reduced through the application of measures and guarantees to a tolerable level.  

◤ Requires its completion before the commencement of processing activities.  

◤ Requires the advice of the DPO when one must be designated or has been voluntarily appointed by the controller.  

◤ Will take into account compliance with approved codes of conduct and applicable certifications.

In accordance with Article 11 of the GDPR, if the purposes for which a controller processes personal data do not require or no longer require the identification of a data subject by the controller, the controller shall not be obliged to maintain, obtain, or process additional information for the sole purpose of identifying the data subject in order to comply with the GDPR, and in such cases, if the controller can demonstrate that it is not in a position to identify the data subject, it will inform them accordingly, if possible, and Articles 15 to 20 of the GDPR (rights of access, rectification, erasure, right to restriction of processing, and data portability) shall not apply, except when the data subject, for the purposes of exercising their rights under those articles, provides additional information that allows for their identification.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE REGULATION  
11.  
Given the current technological situation, it is possible that personal data processing may occur that combines the use of Wi-Fi tracking technology and artificial intelligence (AI) systems. This type of personal data processing is subject to the system of principles, obligations for controllers, and rights for data subjects established by the GDPR. Additionally, the use of certain AI systems will be regulated by the Artificial Intelligence Regulation (RIA).  
At the time of the publication of this Guide, the Artificial Intelligence Regulation (RIA) has not yet been published, which will come into force 20 days after its publication and will be applicable in most of its provisions two years after that.  
From the perspective of personal data protection, the RIA is not intended to affect the application of the fundamental right to data protection. The RIA is complementary to the GDPR and will apply without prejudice to it in order to enable controllers and processors to comply with their obligations regarding data protection when incorporating AI systems into their processing (Recital 78 GDPR) to implement data protection by design.  
Therefore, the introduction to the market, the commissioning, and the use of AI systems must facilitate effective application and allow the exercise of the rights and other remedies of data subjects guaranteed by data protection legislation, as well as other fundamental rights.  
This includes the obligations of providers and controllers of deployment, or other interveners and operators when appropriate, of AI systems to the extent that the design, development, or use of AI systems involves the processing of personal data, as well as the functions and powers of independent data protection supervisory authorities. Among others, these authorities will have the power to request any documentation created or preserved under the RIA related to high-risk AI systems mentioned in Annex III of that Regulation when access to such documentation is necessary for the effective fulfillment of their competencies.  
It is also worth clarifying that data subjects continue to enjoy all the rights and guarantees conferred upon them by data protection legislation, including rights related to fully automated individual decisions, such as profiling.  
In this regard, as an example, when decisions that produce legal effects for a person or significantly affect them are implemented based on data obtained through Wi-Fi tracking technology as input information, using AI systems (regardless of their type) based solely on the automated processing of personal data, the provisions already established in the GDPR (Articles 13, 14, 15, and 22 and Recital 71 GDPR) will apply.  
Furthermore, in this case, if the AI system on which the decision is based is classified as high-risk according to the RIA, in addition to the rights provided in the GDPR, when the person considers that the decision has an adverse impact on their health, safety, or fundamental rights, the provisions of the AI Regulation will apply regarding the affected person’s right to receive clear and meaningful explanations about the role of the AI system in the decision-making process and the main elements of the decision made.
