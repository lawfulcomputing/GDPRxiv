I. Framework for the Protection of Minors  
"Protection of minors on the Internet," the role that those who hold parental authority must also play in protecting minors regarding the use of the Internet:  
1. Parents, guardians, curators, or legal representatives shall ensure that minors make balanced and responsible use of digital devices and information society services in order to guarantee the proper development of their personality and preserve their dignity and fundamental rights.  
Some specific data can provide an idea of the current situation regarding the use of the Internet by minors. For example, the survey by the National Institute of Statistics on Internet use in households in 2022 shows that 90% of the population under 10 years old uses the Internet, a percentage that rises to 98.3% at age 15, and that one third of them use the Internet for more than 5 hours a day. Transparency Market Research estimated in 2021 that the digital marketing market aimed at minors amounted to 2.9 billion dollars, with a growth outlook of 21% annually.  
Save The Children has published studies in 2020 showing that 62.5% of the adolescent population aged 13 to 17 has consumed pornography, that the average age of first consumption is established at 12 years, that 54% consider pornography a source of inspiration for their sexual relationships, and that 55% wish to put it into practice, with 20% engaging in sexting. This situation is causing problems in the neurodevelopment of minors, in their attention capacity, in their learning, in their emotional development, and in the emergence of aggressive attitudes in an irreversible manner.  
In light of this situation, the AEPD has promoted, together with the Attorney General's Office, the proposal for a State Pact promoted by civil society organizations involved in the rights of children and adolescents.

II. Age Verification in a System for the Protection of Minors Against Inappropriate Content  

A. Definition of Terms  
The Convention on the Rights of the Child establishes that:  
Article 1 For the purposes of this Convention, a child is understood to be any human being under the age of eighteen years, unless, under the law applicable to them, they have attained majority earlier.  
The DSA, in its Recital 89, establishes the requirement to protect minors in relation to content that may harm their physical, mental, or moral development. Throughout this document, the term "minor" or "minors" will be used for those individuals who, based on their age (under 14 years, under 18 years, or other cases depending on the situation), need to be protected from inappropriate content according to their age, and the term "adult" will be used in the opposite sense. The term "inappropriate content" will be used for those websites restricted only to adults, content classified as "18 years and older" (pornography, extreme violence), websites limited to access by those over 14 years old, and harmful, addictive, or advertising content prohibited to minors.

B. System for the Protection of Minors Against Inappropriate Content.  
The purpose of protecting minors on the Internet is to safeguard them from uncontrolled access to inappropriate content, which means that the ultimate goal is different from merely verifying their age or subjecting them to surveillance and monitoring. Inappropriate content for minors must be freely accessible to those Internet users who, having decided to consult it, can demonstrate that they meet the established age requirements.  

The verification of the user's age is only the first step in a system aimed at protecting minors from inappropriate content. This system will basically consist of the following elements:  

■ A mechanism for age verification, which will provide accurate information regarding the authorization of access to content aimed at adults.  

■ Policies for the classification of sites and content based on age, which will allow for criteria on which Internet sites or what content on generalist sites are considered adult-oriented or have established age-based access restrictions.  

■ A classification of sites or content based on the application of the previously established policies. This classification involves the application of the aforementioned policies.  

■ An execution of access policies based on the established policies, the classification of content, and the user's access authorization, which will filter the content. This execution must involve not only the entities responsible for websites and social networks but also Internet search engines, mobile phone companies, and manufacturers of video games or devices, among others.  

C. Protection of the Best Interests of the Child.  
Recital 89 of the DSA emphasizes that the obligation of large platform providers and search engines is not limited to age verification or protection against inappropriate content for minors. This obligation must encompass the protection of the best interests of the child in all its facets:  

“Very large online platform providers and very large online search engine providers must take into account the best interests of minors when adopting measures such as adapting the design of their service and their online interface, especially when their services are primarily aimed at minors or are predominantly used by them.”  

The best interests of minors must guide the design and implementation of protection systems, taking into account, among other things, their right to privacy and intimacy, limiting the exposure of their status as minors to avoid risks that are different and could be more severe than access to such content. Therefore, such systems cannot be conceived with a narrow view focused solely on limiting access or only on age verification, but must consider the complexity of the context of processing and the need to protect that best interest, as well as the general framework of fundamental rights. Among other things, it is necessary to protect minors from the illegitimate, ongoing, and massive collection of their personal data, as well as from profiling and the constant exposure of this vulnerable group to advertising that enriches these sites.  

The implementation of a protection system for minors against inappropriate content requires the cooperation of multiple stakeholders with a genuine commitment to protecting the fundamental interests of the child in all their dimensions and the fundamental rights of all Internet users concerning the protection of their personal data. Such stakeholders include all individuals and institutions legally responsible for the welfare of minors, regulatory bodies, associations, and foundations for the protection of minors, content providers.

D. Protection of citizens' rights on the internet  
The systems for protecting minors from inappropriate content, although designed for their protection, are in practice applied to all citizens accessing the Internet; therefore, these systems must be designed to respect the fundamental rights of all of them. Moreover, they must be aimed at being used by adults, and not by minors, as it is adults who must prove their status as "authorized persons to access." These protection systems, to the extent that they involve the processing of personal data, must be legitimate, suitable, necessary, and proportional. In particular, the prohibitions on processing special categories of data (Articles 9 and 22 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), of 2016) such as biometric identification and authentication, and their exceptions, must be taken into account.  
In this regard, Article 28 of the DSA "Protection of Minors Online" establishes that age verification, and the protection of the best interests of the child, is not a legal basis that legitimizes the additional processing of data of a minor:  
“3. Compliance with the obligations established in this article shall not require online platform providers to process additional personal data in order to assess whether the service recipient is a minor.”  
The systems for protecting minors from inappropriate content involve high-risk processing for the rights of individuals, but they can also have a significant impact on society as a whole. The high risk of these systems implies that the most appropriate strategies for managing it are those that preserve the anonymity of the user in relation to Internet service providers and third parties in the context of age verification. Furthermore, they must provide transparent, auditable tools, under the control of the user, to verify authorization for access to inappropriate content and that generate trust. All this is without prejudice to the obligation to implement all necessary privacy measures resulting from conducting an impact assessment for data protection and to pass a suitability, necessity, and proportionality analysis.

PRINCIPLE 1:  
The system for the protection of minors against inappropriate content must ensure that it is not possible to identify, track, or locate minors through the Internet.  
A protection system must preserve the best interests of the child. This interest is much broader than merely limiting their access to inappropriate content; it must also preserve their privacy, security, physical and mental health, education, and their right to the free development of their personality and personal capabilities.  
Protection systems for minors against inappropriate content must prevent the identification of Internet users who are minors, so that potential aggressors, pedophiles, addictive schemes, or anyone intending to locate or emit specific content for minors with malicious purposes are unable to construct deceptive services to locate them. Any system that is based on the minor having to reveal their status as a minor must be avoided.  
The obligation that different parties may have to verify the age of those who wish to access inappropriate content is not a legal basis for the processing of data of minors. A system based on the collection of data from the minor implies the processing of data of a minor that must itself be legitimized, suitable, necessary, and proportional.  
Systems based on profiling Internet users on servers of service providers, or third-party entities acting as intermediaries between the user and the content, allow for the identification of minors. Similarly, systems based on facial recognition or biometric information executed on such servers, not exclusively on personal devices, carry the risk of being incorporated into malicious services with the purpose of identifying minors. These systems may incur additional risks when constructed using centralized databases that accumulate extensive information regarding the identity and browsing habits of a large part of the population, particularly minors.  
When applying access limitation policies to content, the processing of the information “authorized person to access” inappropriate content must have the least possible dissemination to avoid the detection of minors. That is, the fact that the status of “authorized person to access” has not been verified must be treated in a way that prevents inferring that one is dealing with a minor, both in the process of accrediting access authorization and in the content filtering process, or analyzing their browsing.  
It is also necessary to eliminate, from the design, the impact that potential breaches of personal data of minors could have if they were in the hands of verification services of third parties or in the Internet services themselves.

A possible solution is to process identity information, that of the "authorized person to access," and the implementation of access limitation policies on the devices in the possession of individuals, without the need to resort to the servers of service providers or third-party intermediary entities. In this regard, it is important to consider the requirements set forth in Article 25.2 of the GDPR concerning the minimization of personal data.  
An example of an additional guarantee could be that the system provides null information, or at most the condition of "unauthorized person to access," in multiple circumstances: when an adult has decided not to authenticate, when the protection system is not present on the device, when age verification has failed, when it concerns a person who does not meet the age requirements, or when the condition "authorized person to access" applies to more cases (which would depend on the type of service).  
Another possible guarantee is that the browsing of users can be masked or obfuscated so that there are no access patterns that allow the identification of minors.  

PRINCIPLE 2:  
Age verification must be aimed at ensuring that individuals of the appropriate age can authenticate their status as "authorized persons to access," and must not allow the authentication of the status of "minor."  
The purpose of protection systems should not be to ascertain the age of minors. Verifying the status of "minor" would imply that mechanisms must be constructed to validate the identity of minors, with age authentication capabilities suitable for use by minors.  
These mechanisms could include biometric analysis, profiling, or obtaining credentials from minors. All of these would put the minor at risk, either by exposing them to malicious services, by collecting excessive data, or by identifying a person as a minor to service providers or third-party intermediary entities between the user and the content. Furthermore, they involve the processing of data from minors, which would need to be legitimized and comply with other GDPR requirements relating, among others, to specific information for minors or limitations on obtaining consent and entering into service contracts.  
Therefore, verification mechanisms must be aimed at use by those individuals who can authenticate having "access authorization," meaning they should not be tools for minors that expose them to additional processing, nor conditioned on having resources for authentication and identity verification that are available to adults.

PRINCIPLE 3:  
Accreditation for access to inappropriate content must be anonymous for Internet service providers and third parties  
The protection system must guarantee the privacy of individuals while browsing the Internet; it must not expose their identity, especially that of minors. The application of this principle must be carried out without prejudice to the fact that, for other treatments offered by the Internet service, such as the sale of products, customer identification may be necessary, or as required by Union Law or the laws of Member States. Accreditation for access to content inappropriate for minors and identity accreditation before third parties are two distinct treatments. The treatment by Internet service providers and third parties of accreditation for access to inappropriate content must be anonymous and independent of treatment for other legitimate purposes.  
Content for adults can be very varied: videos, texts, books, audios, or other products. It should be noted that access to services and products via the Internet is not a residual option, but increasingly, the only option for a large part of the population to develop their personal and economic lives. Therefore, any registration or tracking of this can have a significant impact on their privacy in general. In particular, when digital identity systems are developed specifically aimed at access to content inappropriate for minors.  
Loss of anonymity may occur when identity is verified before the Internet service, when there are intermediary third parties involved, or when a provider of identity credentials or access credentials has the ability to link the generation of credentials with actual access to a service or content. One case, for example, may occur when the user must be accredited before a third party, and this third party in turn sends a positive or negative value regarding the outcome of the authorization for access to the Internet service provider. Another case may arise when the user is accredited before a third entity and is associated with a unique identifier that allows access to the services. Although third parties may be trustworthy, they are not free from intervention by judicial authorities, intelligence services, personal data breaches, future regulatory changes, changes in their ownership, etc. Moreover, those third parties that provide the service for a fee would have the obligation to implement traceability and access auditing for accounting and billing purposes.  
Anonymity will be lost when signed certificates or attributes associated with unique identifiers linked to an identifiable person are used, instead of certificates or attributes that cannot be linked to the user. Anonymity will be even more exposed when third parties process biometric traits (through photos or videos, for example) to extract biometric templates.  
The protection system for minors against inappropriate content must prevent third parties from acting as intermediaries between the user and the Internet service provider using strategies that allow identification, tracking of browsing, and/or profiling of the person. This could be achieved, for example, by providing tools so that the personal device executes the verification mechanisms without using external resources, including the execution of access limitation policies to content on the same device. It could also be a strategy that identity-providing entities, when providing accreditation of the status of "authorized person to access," do so through attributes that prevent linkage with the user, ensuring that the request for the attribute does not record the intention to access such content and that the application process does not generate metadata linked to the person.

PRINCIPLE 4:  
The obligation to prove the status of "authorized person to access" will be limited solely to inappropriate content.  
The requirement for every person to prove their status as an "authorized person to access" for any type of content would not comply with the principles of data minimization, nor with the necessity requirement, and would identify all minors accessing the Internet by default. The general rule should be free and anonymous browsing without having to prove any status. Only in the case of wanting to access specific sites with age restrictions, or content inappropriate for minors, should the status of an authorized person to access be required due to being over 18 years old, or 14 years old, as for example, in the case of accessing a social network.  
For instance, protection systems based on profiling Internet users to determine if they are minors imply systematic processing of personal data. This processing would link each person accessing the Internet to an analysis of their historical activity, relationships, conversations, reactions to content, etc. The use of profiling techniques involves continuous monitoring of all individuals even when they are not accessing content inappropriate for minors, and constitutes a disproportionate processing of personal data.  
Therefore, a protection system must allow a person not to have the obligation to define themselves as an "authorized person to access" on every occasion. In a service that provides content for both adults and content without age restrictions, it should only be necessary to prove the status of "authorized person to access" when accessing adult content.  
An important aspect that must be avoided is the expansive interpretation of protection against inappropriate content. Protection should not systematically affect cultural content, in such a way that it is used to apply policies beyond the strict protection of minors, nor should it limit freedom, diversity of thought, or the educational role of families.

PRINCIPLE 5:  
Age verification must be carried out accurately and categorized as "authorized person to access."  
Age verification must be carried out accurately, not probabilistically or estimated, and aimed at categorization as "authorized person to access." Under no circumstances should it involve the specific revelation of age or date of birth.  
On one hand, verifying (as established in various regulations) is a different term from estimating. Furthermore, age estimation is inevitably subject to errors, biases, and discrimination13, and often requires checking more information about the person (gender, race, etc.) to be sufficiently accurate.  
Verifying "authorization to access" is different from providing the value of age or date of birth. The generation of quantitative attributes about age poses a significant risk in the case of minors, especially for those between 14 and 18 years old, but also poses a risk for older individuals. This is without prejudice to the fact that in treatments with other purposes it may be necessary to accurately collect age, treatments that must be independent of age verification for the protection of minors against inappropriate content.  
It should also be noted that age verification strategies for minors by age brackets (for example, under 14 years old, and between 14-18 years old) must be implemented in such a way that attempts to access unauthorized adult content cannot be linked to successful attempts to access content for those aged 14-18, thus revealing information about the age bracket (or even the status of being a minor).  
Therefore, age verification mechanisms must provide a certain value, categorized solely as "authorized to access" and under no circumstances allow service providers or third parties to process a person's specific age, or for it to be inferred.  

PRINCIPLE 6:  
The system must ensure that individuals cannot be profiled based on their browsing.  
To determine that a specific site or content is only suitable for an adult, some type of labeling is necessary. This labeling can be done through a "suitable/not suitable" rating, as is the case with the application of age restrictions on websites. It can also be done by assigning multiple labels to each piece of content ("violent," "explicit sex," "racist," "substance abuse," etc.14) to assess whether the content is inappropriate for minors. The labeling of sites or content could be carried out by human reviewers or automatically15, and in a static manner or through dynamic analysis (the latter, for example, in chats).

At some point between the server, which provides the content, and the user, who requests the content, the access limitation policy must be executed. The execution of this policy on the servers themselves, or on third-party entities intermediating between the user and the websites, carries privacy risks. Among these risks are those arising from profiling or monitoring the accessing individual, which in this type of content could include special categories of data. Adding multiple tags to the content accessed by a person could mean constructing a profile that labels them based on the content they have accessed. The risk will be greater when the same third entity filters all traffic corresponding to the same person. Profiling and monitoring, particularly with special categories of data, in addition to having to lift their general prohibition on processing and needing legitimization, is a high-risk processing that would have to pass a proportionality assessment.  
The execution of access restrictions to websites locally on the devices of Internet users would eliminate the risks of profiling or monitoring. Local content filtering is technically feasible, as demonstrated by existing malware protection systems or some tools used for parental control. Local protection through the verification of the attribute "authorized person to access," even dynamic local tagging, would be possible on the devices themselves. Either in their operating systems or through the adaptation of Internet service applications (whether social networks, search engines, chats, etc.). It even allows for the development of more effective strategies such as the application of regional, cultural, or family criteria in the tagging process and interpretation of the tags. Without prejudice to the use of the previous strategies, dynamic tagging could also be performed, including filtering, on home routers or educational centers. The objective would always be the same: to execute protection based on age while minimizing the data of the individuals being processed to avoid the risk of locating minors or general profiling.  

PRINCIPLE 7:  
The system must ensure the non-linkage of a person's activity across different services  
A system that allows linking the activity of an Internet user across various services can identify and profile them, inferring behavioral characteristics of the interested party.  
Systems that, for age verification, use unique codes to be used across multiple platforms allow for tracking the individual across different services. The same occurs with systems based on signed attributes that include unique identifiers. It should be noted that the interaction of the user with the service is usually more complex than just accessing content; for example, it may allow for comments or conversations, or in some specific services, identification of the person will be required (for example, on online gaming sites). When websites or services offering content deemed inappropriate for minors extend to multiple areas of digital life, linking accesses can allow not only for very intrusive profiling but also reveal more identifying attributes.  
This can also occur with any type of unique identifier that is reused across services, platforms, or content, such as biometric patterns. Even more complex situations can arise when adult content is linked to access to certain venues and the downloading of credentials for age verification collects geolocation information, for example.  
Therefore, the system must avoid common unique identifiers across different services and the use of mechanisms that reveal metadata that allows for the identification of the user both directly and through the aggregation of additional information.

PRINCIPLE 8:  
The system must guarantee the exercise of parental authority by the parents  
Any system for the protection of minors against inappropriate content must ensure the right of those exercising parental authority to actively participate in the education of the minors under their care, maintaining respect for their cultural, political, and belief diversity, as well as the particular conditions of the minor. Protection against certain content will be part of that education. Families, educational institutions, associations and foundations for the protection of minors, researchers and education experts, and the State have the right to actively participate in establishing the criteria for what they consider inappropriate. It cannot be a commercial entity that dictates the content to which a minor can access. The reality is that, in many cases, the economic interest of such entities and the monetization of the data of minors could take precedence, allowing and even promoting inappropriate content.  
Therefore, systems must establish policies taking into account families, either directly or through their representatives, associations, and foundations aimed at the protection of minors.  
PRINCIPLE 9:  
Any system for the protection of minors against inappropriate content must guarantee the fundamental rights of all individuals in their access to the Internet  
The importance of digital life, enhanced every day by all institutions, implies that any limitation or control over digital engagement, if not applied correctly, could represent a limitation of fundamental rights, both for adults and for minors.  
Thus, there would be an intrusion into the fundamental right to personal privacy whenever the systems for the protection of minors against inappropriate content allow linking content with an identifiable person, allow profiling their intimate aspects, or linking them with other information extractable from the metadata generated in the intervening entities of the protection system. Even the mere declaration that a person has sought to access inappropriate content for minors may constitute an intrusion into their privacy.  
Regarding the right to personal freedom, information, thought, conscience, and religion—all of which are also rights of minors—a protection system cannot restrict access to certain content due to excessive zeal, or due to the application of certain values, interests, or beliefs. It must be taken into account that inappropriate content for minors could include books, opinions, or educational material. When systems are so intrusive to privacy that they generate self-censorship, it could be a case of limiting the freedom of information. Self-censorship will also arise when the desire to access inappropriate content must be verified before third entities. This is also the case when probabilistic systems or biases are used that prevent certain individuals, for example, those at age limits or belonging to minorities, from accessing content to which they have the right to access.

In the same vein, and as previously discussed, the concept of inappropriate content for minors should not have an expansive nature that regulates all aspects of digital content, such as cultural content, nor should it be established by commercial services or States based on ideology.  
Regarding the right to personal integrity, it is evident that the possibility for systems to locate minors via the Internet may pose a physical risk to their personal integrity, but it can also enable the identification of vulnerable adults, especially psychologically, who are being profiled based on their habits concerning certain content.  
Concerning the right to one's own image, the rights of citizens would be restricted in those protection systems that store or process personal images through facial recognition systems by the Internet service provider or third parties, when such processing is not technically necessary, and when it could be executed, in any case, on devices under the control of the users themselves.  
The right to legal capacity, as the ability to validly perform legal acts, exercise rights, and assume obligations, is closely linked to the identity and the possibility of identifying the person. Currently, much of the legal capacity must be developed in the digital environment. Therefore, this right could be violated in systems that limit legal capacity based on service conditions and without a legal foundation.  
Regarding the right to non-discrimination, among other examples, these systems must not prevent access to content for older adults or any other group simply due to the choice of certain technological options that do not accept diversity. It should also not be allowed for verification systems or access to additional identity information to permit or implement biases based on gender, race, age, nationality, etc.  

PRINCIPLE 10:  
Every system for the protection of minors against inappropriate content must have a defined governance framework  
Every system for the protection of minors against inappropriate content must have a defined governance framework to ensure compliance with these principles, the protection of fundamental rights, and to articulate the participation of those who hold parental authority, educational institutions, associations and foundations for the protection of minors, researchers and privacy experts, the State, or technology and service providers in the digital society, among others.  
To this end, the governance framework must ensure that the protection system is implemented and deployed with technologies that preserve privacy. It must also meet a minimum level of effectiveness, assuming that no technological system is perfect. This effectiveness must be evaluated objectively and with a critical spirit, including in the analysis the collateral effects on individuals and society. The system, in its use and operation, must be transparent to users, particularly regarding the anonymity of browsing and the criteria for content limitation, in addition to being effectively auditable by authorities and independent third parties.  
A system that does not have minimum effectiveness will not meet the suitability requirements for processing. A clear example is those systems based on the age declaration made by the user, which have only served to provide purely formal legal guarantees to Internet service providers. Another example is some of the parental control systems currently in service, which in many cases have not been properly validated for their robustness against manipulation. Also, those systems that generate distrust and are rejected by the citizenry.

The collateral effects that the application of protection systems for minors against inappropriate content may have must be evaluated critically. In particular, it is necessary to assess the impact that personal data breaches may have on each of the intervening entities. This will be particularly serious when services from third-party entities, whether for identity or age verification, accreditation of the status of "authorized person to access," as well as content filtering, suffer security breaches, corruption, or blackmail for credential theft or intervention by States.  
Other collateral effects may occur when protection systems discriminate against individuals who, for any reason, cannot or are not able to use those systems, or when the systems do not interact correctly with them due to the incorporation of some bias. In particular, this relates to individuals with functional diversity or older adults.  
The protection system requires having different options, overlapping or not, that respond to the various platforms and social situations. It must also consider its integration with current and future national and European identity management systems, such as the Digital Wallet established in the proposed eIDAS218.  
A key aspect for effectiveness is the trust of users, who cannot be required to have blind faith in technological services. Therefore, the systems must be configured to be auditable both by supervisory authorities and by independent research centers fully competent to carry out such tasks. The auditability of these systems and the platforms on which they operate must allow for obtaining evidence that there is no manipulation, lack of diligence, that profiling or tracking of individuals' activities is not possible or being carried out, that there is no discrimination, that they are not vulnerable systems, and that all principles, rights, and obligations established in the GDPR are applied, particularly the principle of proactive responsibility. The concentration of a few services from third-party entities that are not accessible to independent supervision for their audit increases the impacts of breaches, collateral effects, and the difficulty of effective supervision.  

IV. CONCLUSIONS  
The protection of minors is the responsibility of society as a whole. All its participants must form the network of care, defense, assistance, and support for the minor in their journey towards development as an adult. The protection of minors is complex and extends to many more aspects than just filtering content on the Internet. Seeking simplifications based on "technological solutions" that ignore basic social dysfunctions could exacerbate the problems they create.  
Technology can be a great support in the defense of minors if it is integrated into a general protection framework involving all social participants: families, authorities, educational institutions, associations and foundations for the protection of minors, researchers and privacy experts, technology providers, and digital society services, etc.  
Perfect technology does not exist. However, there is suitable technology that acts harmoniously with educational, cultural, social, responsibility, and security elements to effectively protect the best interests of minors and the fundamental rights of citizens.  
The education of minors, particularly in the digital environment, and the selection of appropriate content is a shared responsibility among families, governments that must promote effective public policies and protective regulations in this area, and the industry. Urgent measures for the protection of children and youth in the digital environment are needed, in line with the actions proposed in the aforementioned State Pact.
