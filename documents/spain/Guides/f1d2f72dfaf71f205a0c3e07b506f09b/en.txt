**II. INTRODUCTION**  
Before implementing technological solutions to confront COVID-19, it is essential that they are integrated within the framework of a strategy of realistic, effective legal and organizational measures, based on scientific criteria, legitimate, and proportional.  
Proportionality is established through an analysis of the cost and benefit to society and the rights and freedoms of the individual. The benefit must be measured in terms of reduced infection spread globally, with the possibility of regaining freedom of action, and protecting the health of individuals.  
Health data holds significant value, thus it is necessary to prevent abuses by third parties that could exploit the uncertainty caused by an emergency situation, leading to loss of freedoms, discrimination, or other harms to citizens' personal situations.  

This document will provide a brief analysis, for educational purposes and without aiming to be exhaustive in its development, of some technologies in the fight against COVID-19, the benefits they promise to bring in combating the pandemic, and the costs to individuals' privacy that they may entail, particularly:  
• Geolocation through information collected by telecommunications operators  
• Geolocation on social networks  
• Apps, websites, and chatbots for self-testing or prior appointment  
• Apps for collecting information on infections  
• Contact tracing apps  
• Digital immunity passports  
• Infrared cameras

**III. GEOLOCATION OF MOBILE PHONES BY TELECOMMUNICATIONS OPERATORS**  
This technique consists of mobile phone operators providing anonymized information about the location of their users in the cell areas defined by their antennas. Operators typically collect positioning data from their subscribers, which they calculate based on the strength of the signals received from each mobile phone at various antennas in an area. With this information, which is necessary to provide the service, an operator can estimate which phone numbers are in each cell at a given moment and even provide an approximate location of any active mobile phone in a cell. This information, when not anonymized, can be requested by the Security Forces and Corps, always requiring a judicial order. On the other hand, its anonymized use has been employed by the National Statistics Institute for mobility studies. During the management of the COVID-19 crisis, the Government and the European Commission have requested that operators provide this type of anonymized information to observe population movements.  

**DO THESE DATA REPRESENT A THREAT TO PRIVACY DURING THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?**  
With careful management, appropriately anonymized access to this information should not pose a greater threat than it did before. That is to say, there is always the possibility of incomplete anonymization, lax subcontracting, or a cyberattack that could put the location of users' mobile phones in the hands of a third party, although this risk existed prior to the pandemic. By making greater use of these anonymized data, there may be a higher risk, but not exponentially greater.

**DO THESE DATA REPRESENT A SIGNIFICANT BENEFIT IN THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?**  
Understanding the mobility patterns of the population, seeing where people move when they work or on weekends, can be beneficial for an administration at any time. Consider that infrastructure, healthcare, or police are elements that could be dynamically sized with a good mobility model. Think, for example, about how these factors are resized when an event occurs, such as a football match or a demonstration. However, it is necessary to continuously evaluate their usefulness against the changing scenarios of global or partial confinement.  
One derivative that has been pointed out is the possibility that anonymized geolocation data could be used to observe global movements, but with the possibility that the police could request re-identification in certain cases according to the criteria established by health authorities to ensure epidemic control.  

**IV.**  
**GEOLOCATION OF MOBILE PHONES BASED ON SOCIAL NETWORKS**  
The geolocation of mobile phones based on social media data is not a novelty. The IP addresses from which we access the Internet can be known by the administrators of web pages and are commonly used for advertising purposes. Some large providers such as Facebook4 or Google5 have recently published aggregated data in the form of large dashboards.  

**DO THESE DATA REPRESENT A THREAT TO PRIVACY IN THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?**  
To the extent that the processing locates the data subjects, this practice can be a threat to privacy, but it is not exclusive to this moment or this situation. This threat can be more critical if this information is enriched with personal information derived from user profile activity or if actions are taken based on their profiles. The terms of use and privacy policies of these services are not an adequate legal basis for carrying out these treatments.

**V.**  
**APPS, WEBS, AND CHATBOTS FOR SELF-TESTING OR PRIOR APPOINTMENTS**  
Within this broad group of solutions would be applications or apps6, websites, and chatbots that implement question-and-answer tests, information inquiries, or even registration for prior appointments in health services. In this document, they are grouped into the same category as they may be the least novel solutions from a technological point of view, although, on the other hand, they may be among the most used.  

**DO THESE APPLICATIONS REPRESENT A THREAT TO PRIVACY IN THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?**  
Depending on how they are designed and what their objectives are, threats to privacy may arise. These may appear due to the urgency in providing operational solutions that relax the controls and requirements to protect citizens' data. In some of these applications7, potential threats to privacy have been found in their implementation. On the other hand, it should not be forgotten that an app or a website is merely an interface to display and transmit data to a server, and it is there that the quiet work of addressing requests is done.  

**DO THESE DATA REPRESENT A SIGNIFICANT BENEFIT IN THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?**  
In general, when well-constructed, they do represent a great benefit as they bring health information and services closer to the people who use their services. As a secondary effect, they help to relieve traffic from other channels such as telephone,

VI.  
VOLUNTARY CONTAGION INFORMATION APPS (COVAPPS)  
This category would include some mobile applications that have emerged almost instantaneously, in some cases from citizen initiatives, and that aim to create their own maps and statistics of the spread of COVID-19 based on data voluntarily provided by users. In a very open and transparent environment, it appeals to selfless collaboration to download these applications and upload location data and data of possible infection, thus contributing to the creation of maps and dashboards with information that theoretically is not filtered by the authorities.  

**DO THESE APPLICATIONS REPRESENT A THREAT TO PRIVACY DURING THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?**  
These apps could be a threat if the stated purposes are not as altruistic as they promote or if the urgency leads to developments without guarantees for privacy. Let us not forget that what is being given to servers on the Internet are health data and precise locations. If the quantity and quality of this data were sufficient, thanks to a significant number of users, conclusions could be drawn such as neighborhoods with a high level of infection or toxic areas, with the social stigma that this may entail for their inhabitants or their businesses. That is to say, it is necessary to have a significant sample and that no one, maliciously, is providing false or manipulated information to benefit or harm the whole.

VII.  
BLUETOOTH CONTACT TRACING APPS (CONTACT TRACE APPS)  
This type of application uses the Bluetooth technology of mobile phones that allows connection with nearby devices such as headphones, speakers, or watches.  

In this case, the apps use Bluetooth to send the corresponding "card" to the user with the mobiles they encounter along their way, while also collecting the "cards" from those same mobiles. Each card does not have a real identification of the user, but rather a pseudonym of their identity. In this way, each mobile has an anonymous collection of "cards" of the people they have been in contact with at work, during transport, or in their leisure time. If a particular user finds out that they are infected, they have the option to "declare" it through the app to a central server. At that moment, it is assumed that those people with whom they were in contact in the last few days will receive a notification to evaluate what actions to take, such as self-isolating, contacting their health services, or getting tested.  
Several strategies have emerged to implement this technology, which basically vary in who has control over the identities and the network of contacts for each user. There are options where that control rests with the user themselves (decentralized), and others with centralized control, supposedly by an authority.  
In the technical debate about what type of solution will be applied in Europe, a new factor has recently entered, which is that the giants Google and Apple have allied to offer a tracking solution using their iOS and Android systems. **DO THESE APPLICATIONS DURING THE PANDEMIC CRISIS POSE A THREAT TO PRIVACY?**  
The main threats14 to the privacy of this type of solution come from the creation of relationship maps between individuals, re-identification through implicit location, the fragility of protocols when constructing "almost anonymous" cards, and the dispersion of contagion signals in such a way that the identity of the infected individuals is not identified in any case. It should be noted that the processing of information not only affects the application user but also all third parties with whom they have been in contact, so this processing must comply with data protection principles.  

There are studies15 on the robustness of cryptography and anonymization protocols, and there is always a possibility that, given enough time and computing power, they could be broken and associate anonymous nicknames with phone numbers and individuals. From a privacy perspective, the more computation is done on the server side, the less control users have, which is why centralized solutions always seem less respectful of privacy than distributed ones16 17. The possibility that, due to the accumulation of data in a centralized manner, an abuse could occur in an unethical company, that the purposes of processing could be expanded, or that one could fall victim to a cyberattack constitutes another of the greatest threats of this type of solution.  

**DO THESE DATA DURING THE PANDEMIC CRISIS REPRESENT A SIGNIFICANT BENEFIT?**  
Once again, it is necessary to highlight that technical solutions cannot be considered in isolation. The success of such solutions is based on many factors that do not depend on technology. Firstly, the involvement of a large number of users is necessary; some studies18 suggest at least 60% of a population, which, taking into account children and the elderly, accounts for almost all mobile users. On the other hand, it depends on a responsible declaration of personal infection status, preferably supervised by a professional to avoid misinformation strategies. Finally, access to tests is necessary, not only for all users but also to be able to update information periodically and for those who are notified of having been in contact with an infected person to be able to get tested promptly.

VIII.  
IMMUNITY PASSPORTS  
In some countries, the use of apps equivalent to what would be a passport or a paper safe conduct has begun to be considered, displaying on screen a color code or a QR code, so that a guard or an access control system can allow or deny entry to the bearer. This procedure is similar to that used with boarding passes at airports, except that instead of indicating that a user has a valid plane ticket or is on a list, it reveals whether the bearer is infected or presumably immune due to having had the disease.  

DO THESE APPLICATIONS REPRESENT A THREAT TO PRIVACY DURING THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?  
These mobile applications are anticipating what could be a future identity document on mobile devices, with the added risk of including and displaying health data, and encompassing all the risks arising from the vulnerabilities of such systems: access by cybercriminals, cross-referencing with other data such as location, incorporation of metadata, remote reading, or simply not being accessible to many people who cannot use smartphones.  
Unlike a boarding pass, the tests to determine whether a person is suffering from or has overcome the disease should be conducted in person, and the healthcare personnel performing it could provide the user with a paper certificate or any low-tech support to show when required, along with their identity document. A mobile identity system can only have advantages when registration can be done remotely or if the information it manages changes rapidly, as in the case of a digital wallet, which is not the case.  

DO THESE APPLICATIONS REPRESENT A SIGNIFICANT BENEFIT DURING THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?  
The immunity passport incorporates sensitive data, such as any health data, but it has also been tasked with serving as a safe conduct for access. There are reports that advocate for an advancement of mobile health applications (mHealth) allowing, for example, a patient to carry their medical history to show to a doctor and receive treatment. Likewise, the exercise of certain activities such as jobs or intense physical activities may require the candidate to show a medical certificate before gaining access. A well-managed use of apps for certifications or health records, which keeps them updated, secure, and interoperable, will have certain utility in specific areas as long as access to such information is carried out by personnel linked to the fulfillment of purposes related to public policies for controlling the pandemic. However, as with all applications that require the use of smartphones and the evidence of a reliable test for infection or antibodies, we are far from reaching the entirety of the population, so we can only question the benefits they might have in very specific areas.  

IX.  
INFRARED CAMERAS FOR MASS TEMPERATURE READINGS  
In recent weeks, the debate around surveillance cameras with facial recognition has been overshadowed by the discussion around another type of camera that adds the capability to take the temperature of individuals crossing an area, often without requiring any action on their part. These cameras identify human faces using artificial intelligence algorithms, discriminate them from other elements appearing in the image, and reveal the approximate body temperature of each individual.

DO THESE SYSTEMS REPRESENT A THREAT TO PRIVACY DURING THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?  
The AEPD has already expressed its concern about the use of these devices and the need to have prior guidance from health authorities before proceeding with their installation. The use of cameras or other devices to record individuals' temperatures constitutes the processing of special categories of data that must respect the principles of legality, purpose limitation, and accuracy.  
The thermal camera and the collection of the data can only be understood as part of a larger processing operation, and a health data point cannot be taken from a person and processed spontaneously by any manager of a public place simply because they believe it is the best for their clients or users. In these cases, we will have a risk of discrimination, stigmatization, and perhaps public dissemination of health data. All of this can be exacerbated by the risk of leaks of sensitive information and the conflict with those individuals who perceive the measure as an infringement of their rights.  
In some environments, such as occupational risk prevention regulations, temperature taking could be useful within the framework of a broader treatment that includes other checks and additional guarantees that, in any case, respect the rights and freedoms established in the GDPR.  
DO THESE SYSTEMS REPRESENT A SIGNIFICANT BENEFIT DURING THE PANDEMIC CRISIS?  
Fever is one of the most likely clinical indicators associated with a symptomatic Coronavirus infection, but it must also be considered that the percentage of asymptomatic infections is high and that high temperature can be associated with other pathologies. Applying these measures without an established criterion from health authorities regarding what fever value is significant, which other symptoms should be checked, and with manipulation that may lack sufficient precision in the hands of unqualified personnel, the use of these systems could create a false sense of security that facilitates contact with individuals who are actually infected.  
X.  
CONCLUSION  
This document has provided a brief overview of the main technologies proposed in the fight against the pandemic, without intending to be a deep analysis of them, and with the purpose of compiling those options being considered to control its spread.  
Our society is at a critical turning point, not only due to the pandemic situation but also concerning the approach to the model of rights and freedoms. Therefore, it is essential to be particularly careful when taking measures that may have irreversible consequences and may be guided solely by urgency, fear, or, worse, other interests.
