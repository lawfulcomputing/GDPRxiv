**Report on the Implications of Addictive Patterns in the Processing of Personal Data**  
Entities that use the Internet to provide platforms, applications, and services process personal data. Such processing of personal data must have a legal basis, which is usually the execution of a contract established in the terms of service. The processing of personal data may have other bases; in this context, they are often the consent of the data subject or the satisfaction of the legitimate interests of the provider (in relation to security, for example).  
According to the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), “Although users receive some of these services upon payment of a fee, others are offered without the consumer having to make any payment, as they are financed through the sale of online advertising services that allow targeted advertising to be directed to the data subjects. The tracking of user behavior, with a view to offering this type of advertising, is usually carried out through means that the user is often unaware of and that may not be immediately obvious considering the nature of the service provided, which, in practice, makes it almost impossible for the data subject to make an informed choice about the use of their data.”  
With this model, the economic benefits of providers largely depend on the amount of time the user spends using their products, user engagement, and the amount of personal data collected both from the subject itself and from their network of personal contacts. An improvement in these factors can increase the return on investment as it allows for refining user segmentation for marketing and advertising, increasing their loyalty, or finding new ways to monetize personal data.  
For this reason, some Internet providers attempt to keep users on the platform, application, or service for as long as possible, influencing or manipulating their behavior, including additional operations in the processing of personal data based on deceptive and addictive design patterns.  
Deceptive patterns are considered interfaces and user experiences that lead users to make unintended, involuntary, and potentially harmful decisions regarding the processing of their personal data. Addictive patterns will be defined later in this document as characteristics, attributes, or design practices that determine a particular way of using digital platforms, applications, and services aimed at getting users to spend much more time using them or with a higher degree of engagement than is expected, convenient, or healthy for them. Both characteristics of a pattern, its deceptive and addictive nature, are closely related to each other, although they are not exactly the same.

Introduction  
The addition of operations that implement addictive patterns to the processing of personal data has implications for various aspects of personal data protection, such as the lawfulness of processing (particularly in relation to the conditions of consent or the prohibition on processing special categories of data), fairness and transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, data protection obligations by design and by default, and, in general, the application of the principle of proactive accountability.  
Regarding this principle of accountability, the use of such patterns raises concerns about the lack of awareness of the risks to the rights and freedoms of users that such operations entail.  
Their impact can be particularly severe on the right to physical and mental integrity of children and younger users, affecting their decision-making processes, social interactions, or mental well-being.  
In the European Union (EU), there is a growing concern about deceptive and addictive design patterns in online services.  
The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) addressed the issue of deceptive patterns in the document “Guidelines 03/2022 on deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces: how to recognise and avoid them.” These guidelines focus solely on deceptive patterns for specific use cases in social media and address their implications for fairness, transparency, accountability, data protection by design, and compliance with the GDPR.  
Furthermore, in this guidance, the EDPB establishes that data protection authorities are responsible for sanctioning the use of deceptive design patterns if they violate the requirements of the GDPR.  
The European Parliament adopted a resolution in December 2023 that explicitly addresses the addictive design of services and consumer protection in the EU single market. The resolution calls for a ban on addictive practices such as infinite scrolling or autoplay that encourage prolonged engagement, a shift from attention economy to ethical design, the introduction of a digital right to “not be disturbed,” and empowering users to control their online experiences and ensure that all platforms, applications, and online services are safe for children, as well as the introduction of new legislation for consumer protection specifically targeting addictive practices.  
Outside the EU, various countries and international bodies have also recognized the impact of addictive practices. For example, the United Nations has highlighted the need to address digital addiction and protect children's rights in the digital environment. However, regulations vary and are country-specific. Some have implemented guidelines or laws related to the addictive characteristics of technology, while others are still exploring practical approaches. For instance, the Stop Addictive Feeds Exploitation (SAFE) for Kids law, passed by the New York Legislature in 2023, establishes the Stop Addictive Feeds Exploitation (SAFE) for Kids act prohibiting the provision of addictive feeds to minors.

B. PROJECT CARRIED OUT BY THE AEPD  
Sections II and III of this document provide a summary of the results of the developed project.  
This project allows for a comprehensive analysis of the implications of including misleading and addictive patterns in the processing of personal data for data protection. Thanks to it, conclusions have been drawn at different levels regarding the implications of these patterns on data protection and on the rights and freedoms of individuals, which are summarized in Section IV.  
The development of addictive patterns begins with the concept of persuasive technology that was promoted in the 1990s. The idea was to leverage specific aspects of technological advancements to positively influence users, specifically in their everyday attitudes and behaviors: health, sleep, study, etc. The definition given by Fogg at that time was “interactive computer systems designed to change people's attitudes and behaviors without using coercion or deception.”  
Over the years, persuasive technology has become ubiquitous and has integrated into video games, social networks, and mobile applications. This evolution has been primarily motivated by the need of technology companies to prolong the time users spend on their platforms and services (indirect monetization through marketing and digital advertising) and to engage them with a higher level of commitment (direct monetization through purchases of various goods and services). Therefore, the definition of persuasive technology has changed. For example, it can be defined as “any information system that proactively affects human behavior, in favor of or against the interests of its users.”

Addictive Patterns: Definition and Context  
Previous works have identified different characteristics related to persuasive technology, such as the establishment of goals, feedback, reminders, alerts, triggers, rewards, points, credits, tracking, monitoring, or social support, exchange, and comparison. Most of these characteristics need to be personalized to function properly, which means that the provider must collect and process the personal data of users. For this reason, among others such as scalability or cost reduction, all these characteristics share their autonomy and high degree of automation. Advances in paradigms such as machine learning or artificial intelligence have greatly favored both aspects.  
Persuasive design, when applied to platforms, applications, and services, makes them addictive. A small set of persuasive characteristics aimed at manipulating users' attention is routinely included in many platforms, applications, and services as a design model or guideline, which turns them into patterns. Addictive patterns, to be precise.  
In this document, an addictive pattern is defined as:  
A characteristic, attribute, or design practice that determines a specific way of using digital platforms, applications, or services that seeks to make users spend much more time using them or with a higher degree of engagement than is expected, convenient, or healthy for them.  
Addictive patterns provoke more effects on users than merely increased usage time of platforms, applications, or services.  
These patterns can generate a higher degree of engagement, leading to dependency, which implies that users prefer their use over engaging in other activities, even essential ones, such as eating, sleeping, or interacting with other people, fostering, for example, disproportionate financial spending or sharing sensitive information.  
Problems can be observed in establishing personal relationships, fragmentation of attention, disruption in mental decision-making mechanisms, or a negative perception of life satisfaction, effects that are especially severe in childhood and adolescence.  
In almost all scenarios, users cannot deactivate these characteristics, even when they themselves perceive their influence or negative impacts.

A. DECEPTIVE AND ADDICTIVE PATTERNS  
There is a certain degree of intersection between the concept of deceptive interface (or dark pattern if translated directly from the English term dark pattern) and that of addictive pattern, which is worth clarifying at this moment. Let us consider, for example, three definitions that can serve to provide some initial context.  
The first of these is the one that has already been mentioned, which is provided in the guidelines of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB): “Deceptive design patterns are interfaces and user experiences implemented on social media platforms that lead users to make unintended, involuntary, and potentially harmful decisions regarding the processing of their personal data. They aim to influence user behavior and may hinder their ability to effectively protect their personal data and make informed decisions.”  
The second is the OECD's definition of the term dark pattern. Dark pattern is a general term that refers to a wide variety of practices commonly found in user interfaces that lead consumers to make decisions that are often not in their best interests. The definition given by the OECD is: “Dark patterns are business practices that employ elements of architecture for decision-making in the digital realm, particularly in online user interfaces, that undermine or harm the autonomy, decision-making, or choice of the consumer. They often deceive, coerce, or manipulate consumers and are likely to cause them direct or indirect harm in various ways, although in many cases it may be difficult or impossible to measure that harm.”  
And the third is the definition provided by the Digital Services Act in its preamble (recital 67): “Deceptive interfaces of online platforms are practices that deliberately or effectively distort or substantially diminish the ability of service recipients to make autonomous and informed decisions. These practices may be used to persuade service recipients to adopt unwanted behaviors or make unwanted decisions that have negative consequences for them.”  
Research conducted in the past provides definitions very similar to these three and focuses on the same aspects: deception, manipulation, influence, decision-making against one’s own interests, harm or potential negative consequences, lack of alternatives and information, etc.  
Therefore, we can conclude that all addictive patterns can be considered deceptive patterns, but not all dark patterns are addictive.

C. OPERATIONAL MODEL OF ADDICTIVE PATTERNS  
Many social media platforms offer targeting and segmentation services as part of their business model. These services allow individuals or organizations ("targeters") to convey specific messages to social media users to promote political or commercial interests. The critical aspect of segmentation and targeting is the alignment between the individual or target group and the message conveyed. The underlying concept is that the better this alignment, the higher the reception rate (conversion), which will lead to a more effective campaign (return on investment)18. In the case of addictive patterns, the "targeters" are the platform, application, and digital service providers themselves, not a third party. Their strategy does not seek to deliver a specific political or commercial message to a particular person or group, but rather to ensure that the user remains connected for a longer time or with a greater degree of engagement.  
The framework shown in Figure 2 is based on already validated models19 and includes the different elements related to addictive patterns and their context. Platforms, applications, and services are designed to maximize their benefits according to their specific business model. In this document, we will use the term addressability to refer to these ultra-personalized strategies developed for addictive purposes rather than political or commercial ones.  
Addressability, like targeting, makes users directly accessible to attract or influence them. In this case, through their weaknesses or vulnerabilities, as individuals or as members of a dynamic and small group. However, the techniques used, and the treatment of personal data involved, are similar to those of political or commercial targeting that have been much more analyzed until now. The European Data Protection Board (EDPB), in its document "Guidelines 8/2020 of the EDPB on the Targeting of Social Media Users," provides guidelines that are useful in the context of targeting carried out by operations implementing addictive patterns.  
The Stigler Committee20 concluded in 2019 that the business model of major online platforms is based on addictive user interfaces designed to maintain the attention of such users.

These addictive patterns can feed on users' personal data and can have an impact on their health and well-being. What specific data could be collected and what these impacts would be depends on who these users are, the reasons they use the platforms, applications, and services, and how they use them. Users with different mental, social, technological, and behavioral backgrounds may react differently to the same design feature21.  
Platform, application, and service providers have incentives to design their products in a way that performs well in terms of benefits and metrics relevant to their business model. According to these metrics, and given the current market pressure, they may achieve better results by employing addictive patterns22.  
All these providers monetize user engagement (time and commitment), primarily through advertising. Therefore, the metrics relevant to their business model are the standardized metrics within the advertising or digital marketing market23. These metrics identify the measures that providers must take to certify that an advertisement has been shown to a user and that they should be billed for it. They also allow providers to explore aspects related to design and content that optimize the values of these metrics.  
In summary, data represents two different types of value in this context:  
1. They allow providers to quantify their performance through standardized metrics and thus claim the corresponding payment from advertisers: audience measurement (unique users, visits, time spent).  
2. They allow providers to optimize their performance through precise knowledge of what they need to offer each user to keep them connected for as long as possible with the highest degree of engagement possible. In this case, there are no standardized metrics. Providers may need to know or infer, through different methods, data such as:  

◤ User data: Account name, email address, persistent identifiers or pseudonyms, language, age/date of birth, gender, etc.  

◤ Unique connection fingerprint: Device characteristics (processor, memory and graphics card, screen settings, audio settings, operating system version, time zone), browser characteristics (version, list of plugins, list of languages, user-agent, ad-blocking information, web storage mechanisms, screen resolution), IP address, geolocation, etc.

The benefits that providers obtain largely depend on the number of users, the amount of time each user spends online, their level of engagement, and the amount of data a user shares, directly or indirectly. Recent research has coined the term "data attention imperative." This imperative occurs in both directions. On one hand, providers accumulate and use data to design more engaging and addictive experiences for users. On the other hand, they are driven to increase engagement and attention to obtain more data and generate profits.  
The role of data, in this context, is to transform users' attention and behavior into a monetizable asset within a cyclical process: the data collected during users' interactions with platforms can be directly marketed, for example, to advertisers. But they can also be used to exploit users' vulnerabilities, gain their attention and engagement, and make them spend more time online.  
Available research points to psychological weaknesses or vulnerabilities and persistent, widespread cognitive biases that are often exploited by addictive patterns to achieve this goal, such as (this is not an exhaustive list):  

◤Affect Heuristic: Content that evokes positive emotions significantly influences the user's decisions.  

◤Anchoring: Users rely too heavily on the first information provided (the anchor) when making decisions.  

◤Automation Bias: Users tend to excessively trust automated or algorithmic systems.  

◤Default Effect: Users are unlikely to change default settings and configurations to uncheck a preselected checkbox, etc.  

◤Effort Justification: Users attribute a higher value to an outcome if they have had to exert effort to achieve it.  

◤Framing Effect: Users draw different conclusions from the same information depending on how that information is presented.  

◤Illusion of Control: Users tend to overestimate their degree of influence over external events.  

◤Instant Gratification: Users tend to sacrifice future gains for immediate pleasure or profit.  

◤Commitment: If the user is engaged with the task, they want to see the result or the end.

CLASSIFICATION OF ADDICTIVE PATTERNS

This section proposes a classification of addictive patterns following the most recent three-level ontology (high, medium, low) in the literature that allows for the creation of a shared vocabulary on this topic. In the proposed classification, the so-called high-level patterns correspond to the most abstract knowledge, referring to general strategies independently of context and application.  
Four high-level patterns have been identified:  

Forced Action  
Social Engineering  
Interface Interference  
Persistence  
Medium-level patterns describe more specific approaches to follow these strategies, exploiting the psychological weaknesses or vulnerabilities of specific users, but they are still independent of context and application.  
Finally, low-level patterns correspond to the specific execution of the different approaches and are often context- or application-specific. The latter can be detected through algorithmic methods, manual means, or other technical methods.

For the classification, different patterns have been merged or divided to avoid redundancies or gaps, and an effort has been made to unify the nomenclature.  
The classification provided is as comprehensive as possible, but new patterns could be found (especially low-level ones, as they are highly context-dependent and vary by platform, application, or service) or other different ones could be created.  
Furthermore, the already identified patterns will evolve in the future. That is, it is expected to be a living classification that will be reviewed over time.  
It should also be noted that some patterns tend to appear together, or the same pattern could appear in two different high-level categories. An effort has been made to make them as independent as possible from each other and to classify them always in a main category with which they are most related.

□ Forced Continuity  
This approach is directly related to users spending more time than expected connected or doing so at times that are not the best for them: even if there is coercion or pressure, users must perceive the prolongation of their session as something natural and fluid, freely decided.  

◤ Pull to Refresh: This is based on a gesture that consists of touching the screen with a finger and then lifting it, as a signal to the application to update the content of the screen in real time. It is often combined with the pattern of algorithmic recommendations to offer the user personalized content after each update that is more likely to capture their attention.  

◤ Infinite Scrolling: Users can scroll through content without limitations, and there is always more, instead of clicking on different pages. It is generally combined with the pattern of algorithmic recommendations to offer the user personalized content that is more likely to attract their attention.  

◤ Infinite Streaming: When a song or video ends, the next one begins without user interaction (similar content, the next episode, etc.). It is also often combined with algorithmic recommendations to offer the user personalized content that is more likely to attract their attention.  

◤ Timers: This pattern is based on making users wait for something for an arbitrary period. For example, some online games make users wait a certain amount of time before completing a task. Social media often also rewards constant participation: users are encouraged to log in during a specific arbitrary time period to earn rewards or complete challenges.  
Example of Timers:  
Let’s imagine a fantasy video game. The player wants to forge a legendary sword, and the game allows them to create it, but this task will take 4 hours to complete. The player cannot speed up the process; they can only wait.  
During these 4 hours, the player remains connected to the game. It may be running in the background, but it is likely that the player will periodically check the progress of the process.  
The player will need 1 day to upgrade the castle. To train troops, 2 days. And so on for other valuable resources.  
This is effective because it ensures retention. Once the timer ends, players will interact with the game to claim the results (the sword, the new castle, etc.). In some cases, this pattern allows for monetization. For example, the player can skip the waiting time using in-game currency (gems, coins). In this case, the pattern encourages engagement.  
If the timers are too long, too frequent, or related to resources that players perceive as “low value,” they may feel frustrated or lose interest. Game designers must achieve a balance between anticipation and annoyance. They profile players according to their habits (session duration, time of play, frequency of play, game preferences, spending history, etc.) to optimize all these features.

◤Scheduled Play: Users do not decide when to connect or play; other users or the provider decide for them. If a user does not connect or play according to this "appointment," they could be penalized. This pattern, as its name suggests, is commonly found in online video games. However, adult content platforms can make users wait for new content, social networks for a specific post from a prominent profile, or for the announcement of a promotion. Scheduled content releases, live broadcasts, or special events have effects similar to scheduled play: flash sales, live streams from influencers, etc.  
Example of Scheduled Play:  
Let’s imagine a brand that conducts a flash sale for 1 hour on a social network where users can access exclusive products and discounts. Users have had to interact sufficiently with the social network hosting the sale to know the specific timing of the promotion and connect during that time to access the promised benefits in advance. If the flash sales are not sufficiently disseminated among their target audience and they do not learn that they are happening, or they have the information but the timing is not convenient for them at all or does not motivate them enough, the performance metrics of this pattern will be poor. Campaign designers must find a way to ensure sufficient engagement, determining when and to whom certain content is shown. They profile social media users based on their habits and preferences to optimize all those characteristics related to dissemination (who receives the information, how, and when) and to the campaign itself (how long it lasts and at what time, how frequently it occurs, what products or discounts are offered, and how they are offered).  

□Gamification  
This approach is directly related to engagement, as it is based on offering the user the possibility to play or compete, something usually pleasurable for their brain.  

◤Guided Competition: This pattern is based on the fundamental mechanism of gamification, fostering competition against oneself, against other users, or against the algorithm (automated competitor) to promote a sense of achievement or reward. In many cases, very difficult objectives are set that require users to be connected for a considerable amount of time to win the proposed competitions or the game itself. Or to pay for some type of help or advantage. Social networks often propose competitions and challenges. But this is a very widespread pattern across all digital products.  

Role-playing games are also a common pattern within this category, where users do not compete as themselves but as fictional characters or avatars.  
Example of Guided Competition:  
Let’s imagine an online learning platform that has a leaderboard showing users with the best performance based on points, badges, or completion rates. Users are encouraged to compete for higher rankings, which can motivate them to participate more frequently or with greater commitment. Economic rewards can also be offered, either direct (in the form of money) or indirect (in the form of gift cards, material prizes, virtual money). Example of Grinding:  
Let’s imagine a role-playing video game on a farm. The player, acting as a farmer, needs to repeatedly feed the animals on the farm to progress, seemingly for arbitrary reasons. Each level of the game forces the player to complete more feeding cycles before being able to advance to other more enjoyable tasks proposed in the game. If the number of repetitive tasks required is too large or requested too frequently, players may feel frustrated or lose interest. Game designers must achieve a balance between engagement and boredom. They profile players based on their habits (session duration, play schedule, play frequency, game preferences, etc.) to optimize all these features.  

◤Endowment: Every time users visit the platform or use the application or service, investing time in it, they improve their situation or reputation (points, followers, trophies, level, etc.). This makes it very difficult for them to disconnect from the application or service, or even delete their account, because they would lose everything they have earned so far. For example, if you delete your account on a social network, you will have to start from scratch if you log back in the future. Many games have specific points or locations that players must reach before they can safely save their progress (without losing their achievements) and stop playing (this is sometimes referred to as the “Can’t stop or save” pattern in this context).  

◤Periodic Rewards: Some platforms, applications, and services provide a reward the first time they are used every hour, day, or week, for example. Conversely, if a user does not log in during a specific period, they are penalized. In some cases, streaks and rewards are established. For example, the “perfect week” if the application is used every day for a week.  

◤Completing the Collection: This pattern is based on offering users something to collect, such as badges, trophies, skins. Generally, different types of items or characters tend to have different associated values. Obtaining 100% of collectible items implies a high degree of commitment to the application or service. Sometimes collectible items are associated with point programs, which, once obtained, can be redeemed. This pattern is often used in online video games but also in learning or health applications. For example, when users complete a specific activity, they receive a badge.  

◤Grinding and Unnecessary Exposure: Users are required to perform easy or repetitive tasks, even just to stay connected or exposed to some content, to earn points, credits, or some type of reward. For example, users can be given extra lives if they watch ads. Many games have an automated mode that can be played without user intervention and that improves the player’s situation or reputation (through rewards) simply by keeping the game running on the device.

□ Scarcity  
This approach is related to time and commitment, based on creating a sense of limited availability, which motivates users to take immediate action.  

◤ High Demand: This pattern is based on showing a substantial need or desire for something by other users. The goal is to encourage hasty decisions and increased participation by compelling users to engage in something that many others like: users tend to follow what others do. Many social networks display messages of limited availability or offer content that is only available by invitation.  

□ Social Approval  
This approach is related to time and commitment, based on creating illusions of popularity, credibility, consensus, support, or any indication that other users find value or importance in what a person does or shares.  

◤ Endorsement, Reaction, and Social Reward: This pattern is based on stimuli that provoke positive experiences involving other users, including a wide variety of verbal and non-verbal expressions or gestures such as praise, a smile, a thumbs up, applause, good reputation, and all their digital equivalents. For example, the "Like" button, "Repost" buttons, and "Heart," etc. Users can also send reactions to content, respond with messages or images, or comment on almost all social networks. Or gain followers. Lenses and filters offered as tools or functionalities that allow altering appearance, editing photographs and videos, generating "masks" for characters, etc., can also be classified within this pattern.  

◤ Social Pressure or Comparison: This pattern is related to the previous one, but in this case, it appeals to the negative aspect of the social element. It is based on implicit competition, not directly proposed by the provider (as in Guided Competition) but provoked by the competitiveness that can arise among users for having better social statistics (more Likes, followers, etc.). In almost all social networks, users can see, as they are usually public data by default, the followers that other accounts or profiles have, the comments and interactions they generate, etc.  
Example of High Demand:  
Let’s imagine a social network that offers exclusive content to a limited number of users. Some users may feel compelled to register as soon as they see how the number of available invitations decreases rapidly over time, even if they were not initially interested.  
Designers must find a way to ensure sufficient participation. They profile social network users based on their habits and preferences to optimize all features related to dissemination (who receives the information, how, and when) and the exclusive content itself (how often it is offered, what type of content).

◤Activity Notifications: This pattern is based on displaying advertisements of user activity. For example, “Alicia has a new post” on almost all social networks, or “Alicia liked this post.” In this case, the pattern is directly related to the first one in this category, as what is usually notified is the receipt of some type of social reward, in real time, thereby amplifying its impact. However, notifications can also be used to compare activity streaks with other users (“Bob is posting a lot this week”) or to show users their lack of participation (“You haven't posted in a long time...”). A different type of activity notification is shown in real time within messaging applications, notifying users that their contact is typing (“…”, “is typing”). Messaging applications use this pattern to keep users on the screen waiting for a response, for example.  
Example of Activity Notifications:  
Let’s imagine a social network designed to produce activity notifications only for content updates on users' favorite accounts, or only for important content updates (with interaction above a threshold). However, there may be users who might be interested in knowing any slight change in the profile of someone they follow, in all new content, etc., or who want a “summary” notification every so often.  
Users can customize, in some aspects, the type of notification they wish to receive. The rest of the settings are done automatically. A high notification frequency can generate greater connection time or engagement among specific user groups (new users, young people, etc.). But other users may feel annoyed when they see many notifications.  
Designers analyze user data that shows behavior, preferences, habitual use, or location, for example, to personalize the content and frequency of notifications and achieve the desired results in all cases.  

□Urgency  
This approach is related to time and engagement, based on generating false urgency and loss aversion, with an impact on users' decision-making under the pretext of emergency.  

◤Alert Messages and Push Notifications: This pattern is based on proposing urgent actions or showing warnings that exaggerate the consequences of not doing something to pressure users into taking specific actions. Almost all social networks use this type of message to alert users when they receive direct communication, for example.  
Example of Alert Messages:  
Let’s imagine a social network designed to automatically produce an alert message as a reminder of a friend's birthday.  
Designers analyze users' contacts, collect their birth dates, and decide the best time to produce the alert message for this specific user. If the alert is repeated because the user ignores it the first time, in that case, they decide the text to include in subsequent alerts, etc.

◤Countdown: This pattern is based on the same principles as the previous one but shows a countdown in fixed units from an arbitrary starting number to mark the time remaining before an event. Different platforms, applications, and services use this type of timer before a flash sale, a live event, or the expiration of a deadline as a streak that breaks if the user does not log in or complete a task.  
Example of a countdown:  
Let’s imagine a learning platform designed to show users a countdown timer with the time they have left to complete a lesson or pass an exam if they do not want to lose a badge or any other reward at stake.  
A high frequency of use of this pattern can generate greater connection time or engagement among specific user groups (new users, committed students, etc.). But other users may feel annoyed when they see countdown timers too frequently.  
Designers analyze user data showing behavior, preference, habitual use, or location, for example, to personalize the use of this pattern and achieve the desired results in all cases.  

□Guilt  
This approach is related to time and engagement, and is based on inducing feelings of guilt or shame in users.  

◤Limited-time messages: This pattern is based on offering users a limited window of opportunity to perform specific actions. It may be related to High Demand, Alert Messages, Countdown, or patterns within the Fear of Missing Out category. However, in this case, when the window of opportunity is not taken advantage of, guilt is primarily used with messages such as “Your friends have missed you,” “Your followers have been waiting for your response,” “You missed something important,” etc.  
Example of limited-time messages:  
Let’s imagine a social network where messages and shared content are only available for a brief period before becoming inaccessible.  
If the recipient of these ephemeral messages does not read or open them in time, the sender, creator, or originator will know.  
Designers analyze user data that shows behavior, preferences, habitual use, or location, for example, to personalize the use of this pattern and achieve the desired results in all cases.  
The main decision in this pattern is the optimal length of the time window for each user/content.

□Fear of Missing Out  
This approach (FOMO in English, Fear Of Missing Out) is based on exploiting the anxiety or discomfort caused by the fear that others are experiencing something more interesting elsewhere. This fear leads users to remain constantly connected (time-related approach), for fear of missing something important.  

◤Regression to the Mean: This pattern is based on offering users the most popular or viral content at different times and locations, so that, although they consume it repeatedly, it seems positive to them because they are guaranteed not to miss it. Almost all social networks use this pattern, repeating specific content to ensure that users have the opportunity to see it and pay enough attention to it.  
Example of Regression to the Mean:  
Imagine a social network where the most popular content is offered among the rest of the content, but is periodically repeated, for example, over 24 hours. Always labeled as “trending.” Additionally, there is a specific tab for this type of content that summarizes what is most popular on this network at this moment.  
A good use of this pattern can lead to increased connection time or engagement among specific user groups. However, other users may feel annoyed or bored if they see the same content too frequently.  
Designers analyze user data showing behavior, preference, habitual use, or location, for example, to personalize the use of this pattern and achieve the desired results in all cases.  

◤Information Refresh: This pattern is based on the constant updating of content, in real-time or streaming. The user perceives that during the time they are disconnected, they will miss all those updates, among which there will surely be something interesting or important. Almost all social networks use this pattern, combined with algorithmic recommendations to offer the appropriate content to each user.  
Example of Information Refresh:  
Imagine a social network where users' timelines are automatically updated every minute and a counter indicates the number of new posts or content that has not yet been seen.  
A good use of this pattern can lead to increased connection time or engagement among specific user groups. However, other users may feel overwhelmed by the speed of information updates. Even with automatic updates, if it is too fast, it can interrupt them while enjoying some content.  
Designers analyze user data showing behavior, preference, habitual use, or location, for example, to personalize the use of this pattern and achieve the desired results in all cases. The main decisions in this case are the refresh frequency and the default automatic refresh settings. Additionally, the inclusion of counters or labels drawing attention to new or unseen content.

□ Personalization  
The success of most patterns that depend on personal data lies in personalizing the configuration of that pattern to find the perfect balance that keeps the user connected for as long as possible with the highest degree of commitment possible without feeling annoyed by these patterns. Personalization can be offered in different ways.  

◤ Confirmshaming: This pattern is strongly related to the category of Guilt, but it has been included here because it is based on manipulative personalization, using emotional language (often combined with the pattern of Persuasive Language) and psychological pressure on a specific user to confirm a specific action or task in a specific context. Different learning and health applications utilize this pattern, for example, “Are you leaving so soon?” when the user's session is shorter than usual. Social networks and online games also rely on this pattern.  
Example of confirmshaming:  
Let’s imagine a video game designed to make the player feel guilty or ashamed for rejecting other players' invitations to join them in a game.  
Designers analyze user data that shows behavior, preferences, usual usage, or location, for example, to personalize the use of this pattern and achieve the desired outcomes in all cases. The main decision is which actions or tasks it is appropriate to use the pattern and how to combine it with others, such as those related to language use, to amplify its effects.  

◤ Social Connectors: This pattern is based on making online experiences more enjoyable by sharing them with friends or family. Additionally, some providers offer users a reward when they invite new users or connect with them from their accounts (social pyramid schemes). Furthermore, social connection amplifies the impact of patterns in the categories of Social Approval, Guilt, and Fear of Missing Out (FOMO). Almost all social networks offer the functionality to retrieve users' contacts from their address books and from other networks or repositories. This is also to locate them online and invite them if they do not yet have an account. All these functions are periodically offered so that online contacts are updated at the same pace as real-life relationships. Combined with algorithmic recommendations, this pattern offers new contacts. With Alert Messages and Push Notifications, it notifies when a contact that may interest the user joins the network, for example.  
Example of social connectors:  
Let’s imagine a social network designed to keep users in touch with their friends and family, so that daily life, important events, etc., can be shared with all of them.  
To achieve this, both at the account creation stage and after that moment, it offers a multitude of static and dynamic mechanisms that allow for obtaining data to locate friends and family in that same network.  
The necessary data to locate each user's closest contacts and enable their connection in the network.

◤Algorithmic Recommendations:  
This pattern is often combined with many of those already mentioned, as it is based on personalizing user experiences to increase their engagement. Therefore, algorithmic recommendations reinforce user behavior, amplifying the impact of other addictive patterns. Recommendations are usually based on past behaviors, user profiles (habits, preferences), and current topics. Their objective is to recommend content, social connections, or specific activities. Before the introduction of algorithmic recommendations, users had to autonomously explore platforms, applications, and services, finding for themselves the functionalities, contacts, or content they liked the most. Algorithmic recommendations significantly influence users' decision-making, with a specific emphasis on time and engagement. This is the pattern that makes the most intensive use of personal data, as the more providers know about the user, in all possible dimensions, the more tailored (to their taste) the recommendations will be, and the more effective they will be in increasing connection time and level of engagement. All social networks use this type of algorithmic recommendation, as do streaming platforms, online learning services, etc. Different recommendation algorithms attempt to optimize different metrics36: weighted averages of all types of interaction that a piece of content can have; expected viewing time, a combination of likes, comments, and playback time, etc. For example, the recommender may focus on suggesting content that is predicted to be very likely to be liked by the user at the moment they are expected to log off, thus trying to extend their duration.

□ Zeigarnik Effect / Ovsiankina Effect  
This approach is based on the user perceiving that they have unfinished tasks, helping to generate a certain discomfort in the user because these tasks remain in their mind longer than completed tasks. These effects can be exploited to increase connection time and user engagement.

IMPLICATIONS FOR DATA PROTECTION  
4.  
The analysis conducted in the previous sections shows how the processing of users' personal data on numerous platforms, applications, and services includes specific operations to increase their connection time or level of engagement, thereby influencing their decisions and using their personal data for this purpose or to generate new data and perform targeting. All these operations have different implications for data protection, which are discussed in this section.  
As previously mentioned, the greater the connection time and level of engagement, the more personal data the provider can collect. This means that, in addition to some addictive patterns being fed by personal data, the result of including this type of operation in the processing (those carried out to provide digital services or products) could result in collecting more personal data or processing it to a greater extent.  
The processing of personal data carried out by the provider of the platform, application, or service is specified in the terms and conditions of service or in its privacy policy and has one or more legal bases that legitimize it. However, the operations related to addictive patterns occur within the framework of that processing. The addictive pattern is not executed in isolation but involves additional operations that determine how the processing is carried out, extending over time, generating more data, conditioning the user's decisions, etc. Among its consequences, new risks for the rights and freedoms of users are generated, or existing ones are aggravated.

Table 1 summarizes the classification of addictive patterns proposed in the previous section. For each pattern, an estimate of the intensity of personal data consumption, its capacity to collect or generate personal data, and the potential breaches of the GDPR is provided.  
In preparing this table, the concept of “Intensity of Personal Data Consumption” has been introduced as the degree or extent to which a specific pattern relies on personal data to achieve its objectives. NA means Not Applicable, because in principle that pattern does not need to consume personal data to function properly; the symbols +, ++, and +++ have been used to indicate low, medium, and high intensity respectively. It can be observed that the patterns within the categories Forced Action and Social Engineering consume users' personal data to achieve their objectives. Some of them do so intensively, as is the case, for example, with algorithmic recommendations.  
The personal data processed in this context of addictive patterns can be of four types:  

◤ Provided actively or directly by the user: User data mentioned in section II, information provided when creating an account, writing a profile, through a contact form, etc., such as name, age, gender, or email address.  

◤ Provided passively or indirectly by the user: Unique connection footprint mentioned in section II, information provided during interaction with the platform, application, or service related to devices, browsers, IP addresses, or geolocation. But also, User Behavior and User Habits and Preferences, such as created content, shared content, or liked content, contacts made or consents granted, duration of sessions, days and times of connection, etc.  

◤ Provided by external sources: User data, User Behavior, or User Habits and Preferences coming from third parties outside the platform, for example, data intermediaries or data management providers.

**A. PROACTIVE RESPONSIBILITY (ACCOUNTABILITY)**  
According to Article 24, paragraph 1 of the GDPR, data controllers must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that processing is compliant with the Regulation; these measures must take into account the risks of varying likelihood and severity to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.  
The EDPB has already established that “the reference to ‘the rights and freedoms’ of data subjects mainly concerns the rights to data protection and privacy, but may also involve other fundamental rights such as freedom of expression, freedom of thought, freedom of movement, the prohibition of discrimination, the right to liberty, and freedom of conscience and religion.”37 Furthermore, “the risk-based approach goes beyond a narrow ‘harm-based approach’ that focuses solely on the harm caused and must take into account all potential and actual adverse effects, assessed on a very broad scale ranging from an impact on the individual affected by the processing in question to a general social impact (for example, loss of social trust).”38

These risks must be addressed and are unlikely to successfully overcome the required assessment of necessity and proportionality established in Article 35, paragraph 7, letter b) of the GDPR.

B. DATA PROTECTION BY DESIGN AND BY DEFAULT  
C. TRANSPARENCY  
Providers that employ addictive patterns do not comply with their obligation of data protection by design and by default, as described in Article 25 of the GDPR. This article establishes that the data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with this Regulation. To this end, they must take into account the risks of varying likelihood and severity to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, as well as other circumstances such as the context and scope, for example, whether such processing affects children.  
The implementation of misleading and addictive patterns in data processing is a design decision contrary to the obligation of data protection by design and by default. The principle of transparency is established in Article 5, paragraph 1, letter a), of the GDPR. However, providers often are not transparent, open, or clear with users about how they will process their personal data to enhance the performance of addictive patterns or how they will carry out targeting using such patterns. Users are typically not informed of the additional purposes of processing (to feed addictive patterns or to derive benefits from them), of the potential risks to their rights and freedoms (consideration 39 of the GDPR), of the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling, of the logic involved, as well as the significance and expected consequences of such processing for the data subject (Article 13, paragraph 2, letter f and Article 14, paragraph 2, letter g of the GDPR).  
As a result, users may not fully understand the implications of such processing and may be unable to exercise their rights enshrined in Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR and meet the conditions for consent, especially when they are children or young people.

D. LEGITIMACY  
The processing of personal data must be lawful to comply with the GDPR.  
This means that personal data must be processed based on one of the legitimate bases established in Article 6 of the GDPR. Among those numbered in that article, the ones that could initially be considered for the legality of including addictive patterns in the processing of personal data are the consent of the data subject, the performance of a contract, or legitimate interest.  
However, the consent of the data subject would not be valid if they are not adequately informed, if consent for additional processing operations (such as addictive operations) is not provided through a clear affirmative act (Recital 32 of the Regulation), if the performance of the contract, including the provision of the service, is conditioned on consent to the processing of personal data that is not necessary for the execution of that contract, or if Article 8 of the GDPR regarding the conditions applicable to the consent of children in relation to information society services is not complied with.  
If the processing is carried out within the framework of the performance of a contract to provide a service, in which it is claimed that addictive patterns are an inherent part of it. In this sense, Article 6(1)(b) of the GDPR only applies when the processing in question is objectively necessary for the performance of the contract with the data subject or the processing must be objectively necessary for the application.  
This article does not cover processing that is useful but not objectively necessary to execute the service that is the subject of the contract or to apply the relevant pre-contractual measures at the request of the data subject, even if they are necessary for the other commercial purposes of the data controller. The expression "necessary for the execution" clearly indicates that a mere contractual clause is not sufficient.  
The distinction between the different types of data that a provider may collect or infer is important when determining the roles in each processing (controller, processor) and therefore the obligations that such roles imply in terms of transparency or exercise of data protection rights. For example, to meet the requirements of Article 15, paragraph 1 of the GDPR and ensure full transparency, providers must implement mechanisms for data subjects to verify all their personal data, including data provided by external sources or inferred, the sources, and the methods. The data subject has the right to access the data used as input for the different addictive patterns, as well as the rest of the information required by Article 15 of the GDPR.

E. LOYALTY  
The principle of loyalty established in Article 5, paragraph 1, letter a), of the GDPR is the starting point for assessing the existence of addictive design patterns.  
Loyalty is a general principle that requires personal data not to be processed in a manner that is harmful, discriminatory, unexpected, or misleading to the data subject, as already stated by the EDPB.  
The principle of loyalty includes, among other things, the recognition of reasonable expectations of the data subjects, the consideration of the possible adverse consequences that the processing may have on them, and the consideration of the relationship and the possible effects of the imbalance between them and the data controller.  
Providers often use addictive practices to process users' data in ways that contradict their reasonable expectations and may involve unexpected or unwanted uses of personal data. This may include manipulative language or designs. Furthermore, addictive patterns could have harmful and detrimental effects on users, particularly concerning the risk to the fundamental right to physical and mental integrity.  
As data subjects, they are often not given sufficient autonomy or decision-making capacity to determine how their personal data is used, or the scope and conditions of the processing of such data.  
In some cases, users may be compelled to use platforms, applications, and services that employ addictive patterns, as there are no options in the digital market free from them.  
As already mentioned, the processing of personal data involved in addictive patterns may involve merging personal data from external sources with data provided by the user to the provider's platform, application, or service, and allowing for inferences to be made.  
As a result, personal data could be used for purposes that go beyond the original and in ways that individuals could not have reasonably foreseen.  
The principle of loyalty has a general function, and all addictive patterns are incapable of fulfilling it, regardless of compliance with other data protection principles.

H. PROCESSING OF SPECIAL CATEGORIES OF DATA  
The purpose of data collection must be clearly and concretely identified: it must be detailed enough to determine which processing activities are included and which are not included in the specified purpose, and to allow for the assessment of compliance with the regulations and the application of the safeguards related to data protection. CONCLUSION  
5.  
Providers offering platforms, applications, and services process personal data of their users.  
In many cases, the business model of these entities leads them to attempt to prolong user sessions when using their products or to increase their level of engagement and the amount of personal data collected about them.  
All these factors would have a positive impact on the return on investment made in the development and maintenance of applications and services that are theoretically offered for free or at a very low cost.  
For this reason, some providers add additional operations to the processing of personal data of their users to implement deceptive and addictive design patterns aimed at manipulating users' actions and decisions.  
The European Data Protection Board has already addressed deceptive patterns in the document “Guidelines 03/2022 on deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces: how to recognize and avoid them.” These guidelines focused on such deceptive patterns for a series of use cases in social media.  
The AEPD, within the framework of its obligations, has conducted a systematic review of the existing scientific evidence regarding addictive patterns across different platforms, applications, and services (social networks, but also video or music platforms, adult content, games, learning environments, health and wellness applications, etc.), which involves addressing, from a complementary perspective, new use cases. In this way, the EDPB guidelines can be complemented by a unified approach to deceptive and addictive patterns.  
Addictive patterns have been defined in this report as characteristics, attributes, or design practices that determine a specific way of using digital platforms, applications, or services that aims for users to spend much more time using them or with a higher level of engagement than is expected, appropriate, or healthy for them.

Conclusion  
The analysis conducted shows how the processing of users' personal data on numerous platforms, applications, and services includes specific operations, all of which are misleading, to increase their connection time or level of engagement, thereby influencing their decisions and utilizing their personal data for this purpose or to generate new data and perform targeting (which allows for a very fine degree of personalization of addictive strategies).  
This document proposes a classification of addictive patterns following a three-level ontology (high, medium, low). The so-called high-level patterns are general strategies independent of the context and the application, and four have been identified: Forced Action, Social Engineering, Interface Interference, and Persistence.  
The medium-level patterns describe more specific approaches exploiting the psychological weaknesses or vulnerabilities of specific users.  
Finally, the low-level patterns correspond to the specific execution of the different approaches and are often context- or application-specific.  
All these patterns could require personal data as input, collect or generate new personal data, or influence user behavior and decision-making within the framework of personal data processing.  
The incorporation of operations that implement addictive patterns into personal data processing has significant implications for various aspects related to data protection, such as proactive accountability, the effective application of data protection obligations by design and by default, transparency, legality, fairness, purpose limitation, data minimization, automated decisions that significantly affect users, or the processing of special categories of data.  
It also poses a risk to the rights and freedoms of all users. In particular, it affects the right to their physical and mental integrity, but it can also lead to discrimination, exclusion, manipulation, undermine individual autonomy, influence their thought processes, emotions, behavior, limit their freedom of information and expression, generate self-censorship, and affect autonomy and development. These consequences can be especially severe for children and younger users.  
The patterns described in this document are not exhaustive; new patterns dependent on the context and the platform, application, or service could be found, or different ones could be created. Moreover, the already identified patterns will evolve in the future. Therefore, it is necessary to continue developing this type of review of the available evidence and subsequent analyses for the effective protection of users in the digital environment.
