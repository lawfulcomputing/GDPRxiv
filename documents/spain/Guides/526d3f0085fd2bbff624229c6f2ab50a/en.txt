EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  
These guidelines aim to serve as a guide for conducting a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) within the framework of preparing the Regulatory Impact Analysis Report (MAIN), when legislative initiatives from Public Administrations, which fall under the competence of the AEPD, involve the processing of personal data.  
The DPIA of a regulation that proposes the processing of personal data must evaluate the impact that such processing has on the fundamental rights and freedoms of individuals taken individually and as a society. Therefore, it is not an evaluation of legal risk or compliance. The jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) and the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) indicates that necessity and proportionality in data protection regulation is a fact-based concept, rather than a merely abstract legal notion, and that processing must be considered in light of the specific circumstances surrounding each case, as well as the provisions of the measure and the specific purpose intended to be achieved. Therefore, the DPIA requires the application of a step-by-step methodology without automatism.  
This document is aimed at the bodies of the Public Administrations, which fall under the competence of the AEPD, that promote regulatory projects involving the processing of personal data to which Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation or GDPR), as well as Organic Law 7/2021, of May 26, on the protection of personal data processed for the purposes of prevention, detection, investigation, and prosecution of criminal offenses and the execution of criminal sanctions (L.O. 7/2021), applies. It is also directed at the Data Protection Officers (DPOs) of the aforementioned bodies in order to contribute to the performance of their advisory functions regarding such regulatory projects.

Keywords: Data Protection Impact Assessment, DPIA, suitability, necessity, proportionality, risks, rights and freedoms, data protection officer, DPO, Public Administrations, Regulatory Impact Analysis Report, MAIN, legislation.

I.  
INTRODUCTION  
The purpose of these guidelines is to serve as a guide for conducting a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) within the framework of the preparation of the Regulatory Impact Analysis Report (MAIN), when legislative initiatives of the Public Administrations, which fall under the competence of the AEPD, involve the processing of personal data.  
The DPIA must be carried out from the design of the regulation, as established by the Methodological Guide for the Preparation of a Regulatory Impact Report (R.D. 931/2017):  
• The Regulatory Impact Analysis is a continuous process that must allow for the adaptation of the regulation to minimize such impact.  
• It is not a mere procedure that must be fulfilled once a new regulatory proposal has been drafted.  
• Nor is it a procedure that is exhausted with the preparation of the Report.  
• The Report shall be prepared simultaneously with the drafting of the regulatory project, from its inception to its completion.  
This document is aimed at the bodies of the Public Administrations, which fall under the competence of the AEPD, that promote regulatory projects involving the processing of personal data to which Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation or GDPR), as well as Organic Law 7/2021, of May 26, on the protection of personal data processed for the purposes of prevention, detection, investigation, and prosecution of criminal offenses and the execution of criminal sanctions (L.O. 7/2021), applies. It is also directed at the Data Protection Officers (DPOs) of the aforementioned bodies in order to contribute to the performance of their advisory functions concerning such regulatory projects.

• EDPS: Guide to assess the proportionality of processing in legislative policies and measures.

II.  
PRIOR REQUIREMENTS FOR CONDUCTING THE DATA PROTECTION IMPACT ASSESSMENT  
Before conducting a DPIA, it is necessary to determine whether certain minimum requirements are met.  
A.  
DETERMINE THE EXISTENCE OF PERSONAL DATA PROCESSING  
The notion of personal data is very broad, as it includes any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person; an identifiable person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by means of an identification number or by one or more specific factors related to their physical, physiological, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity. Therefore, a name, a surname, a vehicle registration number, a telephone number, a passport number, an IP address, a profile linked to a person in any of the aforementioned areas, any other unique identifier, including data or sets of data that act as pseudonyms, and those linked to them, will be considered personal data.  
Personal data processing is understood as any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of enabling access, comparison, or interconnection, restriction, erasure, or destruction. (art. 4.2 GDPR and art. 5.b L.O. 7/2021). In the case of non-automated processing, data protection regulation applies when data is contained or intended to be included in a file.  
The processing of personal data for the purpose of implementing network security measures, information security, or others, in itself, constitutes personal data processing.  

Milestone: If the regulation does not propose or imply any processing of personal data, it is not necessary to carry out the DPIA.

The impact assessment of a regulation concerning data protection must be carried out in cases where the proposed legislative measure involves the processing of personal data. Any operation of data processing provided for by legislation implies a limitation of the right to the protection of personal data, regardless of whether such limitation may be justified.  
In turn, the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) has maintained that the storage by a public authority of data or information related to a person's private life amounts to a limitation of the right to respect for private life.  
The repeated case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) establishes that "to determine the existence of an interference with the fundamental right to respect for private life, it is irrelevant whether the information is sensitive or whether the affected individuals have suffered any kind of inconvenience."  
Distinct processing operations or the set of operations (that is, collection and other operations, such as storage or transfer or access to the data) may constitute independent limitations on the right to the protection of personal data and, where applicable, on the right to respect for private life.

D.  
DETERMINING THE QUALITY OF THE NORM FROM THE PERSPECTIVE OF DATA PROTECTION  
Any legislative measure that enables processing must comply with the premise of "provided for by law." This implies that it must be clear and precise, and its application accessible and predictable for its recipients, in accordance with the ECHR10, the CJEU11, and the Constitutional Court (TC)12. Therefore, the norm must clearly define, with precision and appropriately:  
1.- The purpose or purposes of the processing.  
The purpose of the processing must be ultimate. For example, biometric surveillance processing does not constitute an end in itself, but rather a means (among others) to implement an ultimate purpose such as state security, the security of facilities, or others. In the same sense, a technology is not an end, but a means.  
2.- The legitimacy of the processing  
Consent (6.1.a GDPR) is not, in general, the appropriate legal basis for processing established by norm13 due to the clear imbalance between the data subject and a responsible public authority, although in certain cases it may be required as an additional guarantee, provided that the requirements for consent in the GDPR are met, particularly that it is free by offering equivalent alternatives.  
3.- The description of the implementation14 of the processing in its relevant aspects, such as the operations and procedures determining the processing (for example, collection, storage, access, transmission, dissemination,…), the technologies proposed to implement the operations (artificial intelligence, cloud storage, biometrics, IoT, mobile devices, video surveillance,…), the existence of automated decisions, as well as the participation or possible participation of processors and/or sub-processors in various operations of the processing, among others.  
In section III.B of the guide Risk Management and DPIA, the elements that define the nature, context, scope, and purposes of a processing are developed.

III.  
DATA PROTECTION IMPACT ASSESSMENT  
The data protection impact assessment requires an evaluation based on objective facts. There must be a solid justification for the proposed measures capable of withstanding scrutiny. Therefore, the proposed measures must be based on research, statistics, evidence-based forecasts, etc.  
The depth and formality of the DPIA must be more exhaustive when there is a high risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects (WP248).  
The elements to be evaluated will be:  
• The limitations and risks to rights and freedoms.  
• Respect for the essence of the right.  
• The purpose.  
• The proportionality of the processing, including the assessment of suitability, necessity, and proportionality in the strict sense.

Evaluating each of these elements is not reduced to a mere assertion or statement of compliance. The evaluation is a process to rationally construct a conclusion based on the examination and study of concrete evidence.  
A.  
EVALUATE THE LIMITATIONS AND RISKS TO RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS  
Within the framework of the DPIA, the limitations and risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals that the processing, or the necessary processing to achieve the objectives of the regulation, may entail must be identified.  
The mere fact that a measure limits or poses risks to the exercise of these rights does not mean that the measure should not be proposed. However, the measure must be presented in such a way that it overcomes a DPIA, that is, that the risks to individuals have been adequately mitigated and that the analysis of suitability, necessity, and proportionality in the strict sense has been surpassed.  
The rights and freedoms of the data subjects mainly concern the rights to data protection and privacy, but they also refer to other fundamental rights (WP248), such as freedom of expression, freedom of thought, freedom of movement, the right to personal self-determination, the prohibition of discrimination (differential treatment among individuals), freedom of conscience and religion, the inviolability of communications, the right to effective judicial protection, the freedom to receive information, the right to assembly and demonstration, etc.  
A deeper analysis is necessary than simply determining whether there is processing of special categories of data. Those initiatives that involve processing in which artificial intelligence, automated decisions, biometrics, mass surveillance, large-scale centralization, mass data processing, data of minors, data of vulnerable individuals, etc., are involved in their implementation, could imply additional risks and unwanted collateral impacts.  
Considering that the processing carried out by public authorities affects large social groups, if not the entire society, the risks must be studied in two dimensions:  
• Risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals.  
• Risk to society itself (or to a representative group of it).  
It is important to note that the realization of a risk factor may have a lesser impact concerning the individual in question and, however, be significant or very significant concerning society as a whole. Some hypothetical examples are: damage to the electoral and political process (misuse of data for political manipulation); the creation of illegal profiles and discrimination, which provoke distrust towards public authorities; the "chilling effect" on freedom of expression from surveillance measures.

B.  
RESPECT FOR THE ESSENCE OF THE RIGHT  
The processing of personal data implies a limitation on the right to data protection. As established by Article 52.1 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, as well as the CJEU, the limitation must respect the essence of the right to data protection so that its fundamental elements are not rendered devoid of content, thereby preventing the exercise of this right.  
The assessment of respect for the essence of the right may, in some cases, require a profound legal analysis and be the most critical point of the DPIA.  
Milestone: If the essence of the right were to be affected, the measure would be illegal and would need to be reformed before continuing the DPIA.

D.

E.  
PROPORTIONALITY ASSESSMENT33  
Data protection is not an absolute right and can always be limited within a fair balance. A processing developed in a regulation must respect the principle of proportionality, which "restricts authorities in the exercise of their powers by requiring a balance between the means used and the intended objective (or the result achieved)"34.  
As with the rest of the evaluations, assessing proportionality is not merely a statement of it.  
The assessment of proportionality requires a positive evaluation of the necessity assessment35 and is informed by the conclusions derived from it. Therefore:

F.  
SAFEGUARDS  
Article 24.1 of the GDPR and Article 27.1 of L.O. 7/2021 establish that the data controller shall apply appropriate technical and organizational measures, taking into account the nature, scope/extent, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks of varying probability and severity to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, in order to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with data protection regulations. These measures shall be reviewed and updated as necessary.  
They must be reviewed and updated at least when the nature, context, scope/extent, purposes, or risks change. Additionally, in the case of security measures, these must be reviewed periodically (Article 32.1d GDPR).

IV.  
EVALUATION OF THE QUALITY OF THE DPIA  
The DPIA carried out within the framework of regulatory development must meet the following requirements to be considered acceptable:  
• It must be conducted from the design of the regulation and incorporated into the Regulatory Impact Analysis Report.  
• It must address all issues identified in Chapters II and III.  
• It must meet the milestones established in the aforementioned chapters.  
• All responses must be based on appropriate evidence, establishing that the required evaluations have been carried out in these guidelines and preserving (recording and storing) all relevant documentation obtained or produced during the evaluations and the drafting of the DPIA Report. This documentation must be pertinent and sufficient to justify or identify the critical issues of the measure being examined and must be mentioned in an annex of the report.  
• Achieve symmetry of information, for example, in the evaluation of proportionality, in cases where there are known benefits but unknown costs, or vice versa, it will be difficult, if not impossible, to establish whether the measure is proportionate. (Step III.6.3 of the SEPD Proportionality Guide). The same may occur in the evaluation of the least intrusive measure.  
Milestone: If the above conditions are not met, it will be presumed that the DPIA has not been correctly carried out and must be reviewed.

The case law of the CJEU and the ECtHR indicates that necessity and proportionality in data protection regulation is a concept based on facts, rather than merely an abstract legal notion, and that processing must be considered in light of the specific circumstances surrounding the case, as well as the provisions of the measure and the specific purpose it aims to achieve (Section II.6 of the SEPD Necessity Guide).  
The DPIA of a regulation that proposes the processing of personal data is not a legal report that justifies processing from a position of immutability of the pre-established idea. Although it has a very important legal analysis component, it also includes a part on managing limitations and risks to fundamental rights and freedoms, organizational management measures, and a framework for legal and technical measures.  
The DPIA requires the application of a step-by-step methodology; it is not an activity that can be automated, although tools that assist in the process of conducting it, such as Evalúa-Riesgo46, can be employed.  
Finally, it should be noted that in the Public Administration Area of the AEPD website, the most relevant reports from the Legal Advisory Office regarding the conduct of an impact assessment for data protection in regulatory development will be collected.
