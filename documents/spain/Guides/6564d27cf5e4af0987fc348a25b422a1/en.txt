PRESENTATION
The Spanish Agency for Data Protection 
(AEPD) has always had a strong interest in ensuring 
compliance with data protection regulations in the 
processing of health data. To facilitate the understanding 
of the particularities of this type of data, three 
Official Plans have been carried out. The Agency's 
official plans, which are conducted preventively, analyze 
compliance with regulations in specific sectors or 
areas to obtain a comprehensive and joint view that 
allows for the detection of deficiencies and the 
making of corresponding recommendations to the 
analyzed sector.
Throughout our lives, we are all users of the 
healthcare system; that is, our most sensitive data 
will be processed by doctors, healthcare professionals, 
health centers, and hospitals, both in public and 
private healthcare. 
Due to the full application of the General Data 
Protection Regulation (GDPR), starting from May 25, 
2018, the Ministry of Health, Consumer Affairs, and 
Social Welfare has conducted a Study on “Knowledge, 
Attitudes, and Opinions Regarding the Privacy of 
Personal Health Data.” The request for information 
was directed to various stakeholders: users of public 
and private healthcare; users of health applications 
and platforms; members of various patient and 
family associations in the Community of Madrid; 
healthcare professional organizations; and professionals 
from both public and private healthcare. 
Among the conclusions of the report is the positive 
assessment of public healthcare in Spain, and the 
quality of training of all healthcare professionals 
and managers; which leads users to have the 
certainty that their data is in good hands and is 
used responsibly.
On the other hand, stakeholders indicate that 
databases in the healthcare field are recent, 
recalling the folders in which information about 
patients was handwritten. The expansion of 
information technology in medical records is viewed 
positively by users of the healthcare system and is 
considered a fundamental tool for identifying all 
treatments and pathologies of the user/patient; 
for preventive medicine; for developing epidemiological 
lines; for generating risk statistics for large 
population sectors; for anticipating future health 
incidents in the population; and for planning primary 
care systems in the short, medium, and long term.
This guide aims to be useful to all users of the 
healthcare system, facilitating their understanding 
of their rights within the framework of the 
processing of personal data. 
The rights and obligations of patients and users 
of healthcare are primarily outlined in sectoral 
health regulations (for example: the right to receive 
information or not receive it about their illness, 
to be informed or not informed about family members 
or third parties, to request their consent to undergo 
an operation…). Some of these rights are known as 
the right to patient autonomy. However, this guide 
refers to the rights within the legislative framework 
of the processing of their personal data. 
The guide addresses, first of all, general issues 
of data protection regulations that apply to the 
processing of health data carried out by healthcare 
professionals.
On the other hand, the guide considers the rights 
of the interested parties with the changes 
contemplated in the GDPR and in the LOPDGDD, which 
introduces some novelties. Additionally, it will 
address the issues that particularly concern users 
of the Spanish healthcare system regarding the 
processing of their data.

General Aspects of Health Data. Medical History  

1. GENERAL ASPECTS OF HEALTH DATA. MEDICAL HISTORY  
Personal data related to health are considered sensitive data, specially protected, and, following the publication of the GDPR, as a special category of personal data. Two of the questions that healthcare users raise are what types of personal information are considered data and which are not; and the scope of the concept of “personal” in a health database.  
The GDPR defines “personal data” as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  
“Health data” is defined as: “personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status.”  
Genetic data are also considered health data; these are personal data related to the inherited or acquired genetic characteristics of a natural person that provide unique information about the physiology or health of that person, obtained in particular from the analysis of a biological sample of that person.  
Therefore, the personal data existing in health databases are of two types: those that identify the person, such as name and surname, address, phone number, ID number, health card number, etc.; and all information regarding their health status, such as diagnostic tests, surgeries, medications, family history, etc.  
All this personal and health information constitutes what is known as the “Medical History,” which is defined by Law 41/2002, of November 14, the basic regulation governing patient autonomy and rights and obligations regarding information and clinical documentation (LAP) as: the set of documents that contain data, assessments, and information of any kind about the situation and clinical evolution of a patient throughout the care process.  
The medical history includes all documents related to the care processes of each patient, identifying the doctors and other professionals who have intervened in them, with the aim of achieving the maximum possible integration of the clinical documentation of each patient, at least within the scope of each healthcare center.  
The medical history will incorporate information deemed essential for the truthful and updated knowledge of the patient’s health status. Every patient or user has the right to have a written record, or in the most appropriate technical support, of the information obtained in all their care processes, carried out by the health service both in the primary care and specialized care settings.  
The main purpose of the medical history is to facilitate healthcare, documenting all those data that, under medical criteria, allow for that truthful and updated knowledge of the health status referred to in the previous paragraph.  
Healthcare users question who owns their medical history. From the perspective of data protection regulations, this concept of ownership is not addressed.

It is established that the data controller of the data that forms part of the clinical history is the doctor or the healthcare center, whether public or private; they have the obligation to prepare it, safeguard it, and implement the necessary security measures to ensure that it is not lost or accessed by third parties. The patient/user has the right to request a copy of their clinical history, which they can take to another center or to another specialist; upon reviewing it, they may request rectification or deletion of certain data, etc.  

2. LEGITIMIZATION FOR THE PROCESSING OF HEALTH DATA  
IS IT NECESSARY FOR THE DOCTOR OR THE HEALTHCARE CENTER TO REQUEST CONSENT FROM THE PATIENT/USER TO COLLECT AND USE THEIR PERSONAL DATA?  
It is not necessary for the doctor or the healthcare center to request consent from patients for the collection and use of personal and health data if they are to be used for purposes of preventive or occupational medicine, assessment of the worker's capacity, medical diagnosis, provision of healthcare or social treatment, or management of healthcare and social assistance systems and services. (The basis for legitimization for this data processing is established in Article 6.1.b) of the GDPR for private health insurance entities, and in Article 6.1.c) of the same Regulation for public health).  
It is also not necessary if the data processing is carried out for reasons of public interest in the field of public health, such as protection against serious cross-border health threats, or to ensure high levels of quality and safety in healthcare and in medicines or health products or the inspection of citizens' complaints. (The basis for legitimization is established in Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR).  
Health data can also be processed without requesting consent when the processing is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or another natural person, in the event that the data subject is not physically or legally capable of giving consent, or when requested by a judicial authority. (The basis for legitimization is established in Article 6.1.d) of the GDPR).  
Individuals who process the data must be professionals subject to the obligation of professional secrecy, or who are under their responsibility. Consent must be requested, for example, in the case that the dentist or physiotherapist wants to send us advertising.  

3. RIGHT TO INFORMATION FOR THE PROCESSING OF HEALTH DATA  
As indicated, generally, when a user visits the doctor or the healthcare center, whether public or private, they do not have to request consent from the patient for the processing of their personal data, since they almost always visit to receive healthcare; however, it is mandatory that they be informed of the following:  
• The identity and contact details of the data controller and, where applicable, their representative. (This may be the private doctor, healthcare professional from the subscribed health insurance company, public or private hospital, or Health Service of the Autonomous Community)  
• The contact details of the data protection officer, in the event that it is mandatory to have a designated officer. For example,

• The period during which personal data will be retained or, when that is not possible, the criteria used to determine this period. It should be noted that clinical history data will be retained as long as necessary to ensure adequate healthcare for patients and, at a minimum, for five years. Some regional laws have extended this minimum period.  
• The right to request the data controller access to personal data concerning the data subject, and its rectification or deletion, or the limitation of its processing, or to object to the processing, as well as the right to data portability. However, it should be noted that these rights may have limitations for reasons of public interest related to health or for compliance with legal obligations.  
• The right to withdraw consent at any time, without affecting the lawfulness of the processing based on consent prior to its withdrawal.  
• The right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority. These complaints should be submitted to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, or the data protection authorities of Catalonia, the Basque Country, or Andalusia, when the data controller is part of the public healthcare system of these autonomous communities.  
• Whether the communication of personal data is a legal or contractual requirement, or a necessary requirement for entering into a contract, and whether the data subject is obliged to provide personal data and is informed of the possible consequences of not providing such data.

4. PRINCIPLES IN DATA PROCESSING  
• Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency: data shall be processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject.  
• Purpose limitation: data shall be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and shall not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes. If we have consented to the use of our data for the purposes of a specific scientific research (for example, colon cancer), it may be used for other oncological research. This second use is not considered incompatible.  
• Data minimization: only data that is adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which it is processed shall be used; that is, only the minimum necessary data will generally be collected to provide the best healthcare assistance. Although this information may be extensive given the variety of factors that can affect health (food, beverages, family history, habits…)  
• Accuracy: the data collected in health databases, as in all others, shall be accurate and, where necessary, shall be updated. All reasonable measures shall be taken to ensure that inaccurate personal data is erased or rectified without delay concerning the purposes for which it is processed. In clinical records, it is the healthcare professional who determines which data can be erased or rectified.  
• Storage limitation: maintained in a manner that allows for the identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of processing personal data. Personal data may be retained for longer periods as long as they are

5. COMMON ISSUES REGARDING THE EXERCISE OF ALL RIGHTS  
•  Obligation to address the rights unless the impossibility of identifying the interested party is proven.  
•  Deadline: one month extendable by two additional months, depending on the complexity and number of requests.  
•  Response by electronic means if the right was exercised through such means, unless the interested party states otherwise.  
•  If the request is not processed: obligation to inform within one month about the reasons and the possibility of appealing to the supervisory authority or judicial bodies.  
•  Free of charge except in cases of requests that are "manifestly unfounded or excessive, particularly due to their repetitive nature," in which it will be possible to:  
·· Charge a reasonable fee based on the administrative costs incurred to provide the information or communication or to carry out the requested action.  
·· Refuse to act regarding the request.  
·· Access is considered repetitive if requested within periods shorter than six months, unless there is a justified reason (e.g., fewer proofs or days...).  
•  Possibility to request additional information to ensure the identification of the requester since the rights are personal.  

6. RIGHT OF ACCESS TO THE MEDICAL RECORD  
Users/patients have the right to address the data controller (doctors, health centers, healthcare facilities, both public and private) requesting access to the documentation that constitutes their medical record. Access includes both electronic and paper documentation.  
The interested party has the right to obtain from the data controller confirmation of whether or not personal data concerning them is being processed and, in such case, has the right of access to personal data and the following information:  
•  A copy of the personal data being processed (if more than one copy is requested, a fee may be charged for subsequent copies);  
•  The purposes of the processing;  
•  The categories of personal data concerned;  
•  The recipients or categories of recipients to whom the personal data have been or will be communicated,

8. RIGHT TO  
ERASURE OF  
CLINICAL DATA  
In the field of healthcare, the right to erasure of clinical data is very limited as long as those data are being processed for purposes of preventive or occupational medicine, assessment of the worker's capacity, medical diagnosis, provision of healthcare or social treatment, or management of healthcare and social assistance systems and services; or when the processing is necessary for reasons of public interest in the field of public health, such as protection against serious cross-border health threats, or to ensure high levels of quality and safety in healthcare and in medicines or health products. Only the healthcare professional can determine whether the health data can be erased.  
It must be taken into consideration that the fundamental purpose of the clinical history is to ensure adequate assistance to the patient; but it is also used for judicial, epidemiological, public health, research, or teaching purposes; which are related to the guarantee of public interest or compliance with legal obligations, so the cancellation of the data that forms part of the clinical history is exceptional.  

9. FREQUENT  
QUESTIONS  
Question 1: Are the patient data recorded in the clinical history secure?  
Answer: Both private practice physicians and public and private healthcare centers are required to apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure an adequate level of security, taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the variable probability and severity risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals.  
In any case, the implemented security measures must guarantee the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the data; being able to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident.  
The incorporated security measures must be evaluated periodically.  

Question 2: Can anyone access my clinical history?  
Answer: No. The responsible party (usually the physician, the healthcare center, or the health administration) and the data processor (external service providers, such as those performing blood tests and other examinations contracted with third parties, for example) must take the necessary measures to ensure that anyone acting under their authority and having access to personal data can only process such data following their instructions.  
When a healthcare professional accesses a clinical history, they must do so because it is necessary to perform their job. It is not lawful for them to access it out of curiosity, to provide information about a patient to an acquaintance, neighbor…

The Spanish Agency for Data Protection  
has sanctioned, on numerous occasions,  
the Health Services of the various  
Autonomous Communities where the professional  
who improperly accessed a user's medical record worked,  
understanding that, as the data controller,  
they had not established adequate security measures  
to prevent it. The Agency does not  
usually sanction the healthcare professional  
who accesses a medical record without justification,  
as they are a user of the system,  
with the employer being responsible.  
Furthermore, it should be noted that the healthcare professional  
who unlawfully accesses health data  
may incur a crime of discovery and revelation of secrets,  
as provided for and punished in the Penal Code. A  
ruling from the Supreme Court (there are several)  
upheld the conviction of a doctor who accessed  
the records of patients of their colleagues  
at the health center, without them being  
their patients. The accused doctor, taking advantage  
of such condition and using their personal  
username and password, repeatedly entered  
the database without authorization and without  
any care relationship between them,  
a total of twenty-five times, consulting the medical records  
of patients of their colleagues in the basic  
health area, discovering through their actions  
reserved data of these individuals of special  
importance (health) and thus violating their  
constitutional right to personal privacy.  
The Court, understanding that the doctor's actions  
also violated the Medical Deontology Code  
(section 3 of article 27: Being a doctor does not authorize  
one to know confidential information about a patient  
with whom there is no professional relationship)  
declared the accused guilty of a crime of  
discovery and revelation of secrets,  
as provided for and punished in articles 197.1, 2, 6 and  
198 of the Penal Code, imposing penalties  
of two years, six months, and one day of deprivation  
of liberty, a fine of seventeen months at the rate  
of fifteen euros per day, with personal subsidiary  
liability in case of non-payment,  
absolute disqualification for a period of six  
years, and the payment of legal costs.  
Question 3: Can health data be transferred  
to entities other than those that have  
collected and processed them?  
Answer: Yes, they can be transferred if there is  
legitimacy for it. An example is when  
one goes to a doctor or a healthcare center  
as users of private healthcare (with the  
insurance company card), the doctor  
provides the entity with the minimum  
necessary information for it to pay for the  
healthcare service rendered.  
Question 4: Can the employer be informed  
about the health data of their employees  
when they attend the occupational risk prevention  
check-up?  
Answer: No. The information provided  
to the employer is whether the worker is FIT or NOT  
FIT for work, or if they are Fit and need  
some adaptation; but they cannot be informed  
of the results of the medical tests of  
their workers.  
Question 5: What information must the  
sick leave certificates contain? Must they be  
provided to the employer?  
Answer: Sick leave certificates are issued  
by the physician who follows the  
worker's incapacity process, providing two  
copies of the certificate: one for the worker and another  
to be submitted to the company where they work.  
The medical sick leave certificate will include the  
personal data of the worker and, in addition, the  
date of the sick leave, the causative contingency, the  
diagnosis code, the national occupation code of  
the worker, the estimated duration of the process, and, if applicable,  
the clarification that the process is a relapse of a previous one, as well as,  
in this case, the date of the sick leave of the process  
that originates it.  
It will also state the date on which the  
next medical examination will take place.

Question 6: Can parents who hold parental authority access the medical records of their minor children aged between 14 and 18 years?  
Answer: Yes. The Civil Code establishes that parental authority is exercised for the benefit of minor children; likewise, parents must care for them, provide for them, and educate them. Access to health information is essential to safeguard minors. The right is limited to those who hold parental authority, not to other relatives.  
Question 7: Can they provide telephone information to a patient about their health status or the results of tests performed?  
Answer: In principle, there would be a risk of providing information to a third party and, therefore, for security reasons, it should be avoided. It could be done if there is a protocol for identifying the requester of the information, by requesting their full name, ID number, phone number from which they are calling that matches the one provided to the professional, email address, health card number, etc. There should be certainty that the person requesting the information is the holder of it.  
Question 8: Can health care attendance certificates be issued to persons who are not the patient themselves to justify work absences?  
Answer: The accompanying person has a legitimate interest in that treatment and must prove their relationship with the patient. The content of the certificate must comply with the principle of data minimization, including the patient's full name, admission date/time, and approximate days of admission. It should not specify the illness suffered, the admission unit, or the type of surgery.  
Question 9: Can there be surveillance cameras in a hospital or health center?  
Answer: Surveillance cameras are intended to ensure the safety of people and facilities; therefore, they can be installed as long as their installation is communicated, usually through an informational sign. Information can be requested regarding the purposes of the cameras, the duration for which recordings are kept, the possibility of exercising rights, and the person responsible for the installation. They are usually located in access areas, hallways, and corridors; not inside the medical consultation room.  
Question 10: Can a hospital inform about my admission and location?  
Answer: No. They must obtain consent from the patient or their relatives (if the patient is not capable) to provide that information. To avoid conflictual situations, it is very important that this information is provided prominently and that the patient understands its practical consequences.  
Frequently Asked Questions  

PATIENT RIGHTS  
Do they have to ask for my consent for the processing of my data when I go to the doctor for medical assistance?  
NO, the doctor is authorized to do so, but they must inform you if the healthcare center has not been previously informed.  
What do they have to inform me about?  
Who is responsible for the processing; purposes of processing your data; legitimacy for processing; the possibility of exercising your rights, etc.  
Can I request a copy of my medical records?  
YES. They must provide it within one month, although the deadline can be extended. You can also request the medical records of deceased relatives, unless they opposed it during their lifetime.  
Can they charge me for providing a copy of my medical records?  
NO, unless more than one copy is requested or the requests are very repetitive.  
Can I request the rectification and deletion of data from my medical records?  
YES. But if it concerns medical data, it will be the healthcare professional or the health administration who decides whether to rectify or delete it.  
Can any doctor or healthcare personnel access my data?  
NO. Access is only allowed when there is justification for it: medical consultation, management of health or social services, medical appointments, reasons of public interest in the field of public health…  
Will my medical data be shared?  
NO, unless it is necessary or you have consented. The minimum information is shared with private insurance companies to pay the service to the doctor or laboratory, for example.
