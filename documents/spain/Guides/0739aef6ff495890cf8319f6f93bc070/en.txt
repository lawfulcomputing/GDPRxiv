EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  
This report aims to verify, in the online environment, the adaptation of companies to the new regulations applicable since May 25 regarding the protection of personal data and to provide recommendations for its application across different sectors.  
Due to the magnitude of the subject analyzed, a sample has been selected, focusing the analysis on entities from four sectors: hotels, transportation, e-commerce, and insurance. Additionally, companies dedicated to the sale of tickets online and to music and real-time content streaming services have also been reviewed.  
For this report, compliance with the duty to inform the data subject when their personal data is obtained has been analyzed, and for those cases where the processing of personal data is based on the consent of the data subject, the information provided to ensure that consent is informed, as well as the method of obtaining that consent. An analysis has also been conducted on the role of the Data Protection Officer, in those cases where one was present.  
To this end, privacy policies and personal data collection forms from various entities have been reviewed, using a framework that allowed for the analysis and comparison of the assessments made for each entity.  
In summary, it is noteworthy that, in general, it has been detected that the documents detailing privacy policies are excessively lengthy and do not facilitate an average user’s completion of their reading or understanding. This is particularly evident when enumerating the purposes for which personal data is collected, which are often explained in detail, sometimes leading to confusion for the data subject.  
It has also been noted that the bases legitimizing the processing of personal data in question are either not mentioned or not correctly explained, as sometimes what is included as legitimate interest is actually processing necessary for the execution of the contract, or what is framed within legitimate interest is not legitimate at all.  
Regarding the language used in privacy policies, ambiguous or overly generic expressions have been found, which do not provide information to the data subject and seem to be used solely to give an appearance of compliance with applicable regulations.

Regarding the request for consent, where applicable, it has been observed that "bulk" collection is widely used, meaning that consent is not obtained for each specific purpose of processing personal data, but rather the formula "I have read and accept the privacy policy" is employed. As general recommendations to comply with current regulations, companies are advised to review the length of their privacy documents, so that they fulfill the duty to inform the data subject without being so lengthy that they produce the opposite effect. The use of a first layer providing summarized information and a second layer with more detailed information is suggested. 

It is also recommended, among other measures, to group the purposes for which consent from the data subject is requested, so that it does not constitute a "bulk" consent collection but also does not become too cumbersome to control the use of their data. Additionally, recommendations are made regarding the language used and the importance of synthesizing and presenting the information correctly. 

Finally, it is worth noting that, while there is an effort on the part of entities to update their sections on Privacy and their data collection forms, it is also true that there is still some resistance to moving from a previously consent-based model, where tacit consent was sufficient, to a new model that expands the information that must be provided to the data subject and requires a clear positive action to consent to the processing of their personal data. A new model that grants more guarantees to the data subject to control the use of their data by different entities and that considers (and prioritizes) other bases to legitimize this processing, such as legitimate interest and the development of the contractual relationship.

INTRODUCTION
Regulation (EU) 2016/679, of April 27, 2016, General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter, GDPR), published in May 2016 and applicable since May 25, 2018, is a directly applicable regulation in the European Union concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data. 

The GDPR supersedes, in all matters contrary to it, the Organic Law on Data Protection (hereinafter, LOPD) and its implementing regulation, approved by Royal Decree 1720/2007, and introduces a series of changes and novelties to which current processing must be adapted. 

Among the novelties, the concept of "processing" (Article 4.2 of the GDPR) is generalized, and additional requirements regarding the need to inform data subjects are incorporated. In this regard, Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR detail the information that must be provided to the data subject when the data is collected directly from them or not, respectively. 

The obligation to inform data subjects about the circumstances related to the processing of their data falls on the data controller. If the data is obtained directly from the data subject, the information must be made available to them at the time the data is requested, prior to collection or recording. If the data is not obtained from the data subject themselves, they must be informed within one month from the time the personal data was obtained, or before or at the first communication with the data subject, or before the data (if applicable) has been communicated to other recipients. 

Another novelty is that contained in Article 12.1 of the GDPR, which establishes that the information provided to the data subject must be concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible, using clear and plain language. 

Regarding data processing based on the consent of the data subject, Article 7 of the GDPR indicates that the data controller must be able to demonstrate that the data subject consented to the processing of their personal data. If consent is given in the context of a written declaration that also refers to other matters, it is required that the request for consent is clearly distinguished from other issues and presented in an intelligible manner, easily accessible, and using clear and plain language. 

Additionally, it is required that additional information is provided to the data subject, including, among others, what the categories of data to be processed will be, the purposes for which they are...

Regarding how to obtain the consent of the data subject for the processing in question, Recital 32 of the GDPR states that this “must be given by a clear affirmative act that reflects the data subject's free, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of acceptance of the processing of personal data concerning them” and that “silence, pre-ticked boxes, or inactivity should not constitute consent.” Furthermore, it requires that consent be given “for all processing activities carried out for the same or similar purposes. When processing has multiple purposes, consent must be given for all of them.” Lastly, it establishes that “if the data subject's consent is to be given following a request by electronic means, the request must be clear, concise, and not unnecessarily disrupt the use of the service for which it is provided.”

OBJECTIVES  
In light of the recent application of the GDPR, in order to verify the adaptation of companies in the online environment to the new applicable regulations and to issue recommendations for its application across different sectors, the Spanish Agency for Data Protection (AEPD) has conducted an evaluation of various privacy policies to ensure compliance with the duty to inform the data subject. Additionally, for those cases where the processing of personal data was based on the consent of the data subject, in addition to verifying that such consent is informed, the manner of obtaining that consent from the data subject for the processing of their personal data has been analyzed. Finally, an analysis has been conducted regarding the information provided about the role of the Data Protection Officer in those entities that have one.  
Given the magnitude of the subject matter of this report, and as a sample, entities from four sectors have been analyzed: hotels, transportation, e-commerce, and insurance. Companies dedicated to the online sale of tickets and to music and real-time streaming content services have also been reviewed.

SCOPE AND METHODOLOGY  
The evaluation of the adaptation to the GDPR of privacy policies on the Internet is structured around the four analyzed sectors (hotels, transportation, e-commerce, and insurance) and is articulated by analyzing the same issues in each of them.  
Additionally, this report includes a section on the current state of affairs, which reflects the results of the analysis of privacy policies regarding compliance with data protection regulations, and a section of recommendations based on the findings.  
From a methodological perspective, the evaluation has been carried out by comparing, in the online environment, the information provided in the privacy policies of the companies in this sample and the forms used to obtain the consent of the data subject when necessary. The websites of several entities belonging to different sectors have been reviewed: hotels (some small and medium-sized enterprises), passenger transportation (generally large companies), e-commerce (large and medium-sized enterprises), insurance (mostly large companies), and an analysis was also conducted of companies dedicated to the online sale of tickets and to music and real-time streaming content services (generally medium-sized companies). Additionally, some of the analyzed companies belong to the public sector.  
In order to standardize the analysis of entities from such different sectors, a table has been developed for the collection of information from the entities, which allows for the comparison of the information that must be provided to the data subject at the time of collection, as well as the additional information that must be provided in case consent is requested. Furthermore, the method of obtaining this consent and how the obligation to inform is fulfilled has been collected. Lastly, the role of the Data Protection Officer has also been analyzed in those entities where it is mentioned that they have one.

STATUS OF THE SITUATION  
5.1  
INFORMATION PROVIDED TO THE DATA SUBJECT  
In general terms and from the study conducted, the status of the situation detailed below has been observed regarding the information provided to the data subject:  
1.  
Identity and contact details of the data controller and, where applicable, of their representative: all the analyzed companies inform about the identity of the data controller of personal data and provide their contact details in the section dedicated to their privacy policy. Furthermore, the details are usually placed at the bottom of the websites or in their "Legal Notice" section. Some (large) companies provide information about the identity of all subsidiaries of the corporate group. However, none of the analyzed companies provide information regarding the identity of their representative. Regarding the way to contact the data controller for matters related to the use of personal data, almost all provide a postal address to which inquiries can be directed, and three out of four entities provide an email address for this purpose. Lastly, it is noted that a quarter of the analyzed companies have an electronic form enabled for this purpose, and providing a contact telephone number is the exception among the analyzed entities.  
2.  
Contact of the Data Protection Officer: around one third of the analyzed companies do not have a Data Protection Officer. It is noteworthy that two out of three companies in the analyzed hotel sector do not have this figure. Regarding the way to contact them, almost all the analyzed companies prefer to provide an email address for this purpose. Finally, one third of the companies also provide a postal address, and only a few have an electronic form enabled for this purpose.  
3.  
Purposes of the processing: all the analyzed companies use personal data for the provision of the contracted service or product. Additionally, almost all companies mention among the purposes of processing the use of data for sending commercial communications. More than half of the reviewed companies mention compliance with legal obligations and fraud prevention among their purposes. Also, more than half of the companies use the collected data to create profiles on users' consumption habits and to personalize the offered products and improve the service. And only a quarter of the analyzed companies use the data to analyze user behavior on the website and improve its use. Finally, only a few companies mention the use of data for statistical purposes. In general terms, the purposes are usually detailed extensively, which does not always result in clarity in their explanation; rather, it produces the opposite effect, as an average user is unlikely to read and understand privacy policies that list thirty different purposes for processing their data (as found in some cases), or that distinguish purposes based on whether the contract has been signed or not (as seen in some cases where these purposes are identical or very similar, making their wording cumbersome and redundant).  
4.  
Legal basis for processing: of the analyzed companies, half do not detail the legal basis for processing as such. Of these, almost all list the purposes for which the data is collected, and from that section, the bases for processing are inferred or related. For those companies that detail the bases for processing as such, half explain them along with the purposes of collection. Therefore, from the total number of analyzed companies, only a quarter explains what the legal basis for processing is in a specific differentiated section. Regarding the legal basis on which the processing is based, all the analyzed companies mention the consent of the data subject. Of these, almost all also mention the execution of the contract with the data subject. It is curious that some companies in the hotel sector that use consent as the legal basis for processing personal data do not also mention the necessity of processing for the execution of the contract, when it is evident that the data collected to make a reservation is based on this basis rather than on consent. Along with these legal bases, two out of three companies mention that the processing is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests of the data controller, while one third of the analyzed companies mention that the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller. Finally, it should be noted that, although the GDPR recognizes up to six legal bases for considering the processing of personal data lawful, in the private sector, the most common bases tend to be the legitimate interests of the data controller or a third party (art. 6.1.f of the GDPR), or that the processing is necessary for the execution of a contract in which the data subject is a party or for the application at their request of pre-contractual measures (art. 6.1.b of the GDPR).

Legitimate interest: the vast majority of analyzed companies that mention legitimate interest as the basis for the processing of personal data detail what interests are involved. Only a few make a generic reference to legitimate interest, or refer to the company's interest "in getting to know you better and making your experience more personalized," without even mentioning legitimate interest as such. Among the interests listed in the analyzed privacy policies, the most common are those related to customer service (responses to requests, complaints, incidents). The analysis of website usage is also mentioned as an interest of companies, in order to verify user behavior on the site, and the sending of commercial communications about products or services similar to those contracted. In this regard, it should be noted that if it involves the use of data storage and retrieval devices on the terminal equipment of the recipients (cookies), or unsolicited commercial communications via electronic means or telephone calls, the applicable legal regime is that stipulated by Law 34/2002, of July 11, on information society services and electronic commerce (LSSI) and Law 9/2014, of May 9, General Telecommunications Law (LGT), instead of the regime established by the GDPR. Recipients of the collected data: almost all analyzed companies detail the recipients or categories of recipients of the collected personal data. As for the third parties to whom the collected data is transferred, they are usually companies belonging to the same corporate group as the analyzed entity, or collaborating third-party companies. Competent authorities, courts, and Public Administrations are also generally mentioned as recipients of the information, when it is mandatory for the controller. Additionally, mention is made of third-party providers of products or services, to whom information is provided in order to fulfill the contracted service. Finally, it is noted that a few companies include the possibility of being succeeded by another in their business among the categories of recipients of the data.

PROCESSING BASED ON THE CONSENT OF THE DATA SUBJECT 
5.2.1 INFORMED CONSENT 
For consent to be informed, it is necessary to communicate to the data subject certain elements that are crucial for making a choice. To this end, the Article 29 Working Party on Data Protection (now the European Data Protection Board) has considered that at least the following information is required for the consent obtained to be valid: i) the identity of the data controller; ii) the purpose of each of the processing operations for which consent is requested; iii) what (type of) data will be collected and used; iv) the existence of the right to withdraw consent; v) information about the use of data for automated decisions, when relevant; and vi) information about the possible risks of data transfer due to the absence of an adequacy decision and adequate safeguards. Regarding the first point and the last two, these have already been analyzed in sections 1, 12, and 7 of subsection 5.1 of this report, respectively. As for the other aspects that must be communicated when requesting the consent of the data subject, in general and from the study conducted, the following state of affairs has been observed:

16.

RECOMMENDATIONS
6.1
INFORMATION PROVIDED TO THE INTERESTED PARTY
In general and from the study already conducted, the following recommendations are gathered regarding the information provided to the interested party when collecting their personal data:
1. 
Identity and contact details of the controller and, where applicable, their representative: it is recommended to always include this information at the beginning of the document or corresponding privacy section, so that the user can find this information quickly and easily. Regarding the way to contact the data controller, it is considered more appropriate to provide the interested party with an email address or an electronic form, as it is an online environment.
2. 
Contact of the Data Protection Officer: it should be noted that, in the cases detailed in section 1 of Article 37 of the GDPR, it is mandatory for the controller and the processor to designate a Data Protection Officer. Since it is an online environment, it is considered more appropriate to provide the interested party with an email address or enable an electronic form to contact this person. It would also be advisable to unify the designation of this figure in the privacy policies and use the GDPR term, "Data Protection Officer," to avoid confusion.
3. 
Purposes of processing: it is mandatory to detail all the purposes for which the personal data of the interested parties is processed. However, an overly extensive enumeration discourages reading and hinders understanding. It would be advisable to adjust the length of these explanations and group the purposes into categories (for example, service provision, commercial communications, compliance with legal obligations, among others).
4. 
Legal basis for processing: it is recommended to clearly differentiate in the information provided to the interested party, in addition to the purposes for which the information is processed, what the legal basis is that legitimizes each processing activity. It is suggested that this information be related to or placed following the information about the purposes for which the processing is carried out. In order to avoid the effect
REPORT ON INTERNET PRIVACY POLICIES. ADAPTATION TO THE GDPR

24

contrary to the desired outcome, it is also recommended to adjust the length of these explanations and group the processing activities according to the legal basis that legitimizes them. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that the information provided must be accurate. Therefore, the basis on which it is stated that legitimizes the processing of data must be correct. This is particularly relevant in cases where the basis that legitimizes the processing of personal data does not fit into any of the scenarios listed in letters b) to e) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, and it is necessary for the processing to be based either on the consent of the data subject (Article 6.a) GDPR), or that the processing is “necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that such interests are not overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child” (Article 6.f) GDPR). Special mention should be made of the processing of data for marketing, advertising, and commercial communications purposes. Recital 47 of the GDPR states that the legitimate interest of a data controller “could exist, for example, when there is a relevant and appropriate relationship between the data subject and the controller, such as in situations where the data subject is a customer or is in the service of the controller” and that “the processing of personal data for direct marketing purposes may be considered to be carried out for legitimate interests.” However, when such communications are carried out by electronic means, before resorting to the applicability of Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR, the application of Article 21 of the LSSI must be considered, which requires that these communications have been requested or expressly authorized by the recipients, or that “there is a prior contractual relationship, provided that the provider has lawfully obtained the contact data of the recipient and uses them to send commercial communications regarding products or services of its own company that are similar to those initially contracted with the customer.” Regarding advertising or marketing actions not carried out through electronic means, it could also be exempted from the need to request the consent of the data subject for those communications related to products or services of its own company that are similar to those initially contracted with the customer. In this regard, it should be noted that the processing of personal data for sending commercial communications by means other than electronic, provided that a simple procedure is established for exercising the right to object, could be covered by Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR (legitimate interest) when the affected party remains a customer of the entity and the products or services offered can be considered “similar” to those contracted by the customer.

5. Legitimate interest: when the processing of data is based on the legitimate interests of the controller or a third party, it must be detailed what these interests are for each processing activity. It is not enough to make a generic mention of “legitimate interest” as such or to use abstract formulas like “to know you better.” It is reiterated that the information provided must be accurate and that, therefore, the basis on which it is stated that legitimizes the processing of data must be correct. Finally, it would be desirable that, along with the information about the legitimate interest on which the processing of data is based, the balancing carried out between this interest of the controller and the users' right to the protection of their personal data is documented.

6. Recipients of the collected data: where applicable, when information must be provided about the recipients of the processed personal data, it is also recommended to adjust the length of these explanations and group them by categories. If the personal data of the data subjects are not communicated to third parties, it is considered good practice to inform about this aspect, so that there are no doubts in this regard.

7. The intention of the controller, where applicable, to transfer personal data to a third country or international organization and the existence or absence of an adequacy decision by the Commission, or reference to adequate or appropriate safeguards and the means to obtain a copy of these or the fact that they have been provided: special attention is recommended for this section as it has been observed that it is not fully complied with. The GDPR establishes that it is mandatory to provide this detailed information in the case of international transfers. Therefore, it is not sufficient to inform generically that these transfers are made, but it must also be stated whether or not there is an adequacy decision by the Commission in this regard and, if not, mention the safeguards that are provided (without using generic formulas like “adequate safeguards” but at least a general enumeration without going into detail about each one) and the procedure to obtain a copy of these or that they have been provided. All this information must not be

Information on whether the communication of data is a legal or contractual requirement, or a necessary requirement for entering into a contract, or whether the data subject is obliged to provide personal data. In such cases, possible consequences of not providing such data: the GDPR requires informing about these aspects. It should be indicated whether this information is mandatory or necessary for the contract or whether providing the information is a legal or contractual requirement. It is also recommended to inform about the basis for considering that providing the information is a requirement or an obligation. It is not considered sufficient to indicate that it is "necessary information" or to not indicate anything in this regard. It should also be informed about the possible consequences of not providing personal data, as it is insufficient to state that the desired action cannot be completed if the requested personal data is not provided. It is recommended that this information be detailed in the document or section related to privacy on the website, even if in the data collection form the mandatory fields are marked with an asterisk and summarized information is provided in this regard.  
12. Information about the existence of automated decisions (including profiling) and, at least in such cases, significant information about the logic applied, as well as the importance and expected consequences of such processing for the data subject: the GDPR is clear about the information that must be provided to the data subject when automated decisions are made. Regarding profiling, which is a fairly common activity in the online environment, it is not sufficient to inform that these are carried out; it must also be explained the logic applied for this, what importance this activity has for the data subject, and the consequences that this processing could have for them. 6.2.1 INFORMED CONSENT

For consent to be informed, it is necessary to communicate to the data subject certain elements that are crucial for making a choice. To this end, the Article 29 Working Party on Data Protection (now the European Data Protection Board) has considered that at least the following information is required for the consent obtained to be valid: i) the identity of the data controller; ii) the purpose of each of the processing operations for which consent is requested; iii) what (type of) data will be collected and used; iv) the existence of the right to withdraw consent; v) information about the use of data for automated decisions, when relevant; and vi) information about the possible risks of data transfer due to the absence of an adequacy decision and appropriate safeguards. Regarding the first point and the last two, the relevant recommendations have already been made in sections 1, 12, and 7 of subsection 6.1 of this report, respectively. 6.2.2 REQUEST FOR CONSENT  
In general terms and based on the study already conducted, the following recommendations are detailed regarding how to obtain the consent of the data subject, in cases where the processing of data is based on obtaining this consent:  
16.  
Request for consent for each purpose of processing: it is important to highlight that the user's consent must be requested for all processing activities that are not legitimized by another basis justifying their lawfulness. In this regard, the importance of providing correct and accurate information is emphasized (see section 4 of heading 6.1 of this report). Furthermore, it should be noted that it is not appropriate to request the user's consent in bulk for all purposes of processing their data; rather, consent must be given for each purpose. To this end, it is recommended to group related purposes for which consent is requested, so that the data subject can decide for which purposes they give their consent.  
17.  
Request for consent through a clear affirmative act (neither silence nor pre-checked boxes): the GDPR stipulates that consent must be given through a clear affirmative act and considers that silence, pre-checked boxes, or inaction do not constitute consent. Therefore, it is mandatory that, to obtain consent, the user is provided with an empty checkbox or a similar mechanism. Before providing their personal data and giving consent to the processing of such data, it would be desirable to recommend that the data subject read and understand the privacy policy. Additionally, it would be considered good practice to remind the user of their permission choices and request confirmation of their consent, in the same way that a second confirmation is often requested when the user unsubscribes from an online service or advertising communications.  
18.  
Request for consent that is clearly distinguished from other matters: the GDPR requires that the request for consent be clearly distinguished from other matters. To this end, it is recommended that consent be requested clearly, in a specific section of the form through which their data is collected. It would be desirable that, in addition, a larger font or bold or colored text is used to differentiate the request for consent, rather than using small or italicized text that tends to go unnoticed. Finally, it should be noted that obtaining the user's consent through a button with a generic label such as “I have read and accept the terms of the privacy policy” is not considered sufficient; rather, it must be distinguished what constitutes consent for the processing of personal data from the rest of the relationship of the data subject with the responsible party in question. It is recommended that the request for the data subject's consent include options to granularly activate consent, allowing them to then check the option “I have read and accept the terms of the privacy policy” or equivalent phrases.  
19.  
Request for consent that does not unnecessarily disrupt the use of the service: as mentioned earlier, it is recommended that the request for consent be made in a specific section of the form through which the data is collected, so that it does not distract attention from the processing of personal data necessary for the execution of the relationship that the data subject is contracting. It is important to highlight that the use of pop-up windows to request consent from data subjects for the processing of their personal data, which hinder the use of the requested service, is not considered appropriate.

6.4 DATA PROTECTION OFFICER  
In general and from the study already conducted, the following recommendations regarding the figure of the Data Protection Officer are detailed:  
24. Existence of a Data Protection Officer: according to the GDPR, it is mandatory to designate a Data Protection Officer in the cases contemplated in Article 37.1 (for example, when the main activity of the company consists of large-scale processing of special categories of personal data). However, it would be desirable that, even in those cases where their designation is not mandatory, entities assess the convenience or usefulness of having this figure.  
25. Name by which it is known: it would be advisable to unify the name of this figure in privacy policies and use that of the GDPR in its Spanish translation: “Delegado de Protección de Datos,” in order to standardize the terminology used in these sections.  
26. Location of the DPO figure on the website: it would be advisable for the information about the Data Protection Officer to be displayed at the beginning of the privacy policy of the websites, preferably in a specific section (not in the middle of the information related to the data controller or in the contact section with the controller). It would be useful for interested parties if the contact details of the DPO were also in an easily accessible location on the websites, preferably in the “Contact” section with the company or in the section dedicated to customer service. Finally, it would be considered good practice to add in the personal data collection form, along with a link to the privacy policy, a reference to how to contact the DPO.  
27. Means of contact with the DPO: since it is an online environment, it is considered more appropriate, for contacting the DPO, to provide the interested party with an email address or to enable an electronic form on the website for this purpose.  
28. Description of the DPO's functions: it would be good practice to explain to interested parties what the DPO does and how they can be of assistance.
