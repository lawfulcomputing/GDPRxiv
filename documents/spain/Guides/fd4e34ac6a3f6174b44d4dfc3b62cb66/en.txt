PRESENTATION
The General Data Protection Regulation of the 
European Union includes among the functions of 
Data Protection authorities the promotion of 
awareness among controllers and processors 
regarding the obligations that pertain to them in 
this area; a function that, among other aspects, 
includes providing practical guides that facilitate 
compliance with these obligations.
The Spanish Data Protection Agency (AEPD) has 
been particularly proactive in providing information 
and guidance regarding the processing of health 
data given their relevance as special categories 
of data and a reinforced protection regime. In 
this sense, it is necessary to highlight the 
development of the Guide for patients and users 
of healthcare, as well as the creation on the 
AEPD's website of a space specifically dedicated 
to health information.
In order to continue these initiatives, the 
development of this new Guide for healthcare 
professionals has been proposed, contributing 
to facilitating compliance with this regulation 
and ensuring the rights of users of their services 
adapted to the provisions of the General Data 
Protection Regulation (GDPR) and Organic Law 
3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of 
Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights.
Its main recipients are healthcare professionals 
who carry out their activities on an individual 
basis, although its guidance may be useful in 
some aspects for professionals working within 
healthcare establishments.
The guide begins by describing the basic concepts 
of health data from a broad perspective and then 
describes the legal position of those involved in 
the provision of healthcare services as data 
controllers or as service providers to such 
controllers (processors as defined in the GDPR).
Next, the legal bases for data processing are 
described, differentiating those that are specific 
to the rights of patient autonomy from those 
related to the processing of their personal data, 
which include, along with the informed consent 
of the affected individuals, other legal bases that 
legitimize the processing of their personal data 
without the need for consent.
Access to the medical record is an essential aspect 
of healthcare, and the guide provides guidance on 
who can access it for the various purposes for 
which they are legitimized, as well as the risks 
and responsibilities that may be incurred by those 
who access it unlawfully.
In order to facilitate compliance with the principle 
of proactive responsibility of the Regulation, 
guidance is also provided on the measures to be 
applied and on the guarantee of the rights of the 
affected individuals, with specific reference to 
the limitations they may have within the framework 
of healthcare regulations.
On the other hand, one of the most common risks 
to the confidentiality of patient data is the modes 
of access to their information when they are 
hospitalized in a healthcare facility or when they 
are called to attend the consultation of the 
professionals, which is why the guide offers 
specific criteria to minimize them. Additionally, 
solutions are proposed to manage the security of 
healthcare facilities and the identification of 
professionals in cases where it is appropriate.
Finally, the guide seeks to respond to the various 
situations that arise when healthcare professionals 
provide their services in hospitals or clinics, 
indicating the criteria that allow for the identification, 
in each case, of who are the data controllers of 
the patients' data and the corresponding medical 
records.

1. BASIC CONCEPTS: HEALTH DATA, CONTROLLER, AND PROCESSOR  
A) What type of data does a healthcare professional handle and what protection do they require?  
Healthcare professionals may handle identifying and contact data of patients (name, address, phone number, ID number, etc.) and, primarily, data related to health that is necessary to fulfill the purpose of providing healthcare related to the service rendered. They may also handle identifying data of family members, such as in the case of minors, information related to parents; or in the case of individuals with limited capacity, references to health data of these individuals (such as hereditary diseases or other medically relevant family history) and all useful and necessary data to formulate a diagnosis and treat the patient.  
Among these data, examples include information related to dietary habits, geographical environment, patient habits, travel, or physical activity or sports practiced.  
Health data is any information that provides insight into a person's medical situation or state of health (present, past, or future), including the provision of healthcare services. For example, assessments and information of any kind regarding the situation and clinical evolution of a patient throughout the care process or that allow for the acquisition or expansion of knowledge about their physical or mental health status, or how to preserve, care for, improve, or recover it (diagnostic tests, medication, etc.); the number or symbol assigned to a person for health identification purposes (Clinical History number); information derived from tests or examinations of a part of the body or a bodily substance, including data from genetic information and biological samples; information related to a disease, a disability, the risk of developing diseases, data provided by family members in cases of minors or individuals with limited cognitive capacity, etc. Health data, in addition to the information provided by the patient, can come from various sources (a healthcare professional, a hospital, or a medical device, among others).  
Health data falls within the "special categories of data," also known as sensitive data, and therefore deserves enhanced protection and may only be processed, that is, stored, transmitted, or disclosed, among other forms of processing, under certain conditions and with specific guarantees. Additionally, special privacy measures must be adopted, particularly data protection by design and by default, security, and management of data breaches, as well as appropriate and effective procedures that ensure a high level of protection, suitable to the risks related to the rights of individuals.  
B) Who is responsible for the data processing that is carried out?  
The controller is the one who decides which data will be obtained, for what purposes it will be processed, and by what means it will be managed and protected. It can be either a public entity (public hospital or health center) or a private entity (hospital/clinic), or an individual professional.  
The healthcare professional will be responsible for the processing when they provide healthcare services on an individual basis or the entity that employs them when they work for someone else. The entity could also be a processor of a controller concerning healthcare processing when it has been contracted to carry out the same in a specific manner. In the case that a group of professionals works cooperatively, they may be respective controllers of the processing they carry out on patients or,

(including health data) encompasses any operation  
that can be performed with the data, such as,  
for example, collection, its use for the

its extraction, interconnection, or communication

The processing of patient data can be based on such consent or on another cause provided for by law. Generally, it is not necessary to request the patient's consent to process their personal data in the context of healthcare. Thus, for example:  
• When the processing is necessary for the purposes of preventive or occupational medicine, assessment of the worker's capacity, medical diagnosis, provision of healthcare or social assistance, or management of healthcare and social assistance systems and services, based on the corresponding legislation or under a contract with a healthcare professional. The data must be processed by professionals subject to professional secrecy or by someone under their responsibility. This includes data processing that occurs when healthcare is provided by public or private healthcare centers or professionals, including assistance provided in the socio-healthcare field. It also includes actions related to occupational health (in accordance with occupational risk prevention regulations), with limited access by the employer (fitness or otherwise of the worker).  
• Whenever the processing of data is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or another natural person, in the event that the data subject is not physically or legally capable of giving their consent. For example, in the case of an accident or emergency, when the data subject has lost consciousness.  
• When it is necessary for the formulation, exercise, or defense of claims or when the data is required judicially. Therefore, health data may be processed in relation to a judicial proceeding in which such data is necessary (e.g., claims for medical negligence, capacity modification processes, etc.), but also to file claims before administrative bodies or other non-judicial bodies (arbitration or mediation).  
• If the processing of certain data is necessary for reasons of public interest in the field of public health, under the terms established by law, such as the control of communicable diseases, epidemics, cross-border threats, etc.  
• When carried out for scientific research, archiving in the public interest, or statistical purposes, under the terms established by law.  
In the cases just mentioned, health data may be processed without the need for the data subject's consent. However, it will be necessary to inform them of certain aspects (by the doctor, public or private hospital, corresponding health service, etc.); among others, the following: identity and contact details of the data controller; contact details of the data protection officer, when applicable; purposes of the data processing (healthcare, research, etc.) and its legitimate basis (provision of healthcare, protection of vital interests, etc.); recipients to whom their data will be delivered; retention period of the data; possibility of exercising the rights recognized in the data protection regulations or of filing a complaint with the relevant data protection agency. And if, in addition, the data has not been obtained from the data subject themselves, it is necessary to inform them of the sources and categories of their data that have been collected up to that point. B) If it concerns minors, when can they provide consent for the processing of their data (coordination of Law 3/2018 and Law 41/2002)?  
The processing of personal data of a minor can only be based on their consent when they are over fourteen years old.  
Consent for healthcare actions that require it must be informed and in writing. It has already been pointed out that for the processing of personal data for the purpose of providing healthcare, it is not necessary to request the patient's consent, but it is mandatory to inform them about the data controller, the possibility of exercising their rights, the purposes of the processing, (and all the information required in Article 13 of the General Data Protection Regulation). If this information is provided in writing, it is advisable that the healthcare consent form directed to the patient is separated from the information sheet regarding the processing of their personal data.

3. WHO AND WHEN CAN ACCESS THE MEDICAL HISTORY (MH)?  
In what cases and under what conditions can doctors access it (can residents also access it?); for inspection/evaluation/planning (can access the MH of patients to verify the care process carried out by the professionals who have treated the patient?); by administrative personnel; for teaching (can I use clinical data in courses, classes, etc.? Can students in internships access it? Is access allowed for the completion of final degree projects (TFG) and master's theses (TFM)?); access by judicial and administrative authorities (DGT, AEAT, other competent administrations, work accident and occupational disease mutuals, etc.).  
Access to the MH for the purpose of effective and efficient health care, especially in cases of vital emergency, can never be limited under the pretext of compliance with data protection regulations. On the other hand, the responsible party has the obligation to adopt access control policies, record-keeping, and traceability of such accesses, as well as auditing (automated and manual) of the records to prevent, detect, and diligently correct any abuse that may occur.  
Access to the medical history is limited: not every professional and under any circumstance can access it. Both healthcare personnel and other professionals can access it only for the performance of their functions, without being able to disclose to third parties the data to which they have access. The possibilities for accessing the data of the MH vary according to the type of professional function and the purpose of access to such data:

ACCESS TO THE MEDICAL RECORD BY HEALTHCARE PROFESSIONALS  
What data can healthcare professionals access?  
The purpose of the medical record is fundamentally for care. The healthcare professional or the team directly involved in the patient's care, or those consulted by them to improve therapeutic attention, may access the medical record. In any case, access will be limited solely to the data that is necessary, and if it is not necessary to know the identity of the patient, it should not be accessed.  

Can residents access medical records?  
The resident professional can access the data in the medical record under the terms previously described, that is, when necessary for the healthcare they are providing. Residents are considered healthcare professionals as long as their affiliation with the center lasts.  

Can medical records be accessed from socio-health centers?  
Healthcare professionals in socio-health centers must also be able to access the medical records of the patients they are treating, in order to provide proper healthcare.  

And from contracted private centers?  
Healthcare professionals from contracted private centers must be able to access, if necessary, to provide healthcare to a patient referred to that center, but with access limited to the data necessary to fulfill the function assigned to them. For this, appropriate measures must be arranged so that the destination center has updated knowledge of the patient's health status.  

Can members of an Ethics Committee access the medical record?  
Members of these Committees (some of whom may not be healthcare professionals) will access the information that is strictly necessary to issue the corresponding ethical opinion submitted for their consideration. They must be bound by a duty of confidentiality or sign a confidentiality agreement. Only when identification is necessary to issue the corresponding report or opinion will access be granted.  

Can service-providing companies access patient data?  
Service/equipment providers to patients at home will have access to the strictly necessary data to fulfill their functions. As data processors, they are obliged to comply with data protection regulations and will only use such data following the instructions of the data controller.  

ACCESS BY ADMINISTRATIVE AND MANAGEMENT PERSONNEL  
Administrative and management personnel may only access the data in the medical record necessary for the performance of their functions.  
In this situation are, in general, the administrative and service personnel (admission service, personnel responsible for patient care, appointment management, administrative and personnel management of the center, IT...), as well as the management positions of a center.  
All personnel accessing the data of a medical record in the performance of their functions are subject to the duty of confidentiality.  

ACCESS BY INSPECTION/EVALUATION/ACCREDITATION  
Personnel with functions of inspection, evaluation, accreditation, and planning have access to the medical records to the extent necessary to fulfill their functions (accreditation of the quality of care, respect for patients' rights, or other obligations of the center in relation to patients or the health administration).  
A health inspector, for example, could access the medical records of patients to verify the care process carried out by the professionals who treated them.

ACCESS FOR EDUCATIONAL PURPOSES  
Students, when necessary for their educational activities or for the completion of internships, should have limited access with a student profile (such as regarding access time, functions they can perform - consultation mode, etc.). They should only access, with the appropriate authorization, those data necessary for their proper training and must sign the corresponding confidentiality commitment.  
For the completion of undergraduate and master's thesis work, the general rule should be access to disassociated data (separating identifying data from clinical data).  
Healthcare professionals may use clinical data of patients for educational purposes (to teach classes, courses, etc.) as long as the patient cannot be identified. This implies not only the separation between identifying and clinical data but also avoiding their use as much as possible when, due to the characteristics of the specific case, the person in question can be easily identified (for example, cases with public significance, rare cases affecting few people and/or in geographically small areas, etc.). The same will apply when data is to be used in congresses, conferences, and similar events. For identifiable patient data to be used, explicit consent is required.  

ACCESS FOR PUBLIC HEALTH AND EPIDEMIOLOGICAL PURPOSES  
Epidemiological studies are necessary for the prevention of health risks, as well as for health planning and evaluation, and therefore require the processing of personal and health-related data. Access to the medical record (HC) for these purposes must be carried out by separating personal identifying data from clinical assistance data, unless the patient has provided consent not to separate them.  
Even so, in this area, identifiable data may be accessed for reasons of public interest, such as when there is a serious risk or danger to the health of the population (for example, in the face of serious cross-border health threats, epidemic control, transmissible diseases, etc.). This could also occur for the incorporation of identifiable patient data into a cancer registry of an autonomous community. In these cases, specific guarantees must be applied, such as the person accessing the data being subject to professional secrecy and the Administration must justify the access request) and other data protection principles such as data minimization).  

ACCESS FOR RESEARCH PURPOSES  
Access to the HC for scientific research purposes, as a general rule, must be made to disassociated data (separating identifying data from clinical data) and with the additional guarantees established in the Personal Data Protection Law (additional provision 17.2).  
Identified health data may be used for research if consent is obtained. This consent can be broad (it can be requested for general areas related to a specific specialty or service; thus, in the field of cancer, gynecology, or reproduction...). Also, when consent was previously given for a specific research project and the data is to be reused for a new research project in an area related to the previous one.  
These rules will also apply to public health and epidemiological research. Although authorities with competence in public health surveillance may conduct scientific studies without the need for consent from the interested parties in situations of exceptional relevance and seriousness for public health.

ACCESS FOR JUDICIAL PURPOSES  
When access to a medical record (HC) is requested by the judicial authority, the requested data will be provided to it in the corresponding process, allowing access to non-anonymized data when the unification of identifying and clinical-assistance data is deemed essential.  
However, access should only be granted to the data essential for the case at hand, avoiding the communication of other data concerning the subject or third parties that are not relevant to the case. For these purposes, the judicial body should consider the necessity or otherwise of knowing data contained in the HC that do not have a direct relationship with the matter to be resolved. Thus, for example, if it is a case of liability for stomach reduction surgery, the fact that the same patient underwent a vasectomy 12 years ago is of no relevance; nor is it relevant that the patient underwent a rhinoplasty due to a deviated septum. In summary, the judicial authority must weigh the relevance or, conversely, the inconvenience of accessing and incorporating the complete HC of a person into the judicial file.  
ACCESS BY ADMINISTRATIVE AUTHORITIES  
Apart from access for epidemiological or public health purposes referred to in health legislation, administrative authorities can only obtain data from the HC when they have the consent of the holder or when it is specifically provided for in a legal norm.  
Thus, regarding the General Directorate of Traffic (DGT), neither the general data protection regulation nor that of patient autonomy contemplates any measures in this regard, nor is it established in the current regulations concerning motor vehicle circulation. Consequently, personal data related to health cannot be transferred to administrative authorities without the consent of the affected individuals.  
In relation to the actions of the State Agency for Tax Administration (AEAT), there is a legal obligation to provide data, reports, background information, and justifications with tax relevance, that is, related to the fulfillment of their own tax obligations or derived from their economic, professional, or financial relationships with other persons. However, this obligation does not extend to health data, so that, in the case access to the HC is required, it will be necessary to eliminate the identification of its holder.

First of all, this type of access is sanctioned in the Penal Code as a crime of discovery and disclosure of secrets. The Code provides for penalties of up to five years in prison, depending on the case, in addition to fines, suspension, or disqualification. On the other hand, when the conduct does not have sufficient gravity to be considered a crime, these behaviors may be punished with an administrative sanction. It is important to remember that liability for improper access to the data of the medical history arises not only from the disclosure to third parties of the data known due to access to it, which may initially seem more serious, but that the healthcare professional may face all or some of the described consequences simply with the mere unjustified access to the medical history, including prison sentences. That is, if access is made without the patient's consent, or without any of the purposes protected by law being present (among them, the most common, the provision of healthcare, which determines the consultation of the necessary data to carry it out), that access is already improper and may lead to serious consequences for the professional. Without prejudice to the fact that if, in addition, the improperly consulted information is disclosed to third parties, the legal consequences will be even more severe.  
In any case, it must be taken into account that this type of conduct also affects the obligations related to the notification of security breaches to the data protection authorities and, where appropriate, communication to the affected individuals. It should be noted in the context of personal data breaches in the health sector that the Agency has found that breaches of confidentiality are occurring due to the improper access of organization members to patients' medical history data.  
Other similar personal data breaches frequently reported in the healthcare sector include sending documentation with health or genetic data to incorrect recipients, the destruction of data media without confidentiality guarantees, or the loss of biological samples that allow for the identification of the patient.  
One type of incident that is causing personal data breaches capable of completely paralyzing the activity of a healthcare professional and causing significant harm to the rights and freedoms of individuals are ransomware-type cyber incidents. Ransomware can encrypt personal data and computer systems, affecting the availability of the data and the means to process it. In many cases, the exfiltration of personal data also occurs, which affects confidentiality. These incidents are often accompanied by extortion attempts against both the healthcare professional and the patients affected by the breach, demanding large sums of money to recover and/or not make the data public.  
The implementation of preventive security measures specifically aimed at avoiding this type of incident and having backup systems is essential.

5. OBLIGATIONS IN THE TREATMENT OF HEALTH DATA  
A) To what extent does the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) impose new obligations?  
In general, the professional continues to have the same obligations as under the previous regulation: to ensure that the processing of data is lawful, to inform the patient of their rights and that their data will be processed, and to respect the duty of confidentiality. However, the GDPR imposes new obligations, clarifies the scope of others, and, above all, requires that compliance can be demonstrated (“principle of proactive accountability”).  
In any case, it should be generally noted that:  
• It is necessary to conduct a risk management assessment of the processing, both automated and non-automated, of personal data concerning the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. To this end, the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the data processing must be taken into account, among other circumstances.  
• In order to minimize risks, it is necessary to adopt measures and guarantees in the processing and its implementation that are necessary and proportionate to those risks, in particular, to implement data protection policies, organizational measures, data protection by design and by default, as well as security measures.  
• When processing operations pose a high risk to the rights and freedoms, a data protection impact assessment must be carried out.  
• A record of processing activities (RPA) must be maintained. In the case of public entities, an inventory of processing must be published and made accessible by electronic means.  
• A Data Protection Officer (DPO) must be appointed in cases where it is mandatory, possessing the necessary legal, management, and technical/scientific knowledge to inform, advise, and supervise the data controller in compliance with data protection and health regulations concerning the specific processing carried out. The DPO must be provided with the necessary resources to effectively perform their functions.  
• The data controller must manage personal data breaches to be able to, at a minimum, notify the supervisory authority of those that constitute a risk to rights and freedoms and communicate to the affected individuals those that pose a high risk. These actions must be carried out without undue delay and, in the case of notifications, this period cannot exceed 72 hours. Additionally, breaches, their effects, and the measures taken must be documented.  
• In compliance with the obligation of transparency, information to the data subject regarding the processing of their data must be provided both in cases where the data is obtained directly from the affected person and when, on the contrary, it has been received through other means.

C) When do I have to conduct an impact assessment?  
The data protection impact assessment (DPIA) is an obligation of the controller when, within the framework of risk management, it is determined that the processing poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals. To help determine the level of risk of a processing activity and, if applicable, the need to carry out a DPIA, the AEPD has made the EVALÚA_RIESGO tool available on its website.  
Among the risk factors to be considered are those arising from the nature of the processing, such as remote monitoring systems, telediagnosis, automated decision-making, etc., the scope and extent, such as covering a large part of the population, vulnerable groups, genetic data, minors, etc., the context, such as breaches in the healthcare environment, social sensitivity, etc., and the direct and collateral purposes, such as profiling, automated decisions without human intervention, etc.  
It should be noted that processing carried out by a medical professional as a private consultation will never be considered on a large scale.  
The DPO will advise on the conduct of the DPIA and supervise its application when they have been appointed. If it is not possible to manage a high risk, there is the option to submit a Prior Consultation to the AEPD.

D) In which cases must the Record of Processing Activities (RAT) be published and how?  
The data controller must maintain a record that includes at least the following information for each of the processing activities carried out: the responsible entity, the legal basis for the processing, the purposes for which personal data is used, the group to which it refers, a description of the data being processed, whether communication to third parties is planned or if they will be transferred internationally, the retention periods, and, among other aspects, the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security and privacy of the managed data.  
The RAT must be documented in writing and, where applicable, in electronic format, and it shall be available to the Data Protection Agency or, if the controller is a public entity, to the competent supervisory authority. However, in the case of public entities (including public foundations), the inventory (art.31 LOPDGDD) of personal data processing must be made public and accessible by electronic means (e.g., through the respective website).  

E) Basic security measures in the use of computer devices (access control, password management, password changes, sending clinical documentation in an encrypted manner; use of tablets and other devices by professionals, etc.).  
Security measures are one of the measures and guarantees that the controller must implement to minimize the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. Depending on the risk management related to the protection of personal data, and integrated with other security obligations of the controller, a level of security must be ensured, which includes, among others, the ability to guarantee confidentiality or to restore the availability and access to data in the event of an incident.  
In particular, and due to its impact, the controller, advised by their DPO, must be aware of what personal data breaches are occurring in the context of their processing, in order to adapt the measures and guarantees to be adopted.  
Examples of these measures to be implemented in health data processing would include:  
• identifying the media using understandable and meaningful labeling systems that allow authorized users to identify their content and make it difficult for others to identify;  
• encoding the data in the distribution of media so that such information is not accessible or manipulated during transport;  
• encrypting the contents of portable devices when they are outside the facilities, such as USB drives, and in the transmission of data over electronic networks (e.g., sending via email);  
• keeping a backup copy of the data and the procedures for their recovery in a location different from where the computer equipment processing them is located;  
• recording, at a minimum, the identification of the user, date and time of each access attempt, the accessed file, the type of access, and whether it was authorized or denied;  
• changing passwords that serve as authentication mechanisms at least once a year and storing them confidentially. The use of password managers facilitates the ability to remember and securely maintain different and strong passwords across various services, as well as changing them periodically.  
• Continuous training of staff.

Is the use of social networks and other similar services recommended for managing appointments or communications with patients?  
When managing appointments or communications with patients through social networks or other services such as instant messaging applications, it must be taken into account that they involve additional processing by a third party. Therefore, the responsible party must always offer alternative means of communication.  
The processing would be lawful if its purpose is the provision of healthcare or social assistance and refers only to personal data that the interested party has provided or that is used within that relationship (for example, the phone number). Therefore, the responsible party cannot use the patient's social networks, but they can use their phone number to notify or remind them of an appointment or other particulars, regardless of the specific application used, as long as it is through the phone (via a call or a text or instant message) and such communication does not simultaneously reach unauthorized persons (for example, if an application like WhatsApp or other similar messaging applications is used, it must be ensured that the message is directed solely to the patient and not to a group of which the patient is a part, even if we have access to the mentioned group).  
However, the processing of personal data carried out through instant messaging applications is subject to data protection principles, and the responsible party has a specific degree of responsibility in selecting them, including the necessary diligence in choosing the most appropriate communication channels. In particular, the principle of data minimization must be applied in their use, revealing only the minimum necessary information through these channels. Furthermore, it must be taken into account that due to the very nature of the clinical relationship, it is possible that the contents of some messages transmitted through these applications may contain health-related data, so it is the responsibility of the data controllers to assess the appropriateness of using them from the perspective of data protection, considering, for example, whether they have a weak or strong password system, whether it is easy to impersonate the user, or whether the messages are encrypted or not. In light of the weaknesses of either system, due to the absence of acceptable security measures, it is likely that communication with the patient through these means is not advisable, especially when it comes to sensitive data.

C) What access rights does the patient have regarding their HC? What happens if data from a third party is provided?  
According to state health regulations (Patient Autonomy Law), the patient has the right to access the documentation of their own HC and the data contained therein. In contrast, data protection regulations do not recognize the right to access specific documents of the HC, but rather to obtain confirmation of whether their personal data is being processed or not, and if so, the right to access it through a copy and to certain information. Specifically, if the data is obtained from the interested party: identity and contact details of the data controller, contact details of the Data Protection Officer, purposes of data processing and the legal basis that supports such processing, recipients or categories of recipients and, where applicable, whether international data transfers will be carried out, the retention period of personal data or criteria for determining it, the possibility to exercise rights; when processing is based on consent, the existence of the right to withdraw it, the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority, or the obligation to provide personal data as a legal requirement, and the existence of automated decisions. Furthermore, if the data has not been obtained from the interested party, they have the right to know the origin of the data and the categories of data in question.  
However, the patient's right of access does not include data from third parties contained in the HC in the therapeutic interest of the patient, nor the subjective notes of healthcare professionals, who have opposed this.  
In the event that data from a third party is provided, the duty of confidentiality would be violated. Consequently, both the institution and the professional could incur liability, which may be administrative (fine in the case of a private institution or professional, warning in the case of a public institution, disciplinary for the professional...), civil (if harm has been caused to the third party, they may have the right to compensation from the institution and/or the healthcare professional), and criminal (fine or prison sentence for revealing or disclosing health data to third parties, violating the patient's privacy).  
The patient has the right to know the accesses that have occurred to their HC (how many accesses, purpose of the access, etc.). However, to date, neither data protection regulations nor state health regulations expressly recognize that this right includes the identification (name and surname) of the professionals who have accessed a patient's medical records (although this information could be known in the context of an ongoing judicial investigation due to suspected unauthorized access).  
However, some regional regulations do recognize the possibility of knowing the identity of those who have accessed it, such as in Navarra and Extremadura, in which case the patient may also request this information when exercising their right of access.  
In any case, the administration or healthcare center has the obligation to implement the necessary security measures to control and, if necessary, prevent access to the HC by unauthorized persons.

E) Should a request for the deletion of data from the medical record (HC) be addressed?  
The deletion of data from the HC is very restricted, as its main purpose is to ensure proper healthcare for the patient. It should be noted that medical records can also serve other secondary functions of general interest (in public health, epidemiology, research, etc.). Therefore, the content of the HC cannot solely be left in the hands of the patient. It is the healthcare professional who decides whether or not to delete a piece of data from the HC, based on its clinical significance.  
Thus, when it comes to data that is not relevant for healthcare (such as data related to how a traffic accident occurred that required medical assistance and that does not have health significance), such data should be able to be deleted.  

F) If clinical documentation has been incorrectly deleted, what would be the appropriate course of action? Should it be reported to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection (AEPD) or, where applicable, to the competent Autonomous Authority?  
In addition to breaching the obligation to preserve the medical record, to the extent that this documentation contains personal data, we could find ourselves in situations of breaches regarding the confidentiality, availability, or integrity of the HC, from which corresponding legal liability (penalty, compensation, etc.) may arise.  
Non-exhaustively, some examples of breaches of personal data are:  
• The accidental destruction or loss of personal data, total or partial. This constitutes a breach of availability and/or integrity if there is no other recoverable copy of the data.  
• The incorrect or incomplete destruction of personal data when it should have been deleted constitutes a breach of confidentiality since unauthorized third parties could access that data.  
• Cyber incidents of ransomware type that cause the encryption of personal data and information systems, preventing their processing, constitute a breach of availability. If, in addition, prior to the encryption, there has been exfiltration of personal data, it will also constitute a breach of confidentiality.  
• The improper or unauthorized alteration of personal data (for example, if a third party alters or falsifies the result of a diagnostic test) constitutes a breach of integrity.  
In all these cases, the healthcare professional must assess the risk that the breach may pose to the affected individuals. If such a risk exists, they must notify the AEPD and also inform the affected individuals if the risk is high.  
In the AEPD's guide for the management and notification of personal data breaches, you will find more details on how to act in these cases and when and how these incidents should be reported and/or communicated.

To avoid the communication of health data of a person (without their consent) or the deletion of such data with the consequent harm, mechanisms for identifying the person must be established to verify that they correspond to the interested party (by requesting several identifying details from the patient, such as name, ID number, health card number, phone number, etc., and checking that they match those in the center's database). Information about a person's admission and/or the room they are in cannot be provided if consent has not been obtained to disclose such information. If the patient is not in a condition to give consent (is not physically or legally capable of doing so; for example, because they are unconscious or are a very young minor), family members may consent. In these cases, only information about whether the person is in emergency services or admitted and the room number will be provided, without indicating health data or the medical care provided.

8. MANAGEMENT OF SECURITY IN FACILITIES  
A) Can surveillance cameras be placed in consultation hallways or waiting rooms?  
Yes, as the capture of images by surveillance cameras in those establishments open to the public is aimed at increasing and ensuring the safety of both the facilities and the users and patients.  
The cameras may only capture those common areas, but they should not be directed towards the consultations, to avoid recording their interiors. Furthermore, special consideration must be given to the fact that one or more informative signs must be installed at the various accesses to those monitored areas, in a visible location, warning that access is to a monitored area. The sign must clearly indicate that personal data is being processed, the identity of the responsible party, the possibility of exercising rights, and a reference to where to obtain more information.  
In the event that such cameras are to be used for purposes other than security, such as labor control, the professionals and union representatives must be informed in advance.  

B) Is it contrary to data protection regulations for security personnel at a healthcare facility to request identification from individuals who may appear suspicious?  
No. Security personnel have among their functions the authority to conduct identity checks for the protection of the facility and the individuals who may be present, without being able to retain personal documentation in any case. Refusal to present identification will empower the security guard to deny users access to the facility or to order them to leave.

In this case, where the relationship with the hospital or clinic is limited to the lease of a premises, a place where healthcare is provided, the healthcare professional is responsible for the processing of personal data of their patients, as they are the ones who decide on the purposes and means of the processing. Thus, in relation to the medical records for which they are responsible for custody and preservation, they decide how to provide information regarding data protection to their patients, being responsible for the preparation of the Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), the implementation of the corresponding DPIA, and the establishment of appropriate security measures, etc. In other words, they are attributed all the obligations derived from the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in relation to the processing carried out.  
There is a second scenario where a healthcare professional, although making all decisions regarding patient care, is contracted for this purpose by the hospital or clinic.  
In this case, where the patients belong to the healthcare center, the healthcare professional is an employee of the clinic or hospital. Therefore, the latter is, as the data controller, the one entrusted with all obligations regarding data protection. Consequently, they will be responsible for providing instructions to their employee on how to act in relation to the medical records, the completion of the same, the determination of the security measures to be followed, or what to do if patients request the exercise of one of the rights provided for in Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR, among other issues.

The legal position of professionals providing services in hospitals or clinics is aimed at assigning responsibilities according to the actual roles of the parties (section 12), which implies that in most cases, the specific circumstances of the case must be considered (case by case) based on their actual activities rather than the formal designation of an actor as “responsible” or “in charge” (for example, in a contract), as well as autonomous concepts, whose interpretation must be carried out under the European regulations on personal data protection (section 13), and taking into account (section 24) that the need for a factual assessment also means that the role of a data controller does not derive from the nature of an entity processing data but from its specific activities in a specific context…  
However, we must clarify that in the public sector, the data controller will always be the health authority, as it is the one entrusted by regulations with health competencies, which requires it to determine the purposes and means of processing.  
And all this without prejudice to the obligations of professional secrecy that healthcare professionals must maintain regarding health data they become aware of in the exercise of their profession.  
In the inquiries made by the DPOs in the healthcare sector during the meeting held with them in 2019, a question was raised about the legal position, as data controller or processor, of a diagnostic testing laboratory.  
The Agency's criterion was that the clinical analysis laboratory providing healthcare assistance through functional or laboratory tests that aid in medical diagnosis and disease prevention must be considered a healthcare center, defined by Article 3 of the LAP as “the organized set of professionals, facilities, and technical means that performs activities and provides services to care for the health of patients and users.”  
Article 14.2 of the LAP states that “Each center shall archive the medical records of its patients, regardless of the paper, audiovisual, computer, or other type of support in which they are recorded, in such a way that their security, proper conservation, and recovery of information are guaranteed,” adding Article 17.1 of the same Law that “Healthcare centers have the obligation to preserve clinical documentation under conditions that guarantee its proper maintenance and security, although not necessarily in the original support, for the adequate assistance to the patient for the appropriate time for each case and, at a minimum, five years from the date of discharge from each healthcare process.”  
Along with the possible processing that may arise from the legal relationship existing between the consulting laboratory and the entity requesting the service (whether it is another healthcare center or an insurer), the LAP imposes on the former the obligation to process the data that must be incorporated into the patient's medical record, obviously exceeding such processing from “the instructions of the data controller,” which determines the impossible application of Article 28 of the GDPR and the impossibility of considering the laboratory as a healthcare center to be merely a processor of the entity for which it performs clinical analyses.  
For this reason, it must be considered that the consulting laboratory will be responsible for the processing of personal data of patients, derived from the clinical analyses it has performed.
