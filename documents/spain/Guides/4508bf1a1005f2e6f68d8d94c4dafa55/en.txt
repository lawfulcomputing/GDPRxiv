Foreword  
25 years of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection  
It is an honor for me to preface a book that  
describes the twenty-five years of history of the  
Spanish Agency for Data Protection. An Agency that  
has been characterized by intense and diligent activity  
in defense of the fundamental right to the protection  
of personal data and by a proven criterion of  
independence in the exercise of its powers.  
In recognition of its first director, Juan José Martín-  
Casallo, the initial reference date taken is the date  
of his appointment: October 23, 1993.  
Since that moment until the autumn of 2018,  
twenty-five years of rapid evolution of this fundamental  
right have passed in all areas: regulatory, institutional,  
jurisprudential, international cooperation, public  
dissemination and knowledge, and, above all,  
adaptation to technological developments.  
Today we live in a digital society in which, with great  
ease and low cost, large volumes of information about  
people's lives are stored, exchanged, analyzed, and  
used for multiple purposes.  
Internet activity has undergone an intense process  
of concentration in which the business model of  
most service providers is based on the monetization  
of users' personal information.  
This model translates into the offering of free services  
that are financed with personalized advertising  
whose origin lies in an analysis of the browsing  
habits of Internet users to create detailed profiles  
about their interests and hobbies.  
This revolution has meant that the Agency has  
moved from managing a few dozen complaints  
mainly related to improper inclusion in debt files  
and unwanted advertising by national companies,  
to guaranteeing the protection of personal data  
with respect to multinational companies whose  
services affect more than 4 billion Internet users.  
And, complementarily, that the initial protections of  
the rights of access, cancellation, and opposition of  
citizens in traditional data processing have evolved  
to their protection in the context of Internet search  
services or social networks, configuring new rights  
such as the well-known "right to be forgotten" on the  
Internet.  
Thus, the Agency, after analyzing the compatibility  
of Google's privacy policy with Spanish data protection  
regulations, declared that the search engine did not  
include adequate information about the data being  
collected and about the purpose of its processing,  
which consequently prevented valid consent, that  
data was being stored and retained for indefinite or  
unjustified periods, and that the exercise of rights  
by citizens was being hindered. This declaration by  
the Spanish Agency for Data Protection led to the  
modification of the company's privacy policy at a  
global level.  
The same exercise was carried out when one of the  
most prominent social networks, Facebook, acquired  
the most widely used instant messaging application,  
WhatsApp, and imposed as mandatory, in order to  
use it, authorization to access and

The global village that was heralded by the most famous communication theorist, the Canadian Marshall McLuhan, the global village, we said, that human worldwide interconnection through electronic media, has long since materialized. And Spain, one of the fifteen world powers, stands at the forefront of this wave. Consequently, the social transformations that have occurred over the last quarter of a century in our country have been accompanied by significant technological advancements. In general terms, today's Spanish society has more and better access to new information and communication technologies (ICT) than it did in the early 1990s. The average citizen today uses daily, and increasingly, tools and services that they could not have imagined before. However, not all sectors of Spanish society have enjoyed technological progress equally. The noted social, economic, and cultural inequalities explain the lesser access to technical means that some segments of the population have suffered, resulting in a clear digital divide among the Spanish population.  
In this chapter, a chronological review of technological advancements is conducted, and the most representative indicators of the equipment and use of information and communication technologies by citizens are compiled. Additionally, the use of digital technologies in daily life is described, as well as how these tools are transforming people's attitudes, habits, and environments.  
Before detailing the chronological evolution of technological advancements that have occurred in Spain over the last 25 years, a brief conceptual clarification is necessary. By "information and communication technologies," we understand:  
“Technological devices (hardware and software) that allow for editing, producing, storing, exchanging, and transmitting data between different information systems that have common protocols. These applications, which integrate computing, telecommunications, and networks, enable both interpersonal communication and collaboration (person to person) as well as multidirectional communication (one to many or many to many). These tools play a substantive role in the generation, exchange, dissemination, management, and access to knowledge. The accelerated innovation and hybridization of these devices have impacted various scenarios. Among them, the following stand out: social relationships, organizational structures, teaching-learning methods, forms of cultural expression, business models, national and international public policies, and scientific production (R&D), among others. In the context of knowledge societies, these means can contribute to educational, labor, political, economic development, social well-being, among other areas of daily life” (Juan Cristóbal Cobo Romaní, 2009).  
Throughout this text, we will refer to ICT adhering to this definition, and we will emphasize both the technical and practical aspects of the referred technologies as well as their social consequences.

I. Telecommunications  
   1. Fixed Telephony  
Fixed telephony –something perfectly conventional today; in its origins, a wonderful, almost magical, support developed by Alexander Graham Bell– was the first ICT, as it allowed for immediate connection between interlocutors who until then remained disconnected due to distance. More than a century and a quarter after its arrival in Spain, with pioneering trials and the first connections, by the Government and the Crown, as well as the military sphere. And soon it will be a century since the foundation in 1924 of the National Telephone Company of Spain. The initial low demand, the lack of resources from private initiative, changing legislation, and political fluctuations gave way to such extensive expansion that it reached numerous Spanish households. Fixed telephony remains with high levels of penetration among the population, although since the mid-first decade of this century, the percentage of households using fixed phones has decreased. While in 2006 almost 83 percent of households had a conventional telephone, currently, less than 78 percent do, according to the latest Survey on the Equipment and Use of Information and Communication Technologies conducted by the National Institute of Statistics (INE) in 2017.

3. Internet  
In addition to mobile telephony, the other great global revolution of the 1990s was the arrival and mass commercialization of the Internet. Since then, its impact on the population has progressively increased, particularly during the last decade: in 2006, less than 40 percent of households had a connection to the network, while today the figure has more than doubled, exceeding 80 percent. A very expressive figure of the speed of that growth in the last twelve years. With the Internet as a communication tool, conventional mail has notably decreased, being replaced by electronic mail, whose use, although frequent, is mainly confined to the professional sphere. Because in the individual realm, modern applications dominate: WhatsApp, Telegram, Facebook, and Instagram. As for the use of the Internet as a consulting element, the facts are evident: encyclopedias have ceased to occupy space on domestic shelves and are now a relic in second-hand bookstores, numerous archives and libraries are at our fingertips with a click, and even the Official State Bulletin has ceased to be published on paper and is consulted online.

1. Computers  
According to the Survey on Equipment and Use of Information and Communication Technologies, 36 percent of households in Spain had a computer in 2002. Currently, the proportion is nearly 80 percent. Both figures are quite striking: if twelve years ago the penetration was already noticeable, exceeding one-third of households, today it far exceeds three-quarters, a true sign of modernity.  

Again, we see how the increase in the acquisition of these technologies has been accompanied by an increase in their use. In fact, in 2017, 74 percent of Spaniards used a personal computer in the last three months. However, the ownership and use of personal computers are not distributed evenly among the different segments of the Spanish population. Considerable differences are again observed between age groups (where age is the distinguishing feature) and urban concentrations. According to the Survey on Equipment and Use of Technologies conducted by INE for 2017, 94 percent of Spaniards aged 16 to 24 have used a computer in the last three months, while only 36 percent of those over 65 do the same. Likewise, in urban concentrations with more than 100,000 inhabitants, the proportion reaches 79 percent, while in those with populations under 10,000, the figure is only 66 percent.

2. Smartphones, tablets, and smart devices  
Combining the portability of mobile telephony with the operational power of computers, smartphones have become another device of mass use among Spaniards. These not only allow the user to make phone calls but also to send and receive emails, browse the Internet, and use a wide variety of programs (or applications, “apps”) installed on the smartphone's operating system. According to the Google Consumer Barometer (2018), 87 percent of the Spanish population uses a smartphone—a figure considerably higher than the number of people who use a computer. In fact, smartphone penetration surpassed that of computers in the last five years.  
Beyond computers, smartphones lead penetration rates among various types of next-generation smart devices. According to the Global Mobile Consumer Survey, 2017, by the consulting firm Deloitte, smartphones and laptops are the most used among Spaniards, followed by tablets, e-readers, pedometers, conventional mobile phones, and smartwatches. It is noteworthy that the ownership rates of smartphones and personal computers are on par with or exceed the European averages.  
As with other indicators already mentioned, the penetration of smart devices is not uniform across all segments of society. Significant differences are again observed according to age groups; while 43.9 percent of respondents in the May 2018 Barometer of the Center for Sociological Research (CIS) claim to have a tablet in their home, among respondents over 65 years old, the figure drops to 16.1 percent. Regarding smart TVs and video game consoles, the total averages are 36.3 and 31.6 percent respectively, while among those over 65, the proportions barely reach 13.6 and 3 percent.

IV. Consumption  
The technological revolution is not only affecting the ownership and use of devices and social networks, but it is also changing the purchasing habits of Spaniards. Thus, e-commerce is increasingly gaining ground over traditional shopping. Indeed, by early 2018, the volume of the online shopping market was estimated at nearly 9 billion euros, and the five companies with the highest revenue in this field were Amazon, El Corte Inglés, PC Componentes, Media Markt, and Mercadona.  
In 2017, four out of ten people aged 16 to 74 reported having purchased online in the last three months. This percentage shows a constant upward trend since 2006 (the first year of the series), accumulating an increase of 30 points over eleven years.  
However, among Spaniards, there are differences in the willingness to shop online: men are more likely than women, and young people much more than older individuals. Likewise, Spaniards tend to shop online in greater proportion than foreigners. Finally, large urban centers are more prone to online shopping than less populated areas.  
When including respondents who have purchased online in the last twelve months, the most commonly purchased products or services are: vacation accommodation (54.1 percent), sports equipment and clothing (53.5 percent), tickets for shows (47.6 percent), and other travel services (44.7 percent). At the bottom are online educational materials (11.1 percent) and medications (3 percent).  
However, there are certain reservations, and not everyone is willing to make purchases online, including those who use it for other activities. The main reasons for not shopping online cited by the online population are: preference for shopping in person at a store (80.5 percent), concern for privacy or payment security (49.8 percent), lack of skills or knowledge (39.5 percent), and lack of trust in receiving or returning products, in claims and compensations (37.7 percent). The least mentioned reasons are not having a card that allows online payment (18.5 percent), considering delivery problematic (17.7 percent), and stating that foreign sellers do not fulfill orders in Spain (5.3 percent).

But the arrival of information technologies has not only impacted the growing number of students enrolled in non-presential universities: many also take advantage of the benefits and facilities that the Internet provides to obtain specific training through online courses, without the obligation to study in the university system.  
In this regard, the percentage of people using the Internet to take online courses in Spain has grown from 4 to 12 percent over the last ten years, placing our country five points above the average of the European Union in this regard. The data also shows us that the popularity of this type of course has grown at a faster rate in Spain than in the European Union as a whole, as in 2007 there was only a one-point difference in this chapter.  
In addition to using the Internet to take courses in a non-presential manner, this tool has also become indispensable for seeking information when enrolling in any type of training offer, whether it is presential or not. In this sense, in 2007, 22 percent of citizens in Spain used the Internet to inform themselves about educational course offers, three points above the EU-28 average. Beyond formal education, information technologies have influenced the dissemination of courses or tutorials (a neologism of English origin used to refer to certain short courses or sets of instructions) that are not part of an official educational curriculum. In fact, this type of training is much more widespread in the digital environment than regulated education. In this regard, data from the National Statistics Institute reflect that more than a quarter of Spaniards have used online learning material (for example, audiovisual material or some online learning software) that does not constitute a complete course in the last three months. In contrast, those who have taken an online course in the last three months represent 14.8 percent of the population.  
The difference between these two figures is perfectly understandable if we consider the potential that a digital environment offers to select very precisely the information that one wishes to obtain and that which is less of a priority for the individual. A very significant example regarding the acquisition of knowledge or specific skills can be found on online video platforms such as YouTube, where some of its users (whether individuals or organizations) often upload videos offering training on very specific topics. According to data from the Mikroscopia study by the sociological research firm MyWord, 39.7 percent of Spanish users of this site had accessed it in search of educational or learning videos such as solving a math problem or the pronunciation of words in certain languages. Another related case of specific learning is the video tutorials that can be found on this type of platform, among which YouTube is the most popular today. The same Mikroscopia study indicates that up to 56 percent of users of this platform had accessed it with the aim of searching for tutorials on practical topics or specific manuals. In this type of educational content, we can find everything from guides on how to use software to learning a new cooking recipe, which demonstrates the capacity of the Internet to provide specific knowledge on any topic.

If we look at the data on the number of subscribers to pay television, we can see that at the beginning of 2005, there were just over three million people in Spain subscribed to some pay television platform. From this point on, there was significant growth in subscribers, mainly due to the increase in subscriptions to IPTV services. These services currently have four million subscribers, which represents more than half of the total subscribers (which do not reach seven million) to pay television services in Spain.

IX. Conclusion  
The last few decades have brought significant changes in the way the average citizen uses new information and communication technologies. I. Introduction  
As we have seen in the previous chapter, one of the distinctive features of recent decades is the rapid advancement of technology, which has led to considering our time as the era of scientific and technological revolution. Given the gigantic development of robotics and cyber-physical systems, many scientists and economists even announce the arrival of the fourth industrial revolution. The truth is that everyday observation reveals that the telecommunications market has ceased to be an extension of originally military technology adapted for consumers, such as the first GPS (Global Positioning System), to become a regular part of the daily life of the average citizen. From banking operations, to online shopping and social networks, to virtual realities intended for the leisure of children and adults, the constant evolution of technology is part of our individual and collective identity. It is inconceivable not to take this factor into account: no matter how integrated one is in today's world, a large number of matters and activities involve interacting from a computer or a mobile phone.  

The landscape becomes more complex when we consider that this transformation, which is growing exponentially, influences the lives of members of different generations coexisting in this historical moment differently. In just a few years, there have been profound changes in the notions of information, learning, knowledge, and communication. However, all of this is better absorbed by the younger age groups. And it finds even more ease in establishing itself in the most populated urban centers. But at this point, there is no alternative: political, economic, and social systems are forced to adapt and transform to survive. Individuals and communities find themselves needing to reinvent their environment, their needs, opportunities, expectations, and tools to ensure their well-being and progress in this new era.  

This chapter addresses individual attitudes towards various aspects that are part of the constant technological development, with a special emphasis on the issue of personal data security and the exchange of information between platforms. It also evaluates attitudes regarding internet access for minors, different ways to access digital content, and what the advancement of ICT means for the future of the labor market.  

II. Development of Communication and Information through the Internet  

According to the Royal Spanish Academy (RAE), the Internet is defined as a "global, decentralized computer network, formed by the direct connection between computers using a special communication protocol." We understand, then, that various individual networks form a homogeneous whole that connects us as a world. And through the channels of this technological platform, we consume, produce, communicate, and share digital content.  
As we mentioned in the previous chapter, there are many platforms and devices from which we personally manage the digital content of our preference. Due to the nature of the Internet, the divisions between work, recreational, and social areas in the digital landscape become increasingly permeable. Therefore, it is not surprising to share experiences of work on social networks, through an entertaining conversation in which, for example, sources and professional knowledge are exchanged. Moreover, devices are becoming increasingly efficient, faster, and better connected. This allows anyone to share audiovisual documents in real time. We are no longer mere spectators of what experts preach and present. Now we participate and create content as main actors through ICT.

The results show a clear trend where younger users have a more proactive attitude towards accessing digital content.

Other concerns regarding potential harms of the Internet expressed by more than half of users include worries about the loss of value of books, music, movies, and press due to cheaper or free formats available online. The same proportion of people is concerned that easy and quick access to information online may cause a loss of reflective capabilities.

4. Protection Guarantees  
People's attitudes towards technology and the potential risks of sharing data are also related to how they perceive and value the protection guarantees offered by different services and technological platforms.  

Most Spaniards believe that the highest data protection guarantees are provided by the Public Administration (Ministry of Finance, Social Security offices, health services). In the case of banks, the perception of respondents is that they provide high (42.5 percent) or very high (7 percent) guarantees. Following them, with less positive evaluations, are businesses and service companies.  

The results also show that social networks and Internet search engines have the lowest ratings in terms of data protection guarantees. Seventy-two percent of respondents believe that the guarantees of social networks are low or very low, followed by the negative evaluation of the guarantees of Internet search engines (67 percent).

V. Minors and Internet Access  
The use of the Internet by minors is also a matter of interest—and, of course, concern—for the Spanish population. In this regard, data from the National Institute of Statistics indicate that the percentage of individuals aged between 10 and 15 who use a computer is 92.4 percent. Furthermore, among individuals of the same age, the use of the Internet is 95.1 percent, while mobile phone usage reaches 69.1 percent. Given these high usage rates among minors, the concern regarding the level of access they may have to digital content, the level of adult supervision they may need, and the necessity for responsible Internet use is understandable.  
   1. Education in Schools  
A fundamental pillar for protecting minors from inappropriate online content is education about healthy and effective use of the tool. It is striking that currently the majority of Spaniards evaluate negatively the training provided to minors in schools regarding responsible use and safety on the Internet. This is reflected in the data from the CIS Barometer corresponding to May 2018. In fact, less than 30 percent believe that the level of information received is "much," "quite a bit," or "sufficient," while almost 50 percent think it is "little" or "none." Another 22 percent of respondents in the CIS Barometer do not know how to answer the question, a figure that may indicate not only a lack of knowledge among parents about the school curriculum related to ICT but also failures in the information channels of educational institutions.  
   2. Most Common Risks  
Regarding the most common risks to which minors are exposed on the Internet, first, nearly 40 percent identify "the dissemination of compromising photos or videos," followed by "giving too much information about themselves" (23 percent) and "being harassed or bullied to obtain sexual concessions" (17 percent). The trend of these attitudes has remained stable over the years 2013, 2015, 2016, 2017, and 2018.  
   3. Controls, Restrictions, and Responsibilities  
According to the data from this same Barometer, a very considerable part of the population (around 60 percent) believes that minors "should have quite a few restrictions or controls" when using the Internet. Meanwhile, another 24 percent of the population thinks that "access should be completely restricted." Only about 10 percent recommend little or no control. These trends have remained stable over the years 2009, 2013, 2017, and 2018.  
While the vast majority believes that Internet use by minors should be quite restricted or completely limited, another large majority of people (85 percent of respondents) believes that the primary responsibility for establishing these controls lies with the parents. In second place, with an equal level of responsibility assigned (approximately 25 percent believe this), are "schools," "Internet service providers," and "the Government/public authorities." In other words, protecting young Internet users is a shared responsibility, not only of the minors' parents but also of various entities: educators, service providers, and public authorities. The success of this collaboration can lead to a scenario where the level of awareness among young people about safe Internet use allows controls to take a back seat.

Additionally, we also observe the attitudes that Spanish citizens express regarding the effects that this technological revolution has caused so far, and those it may cause in the near future.  
1. New jobs  
Today, the Internet is the tool that has taken on a symbolic role in the current technological revolution, analogous to how the loom was in the 18th century. Thanks to the Internet, many jobs have become more efficient, new job positions have been created that were unthinkable 25 years ago, and jobs that were no longer beneficial in the new digital environment have also ceased to be generated.  
In the case of Spain, thanks to data from the National Institute of Statistics, we can observe how, since 2008, the number of ICT companies currently operating in our country has increased from 43,708 to 66,155. That is, there has been an increase of 51.4 percent over a period of eight years. Furthermore, to contextualize this data, it is worth noting that this period coincides with the great global economic crisis, which precisely since 2008 has shaken our country very intensely. But despite the recession, we observe how the number of companies dedicated to information and communication technologies continued to grow, which gives an idea of the enormous magnitude of the impact of these technologies in Spain.  
Along with the proliferation of new companies in the ICT sector, there has also been a significant increase in the number of employees in companies in this sector. In this indicator, the effect of the 2008 economic crisis in Spain can be seen more clearly. Between that year and 2013, the number of employed individuals in the ICT sector decreased by 25,600 people, going from 415,605 employees to 390,005. After that year, the trend reversed, and employment went from being destroyed to being created, so that by 2016, the number of ICT workers in our country was 448,498, the highest figure to date.  
It is important to emphasize the magnitude of the phenomenon. Most of these nearly 450,000 jobs correspond to positions that did not exist in Spain in 1993, 25 years ago. Examples of this include specialists in web positioning, also known as SEO (an acronym for Search Engine Optimization, which refers to the activity aimed at optimizing and increasing the popularity of a website) or SEM (an acronym for Search Engine Marketing, which refers to the promotion of a website in search engines through the use of paid advertisements via platforms, such as Google AdWords). The goal of these professionals is to achieve the greatest visibility for their client within the Internet. To do this, it is necessary to have a good understanding of the internal algorithms of search engines, such as Google. This type of professional specializes in integrating keywords within their client's web space, so that their website appears among the top results in search engines.  

This job is currently in high demand by many companies, to the extent that there are companies made up of this type of professionals that offer SEO consulting to the companies that hire them.  
Another job related to the ICT sector involves the processing of data generated by our activity on the web. This other job, which also did not exist 25 years ago, is that of big data analysts. Currently, while browsing the Internet, thousands of data points are generated based on information related to our activity online: what pages we have visited previously, how long it took us to click a certain button, how much time we spent reading a part of the page.  

Big data analysts, among others, are responsible for making the most of this volume of data. These professionals specialize in acquiring and exploiting databases with the aim of finding trends or patterns that may be useful for their company. This type of job profile requires multidisciplinary training, with a strong foundation in computer programming, statistics.

II. The beginnings of the Agency (1993-1999)  
The 1978 Constitution already intuited the potential magnitude of new technologies and the unstoppable development of communication media, by providing in its article 18.4: “The law shall limit the use of computing to guarantee the honor and personal and family privacy of citizens and the full exercise of their rights.”  
The Council of Europe had foreseen in Convention 108 of 1981 the existence of an independent authority to safeguard this right.  
However, it took 14 years for that constitutional provision to have a regulatory development. In October 1992, the Organic Law 5/1992, of October 29, on the Automated Processing of Personal Data, LORTAD, was approved by the Congress of Deputies, which created the Data Protection Agency. Until the arrival of this law, it was the institution of the Ombudsman that was responsible for resolving issues related to the protection of this fundamental right.  
The Law also creates the Advisory Council as a collegiate advisory body for the Agency's management, composed of ten members appointed for a period of four years.  
Spain thus advanced three years ahead of the international configuration of this type of institution.  
At that time, mobile phones barely existed, personal computers were luxury items coexisting with typewriters, while television was expanding.

1. The General Registry of Data Protection  
At the beginning of its activity, the priority of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection was the creation and organization of the General Registry of Data Protection.  
The fundamental purpose of creating the Registry and establishing the obligation to notify and register files was to facilitate free access for any citizen to the information contained in those databases. In this way, citizens could identify the holders of the files in which their personal data had been included, and know the contact address of their responsible parties, the purposes for which the information would be used, the type of data incorporated into them, and the planned transfers and international data transfers. Once identified, they could exercise their right of access to know whether or not they were included in them and, if so, what information had been incorporated.  
Thus, if a person discovered that they were included in a delinquency file when approaching a financial institution to request a credit card or financing for the purchase of a vehicle, they could go to the Agency and request information for free about the existing delinquency files. Once the holders of the files were known, they could exercise their right of access before them to obtain information on whether they were indeed registered or not. And, if they were, to identify in which files they appeared, which entity had classified them as delinquent and communicated their inclusion in the file, the amount of debt, as well as another particularly relevant piece of information such as the evaluations and assessments regarding the risks of the affected individual that had been made in the last six months, and the name and address of those to whom this information had been communicated.  
If the affected individual considered that this inclusion was unlawful, either because they had no relationship with the alleged creditor, or even if they did, because they had no outstanding debts, they could exercise the right of cancellation both before the holder of the delinquency file and before the entity that had provided the information included in the file, a financial entity for...

   2. The first inquiries  
As citizens' knowledge of their rights improved, inquiries about how to know the identity of file holders and how to guarantee their exercise increased.  
From the beginning, the Spanish Agency for Data Protection has been tasked with providing information to citizens about their rights in relation to the processing of their personal data. For this reason, in April 1994, the Citizen Attention Area began operating, which provided telephone, in-person information and responded to written inquiries.  
However, at that initial moment, the Agency's efforts were concentrated on informing the data controllers, which resulted in a relatively low level of awareness among citizens regarding the rights and possibilities for exercising them that the Law and the Agency offered.  
Once this initial moment was overcome, there was a slow but gradual increase in the number of citizen inquiries.  
When comparing the data from the beginning of the period with those at the end, a stability in the number of inquiries handled over these years is observed.    1. The LOPD  
In this stage, the year 2000 brought together two events of special significance for the protection of personal data: on one hand, the entry into force on January 14 of Organic Law 15/1999, on the Protection of Personal Data, the LOPD, which replaced the LORTAD and became the cornerstone of the system of guarantees for this right in our country; and on the other hand, the ruling of the Constitutional Court 292/2000, which will be referred to later.  
This ruling from the Constitutional Court coincides in time with the proclamation of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, made at the Nice Summit on December 7 of the same year. Article 8 of the aforementioned Charter expressly recognizes the right to the protection of personal data.  
Alongside this recognition, Article 8 of the Charter additionally describes a reference to the principles of data processing (“they shall be processed fairly, for specified purposes”), to the legal bases for it (“on the basis of the consent of the data subject or pursuant to another legitimate basis provided by law”), and to some of the rights that comprise its essential content (“Everyone has the right to access data concerning them and to have it rectified”).  
And, notably, it adds a structural element of the system of guarantees for the protection of this right, of an institutional nature, by establishing that “compliance with these rules shall be subject to the control of an independent authority.” Thus, the Charter “constitutionalizes” the independence of the supervisory authorities.

The approval of the LOPD was the direct consequence of the transposition in Spain of the European Directive 95/46/EC, of October 24, 1995, regarding the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data.  
This Directive represented an important step in the regulation of personal data protection within the European Union, not only due to the establishment of a broad regime of guarantees for the protection of this right but also because of the adoption of an essential objective in the European context, which is to ensure the free movement of personal data as a complement to the free movement of goods, persons, services, and capital, for the establishment and functioning of the internal market.  
To achieve this, the Directive sought to establish a harmonized protection system while recognizing the Member States a margin of maneuver in their national law.  
In relation to its predecessor, the new Organic Law introduced significant changes in the regulation of data protection, expanding the scope of the Law to include all data files, whether computerized or not.  
The application of the Law to personal data collected in other media, and fundamentally on paper, completed the application of the regime of guarantees to all personal data processing at a time when paper files represented a significant volume. This expansion, aimed at avoiding serious risks of evasion of the norm, only covered structured manual files with specific criteria related to individuals that allowed easy access to personal data, excluding unstructured folders.  
Likewise, the principle of purpose for the use of data was reinforced, as the purposes for which data may be used must not only be legitimate, as required by the old Law, but also determined and explicit, with the necessity that the affected party is in all cases clearly informed of the purposes for which the data processing is carried out.  
This specification of the principle of purpose was particularly relevant in some data processing activities, such as those related to advertising and marketing, which had generated a higher volume of complaints from citizens. It required companies engaged in this activity to include in their informative clauses for obtaining consent a reference to the specific sectors for which the sending of commercial communications was authorized (such as financial products, insurance, telecommunications...), allowing the affected parties to opt for those that were of interest to them or to reject them otherwise.  
Additionally, information to citizens and options to avoid unwanted advertising were expanded in those cases where the law allowed it to be carried out without the consent of its recipients, for example, by using data from public sources such as telephone directories. In these cases, it was required to provide in each advertising communication information about the origin of the data, the identity of the advertiser, and the rights that recipients have.

   2.  The Judgment of the Constitutional Court  
292/2000  
As a second significant event to highlight during this  
period, reference must be made to the ruling of the  
Constitutional Court through judgment 292/2000. Its relevance lies in the fact that the right to  
the protection of personal data is configured  
as an autonomous and distinct right from the right to  
privacy, which aims to grant the individual  
power of disposition and control over their personal data,  
both in the private sector and the  
public sector.  
The judgment starts from the consideration that,  
unlike the right to privacy, which allows  
for the guarantee of a reserved sphere for the individual, the  
right to data protection grants a power  
of disposition and control over personal information,  
even when it is accessible to third parties, both  
public and private.  
On the other hand, for the exclusive scope of  
data transfers between Public Administrations, the  
judgment restricts their use to cases where  
they exercise similar competencies. Therefore, any  
data transfer between Public Administrations may only  
be carried out if this condition is met, or is  
authorized by a norm with the formal rank of law.

1. The development regulation of the Organic Law on Data Protection  
After the first years of application of the Organic Law, already under the direction of José Luis Piñar Mañas, the need for regulatory development was noted to achieve greater levels of legal security when applying this norm.  
The Regulation finally came to light at the beginning of Artemi Rallo Lombarte's mandate with the publication of Royal Decree 1720/2007, of December 21.  
The regulation decisively contributed to objectifying the application criteria of the Organic Law, benefiting not only the Resolutions of the Agency itself but, above all, the rulings of the Administrative Litigation Chamber of the National Court and the Supreme Court, and fulfilled the obligation to transpose Directive 95/46/EC.  
One of the most notable innovations of the Regulation, in relation to so-called solvency files, was that the requirements for the inclusion of data and for accessing information in these files were specified.  
In solvency treatments, four parties usually intervene: the debtor, the creditor who notifies the debt, the person responsible for the management of solvency data, and the entities that consult this information. To include the personal data of a natural person in a "delinquent file," the following requirements must be met:  
• There must be a certain, due, enforceable debt that has gone unpaid.  
• Six years must not have elapsed since the date when payment of the debt was due or the expiration of the obligation or the corresponding installment of the deferred payment.

The AEPD is not competent to resolve disputes regarding the existence of a debt. The consent of the data subject is not required to communicate their data to a debt collection agency.    •  Cloud computing  
The provision of "cloud computing" services in its various types of cloud (public, private, hybrid, etc.) and service modalities (infrastructure as a service, platform as a service, and software as a service) has altered the traditional relationships between clients—data controllers—and data processors—cloud computing service providers.  
The paradigm shift in these relationships led the Agency to initiate an analysis process regarding its implications and the necessary adjustments in the application of data protection regulations to guarantee citizens' rights.  
This analysis concluded with the preparation of a guide on cloud computing, the most notable aspects of which are as follows:  
-  
The applicable legislation regarding data protection is that of the client processing data in Spain.  
-  
The cloud computing service provider is a data processor, even if it is a large multinational company. The client contracting with the service provider remains responsible for data processing and must act diligently in choosing a cloud service provider. The service provider, for its part, must be diligent in providing guarantees for data protection. Transparency plays an important role in this diligence, allowing for the acquisition of information about these guarantees and, in particular, about those existing in subcontracting services and in international data transfers.  
-  
The client must inform themselves about whether the security measures are adequate for data processing and obtain guarantees to audit them, even if through a reliable and independent third party.

   •  The crisis of the Safe Harbor Framework  
On October 6, 2015, a crisis occurred regarding the system that served as the basis for international data transfers from Europe to the United States. On that day, the ruling of the Court of Justice of the European Union was published, invalidating European Commission Decision 2000/520/EC, known as the Safe Harbor Decision, which regulated the transfer of data from the European Union to entities established in the United States that were part of the "Safe Harbor" system.

The Court's decision was the result of a complaint by Maximiliam Schrems, an Austrian national and Facebook user, in which he requested that Facebook Ireland not transfer data to Facebook Inc., its parent company based in the United States.  
The complainant argued that the laws and practices in force in the United States did not guarantee sufficient protection of personal data stored in its territory against surveillance activities conducted by its public authorities. Mr. Schrems referred in this regard to the revelations by Edward Snowden regarding the activities of U.S. intelligence services, particularly those of the National Security Agency (NSA).  
Among the motivations for the Judgment is the revelation of the existence in the United States of several surveillance programs for the collection and processing of large-scale personal data. It was found that, as a consequence, for example, of the contracting of Internet services, U.S. authorities had access to personal data of European residents and that this data was used for purposes different from and incompatible with those that justified the transfers. It was also established that the affected individuals could not exercise any legal action to access their personal data and that they were equally barred from rectifying or deleting it.  
The ruling affected Internet companies such as Google, Facebook, Microsoft, Apple, or Yahoo, which have hundreds of millions of customers in Europe and transfer the personal data of those customers for processing from Europe to the United States.  
The ruling was also influenced by Mr. Snowden's revelations about the activities of the National Security Agency (NSA) which, under the guise of national security, public interest, or compliance with U.S. law, could access the data of Europeans that had been transferred to the United States, without any rules to limit potential infringements on their rights.  
The oversight of the actions of the intelligence services is carried out through a secret and non-adversarial procedure. Once personal data is transferred to the United States, the NSA and other federal agencies, such as the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), can access it in the context of surveillance and indiscriminate interceptions that they carry out on a large scale.  
All these reasons and others led to the declaration of invalidity of the Safe Harbor system. Therefore, between that date (October 6, 2015) and July 12, 2016, when the Commission approved the Decision of the “Privacy Shield” to replace the annulled one, the Spanish Agency undertook a series of actions aimed at mitigating the lack of guarantees for individuals whose data had been and were subject to transfers to entities in the United States adhering to the Safe Harbor system.

The General Data Protection Regulation. Main innovations. New rights  
On May 25, 2016, the new General Data Protection Regulation was approved, a norm with which the European Union aims to respond to the challenges posed by the widespread use of information and communication technologies.  

138

25 years of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection  
The Regulation can be considered an evolution of Directive 95/46/EC, which it replaces, and from which it reinforces the basic principles of data protection and the rights of data subjects.  
The first of these is that the Regulation is a norm that applies directly in the Member States, without the need for transposition norms. These may, however, have development norms in cases where the Regulation empowers them to do so. This has occurred in Spain with the Organic Law on Personal Data Protection, whose parliamentary processing will conclude in November 2018.  
From the citizens' perspective, this norm, which standardizes European data protection law, guarantees a similar level of protection throughout the Union. It is, moreover, the only case of a fundamental right regulated by an EU Regulation.  
Another highlighted innovation is that its provisions apply to processing that affects citizens residing in the European Union, regardless of whether the controller or processor of that data is established in the Union or not. In cases where there is no establishment in the Union, the Regulation will apply as long as those processes are related to the offering of goods and services to citizens in the Union or with activities monitoring their behavior even when they are in the Union.  
Likewise, the control that citizens have over their personal data is reinforced. Thus, a better regulation of consent is established, which must be given unequivocally through clear statements or affirmative actions, or the information that is now a right linked to the principle of transparency, and new rights such as the limitation of processing or portability are recognized.  
However, from the perspective of procedures and protection mechanisms, the Regulation contains significant innovations.

The Regulation also mentions the "right to be forgotten."  
However, it has refrained from configuring it as an autonomous right, following the line of the Court of Justice of the European Union in the Judgment regarding the Google Spain case, where it stated that this right is an adaptation to the activity of search engines of classic rights such as the right to erasure and the right to object.  
The Regulation also includes a novelty in the case of the right to object, stipulating that it will be the responsibility of the data controller to demonstrate that their interests prevail over the rights, freedoms, and interests of the data subject in relation to their specific circumstances.  
Regarding the obligations of data controllers and processors, the Regulation opts for a new compliance model, which pivots on a proactive conduct of controllers and processors from the perspective of the risks that data processing may pose to the rights and freedoms of data subjects.  
This new approach represents a substantial shift in how compliance with data protection regulations should be understood. In the system that has been in place until now, the focus of regulators and regulated entities has been on results. Data is processed in an attempt to comply with legislation to avoid possible infringements. The supervision of regulators generally begins when that infringement has occurred.  
The Regulation, on the contrary, places the obligations of the obligated parties and also the action of the supervisors in the realm of proactive, preventive measures that must be adopted to ensure that controllers and processors are in a position to comply with its provisions.  
This preventive approach is combined with a risk-based approach to determine what the actions of organizations should be. The convergence of both means that organizations must document and be able to demonstrate that they have analyzed the risks of the data processing they carry out and have applied appropriate measures to eliminate or mitigate them to acceptable levels.  
Among these measures are those of data protection by design and by default, the maintenance of a record of processing activities, the necessity to apply security measures appropriate to the risk arising from the processing, the carrying out of data protection impact assessments for processing that may seem to entail a high risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects, and the also mandatory appointment of a data protection officer in all public organizations and in private ones that carry out certain types of processing.  
Another of the most relevant novelties of the EU Regulation, which marks a clear difference with the Directive, is related to the new model of supervision, which shifts from focusing on verifying whether an infringement has occurred, or whether the prescribed measures are being correctly applied, to having to assess, together with controllers and processors, the processes of risk analysis and the decisions regarding the application of measures that minimize them.  
It is, moreover, a more flexible system, as it opens up the range of possibilities for supervisory authorities to a broad set of

corrective measures (warning, reprimand, etc.) whose application must be assessed according to the specific case, even before imposing economic sanctions.  
All of this does not exclude the fact that the regulation provides for very significant economic sanctions, which can reach up to 20 million euros or an equivalent amount of 4% of the total annual global turnover of the company in the previous financial year. Sanctions that can have a deterrent effect and whose application will depend on the level of diligence adopted for compliance with the Regulation and on various circumstances related to their impact on the rights and freedoms of citizens.  
It is, at the same time, a model of supervision that could be described as cooperative, as the authorities will be obliged to cooperate not only in establishing the criteria for its interpretation but also in its application to the specific case when several authorities may be affected as a result of a particular processing. This obligation of cooperation is regulated in the complex procedures of cooperation and consistency. In this procedure, the European Data Protection Board plays a particularly relevant role and, ultimately, the Court of Justice of the European Union.

The combined effect of all these measures should ultimately result in a change in the data protection culture of organizations and in better protection of citizens' rights.

I. Introduction  
If any single word can define the future of the information society, communication technologies and the processing of personal data in Spain over the last 25 years, that word is dream.  
In the last quarter of a century we have seen amazing technological leaps and all were guided deep down by the hope of making a better, freer, more open, more just world, and with greater opportunities for all.  
In that period, many companies have emerged and many others have gone by the way. The year in which the Agency started was a year full of events related to the processing of information.    1. The LOPD  
The year 2000 brought together two events of special importance for the protection of personal data: on the one hand, the entry into force on January 14 of the Organic Law 15/1999, on the Protection of Personal Data, the LOPD, which replaced the LORTAD and became key to the system of guarantees of this right in our country and, on the other, the ruling of the Constitutional Court 292/2000, which will be explored in more detail below. This pronouncement of the Constitutional Court coincided with the declaration of the European Union’s Charter of Fundamental Rights, made at the Nice Summit on December 7 of the same year. Article 8 of the aforementioned Charter expressly recognizes the right to personal data protection. Along with this recognition, Article 8 also makes reference to the principles of data processing (“that will be treated faithfully, for specific purposes”), to the legal basis thereof (“on the basis of consent of the person affected or by virtue of another legitimate foundation provided by law”) and the rights comprising its core content (“Everyone has the right to access data collected about them, and their rectification”). And, especially, it adds a structural element to the system of guarantees for the protection of this right, of an institutional nature, by establishing that “respect for these norms will be subject to the control of an independent authority”. In this way, the Charter “constitutionalizes” the independence of the control authorities. The approval of the LOPD was a direct consequence of the transposition in Spain of the European Directive 95/46/CE, of October 24, 1995, regarding the protection of persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free circulation of these data. This Directive represented an important step in the regulation of the protection of personal data within the scope of the European Union, not only for the development of a broad system of guarantees for the protection of this right, but also to acknowledge a key objective in the European sphere, which is to guarantee the free circulation of personal data as a complement.

in the fact that the right to the protection of personal  
data is established as an autonomous right and distinct  
from the right to privacy, which aims to give citizens

The ruling is based on the consideration that, unlike the right to privacy, which allows the guaranteeing of a ‘private sphere’, the right to data protection attributes a power of disposition and control over personal information, even when it is accessible to third parties, both public and private.  
On the other hand, for the exclusive sphere of data transfer between Public Administrations, the ruling restricts use to cases in which they exercise similar powers. Therefore, any transfer of data between Public Administrations can only be carried out if this condition is fulfilled, or is authorized by a regulation with formal legal status.  

3. New responsibilities in the field of electronic communications. The Law on Services of the Information Society and Electronic Commerce (LSSI) and the General Telecommunications Law (LGT)  
In 2003, a significant modification of the Organic Law on Data Protection occurred, with regard to the transparency of the Agency’s actions: it was decided to publish its resolutions, preferably through computer or telematic means, which took place from January 1, 2004, through the website of the Agency.

During this period, the regulatory framework for the protection of personal data was extended and updated to new technological environments through the approval of laws 34/2002, Services of the Information Society and Electronic Commerce (LSSI) and of Law 32/2003, on General Telecommunications (LGT). The Directive took into account the introduction of new advanced digital technologies in public communication networks and new electronic communication services, as well as the importance of the Internet in providing a common global infrastructure for the provision of a wide range of services. Consequently, the Directive addressed the challenge of harmonizing the legal provisions of Member States to update the protection of personal data in these environments and to avoid obstacles for electronic communications within the internal market, guaranteeing in any case that the promotion and development of new electronic communication services and networks were not hindered.

The immediate future will offer many new developments in this field. Concepts such as ‘big data’, ‘the Internet of things’, ‘artificial intelligence’, ‘blockchain’ and the associated technologies that make them possible, are going to have, and already have, an enormous impact in the field of personal information.

Modern technologies and their use of personal information have improved our lives, and they will undoubtedly do so even more in the coming years. They are a determining factor for change and innovation.

Its role in the development of scientific research in all fields is crucial, but in particular in the field of health. The possibilities that technologies offer for the collection and processing of personal information are presented to us as practically unlimited.

Search engines have become an essential tool in the daily life of all Internet users. Without them, it would be enormously difficult to access existing information on the network. But, at the same time, their potential is increasingly problematic, because they allow us to be located instantaneously, overcoming all barriers of time and space, and provide information of all kinds relating to a person. The recovery and aggregation capabilities of search engines can cause considerable harm to individuals, both in their personal lives and in their social relationships.  
One of the most important issues in the current debate on privacy on the Internet is related to what has been called the ‘right to be forgotten’.  
The resolutions of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection in terms of protection of rights, and particularly to the right of deletion, had been accepted at specific moments by those responsible for a number of web pages, but the same did not happen when a decision affected Google: in fact, the largest search service provider systematically challenged the Agency’s decisions before the contentious-administrative jurisdiction.  
The National Court decided on February 27, 2012 and before the resolution of the appeals filed by Google, to file a preliminary issue before the European Union’s Court of Justice. It sought to re-interpret the European Court’s interpretation of several sections of Directive 95/46/EC which were relevant for the resolution of more than 200 appeals pending before the Hearing; in particular, those relating to a number of key questions about the applicable law, the responsibility of search engines and of website owners, the powers of the data protection authorities and the possibility of avoiding the indexation of personal information.  
On May 13, 2014, with the Director of the Agency, Mr. José Luis Rodríguez Álvarez, at its head, the Court of Justice of the European Union issued its ruling in case C 131/12 (Google Spain, SL, Google Inc. vs. The Spanish Agency for Data Protection (AEPD), Mario Costeja González), resolving the aforementioned question referred in 2012 by the National Audience.  
The Judgment fully accepted the thesis that the Spanish Data Protection Agency had put forward, and its verdict led to a change in Google’s privacy policy, making the Agency a reference worldwide among the Control Authorities.

25 of the Spanish Data Protection Agency  
In essence, the Judgment established that European law, and more specifically Spanish legislation, are applicable to Google, and requires the company to have an establishment in European territory. On the other hand, the judgment considered that the technical operations performed by search engines to find information on the Internet are integrated into the definition of ‘processing’ offered by the Directive, and that the search engines themselves are responsible for that treatment because they decide on the means and on the ends. The European Court also stressed that the activity of search engines has a significant impact on the fundamental rights of respect for privacy and protection of personal data, since in searches carried out on the Internet from the name of a person, it is possible to obtain a complete and structured view of all the existing information about them and this would allow more or less detailed profiles to be developed. For the Judgment, it is the universal dissemination and accessibility offered by the search engines that can lead to injury to the rights of people in a much more intense and serious way than the original publication of information. The Court of Justice did not accept the thesis that the activity of the search engine would be, in some ways, legitimized by the right of freedom of expression. For the Court, the interest that the search engine has in processing information is merely economic and is insufficient to justify the serious interference with individual rights that it entails. In general terms, the rights of the affected person would also prevail over that interest in locating a piece of information through nominative searches, but in each specific case that balance will depend on the relative weighting between the nature of the information in question, its sensitive nature for the private life of the affected person and the public interest in having the information. It is important to note that the Court expressly states that it is not necessary that harm be caused to exercise the right against the search engine. In May 2015 the Agency, after the appropriate inspection actions, declared illegal Google’s practice via which, through the Webmaster Tools service, it communicated to the people responsible for the different web pages what information on their websites was being removed from the results of a search. In this way, the editors of those web pages, simply by re-editing the information or with a small change in the URL, retrieved the deleted information, which reappeared immediately in Google searches.

   • Google’s privacy policy

   •  Information in the cloud or “cloud computing”  
The provision of cloud computing services in its different ‘cloud types’ (public, private, hybrid, etc.) and service modalities (infrastructure as a service, platform as a service and software as a service) has modified traditional relationships between the clients - responsible for the treatment of the data - and those in charge of the treatment - providers of cloud computing services -.  
The change of paradigm in these relations led to the Agency initiating a process of analysis on its implications and on the necessary modulations in the application of data protection regulations to guarantee the rights of citizens. This analysis concluded with the preparation of a guide on cloud computing, whose most outstanding aspects are the following:  
- The applicable legislation on data protection is that of the customer who processes data in Spain.  
- The provider of cloud computing services is a processor, even if it is a large multinational company. The client who contracts with the service provider is still responsible for the data processing and must act diligently in the choice of provider of cloud services. The service provider for its part must be diligent in providing guarantees for data protection. In this diligence, transparency plays an important role in obtaining information on these guarantees and, in particular, on those existing in the outsourcing of services and in international data transfers.  
- The client must inquire about whether the security measures are adequate for the treatment of the data and obtain guarantees to audit them, even if it is through a (reliable and independent) third party.  
- The client must obtain guarantees so that, at the end of the contract, s/he can recover the personal data, or transfer them to a new provider of these services.  
   •  The crisis of the Safe Harbor System  
On October 6, 2015, there was a crisis in the system that was employed as the basis for international data transfers from Europe to the United States. That day the ruling of the European Union’s Court of Justice was published, which invalidated the European Commission Decision 2000/520/EC, known as the ‘Safe Harbor’ Decision, which regulated the transfer of data from the European Union to those entities established in the United States and were sheltered under the Safe Harbor system.  
The Court’s decision was the result of a claim by Maximiliam Schrems, an Austrian national and Facebook user, who had asked Facebook Ireland not to transfer the data to Facebook Inc., its parent company based in the United States.

The complainant alleged that the current United States law and practices did not guarantee sufficient protection of personal data kept in its territory against the surveillance activities practiced by its public authorities. Among the reasons for the Judgment was the revelation of the existence in the United States of several surveillance programs for the collecting and processing of large-scale personal data. The General Regulation of Data Protection. Main  
novelties. New Rights  
On May 25, 2016, the new General Regulation for Data  
Protection was approved, a standard with which the  
European Union intends to respond to the challenges  
posed by the widespread use of information and  
communication technologies.  
The Regulation can be considered an evolution of  
Directive 95/46/EC, which it replaces, and which collects  
together and reinforces the basic principles of data  
protection and the rights of interested parties.  
Additionally, from the point of view of protection  
procedures and mechanisms, the Regulation contains  
important novelties.  
The first is that the Regulation is a norm that is applied  
directly in the Member States, without the need for  
transposition rules. States may, however, develop rules  
in cases where the Regulation enables them to do so.  
This has happened in Spain with the Organic Law on  
the Protection of Personal Data, whose parliamentary  
procedure concluded in November 2018.  
From the point of view of citizens, this norm, which  
standardizes the European data protection law,  
guarantees a similar level of protection throughout the  
Union. It is also the only case of a fundamental right  
regulated by an EU Regulation.

The Regulation also emphasizes the importance of training and awareness-raising for staff involved in data processing activities. Organizations are required to ensure that their employees are adequately informed about data protection obligations and the importance of safeguarding personal data. 

In addition, the Regulation introduces specific provisions regarding the processing of special categories of personal data, such as those related to health, race, or sexual orientation. The processing of such data is subject to stricter conditions and requires explicit consent from the data subject, except in certain circumstances defined by the Regulation.

Moreover, the Regulation establishes the obligation for data controllers to carry out Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) in cases where the processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. This assessment must be conducted prior to the processing and must include a description of the processing operations, an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing, and measures to mitigate risks.

Finally, the Regulation sets forth significant penalties for non-compliance, which can reach up to 4% of the total annual worldwide turnover of the preceding financial year or €20 million, whichever is greater. This underscores the seriousness with which data protection is treated under the new legal framework.

In summary, the new Regulation represents a comprehensive overhaul of data protection laws in the European Union, aiming to enhance the rights of individuals and ensure that organizations take a proactive approach to data protection. The AEPD plays a crucial role in enforcing these regulations and safeguarding the fundamental rights of citizens in relation to their personal data.

Another of the most significant developments of the EU Regulation, which is clearly different from the Directive, is that relating to the new supervision model, which goes from concentrating on verifying whether an infringement has occurred, or correctly applying the assessed measures that the legislation foresees, to having to assess, together with the responsible person and those in charge, the processes of risk analysis and the decisions on the application of the measures that minimize them.  
It is also a more flexible system, insofar as it opens up greater possibilities for the supervisory authorities to a broader range of corrective measures (cautions, warnings, etc.) whose application must be assessed according to the specific case, before considering the imposition of economic sanctions.  
All this does not exclude the fact that the Regulation provides for very severe economic sanctions, which can reach up to 20 million euros or an amount equivalent to 4% of the total annual global business volume of the company in the previous financial year. Sanctions that may have a dissuasive effect and whose application will depend on the level of diligence adopted to comply with the Regulation and various other circumstances related to their impact on the rights and freedoms of citizens.  
It is, at the same time, a model of supervision that could be described as cooperative, since the authorities will be obliged to cooperate not only in the establishment of the criteria for its interpretation, but also in its application to a specific case when several authorities may be affected as a consequence of a particular processing issue. This obligation of cooperation is regulated in the complex procedures of cooperation and coherence. In this procedure, the European Committee for Data Protection plays an especially important role and, ultimately, the European Union’s Court of Justice.  
This legal framework has yet to be completed by two other important European standards pending transposition or approval: Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April 2016 on the protection of citizens as regards the processing of personal data by the competent authorities for purposes of prevention, investigation, detection or prosecution of criminal offenses or execution of criminal penalties, and the free movement of such data, pending transposition; and the E-privacy Regulation, currently in process, which aims to update the still current Directive 2002/58/EC to apply the principles and requirements of the General Data Protection Regulation to the field of electronic communications.  
The combined effect of all these measures must ultimately have an effect on the data protection culture of organizations and on the better protection of citizens’ rights.

The recently approved Organic Law for the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights aims to fulfill five basic objectives: to adapt Spanish law to the model established by the Regulation; to introduce innovations and improvements by developing some of the matters contained in it; to reinforce the rights of citizens; to clarify concepts; and to provide legal security for those who process data.

It is important to firstly highlight the aspects relating to citizens’ rights.  
The legal text facilitates, in general, the exercise of rights by requiring, in particular, that the means for their performance be easily accessible by those affected.  
It recognizes, for example, the right of access to the data and, where appropriate, rectification or deletion by people linked to the deceased and their heirs, for family or de facto reasons, by addressing an omission in the previous Law that generated conflict, especially on the Internet, which is beyond the protective scope of the Agency. The Law has introduced a significant change, which even affects the title holder of the standard, relating to what it calls digital rights, regulated in heading X. Under that heading, two distinct blocks of rights can be distinguished, depending on whether the guardianship is, or is not, the responsibility of the Agency.  
Of these, the powers of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection are limited to those regulated in articles 89 to 94 of the Organic Law.  
It should also be noted, regarding these Regulations, that the Law updates guarantees to the right to privacy relating to the use of video surveillance devices and sound recording in the workplace, as well as the right to privacy - both for ordinary workers and public employees - in the use of digital devices at their disposal, complementing this right with the right to privacy rule when geolocation systems are employed in the workplace. These are now considered to be workplace rights that can be reinforced through collective agreements.  
In the sphere of the Internet, social network services and other similar tools of the information society, the regulation has systematized the criteria of the verdict of the European Union’s Court of Justice of May 13, 2014 in the case of the Agency against Google regarding the right to be forgotten in internet searches, in order to facilitate its application and increase legal security.  
In addition, the organic law regulates the right to be forgotten in social network services and equivalent information society services, limiting itself to allow the deletion of personal data when they have been provided by the interested party; deletion that in many cases may be carried out without the need for a request to the person responsible. Additionally, this data may be deleted when they have been provided by third parties; applying the same principles established in the aforementioned judgment.  
In any case, any deletion is exempt from the Regulation when it has been facilitated by individuals exercising their personal or domestic duties.  
The purpose of this provision is also to facilitate operators’ understanding, and guarantee legal certainty.

**The Agency of the future**  
The Agency of the next few years must be in a position  
to face with decisiveness and effectiveness the great  
challenges, current and future, that privacy faces.  
The fulfillment of this objective will mean aligning  
its activity and operating mode to the fundamental  
assumptions established in the General Data Protection  
Regulation, prioritizing diligence and proactivity and  
applying a new supervision model with preventive,  
corrective and - when appropriate – dissuasive action;  
when faced with breaches by those in charge.  
This is without forgetting the data protection authorities’  
responsibility for the investigative abilities common to all  
of them, in order to effectively carry out their functions  
and, where appropriate, impose dissuasive economic  
sanctions to ensure that the law is respected.  
The Spanish Agency is making an enormous effort  
to adapt the entire organization to respond to these  
challenges.
