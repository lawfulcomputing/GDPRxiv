Data Protection in Employment Relationships  
Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter, GDPR) not only recognizes the right to personal data protection but also requires each Member State to establish an administrative infrastructure that allows for the adequate protection of that right. To this end, each Member State must have a supervisory authority that will exercise the competencies and functions related to personal data protection, including the sanctioning power in cases of non-compliance in this area. Specifically, Article 57 of the GDPR confers on the supervisory authority functions such as:

◤ "Promoting public awareness and understanding of the risks, rules, safeguards, and rights in relation to processing."

◤ "Promoting awareness among controllers and processors regarding their obligations" in terms of data protection.

In Spain, the role of the supervisory authority, concerning data processing in the private labor sector, corresponds to the Spanish Data Protection Agency (hereinafter, AEPD), which, for decades, has published various guides to promote and clarify the application of data protection legislation. One of the main objectives of these publications has been to provide tools to help organizations, both public and private, to comply adequately with current legislation. 

In this context, during the validity of the previous regulation, a "Guide on Data Protection in Employment Relationships" was published to examine aspects of data protection that are either fundamental from the perspective of application and regulatory compliance or pose difficulties in interpretation or practical application. 

The new regulatory framework established by the GDPR, along with the subsequent approval of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD), has introduced numerous changes in the regulation, which require an adaptation of the guide to this new legislative reality. The LOPDGDD, on the other hand, includes Title X, which recognizes a broad set of "digital rights" over which, according to its Article 2.1, the AEPD is competent to ensure compliance with Articles 89 to 94.

The purpose of the Agency is not solely to summarize or structure the content of these regulations but to provide practical, non-binding guidance that facilitates compliance with the legislation for data controllers and for individuals or entities acting as processors (in the terminology of the GDPR and hereinafter, "processors"), supported by the experience accumulated by the AEPD over the years. 

The preparation of this document has been carried out with the participation of both the Ministry of Labor and Social Economy and business organizations (CEOE and CEPYME) and trade unions (CCOO and UGT).

1. CONCEPT OF PERSONAL DATA PROCESSING  
Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines personal data as "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person."  
By way of example:

◤ Information that can be associated with a specific individual: birth, marriage, address, etc.

◤ Numerical, alphabetical, graphic, photographic, acoustic information, or any other type.

◤ Information about childhood, academic, professional, or work life, lifestyle and consumption habits, personal relationships, or religious beliefs and ideologies.

◤ Name and surname, identification numbers, location data, an online identifier, or one or more elements specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity.

◤ Evaluations and assessments referring to specific individuals are also considered personal data.

General Aspects  
Article 9.1 of the GDPR considers special categories of personal data those that reveal racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, and data concerning health or data concerning the sexual life or sexual orientation of a natural person. 

In general, the processing of special categories of personal data is prohibited. However, Article 9.2 of the GDPR allows for their processing when, among other cases, it is necessary for compliance with obligations and the exercise of specific rights of the data controller or the data subject in the field of labor law and social security and protection, to the extent that it is authorized by the law of the Union of the Member States or a collective agreement under the law of the Member States that establishes adequate safeguards for the respect of fundamental rights and the interests of the data subject. 

Example.  
Processing of the disability data of the employee for the purposes of reductions or exemptions of contributions to Social Security.

The right to data protection safeguards individuals against the "processing" of their data, which is defined in Article 4.2 of the GDPR as any operation performed on personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or modification, extraction, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, limitation, erasure, or destruction.  
Data protection legislation applies to the processing of data carried out by an employer concerning employees, without prejudice to Article 88 of the GDPR allowing for "more specific rules" to be established through legal provisions or collective agreements, with the aim of providing better and more tailored protection of the right to data protection in the field of employment relationships.  
Data processing carried out in the exercise of exclusively personal or domestic activities (Article 2.2.c of the GDPR) is not considered subject to the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.  

2. LEGAL BASES FOR THE PROCESSING OF PERSONAL DATA  
The processing of personal data cannot be carried out for reasons of convenience, for the easy acquisition of such data, or in case they might be useful in the future; rather, it requires that the data controller has a "legal basis" that legitimizes such processing. In the context of employment relationships, the main legal basis is the execution of the employment contract, as the consent of the affected individual is not valid when provided in a context of "clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller."  
The imbalance between the company and the employee requires heightened caution and, in particular, respect for the principles of proportionality and purpose limitation.  
"It is very unlikely that consent will constitute a legal basis for data processing at work, unless employees can refuse without adverse consequences […] Except in exceptional situations, employers will have to rely on a legal basis other than consent, such as the necessity of processing data for their legitimate interest. However, a legitimate interest in itself is not sufficient to outweigh the rights and freedoms of workers" (Opinion 2/2017 on data processing at work by the Article 29 Working Party).  
Example:  
"In effect, according to the doctrine of this Court, the constitutionality of any restrictive measure on fundamental rights is determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality. For the purposes relevant here, it is sufficient to recall that to verify whether a restrictive measure on a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the requirements of proportionality."

Thus, the processing of data of workers by the employer is lawful, firstly, when it is necessary for the execution of a contract in which the interested party is a part or for the application at their request of pre-contractual measures (art. 6.1.b) GDPR).  
The company may process data such as the name and surname of workers, their date of birth, their sex, their nationality, their prior training, or any other data essential for formalizing the contract and executing the work.  
Secondly, the processing of data is also lawful “for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller” (art. 6.1.c) GDPR). This legal basis allows the processing of data when it is necessary to meet the requirements imposed by law (for example, social security contributions, tax obligations, work time registration, information and consultation with workers' representatives, etc.) or by a collective agreement.  
Example:  
Request from the company to workers for data that proves they maintain the necessary authorizations for the performance of the activity, such as a driving license in the case of drivers.  
Thirdly, the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party is also a legal basis for data processing, provided that such interests do not prevail over the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject that require the protection of personal data (art. 6.1.f) GDPR). The European Data Protection Board has issued the following opinion on this point:  
“If an employer intends to invoke a legitimate interest [Article 7, letter f)], the purpose of the processing must be legitimate; the chosen method or specific technology must be necessary, proportionate, and applied in the least intrusive manner possible, and the employer must be able to demonstrate that appropriate measures have been taken to ensure a balance with the fundamental rights and freedoms of workers […] These limitations could: a) be geographical (for example, control only in specific locations; monitoring in sensitive areas such as religious places and, for example, restrooms and break rooms must be prohibited), b) be data-oriented (for example, personal electronic files and communications must not be monitored), and c) be temporal (for example, sampling instead of continuous monitoring)” (Opinion 2/2017 on data processing at work by the Article 29 Working Party).

It is presumed to be necessary "for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party" the processing of contact data and, where applicable, data related to the function or position held by individuals providing services to a legal entity, provided that the following requirements are met (art. 6.1.f of the GDPR and art. 19.1 of the LOPDGDD):  
a) That the processing refers only to the data necessary for their professional location, such as name and surname, functions or positions held, as well as postal or electronic address, telephone, and professional fax number. However, the processing of data that is not strictly necessary to fulfill those purposes is not permitted, such as would be the case with the ID number. This legal basis does not refer to the relationship between the employer and the employees, but rather to the processing of professional contact data of an individual by a third party, with the purpose of putting them in contact with a legal entity, which may require the intermediation of that individual acting as a representative or liaison.  
b) That the purpose of the processing is solely to maintain relationships of any kind with the legal entity in which the affected individual provides their services (business-to-business relationships, B2B).  
"The processing of personal data for purposes other than those for which they were initially collected should only be permitted when it is compatible with the purposes of their initial collection. In such cases, a separate legal basis, distinct from that which allowed the obtaining of the personal data, is not required" (recital 50 of the GDPR).  
The purpose of the processing must have a direct relationship between those processing the data and the entity, and not between them and someone holding a specific position in the company. Thus, the purpose of the processing must be directed at the legal entity, with the contact data of the individual being merely the means to achieve that purpose.  
Example:  
The data controller is not authorized to use the contact data to send commercial or advertising information to the "contact" as an individual, but rather the ultimate recipient must be the legal entity.  
When, exceptionally, the legal basis for the processing is consent, it must be unequivocal, requiring a manifestation from the affected individual or a clear affirmative action, as the GDPR, unlike previous regulations, states that silence, pre-checked boxes, or inaction should not constitute consent.  
"The default settings of devices and/or the installation of software that facilitates the electronic processing of personal data cannot be classified as consent given by workers, as consent requires an active manifestation of will. The absence of action (i.e., not changing the default settings) cannot generally be considered as specific consent to allow such processing" (Opinion 2/2017 on the processing of data at work by the Article 29 Working Party).

The consent of workers must also be free and specific, and the substitution of individual consent with indirect and plural consent through collective bargaining is not lawful (CJEU (Grand Chamber), October 5, 2014, C-397/01 to C-403/01).  
Article 6 of the GDPR mentions other legal bases that could exceptionally be invoked within the framework of the employment relationship, such as the protection of vital interests of the data subject or another natural person.  

3. INFORMATION ON THE PROCESSING OF PERSONAL DATA  
The data controller, usually the employer, must inform workers in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible manner, using clear and simple language, about the data processing being carried out (Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR).  
“The information provided to a data subject must not contain language or terminology that is excessively legal, technical, or specialized, and vague expressions such as ‘could’, ‘some’, ‘frequently’, and ‘possible’ should be avoided” (Guidelines on Transparency under Regulation 2016/679 of the Article 29 Working Party).  
The duty to inform is part of the essential content of the right to data protection and constitutes a guarantee for the data subject, as it allows them to know what data is being processed about them, with whom the employer shares it, and also the rights of the data subjects and how to exercise them.  
It is advisable to adopt a layered information model, with a first layer containing basic information provided at the same time as the data is collected, and a second layer with more detailed information.  

Regarding deadlines:  
1. When data is obtained from the data subject (for example, through a curriculum vitae), the information must be provided at the same time the employer collects the data.  
2. When data is not obtained from the data subject, the information must be provided within a reasonable timeframe, and no later than one month, with the following specifications:  
   a) If the personal data is to be used for communication with the data subject, they must be informed no later than at the time of the first communication to that data subject.  
   b) If it is intended to communicate it to another recipient, no later than at the time the personal data is communicated for the first time.  

In principle, including data protection information clauses in the employment contract, or in an annex to it, is an appropriate means to comply with the right to information of workers.

4. DATA PROTECTION RIGHTS IN THE WORKPLACE  
Workers can exercise the following rights before the data controller:  

Right of Access  
(Article 15 GDPR)  
Regardless of the information provided, the worker has the right to confirmation that data processing is occurring and, if so, the right to know how that processing is being carried out with content similar to that of the right to information.  
Workers exercising the right of access can request a copy of the personal data being processed.  
Controllers may fulfill this right by providing remote access to a secure system that offers the data subject direct access to their personal data.  

Right to Rectification  
(Article 16 GDPR)  
Workers can demand the rectification of inaccurate data being processed, as well as the completion of incomplete data.  

Right to Erasure  
(Article 17 GDPR)  
Workers can demand the erasure of personal data being processed in the following situations:

◤When personal data is no longer necessary in relation to the purposes for which it was collected or otherwise processed.  

◤When the legal basis for data processing is consent and the employee has withdrawn it.  

◤When the data processing is unlawful.  

◤In the event that the employee opposes the processing of data that is not necessary for the fulfillment and execution of the employment contract.  

◤When personal data should be deleted in compliance with a legal obligation.  
By legal requirement, when personal data is rectified or deleted, it must be blocked. Specifically, the blocking of data consists of the "identification and reservation of the same, adopting technical and organizational measures, to prevent its processing, including its visualization, except for the provision of data to judges and courts, the Public Prosecutor's Office, or the competent Public Administrations, particularly the data protection authorities, for the enforcement of possible liabilities arising from the processing and only for the statute of limitations period of the same" (art. 32 of the LOPDGDD). After this period, it is time to proceed with the destruction of the data.  
Thus, the deletion procedure must contemplate the blocking period, which implies:  
a) A requirement for specificity regarding the period of use of the data and the moment when the purpose that legitimized its processing ceases.  
Example.  
The end of the employment relationship entails the disappearance of the legal basis that justified the data processing.  
b) A provision regarding the period of retention of the data, once the legal basis that legitimized the previous processing disappears, if any legal obligation requires such retention. Under art. 17.3.b) of the GDPR, in these cases, the right to deletion will not be applicable.  
Example.  
Art. 21 of Royal Legislative Decree 5/2000, of August 4, which approves the consolidated text of the Law on Offenses and Sanctions in the Social Order, classifies as a minor infraction "not retaining, for four years, the documentation or the records or computer media in which the corresponding data that prove compliance with obligations regarding affiliation, registrations, cancellations, or variations that, where applicable, occurred in relation to these matters, as well as the contribution documents and the receipts justifying the payment of salaries and the delegated payment of benefits." During this period, the deletion of data is not applicable.  
c) The blocking period, the duration of which is directly related to the statute of limitations for actions to enforce liabilities arising from the processing.

Tax obligations expire after 4 years. Right to Restriction of Processing  
(art. 18 GDPR)  
The restriction of processing consists of "marking the personal data retained in order to limit its processing in the future" (art. 4.3 of the GDPR). It is a right of employees that can be exercised against the employer in the following situations:  

◤ Inaccuracy of personal data revealed by the employee, resulting in the restriction of processing for a period that allows the data controller to carry out the necessary verification.  

◤ Unlawfulness of processing by the employer with a request from the employee for limitation of use and not deletion of the data.  

◤ When personal data is no longer necessary for the purposes of processing, but there is an interest from the employee in its retention for the formulation, exercise, or defense of claims.  

◤ Opposition to data processing by the employee while verifying whether the legitimate grounds of the controller override those of the affected individual.  
Employees have the right to be informed about any rectification or deletion of personal data or limitation of processing, as well as the lifting of the limitation.  

Right to Data Portability  
(art. 20 GDPR)  
When the legal basis for processing is consent or the execution of a contract, the employee has the right to receive the personal data concerning them, which they have provided to a data controller, in a structured, commonly used, and machine-readable format, and to transmit it to another data controller without the original controller preventing it, provided that the processing is carried out by automated means.  
This right does not necessarily require the controller to communicate the data to employees in that format, but it recognizes the affected individual’s right for the data to be transmitted directly from controller to controller when technically feasible.

5. THE PRINCIPLE OF  
MINIMIZATION AND ITS IMPACT  
ON THE EMPLOYMENT RELATIONSHIP  
The execution of the employment contract is the main legal basis for the processing of personal data in the employment relationship. However, this does not imply that the employer can know any type of personal data of the workers, because the principle of data minimization requires that personal data be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (art. 5 of the GDPR).  
Therefore, the employer has the right to know the personal data necessary for the normal development of the employment relationship, including, and by way of mere example and not limitation, the following:  

◤ Name and surname of the worker  

◤ Gender  

◤ ID number, foreign identification number, and Social Security affiliation number  

◤ Nationality  

◤ Disability  

◤ Date of birth  
All of this data can have a direct impact on the fulfillment of business obligations, for example, regarding affiliation acts, registration, and Social Security contributions to verify that the worker meets the relevant requirements to enter into the contract, among others.

16

However, other personal data are not essential for the execution of the employment contract, such as, for example, the username on social networks or messaging services or internet portals. The processing of this data by the employer requires the existence of a legal basis different from the execution of the contract, such as legitimate interest, which must be duly demonstrated in accordance with the principles of balancing and proportionality. Nevertheless, it will be necessary to analyze the characteristics of each employment relationship to determine which data are necessary for the fulfillment of that contract and which are not.  
Example.  
The Bank of Spain may establish certain safeguards and impose certain obligations to verify that the individuals working there comply with the rules of compatibility and regarding access to and use of privileged information. However, these safeguards and obligations must be appropriate, necessary, and proportional, requirements that would not be met by a demand on employees to provide tax data or the income tax return, without further filters, since although the purpose may seem legitimate, it could result in the company obtaining sensitive data without the consent of the employee (for example, ideological aspects or beliefs, such as contributions to the Catholic Church or union affiliation). (SAN 4494/2018, of December 7, Social Chamber).  
Personal data are those that allow the company to locate employees and contact them, such as home address, email address, phone number (landline and/or mobile), or bank account. In general, it seems necessary for the execution of the contract that the employer has some means of communication with the employees, and it is essential that the employee provides the company with some form of contact. However, the employment contract does not legitimize the company to request all of this data from the employee, as the Supreme Court has pointed out regarding the email address or personal phone number (STS 4086/2015, of September 21, Social Chamber). That is to say, the necessity of the processing must be weighed on a case-by-case basis.  
To this end, it will be necessary to analyze in each case the alleged legal basis – which could be the employment contract, consent, or the legitimate interest of the employer – the intended purpose, and the data processed.  
“The creation of an internal database of employee contact information for a company that contains the name, work address, phone number, and email address of all employees, to allow employees to contact their coworkers, may in certain situations be considered necessary for the execution of a contract under Article 7, letter b), but it could also be lawful under Article 7, letter f), if it is demonstrated that the interest of the data controller prevails and all appropriate measures are taken, including, for example, consulting employee representatives” (Opinion 6/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC of the Article 29 Working Party).

On the other hand, the processing of data concerning family members or acquaintances of the employee as a contact person "for emergencies" (for example, accidents) is not strictly necessary for the execution of the contract, therefore the consent of the employees is required (not that of the third party). The principles of proportionality and purpose limitation imply that the employee can freely choose the reference person, without having to specify to the company what relationship they have with that person, and they can also specify the mode of contact (email, landline, mobile phone), providing only the essential data (for example, the company does not need to know the address of that contact person).  
In certain cases, the employer may demonstrate a legitimate interest that justifies the need to collect the personal email address and phone number of the employee, without them being used subsequently for purposes other than those that motivated such collection.  
Regarding the bank account number, the processing of this personal data has been accepted (Opinion 6/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC of the Article 29 Working Party). However, the principles of minimization and purpose limitation imply that the company can only proceed with its processing if the salary is paid by transfer, as it must have the means through which to fulfill the corresponding obligations. Conversely, it could not require this information from employees when payment is made by another means that does not involve the banking entity.  
Nor is the generic assignment by contract of image rights lawful, unless that assignment is necessary for the execution of the contract.

Commercial labor provision that requires video calls, having been expressly established in the employment contract that it is for the purpose of developing an activity typical of telemarketing (STS 1436/2019, of April 10, Social Chamber).  
6. DUTIES AND OBLIGATIONS IN ACCESSING PERSONAL DATA: SECRECY AND SECURITY  
The GDPR and the LOPDGDD include the duty of security along with the duty of secrecy within the principles of data protection.  
"Personal data shall be processed in a manner that ensures an adequate level of security of personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, through the application of appropriate technical or organizational measures ('integrity and confidentiality')" (art. 5.1.f) of the GDPR).  
In particular, art. 32.1 of the GDPR establishes that the technical and organizational measures established must manage the risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, beyond other security considerations, such as security for the organization itself, the State, or other possible interests.

Specifically, Article 5 of the LOPDGDD establishes:  
1. The data controllers and processors, as well as all individuals involved in any phase of the processing, shall be subject to the duty of confidentiality.  
2. The general obligation of confidentiality shall be complementary to the duties of professional secrecy.  
3. The obligations of confidentiality and professional secrecy shall remain in effect even after the relationship of the obligated party with the data controller or processor has ended.  

Both duties are necessary and constitute a guarantee for the fundamental right to data protection. Secrecy and confidentiality ensure that personal data is only known by the affected individual and by those users within the organization whose profile grants them the competence to use, consult, modify, or include the data in the information systems.  

Security measures must guarantee, in addition to confidentiality, the availability of the data, ensuring its recovery in the event of any incident, and its integrity, protecting it against any unauthorized manipulation. The company must have policies in place to comply with these two principles, as they not only guarantee a fundamental right but also provide trust and security to the affected individuals. The implementation of security measures protects assets that are important to the company, such as the data of its clients and suppliers (STSJ CV 5238/2015 of November 11, Social Chamber).  

For the proper fulfillment of these two duties, it is essential to have personnel management policies that clearly define the functional profiles of each position, as well as training procedures for the staff.  

The obligations of secrecy and security regarding data protection constitute very specific duties linked to the act of processing itself and go beyond the traditional concept of professional secrecy. Inadequate compliance puts the fundamental right to data protection at risk and usually causes serious harm to the company's reputation.  

Example.  
The abandonment of documents without destruction in common trash is one of the most common violations regarding data protection for which the company is responsible.  

For all these reasons, it is highly recommended:  
a) To design the functions and responsibilities of the staff based on their relationship with the processing of personal data.  
b) To adequately train employees considering their different levels of responsibility and ensuring that they are aware of their security and secrecy duties. The training should contribute to creating a culture of commitment to data protection.  
c) To warn and train even those employees who, while not having a direct relationship with information systems and personal data processing, may jeopardize the secrecy or security of the data (for example, cleaning staff).  
d) To assess the advisability of appointing a data protection officer (for example, a company or business group), with an expert trained in the field who can advise on compliance with regulations and the principle of proactive responsibility of the data controllers.

7. INTERNATIONAL DATA TRANSFERS  
Due to the impact that international data transfers may have throughout the course of the employment relationship, it is necessary to mention the regime of international data transfers. Such transfers refer to the transmission of personal data outside the European Economic Area (EEA), whether they constitute a communication of data to other data controllers, in which case both the exporter and importer are responsible for processing, or whether they are intended for the provision of services by a data processor established outside the EEA. In the latter case, the exporter may be a data controller or a data processor, and the data importer may be a data processor or a sub-processor.  
The GDPR has simplified the regime of international transfers, which has resulted in a substantial change in the control model that was previously in place under the former regulations. With the GDPR, the obligation to notify the AEPD (Spanish Data Protection Agency) of data transfers to countries that have an adequacy decision is eliminated, or if they are carried out under any of the exceptions provided in Article 49 of the GDPR, except when, due to their uniqueness, the transfers are made by a data controller for legitimate and compelling reasons, provided that the other requirements set forth in Article 49.1, second paragraph of the GDPR are also met.  
The general obligation to obtain prior authorization from the AEPD is also eliminated when transfers are based on the provision of adequate safeguards, which will only be necessary when the safeguards provided for the transfer are included in a contract that does not comply with the standard clauses approved by the European Commission, or adopted by a supervisory authority and also approved by the European Commission, or when the safeguards are included in administrative agreements for transfers between authorities or public bodies.  
Therefore, international data transfers may be carried out without the need for authorization from the Supervisory Authority:  

◤ When the recipient is an entity established in one of the countries or territories declared to have an adequate level of protection by the European Commission.  

◤ When adequate safeguards are provided, ensuring that data subjects have enforceable rights and effective legal remedies through:  
• A legally binding and enforceable instrument between authorities or public bodies  
• Binding corporate rules for multinational companies  
• Standard contractual clauses adopted by the European Commission, or by a supervisory authority and approved by the European Commission  
• A code of conduct or a certification mechanism.  

◤ When any of the exceptional circumstances provided in Article 49 of the GDPR occur, such as the explicit consent of the data subject, or when the transfer is necessary for the performance of a contract between the data subject and the data controller.  

◤ Prior authorization from the AEPD will be required when adequate safeguards are provided through:  
• Specific contractual clauses  
• Administrative agreements between authorities or public bodies  

Data subjects must be informed about international data transfers, and these transfers must be included in the record of processing activities (Articles 13 and 30 GDPR).

2. STAFF SELECTION AND SOCIAL NETWORKS  
Candidates and employees are not obliged to allow the employer to inquire into their social media profiles, either during the selection process or during the execution of the contract.

And the employee has the right to be informed about that processing (Opinion 2/2017 on data processing in the workplace by the Article 29 Working Party).

The company is also not authorized to request from an employee or job candidate the information that they share with others through social media.

5. AUTOMATED DECISIONS  
As previously noted (see II.4), the GDPR generally prohibits decisions based "solely on automated processing, including profiling" when it produces "legal effects concerning the data subject or similarly significantly affects them" (for example, being excluded from a selection process).  
However, these decisions may be made when they are necessary for the conclusion or execution of a contract.  
Since this is an exception to the general rule, such necessity must be interpreted restrictively.  
Example.  
Automated decision-making is permissible in selection processes with numerous candidates to carry out an initial screening excluding those who do not meet any essential condition or requirement, such as the absence of sufficient qualifications (Guidelines on Automated Individual Decision-Making and Profiling for the purposes of Regulation 2016/679 of the Article 29 Working Party).

Example.  
Hiring a significantly higher number of men than women.

To be considered as human intervention, the data controller must ensure that any oversight of the decision is meaningful, rather than merely a symbolic gesture.

Biometric data  
Article 4.14 of the GDPR provides the following definition of "biometric data":  
"Personal data obtained through a specific technical processing, related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person that allow or confirm the unique identification of that person, such as facial images or fingerprint data."  
However, the GDPR does not consider all processing of biometric data as processing of special categories of data, as Article 9.1 refers to "biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person," so from a joint interpretation of both provisions, it follows that biometric data would only constitute a special category of data if subjected to specific technical processing aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person.  
In this sense, it is important to distinguish between cases of biometric identification and cases of biometric verification or authentication. Identification is the process of recognizing a particular individual among a group, comparing the data of the individual to be identified with the data of each individual in the group (one-to-many). Verification or authentication is the process of proving that the identity claimed by an individual is true, comparing the individual's data only with the data associated with the claimed identity (one-to-one).  
This criterion, which was already included in Opinion 3/2012 on the evolution of biometric technologies by the Article 29 Working Party, has been accepted in the Amendment Protocol to the Convention for the Protection of Individuals with regard to the Processing of Personal Data.

However, in general terms, biometric data only qualifies as a special category of data in cases where it is subjected to technical processing aimed at biometric identification (one-to-many) and not in the case of biometric verification/authentication (one-to-one).

10. GRANTING OF SOCIAL ACTION AID  
The request by the employee for social action aid involves the processing of data related to family burdens and income, the legal basis of which is not consent, but rather the employment contract itself and the fulfillment of legal obligations or the provisions of the corresponding Collective Agreements.  
The employee has the right to be informed about all relevant aspects regarding data protection, such as the identity of the data controller, its legal basis, the purpose, the retention period of the data, or the rights of access, rectification, opposition, or deletion.  
The representatives of the employees have the right, as provided in art. 64.7.b) of the ET, to participate in the management or processing of the company's social aid and, therefore, to access a list of the employees benefiting from social action in the exercise of their representative functions, which will occur when the collective agreement grants them a role in the management or processing of the company's social aid.  
However, the principle of data minimization advises against proceeding with mass or indiscriminate information, but rather, as stated in the STS 111/2018 of February 7, Social Chamber, only the communication of those personal data that are necessary to carry out the assigned task and to the extent that it is essential for the exercise of their functions will proceed.  
In other cases, the provision of properly dissociated information that allows representatives to know the circumstances they have been entrusted to monitor will be sufficient (see chapter 6).  
Regarding publicity in the granting of these aids, two scenarios must be distinguished:  
1. In those processes that are not competitive and, therefore, without a maximum number of applications to be accepted by the company, notification must be individualized, so that personal data should not be accessible to third parties.  
2. In competitive processes, that is, when the company limits the maximum number of aids based on the fulfillment of certain requirements, the applicants – and never third parties to the procedure – may know the list of awarded aids, but not superfluous or non-essential data (for example, the ID number).  
Therefore, companies cannot publish the list of awarded and denied aids on a publicly accessible website or on a bulletin board located in an area open to the public, as this would allow third parties not involved in the procedure to access personal data.

13. DATA TRANSFER TO OTHER COMPANIES (GROUPS OF COMPANIES, CONTRACTORS, AND BUSINESS TRANSFERS)  
Groups of Companies  
The GDPR expressly mentions groups of companies in several passages, even allowing the designation of a single data protection officer for all companies in the group (art. 37.2).  
The GDPR refers to groups of companies in relation to prior consultation with the supervisory authority (art. 36), with the content of binding corporate rules (art. 47), with the designation of the data protection officer (art. 37), and with the communication of data between companies in the group (art. 88).  
The group of companies does not constitute a legal entity, but each company in the group has its own legal personality and can be considered as responsible for the processing of the data of employees as long as it decides on the purposes and means of the processing (AEPD Report 494-2008). For its part, the company in the group that carries out the processing (which may consist of the storage and management of personal data) on behalf of another or others will be considered as the data processor.  
In general, the communication of data between companies in the same group will require demonstrating a legitimate interest either of the data controller or of the third party to whom the data is communicated. This legitimate interest may consist of the centralization of administrative activities.

**14. ACCESS TO DATA OF OTHER CANDIDATES FOR A JOB POSITION (ACCESS TO EMPLOYMENT OR PROFESSIONAL PROMOTION)**  
The ruling of the Court of Justice of the European Union, dated April 19, 2012, C-415/2010, interpreting Directives 2000/43, 2000/78, and 2006/54, recognized the right of workers to know the relevant information to support a claim when they believe they have been discriminated against in access to employment or in professional promotion.  
Therefore, in those competitive selection processes, the affected party would have the right to know, if requested, information about the professional qualifications of other candidates, particularly those who have received higher evaluations.  
This right must be compatible with data protection, so:

1. PROCESSING OF DATA BY REPRESENTATIVES OF WORKERS  
The fulfillment of the obligations and the exercise of the rights of the representatives of workers allow for the processing of personal data of workers without their consent.  
However, this processing has a series of limits:  
1. Only data necessary for the exercise of those representation functions may be processed.  
Example.  
The processing of private data of the worker, such as their personal phone number, is not permissible. However, the identification of workers occupying each job position by name and surname (STS 572/2018 of February 7, Social Chamber) and also the corporate email address would be permissible.  
2. The employer must not provide the representatives with more data than is essential to carry out their functions (data minimization).  
Example.

7. CONSULTATION PERIODS (TRANSFERS, SUBSTANTIAL MODIFICATIONS OF WORKING CONDITIONS, SUSPENSIONS, AND COLLECTIVE DISMISSALS)  
The ET and the Regulation on collective dismissal procedures and suspension of contracts and reduction of working hours, approved by Royal Decree 1483/2012, of October 29, require that the employer and the representatives of the workers hold a consultation period before making certain decisions that impact the workers, such as transfer (art. 40 of the ET), substantial modification of working conditions (art. 41), business transfer (art. 44), suspension of contracts (art. 47), collective dismissal (art. 51), or non-application of the collective agreement (art. 82).

Furthermore, both parties must respect the confidentiality of personal data, so the dissemination of negotiations in real-time or their recording requires the consent of the affected individuals.  
The workers, for their part, have the right to challenge the employer's decision, and to do so, they may need to know the reasons that led to its adoption. 1. LEGAL BASES FOR DATA PROCESSING  
The LPRL and its implementing regulations impose on the company the performance of a set of activities whose ultimate goal is to prevent or reduce risks arising from work. For this task, it is necessary to process personal data of workers.  
Example.  
The planning of prevention requires, at a minimum, having a detailed list of job positions and the individuals who occupy them. Additionally, the specific risks of the position must be identified and assessed, often linked to the personal or health characteristics of the worker themselves. The processing of personal data in the area of risk prevention is legitimized by the existence of a contractual relationship whose fulfillment, development, and control make it necessary. 2. ACCESS TO DATA BY THE COMPANY AND THE PREVENTION DELEGATES  
The legislation on occupational risk prevention allows, in certain cases, the communication of personal data. Thus, the legislation legitimizes and obliges the communication of data to prevention delegates, to the health authority within the framework of Law 14/1986, of April 25, General Health Law, to the Labor and Social Security Inspectorate, or to the labor authority, not forgetting the communication of data required by judges and courts, or those necessary in cases of medical emergencies or epidemiological studies.  
The cases that pose the greatest difficulties are those related to access to information by the company itself and by the prevention delegates.  
The powers of access to information by the company are very limited and, in practice, refer to knowing the fitness or unfitness conditions of the worker. The processing by occupational risk prevention services of the medical history, resulting from medical examinations conducted on workers, must be limited to the provisions of Article 22.4 of the LPRL. Therefore, this provision prohibits access to medical information obtained under the provisions of the LPRL by the employer or any third party, including individuals or bodies with responsibilities in prevention matters, other than "medical personnel and health authorities that carry out health monitoring of workers," with the sole exception of the conclusions derived from such monitoring regarding the fitness of workers to perform their job.  
When health monitoring is carried out by external physicians, company doctors do not have access to the medical diagnosis, as this would result in a communication of data without the consent of the worker.  
All of this is without prejudice to the possible communication to judicial bodies or when there is a risk to third parties under the terms provided by law.  
However, it is possible that the employer must specifically access personal data of the worker necessary for the fulfillment of their obligations that go beyond the classification of fit or unfit. In such cases, the legitimacy for processing derives from the regulation itself, but it will be limited to the strictly necessary data.  
Example.

3. WEARABLE TECHNOLOGY  
(MOBILE DEVICES)  
The monitoring of health data through smart devices, such as bracelets or watches, is generally prohibited unless established by law or regulation, for the following reasons:  

◤ It does not fall within the health monitoring typical of occupational risk prevention.  

◤ It involves the processing of a special category of data (health) without a legal basis.  

◤ It lacks a legitimate purpose.  

◤ It violates the principle of proportionality, as it entails permanent monitoring and would allow the employer to access specific health data, and not exclusively the assessment of the worker's fitness to perform the job.  
Given the unequal relationship between companies and workers and the sensitive nature of health data, workers are not truly "free" to consent, and therefore the consent of Health Monitoring

The worker's consent is not a valid legal basis in these cases. Even if the employer uses a third party to collect health data, the processing would still be unlawful, unless a legitimate interest, a specific purpose, proportional monitoring, or complete anonymization of the data is demonstrated.  
Example.  
An organization offers its workers tracking devices for physical condition as a gift. The devices count the number of steps that the workers take and record their heartbeats and sleep patterns over time. The resulting health data should only be accessible to the worker and not to the employer. Any data transferred between the worker (as the affected party) and the device/service provider (as the data controller) is a matter that concerns both parties. Given that health data could also be subject to processing by the commercial party that manufactured the devices or offers a service to employers, the employer, when choosing the device or service, must evaluate the privacy policy of the manufacturer and/or service provider to ensure that there is no unlawful processing of the health data of the workers (Opinion 2/2017 on data processing in the workplace by the Article 29 Working Party).  
4. RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN THE COMPANY AND THE PREVENTION SERVICE  
The development of risk prevention tasks requires the presence of specialized areas of action (safety, ergonomics and psychosociology, industrial hygiene, occupational medicine, and nursing) and professionals with diverse profiles and organizational demands. Adequate means and resources must be available, which are not always at the disposal of all companies.  
The relationship between the company and the prevention service will require data processing, specifically communications of data related to the workers, which do not require their consent because the legal basis is a legal obligation, the prevention of occupational risks.  
Regarding data communication, rulings such as the SAN of May 24, 2007, Administrative Litigation Chamber, must be taken into account. The ruling declares the nullity of the sanctions imposed by the AEPD on the mutual insurance company for using personal data subject to automated processing for purposes incompatible with those for which the data had been collected, and for the transfer of personal data, outside of cases where it is permitted. The data transfer that occurred is covered by the application of the principles of rationality and the unity of medical records and has a legitimate purpose, the correct application of the regulations on Social Security and Occupational Risk Prevention and the prevention of fraud, so the consent of the reporting worker is not required.

The status of the data controller varies depending on whether it is an in-house, external, or joint prevention service:  

◤If it is an in-house service, the company will be responsible for the processing. In the case of an in-house prevention service, there will be different profiles and access rights to health data within the company. The external prevention service will be responsible for the processing. In these cases, there is a complete separation between the company and the prevention service. The external prevention service must limit itself to communicating to the employer the fitness or unfitness of the worker or, if applicable, the need to undertake adaptations or improvements to reduce or avoid risks. Any other communication of data to the employer requires a legitimate interest from the employer.  

◤Joint prevention services can be established among companies that simultaneously carry out activities in the same workplace, building, or shopping center. Such services, whether or not they have a distinct legal personality, will be considered as in-house services of the companies that constitute them. If the joint prevention service has its own legal personality, it is considered an external prevention service and, therefore, responsible for the processing. If the joint prevention service does not have independent legal personality, the companies, in the same way as in the cases of in-house prevention services, will assume the status of data controllers.  

Confidentiality must be fully guaranteed, and therefore the information systems for managing prevention services must take into account:  
1. Implementing logging systems to verify that access corresponds only to authorized personnel.  
2. They must precisely define the access profiles and functions of each user.  
Example.  
The administrative staff member who organizes appointments cannot have the same profile as the occupational physician. Similarly, the person responsible for the ergonomic adaptation of a workstation should be able to access the health data necessary for their activity.  

5. CHANGE OF THE PREVENTION SERVICE  
The LPRL obliges the employer to ensure periodic monitoring of the health status of the workers in their service based on the inherent risks of the work (art. 23) and, to fulfill this task, allows them to choose to designate one or more workers, establish an in-house prevention service, or contract with an external prevention service (arts. 30 et seq.).  
When there is a change in the prevention service, the communication of data related to the health monitoring of the workers to the new entity that will provide the prevention service would be a case of data communication provided for in article 23.1 of the LPRL, in relation to art. 30.3, derived from the obligation to make available to the new service and the obligation to maintain the clinical-labor history (art. 37.3.c) of Royal Decree 39/1997, of January 17, which approves the Regulation of Prevention Services).

However, this data processing must respect the principles of minimization and purpose limitation, which means:

◤ That health data can only be communicated to the new prevention service, and not to any other entity.

◤ That communication must occur directly between the medical services of the involved prevention companies. Therefore, it violates the right to data protection for the outgoing prevention service to provide the data to the employer for the employer to pass it on to the new prevention service.  
In any case, these precautions must be respected:  
1. The data cannot be used for a purpose other than the prevention of occupational risks.  
2. The worker has the right to be informed.  
3. The outgoing prevention service must not proceed to immediately delete the data, as it has an obligation to retain it, under the terms provided in the applicable labor regulations.  
Example.  
Article 9.3 of Royal Decree 665/1997, of May 12, on the protection of workers against risks related to exposure to carcinogenic agents during work, requires the retention of medical records for forty years after the exposure has ended.  

6. CLINICAL HISTORY OF WORKERS  
The clinical history of workers must be governed, in addition to what is provided in data protection legislation, by the principles of Law 41/2002, of November 14, which regulates the autonomy of the patient and rights and obligations regarding information and clinical documentation.  
At the same time, from the provisions of the second paragraph of Article 22.4 of the LPRL, the right of healthcare personnel who carry out health surveillance actions to access the medical information derived from it is established. In this sense, this information will constitute, as provided by the Law itself, the occupational clinical history of the worker, taking into account that, as a clinical history, it would be subject to the provisions of Law 41/2002, of November 14, which regulates the autonomy of the patient and rights and obligations regarding information and clinical documentation, which imposes its management on healthcare centers or professionals who carry out health actions related to the patient, in this case, the worker undergoing tests that involve health surveillance actions.  
Furthermore, procedures must be established to guarantee the rights of access, rectification, and deletion of workers.  
When it comes to access to the clinical history, it should be noted that specific legislation imposes duties such as limiting access to the subjective notes of the healthcare provider, the existence of limits to rectification contained in sectoral regulations regarding Social Security, or the impossibility of proceeding with the deletion of data that must be retained under health regulations.  
In any case, the request must be answered, providing justification for the denial.  
Regarding the clinical history, the requirement to establish user profiles may derive from Law 41/2002, of November 14, which precisely defines the functional profile of each type of worker.  
“1. The clinical history is an instrument primarily intended to guarantee adequate assistance to the patient. The healthcare professionals at the center who perform the diagnosis or treatment of the patient have access to the clinical history as a fundamental instrument for their adequate assistance.  
2. Each center will establish methods that enable access to the clinical history of each patient by the professionals assisting them at all times.
