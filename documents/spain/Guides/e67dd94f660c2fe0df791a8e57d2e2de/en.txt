**EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**  
This document aims to serve as a preliminary approach for compliance with Regulation (EU) 2016/679, General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), for products and services that include components of Artificial Intelligence. Under the label "Artificial Intelligence," or AI, there are many types of solutions that employ different techniques, particularly those based on machine learning. AI has become an additional component of data processing carried out by data controllers and, in many cases, appears in the form of solutions developed by third parties. Artificial Intelligence raises many questions among users, researchers, specialists, authorities, and the industry regarding aspects of regulatory compliance, respect for the rights of data subjects, and legal certainty for all parties involved. These questions represent a challenge for the proper technological development.  
This document does not intend to provide a comprehensive review of what is established in the GDPR, but rather to address the questions raised within the framework of personal data protection and to highlight the most relevant aspects in the relationship between AI and the GDPR that must be considered from the design phase and in the implementation of treatments that include AI. The document will pay special attention, among other things, to the legitimacy for processing, information and transparency, the exercise of rights, automated decisions, accuracy, data minimization, impact assessment, and the analysis of the proportionality of the processing. The text is directed at controllers who include AI components in their processing, as well as developers, processors, or others who support such processing.  

**Keywords:** Artificial intelligence, AI, GDPR, LOPDGDD, rights, privacy, ethics, data protection, design, impact assessment, machine learning, ML, automated decisions, minimization, transparency, accuracy, bias.

I.  
INTRODUCTION TO AI FRAMEWORK AND DATA PROTECTION  
The term Artificial Intelligence, or AI, was first used in 1956 by John McCarthy to refer to “the science and engineering of creating intelligent machines, especially intelligent computer programs.” The High-Level Expert Group on Artificial Intelligence (AI – HLEG) established by the European Commission to develop the European Strategy on Artificial Intelligence applies it to “systems that exhibit intelligent behavior, being able to analyze the environment and perform actions, with a certain degree of autonomy, in order to achieve specific objectives.” Other definitions suggest that AI is the ability of a machine to act as a human mind does, considering its aspects of creativity and the capacity to perform analysis and inferences from complex, and even incomplete, information.  
Depending on the scope and application of artificial intelligence, three different categories of AI are distinguished: strong AI, general AI, and weak AI. General AI could solve any intellectual task that a human can solve; strong AI or superintelligence would go beyond human capabilities. However, the type of AI that has triggered the practical application of this discipline is known as weak AI, which, in contrast to strong and general AI, is characterized by developing solutions capable of solving a specific and limited problem. The application of this type of system is extensive: from video games to defense systems, including healthcare, industrial control, robotics, internet search engines, natural language processing, marketing, personal assistants, human resources, optimization of public services, energy management, the environment, and any other activity one can imagine. The scope of application of AI solutions extends to all sectors, each with specific cases and the need to comply with both general regulations and sector-specific regulations.  
The application of Artificial Intelligence raises doubts among users, researchers, specialists, authorities, and the industry regarding compliance aspects, respect for the rights of data subjects, and legal certainty for all parties involved. These doubts can be an obstacle to the proper technological development, which is why it is necessary to develop guidelines and aids that address and resolve the encountered difficulties. In this document, we will focus on addressing the compliance of treatments that incorporate components of weak AI with the GDPR.  
A.  
AI TECHNIQUES  
There are various ways to approach an AI solution: through neural networks, rule-based systems, fuzzy logic, machine learning, expert systems, adaptive systems, genetic algorithms, multi-agent systems, etc., terms that overlap with each other. What all these techniques aim to achieve is to obtain models to handle complex systems for which it is not known how, or it is not possible to handle with sequential algorithms, due to their difficulty in modeling behaviors governed by multiple variables. Among these variables, non-linear relationships are established, or relationships that cannot be approximated by linear methods, and that may even vary over time.

One of the most successful branches of AI in commercial applications is Machine Learning (ML). ML designs predictive models that autonomously build the relationship between the variables to be studied through the analysis of an initial dataset, the identification of patterns, and the establishment of classification criteria. Once the criteria are set, by introducing a new dataset, the AI component is capable of making an inference. Machine learning is related to data mining techniques, optimization, and big data. Moreover, there are different types of machine learning such as supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement, and their variants, which employ different techniques. There are also specializations of ML such as Deep Learning, and different learning models, such as centralized, decentralized, or federated. A system with an AI component can be said to be adaptive when the inference model dynamically adjusts based on each new input dataset, refining the already established relationships.  
B.  
TREATMENTS WITH AI COMPONENTS  
There may be treatments that include AI components handling data of natural persons, such as in a marketing or electoral profiling model, or there may be treatments in which personal data does not appear, as could happen in a weather prediction model that collects data from geographically distributed stations.  
A treatment that makes automated decisions using artificial intelligence may affect natural persons, such as an authentication system for users, or it may not affect individuals, such as an industrial control system. In the case that decisions are made that affect individuals, these decisions may relate to the person's interaction in their social context, such as access to a contract or service, or relate to the personalization of that service, such as the customization of car controls or the programming of a television. Decisions may make predictions about the subject's evolution, perform an assessment of their current state, or decide on the execution of a set of actions.  
Furthermore, in decision-making, AI can adopt two roles:  
•  
That of an aid for the decision-making process, providing an inference or profile about a subject or situation, for a human to make the final decision.  
•  
That of making and executing the decision.  
In either of the above cases, the AI component will not be isolated but will be part of a broader treatment. Additionally, it will have a physical implementation in a system where other applications will be found, there will be data communications, user interfaces, and other elements. The inherent synergy in applications where AI components are found may relate these treatments to Big Data.

C.  
DATA PROTECTION AND THE ETHICAL DIMENSION  
The protection of individuals in relation to the processing of personal data is a fundamental right. Article 8, paragraph 1, of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union and Article 16, paragraph 1, of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union establish that everyone has the right to the protection of personal data concerning them. The fundamental right to data protection is developed within a regulatory framework that currently includes Regulation 619/2016 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (GDPR), and is complemented by Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD), in addition to all sectoral regulations published before and after the entry into force of the GDPR.  
The ethical perspective of AI, as part of “digital ethics,” is one of the aspects that raises the most concern. The ethics of AI aims to protect values such as dignity, freedom, democracy, equality, individual autonomy, and justice against the governance of mechanical reasoning. The European Commission is working on defining trustworthy Artificial Intelligence, establishing that to achieve this, it must meet seven key requirements: human action and oversight, technical robustness and safety, privacy and data management, transparency, diversity, non-discrimination and fairness, social and environmental well-being, and accountability.  
These requirements must be continuously evaluated throughout the entire lifecycle of an AI system. It is necessary to be vigilant both about the ethical legitimacy of the treatments and the unexpected effects of these. Furthermore, the possible collateral impact of such treatments in a social environment must be considered, beyond the initially conceived limitations of purpose, duration, and scope.  
That is to say, the AI solution must be analyzed per se, but also within the framework of the treatment in which it is integrated, and the relationships of that treatment with the environment in various aspects:  
• In the cultural aspect, with its scale of values.  
• In the context in which the service is deployed, with its quality requirements.  
• In the aspects that arise from the massive interconnection of components in the information society.  
A critical aspect of AI systems is the possible existence of biases. A bias is an inappropriate deviation in the inference process. Biases are particularly serious when, for example, they lead to discrimination against a group.

However, there is another type of bias that may be even more concerning, which is the bias in the interpretation of AI results. This human bias consists of accepting, without critical spirit, the results of an AI as certain and unchangeable, assuming a "principle of authority" derived from the expectations created by such systems.  
The extent and depth of individuals' ethical values largely depend on the cultural environment in which they develop as persons. Privacy and intimacy of individuals form an important part of ethical principles, and similarly, the perception that each society may have of such values can be very different, although some globally accepted principles have been agreed upon internationally.  
D.  
DEFINITIONS IN THE GDPR  
The GDPR establishes definitions and framework concepts, primarily in its Article 4, and without failing to refer to that article, we will further develop some of them below that are particularly interesting in relation to AI:  
Object of the GDPR  
Article 1 of the GDPR establishes the object of the regulation as the protection of the rights and fundamental freedoms of natural persons, particularly with regard to the protection of personal data.  
Personal data  
The concept of personal data is defined in Article 4.1 of the GDPR as "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ('the data subject'); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person."  
This definition is further developed in Opinion 4/2007 on the concept of personal data by the Article 29 Working Party (WP29).

Pseudonymization and Anonymization  
Pseudonymization is defined in Article 4.5 of the GDPR as “the processing of personal data in such a way that the data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such additional information is kept separately and is subject to technical and organizational measures to ensure that the personal data cannot be attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person.”  
Anonymization is the process that allows for the elimination or minimization of the risks of re-identification of an individual from their personal data by removing all direct or indirect references to their identity, while maintaining the accuracy of the results of the processing of such data. In other words, in addition to preventing the identification of individuals, anonymized data must ensure that any operation or processing that may be carried out after anonymization does not lead to a distortion of the actual data.  
Both definitions are complemented in Opinion 05/2014 on anonymization techniques by WP29 and in the Guidelines and guarantees in the processes of anonymization of personal data published by the AEPD.  
Special Categories of Data  
The special categories of data are established in Article 9 of the GDPR as those data that “reveal racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health, or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation.”  
The GDPR establishes in this article a general prohibition on their processing, a prohibition that is extended in Article 9 of the LOPDGDD, unless a series of circumstances detailed in paragraphs 2, 3, and 4 of Article 9 of the GDPR and paragraphs 1 and 2 of Article 9 of the LOPDGDD are met.  
Processing  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines processing as any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.

The profiling (see definition below) and the making of a decision about a natural person are treatments according to Recitals 24 and 72 of the GDPR.  
Profiling  
Article 4.4 of the GDPR defines profiling as a form of processing personal data that allows for inferring more information about a natural person by evaluating, analyzing, or predicting personal aspects.  
A processing operation that involves profiling is characterized by three elements:  
•  
It must be an automated form of processing, including those processes that have partial human involvement.  
•  
It must be carried out with respect to personal data;  
•  
And the objective of profiling must be to evaluate personal aspects concerning a natural person.  
Automated decisions  
Decisions based solely on automated processing represent the ability to make decisions by technological means without human involvement. The GDPR, in its Recitals 71 and 72 as well as in its Article 22, limits and establishes rights regarding the subjects of data not being subjected to exclusively automated decisions that have legal effects or that significantly affect the data subject. Automated profiling falls within this framework of automated decisions. The Article 29 Working Party has analyzed the implications of this right in the Guidelines on Automated Individual Decision-Making and Profiling for the purposes of Regulation 2016/679 of the WP29. However, it should be noted that automated decisions can be made with or without profiling, and profiling can occur without making automated decisions.

Entities that employ such AI on data of data subjects (employees, customers, or others) such as a company that uses AI to determine marketing policies based on the preferences of its customers.

Responsible  
The figure of the data controller is defined in the GDPR, in Article 4.730 of the GDPR, as the person who determines the purposes and means of the processing, and the scope of their obligations, which are framed in Article 24, includes, among others, the obligation to “implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with this Regulation.”  
Joint Controller  
The GDPR introduces the figure of the joint controller in Article 26, as those controllers (two or more) who jointly determine the purposes and means of the processing. The joint controllers shall transparently and by mutual agreement determine their respective responsibilities in complying with the obligations imposed by the GDPR.  
Processor  
The processor is the person who processes personal data on behalf of the controller, as established in Article 4.831 of the GDPR, and the scope of their obligations, which are framed in Article 28, includes, among others, that their relationship with the controller “shall be governed by a contract or other legal act under Union or Member State law, which binds the processor to the controller.”  
Domestic Exception  
In Article 2.c it is established, and further developed in Recital 18, that the GDPR does not apply when the processing is carried out by a natural person in the course of an exclusively personal or domestic activity.  
These activities are, for example, “correspondence and maintaining a directory of addresses, or social media activity and online activity carried out in the context of the aforementioned activities” and, in general, those “without any connection to a professional or commercial activity.”  
However, it should be noted that the domestic exception does not apply to controllers or processors who provide means to process personal data related to such activities.

An AI solution will be a data processing element that will be included in one or more phases of a processing operation. In some cases, the AI component will be specifically developed for that processing; in many other cases, the component will be developed by third parties different from the data controller. The AI component will not be isolated and will be integrated into a specific processing operation alongside other components such as: data collection, filing systems, security modules, user interfaces, and others. Moreover, once developed, an AI component could be integrated into processing operations of different controllers.  
The life cycle of an AI system, from its genesis to its disposal, will go through various stages common to all technological developments. However, depending on the AI technology implemented, it may have some nuances or particularities. These stages are:  
•  
Conception and analysis, in which the functional and non-functional requirements of the AI solution are established. These will be determined by business objectives derived from the processing in which it will be incorporated or from the market where the component is intended to be commercialized. It will include project plans, regulatory constraints, etc.  
•  
Development: including stages of research, prototyping, design, testing, training, and validation. Not all stages will always be present, and their existence will be subject to the specific AI solution adopted. For example, the training stage will be present in AI components based on machine learning (ML).  
•  
Exploitation: this stage includes the execution of various actions, some of which will be executed in parallel: integration, production, deployment, inference, decision-making, maintenance, and evolution.  
•  
Final withdrawal of the processing/component.  
It should not be forgotten that, for the purpose of this document, we have focused on the AI component, but when conducting an analysis of the processing, the AI component and the other elements that make up the processing must be studied as a whole. Additionally, depending on the type of application, some of the previous stages may overlap. For example, validation could overlap during the development and exploitation stages, or the evolution stage could develop simultaneously with the inference stage.

Before continuing, it should be noted that not all AI solutions process personal data at any stage of their lifecycle, nor do they make decisions based solely on automated processing that affects individuals. Some examples of AI solutions without personal data could be industrial product quality control systems, or those decision-making systems regarding the buying and selling of financial products.  
If an AI component processes personal data, creates profiles about an individual, or makes decisions about the same, it will have to comply with the GDPR. Otherwise, it will not be necessary. In many cases, it is not straightforward to determine whether personal data is processed at any stage of the lifecycle of an AI-based system.  
During the lifecycle of an AI solution, personal data may have been used in some way, for example, in the development stage. In that case, that stage constitutes processing and is subject to compliance with the GDPR. In later stages of the lifecycle of the AI solution, for example, when it is integrated into processing, it must be assessed whether personal data is processed to determine if the processing is subject to compliance with the GDPR, at least concerning the AI solution. If it is considered that personal data is not processed, because it has been deleted or anonymized, it must be demonstrated that these processes have been truly effective and assess what the risk of re-identification is.  
If we want to rule out that personal data is processed in later stages of its lifecycle, for example, when the AI solution is integrated into processing, and therefore, this processing is not subject to compliance with the GDPR, it must be demonstrated that the deletion or anonymization of personal data is truly effective and assess what the possible risk of re-identification exists.  
In the operational stage of the system that incorporates the AI solution, we may encounter several situations:  
• The owner of the AI system grants access to it to the data subjects subject to the AI processing. This is, for example, the case of the psychological evaluation of Facebook users conducted by the social network itself in certain countries.  
• The owner of the AI system decides to transfer the rights to use that AI as a component to a third party, treating it as an off-the-shelf module, dissociating from the operation of the system. An example could be an AI driving assistant system that a car manufacturer acquires as a component and incorporates into its models.  
• An entity decides the purposes of a processing operation and hires the owner of the AI system to execute one of the phases of that processing. In this way, the owner of the AI is the one who effectively processes the personal data under the commission and instructions of a controller.

G.  
EVALUATION OF AI SOLUTIONS  
Both the AI model included in a processing operation and the processing itself must have the purpose of addressing a real need of the company and the industry. We are talking about solutions that transcend the experimental realm and will be subject to market laws, a regulated market that is obliged to comply with certain norms and quality standards.  
Regarding their ability to meet the requirements of the processing, there are certain parameters common to any technical solution that must be specified, such as, for example:  
• Precision, accuracy, or error measures required by the processing.  
• Quality requirements for the input data to the AI component.  
• Effective precision, accuracy, or error measures of the AI solution based on the appropriate metric to measure its goodness.  
• Convergence of the model when we encounter training and adaptive solutions.  
• Consistency between the results of the inference process.  
• Predictability of the algorithm.  
• And any other evaluation parameter of the AI component.  
A technical solution that does not have credible answers to these questions cannot be considered based on mature technology, but rather on technology lacking the capacity to meet the basic requirements of accountability, transparency, and legality. Furthermore, those answers will derive essential requirements from the perspective of data protection, such as the application of the principle of minimization.

A.  
LEGITIMACY AND LIMITATION OF PROCESSING  
Establishing a legal basis for legitimacy is the first step in determining the  
compliance of the AI solution with the GDPR. The legitimacy for the different stages of  
the lifecycle and for each processing activity must be established in the conception  
phase of the processing, whether this processing is the actual creation of an AI  
component or a processing activity that involves the use of an AI component. From the  
perspective of Data Protection, legitimacy is the first element that must be established  
within the conception phase of the processing. If a legitimate basis is not found, the  
processing should not be carried out.  
In section 1.E of this document, the different stages of the lifecycle of an AI solution  
in which personal data processing could take place have been listed. Each of these  
stages has a different purpose and, in addition to potentially processing data of the  
subject to whom the service is being provided, may also involve processing data of  
third parties.  
Due to the nature of AI systems, at each stage of the lifecycle, a different legal basis  
could be used for:  
•  
The training and/or validation of the model.  
•  
The use of third-party data in inference.  
•  
The communication of implicit data in the model.  
•  
The processing of the data of the data subject within the framework of the  
service provided by the AI.  
•  
The processing of data of the data subject for the evolution of the model.

Article 6 of the GDPR establishes the six legal bases under which the processing of personal data can be considered lawful. The most common legal bases that will legitimize processing in an AI solution are:  
•  
The processing is necessary for the performance of a contract in which the data subject is a party, or for the implementation of pre-contractual measures at the request of the data subject. This could be the case for developers who hire individuals to use their personal data in the training stage of the system. It could also be that the data controller, who provides a service to third parties that includes the AI solution, uses the data of these parties within the framework of the service contract.  
•  
Legitimate interest, provided that such interests do not override the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require the protection of personal data, particularly when the data subject is a child.  
•  
The consent of the data subjects, which, as established in Article 4.11 of the GDPR, is any manifestation of will that is free, specific, informed, and unambiguous by which the data subject agrees, either by a statement or a clear affirmative action, to the processing of personal data concerning them. In certain more specific cases from the perspective of AI solutions, the following may also be legal bases:  
•  
Protection of vital interests55.  
•  
Reasons of public interest or the exercise of public powers56.  
•  
Compliance with legal obligations.  
It is very important to note that the last two legal bases must be established by EU law or the laws of the Member States, which will establish the legal basis for the processing. That is, a controller cannot claim reasons, for example, of public interest if it is not established in a norm of appropriate rank.  
Another important aspect is that the principle of limitation of processing must be taken into account. A legal basis does not authorize the use of data for any purpose and at all times57, but must be restricted to those specific, explicit, and legitimate purposes that have been identified, avoiding processing them in a manner incompatible with those purposes. Furthermore, the data subjects whose data are processed must be aware of how their data will be used, which is closely related to the principle of information and transparency.  
The extinction of a legal basis for legitimacy, such as the withdrawal of consent, does not have a retroactive effect concerning the results obtained in a processing that has already been carried out. For example, when personal data have been used to train an AI component, the extinction of the legal basis does not invalidate the exploitation of the model, although the data controller must pay attention to requests for the exercise of rights in data protection matters.  
If the data controller uses third-party datasets for the training of an AI component, they must show due diligence in verifying the legitimacy of the acquired data source, including in the purchase or service provision contract the contractual clauses that require evidence and commitments regarding such legitimacy.

Legitimate Interest  
The Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest, from the Article 29 Working Party, detailed how to evaluate the factors that legitimize the controller's interest in processing personal data and balance it with the rights and interests, also legitimate, of the data subjects. Legitimate interest is an alternative basis for legitimizing processing that requires, as in some cases of ML, access to training data, provided that the circumstances allowing its use are present.  
It should be noted that using legitimate interest as a legal basis requires the controller to demonstrate a higher degree of commitment, formality, and competence. It demands a careful assessment that their legitimate interests prevail over the possible impact on the rights, freedoms, and interests of the data subjects. This assessment must take into account, among other things, any compensatory measures derived from maintaining the processing under continuous supervision; the adoption of a high degree of proactive responsibility; the incorporation of stricter privacy by default and by design measures; or the application of good practices such as providing the option to opt-out to the data subjects. In any case, the controller must be able to demonstrate that such impact is not of such magnitude as to prevent the processing from being carried out on that basis, and all this analysis and decision-making process must be documented in compliance with the principle of "accountability."  
If the processing is based on legitimate interest, it is not necessary to obtain the consent of the data subject, but the information obligations provided in Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR remain.  
Special Categories  
To determine the legal basis for processing, it is important to consider that the special categories of data, established in Article 9 of the GDPR, have additional requirements for their processing. In these cases, and before analyzing a legal basis that legitimizes the processing under Article 6 of the GDPR, it is necessary to lift the prior prohibition established in the aforementioned Article 9 based on one of the circumstances contemplated therein, without losing sight of the additional limitations established in the LOPDGDD also in its Article 9. In particular, it must be considered that consent does not lift the prohibition for processing whose main purpose is to identify the ideology, union affiliation, religion, sexual orientation, beliefs, or racial or ethnic origin of the data subject.  
Furthermore, Recital 71 of the GDPR establishes an additional restriction on the processing of special categories of data when they are intended to be used in automated decisions and for profiling, setting the limitation that these can only be employed under specific conditions. In particular, Article 22.4 establishes that decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produce legal effects concerning the data subject or significantly affect them in a similar manner, shall not be based on special categories of personal data unless the data subject has given consent or the processing is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest, based on Union or Member State law, and appropriate measures have been taken to safeguard the rights and freedoms and the legitimate interests of the data subject.

Processing for Compatible Purposes  
As established in Article 6, paragraph 4, it is possible to process personal data for purposes different from those for which they were initially collected. The legal basis for that new purpose could be based on new consent from the data subject, on a legal basis that allows it, but also in cases where the purposes are compatible. Of course, the controller must meet all the requirements for the lawfulness of the original processing and must assess that compatibility taking into account the relationship between the purpose for which the data was collected and the purpose for which it is intended to be processed, the context in which it was collected, the nature of the data, paying special attention, among other things, to the special categories of data, the consequences for the data subjects, and the safeguards implemented in the processing to manage the risk to rights and freedoms.  
In particular, further processing operations for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific and historical research purposes, or statistical purposes must be considered lawful compatible processing operations, with the safeguards and exceptions established in Article 89 of the GDPR.

Conversely, if the model itself contains inaccurate personal data of third parties that can be re-identified, associating erroneous information with those third parties, it is necessary to respond to the right of rectification.

PORTABILITY  
Article 20 of the GDPR establishes that, when processing is carried out by automated means, the data subject shall have the right to receive the personal data they have provided to a controller in a structured, commonly used, and machine-readable format, and to transmit it to another controller, when the legitimacy is based on consent or the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract.  
The existence of this right, developed in the Guidelines on the right to data portability of the Article 29 Working Party, classifies personal data into: provided, actively and consciously by the data subject; observed from the data subject, by virtue of the use of the service or device; and inferred, deduced, or created by the data controller based on the previous data. The right to portability applies to the first two types of personal data.  
The data controller of a processing operation that includes an AI component must evaluate and document whether its processing is required to provide portability for the data provided or observed from the data subject, based on what is established in the aforementioned Article 20. In that case, the portability requirement must be taken into account from the earliest stages of conception and design of the processing, in the selection of the AI component and/or by the developers of AI components.  
Article 20.2 of the GDPR establishes the right to the transmission of data directly from controller to controller, but only when technically feasible. In the event that there are limitations to portability, it is a matter of transparency to inform users in advance of such limitations.

DECISION-MAKING BASED SOLELY ON AUTOMATED PROCESSING  
Applications that offer or support a service based on AI solutions may make decisions that affect individuals, their private lives, their physical safety, their social standing, and their interactions with others.  
The GDPR guarantees the right not to be subject to automated decisions, including profiling, when:

There is no human intervention. For human participation to be considered present, the oversight of the decision must be conducted by a competent person authorized to modify the decision, and this must involve a significant and not merely symbolic action.  
•  
They produce legal effects.  
•  
Or similarly and significantly affect the data subject.  
The following exceptions are allowed when the processing:  
•  
Is based on explicit consent and safeguards are applied to protect rights and freedoms.  
•  
Is necessary for the conclusion or performance of a contract, does not affect special categories of data, and, furthermore, safeguards are applied to protect rights and freedoms.  
•  
Is based on EU or Spanish law and does not affect special categories of data.  
•

When the legal basis is explicit consent, the data controller must design the processing in such a way as to protect the users' freedom of choice. On one hand, by providing viable and equivalent alternatives to the automated decision at the time of requesting consent. On the other hand, ensuring that if the user chooses not to be subject to the automated decision, no bias detrimental to the interests of the affected individual will be introduced into the decision. If these conditions are not met, consent cannot be considered free. These courses of action must be contemplated from the very design phase of the processing.  
As a good practice, and beyond requirements arising from data protection, human oversight should be an option in AI-based processing and, in general, in automated decisions. The design of systems should avoid the "dead man switch" orientation and always provide the option for a human operator to override the algorithm at a given moment, establishing procedures for situations where this mode of action must be chosen. It is advisable to document incidents or inquiries regarding the automated decisions received from data subjects so that, through their analysis, it is possible to detect situations where human intervention is necessary because the processing may not be functioning as expected.  
Regarding the processing of data of minors, Recital 71 establishes that decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, with legal effects or significantly similar effects should not be applied to them. However, since this prohibition is not reflected in the articles, it is not considered to have an absolute character, and exceptions may occur when it is essential to protect the minor's welfare and appropriate safeguards are implemented.

IV.  
RISK MANAGEMENT FOR RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS  
Risk management for rights and freedoms is a continuous activity and is part of the concept of Proactive Responsibility or “accountability” established in the GDPR.  
When dealing with personal data and proportional to the processing activities, the organization must systematize compliance measures in a Data Protection Policy, as established in Article 24 of the GDPR. This policy will coexist with a quality policy, an information systems policy, a security policy, and a decision-making policy, among others.  
The GDPR does not specifically establish which elements of proactive responsibility must be implemented mandatorily or how to implement them, limiting itself to providing a broad degree of flexibility for very different processing activities to comply with the regulation. However, it does establish that such measures must be selected following a risk-based analysis (RBT74), specifically concerning the risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals in relation to the processing of their personal data and the creation of profiles75.  
The RBT has two fundamental phases: the first is the identification of threats and assessment of the existing intrinsic risk level; and the second is the management of this risk through the implementation of appropriate and proportional technical and organizational measures to eliminate it or at least mitigate it, reducing the impact or the probability of the identified threats materializing. Finally, once the selected measures have been implemented, the remaining residual76 risk must be assessed and kept under control.  
Both to determine the risk and to establish the appropriate measures to manage it, it is necessary to conduct a treatment analysis, dividing it into its different phases and managing the peculiarities of each of them.  
To determine the risk level of a treatment based on or containing phases with an AI component, the following must be taken into account:  
• The risks arising from the treatment itself, the most characteristic being that arising from bias in decision-making systems about individuals or their discrimination (algorithmic discrimination77).  
• The risks arising from the treatment concerning the social context and the collateral effects that may derive from it, indirectly related to the object of treatment.

The DPIA is an obligation established in the GDPR when the risk levels associated with the processing are high. This obligation implies going beyond mere risk management of the processing, as it requires an additional formality when executing such management.  
The necessity for each controller to carry out a data protection impact assessment is established in Article 35 of the GDPR when, according to paragraph 1, “the processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.” In particular, but not exclusively, and as established in Article 35.3.a78, a DPIA is necessary when profiling is carried out based on automated processing (but not necessarily exclusively automated79), on which decisions are made that produce legal effects for natural persons or that significantly affect them.  
The DPIA must be carried out before the actual processing of personal data is executed, that is, before starting the processing80. Therefore, validation must be carried out before the design/selection and implementation of the AI solution for a specific processing operation, so that it is possible to identify what privacy requirements need to be incorporated and to effectively apply privacy measures from the design and by default.  
Article 35.2 of the GDPR establishes the obligation of the controller to seek the advice of the data protection officer, if one has been appointed.  
It is also important to note that Article 35.981 of the GDPR establishes that, where appropriate, the opinion of the data subjects will be sought.

C.  
TRANSPARENCY  
According to Recital 7882, the principle of transparency is a default privacy measure to allow, among other things, data subjects to monitor the processing to which they are subjected.  
The principle of transparency is developed in Recitals 39 and 58. In these Recitals, the obligation to inform data subjects is interpreted in a way that goes beyond what is provided in the wording of Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR. In particular, the Recitals comment on the obligation that “all information and communication related to the processing of such data should be easily accessible and easy to understand,” “should be concise,” “should use simple and clear language,” that “where appropriate, it should be visualized,” that “it could be provided electronically,” that “additional information should be provided to ensure fair and transparent processing,” and that data subjects “must be aware of the risks, rules, safeguards” of the processing.  
In the case of AI-based treatments, transparency can be considered a critical aspect. It must allow data subjects to be aware of the impact that the use of such solutions may entail. Hence, transparency is directed both at data subjects and at the operators of the processing. In particular, transparency is linked to truthful information about the efficiency, capabilities, and real limitations of AI systems, which avoids creating false expectations among users and data subjects that may lead to a misinterpretation of the inferences made within the framework of the processing. Additionally, transparency is linked to information about the context and situation of the processing, such as the existence of third parties, the physical/virtual location of the AI solution, etc.  
Transparency is not limited to a specific moment; rather, it should be understood as a principle around which the processing revolves dynamically, affecting each and every element and participant involved in the solution.

Factors Influencing Accuracy  
Regarding the accuracy of the inferred data about the data subject, there are three factors that can influence that accuracy:  
• The implementation of the AI system itself. There are AIs, such as rule-based expert systems, where the design of the system itself can introduce errors that lead to incorrect inferences; or ML-based systems that are unable to model the desired processing. Errors can occur because external elements to the AI, such as biometric readers, introduce errors in the input data. On the other hand, there is the possibility of programming or design errors that incorrectly transfer the model to its practical implementation. In these cases, it can be said that the bias is "hardwired" by the decisions made when constructing the analysis model (evaluation and aggregation biases).  
• The training or validation dataset is flawed with errors, deliberately incorrect information, or biases that make correct inferences impossible. These biases could be inherent if they already exist in the data that feeds the system, such as poor data quality, missing data, or selective sampling. They could also be representation and measurement biases, due to how the dataset is formatted to feed the system.  
• The biased evolution of the AI model. In the case of AI that implements adaptive techniques, the AI solution may be predominantly used by a group of subjects with particular characteristics that introduce new feedback biases.

Verification vs. Validation  
The testing and/or verification of the AI component is a crucial part of its development, as well as an important criterion for selecting one component or another by the data controller. The controller must take into account that the inclusion of a verified AI component in a processing operation does not imply that the entire processing operation is validated, nor does it guarantee the suitability of the AI component for that specific processing operation. Testing of the AI component ensures that the design and development results meet the requirements of the component. Validation of the processing goes further, as it ensures that the resulting products and services meet the requirements related to a specific application or intended use.94 Validation guarantees that the processing, as well as the AI solution on which it relies, meets the planned outcomes for a specific product or service.  
That is to say, the validation of processing that includes an AI component must be carried out under conditions that reflect the real environment in which the processing is intended to be deployed.95 In turn, the validation process requires periodic review, as those conditions or the processing itself evolve.

Extension of data categories in the AI solution  
In each phase of a processing, regardless of whether it includes an AI component or not, a different extent of total personal data relating to the same data subject will need to be processed. It will normally not be necessary to access the entire extent of available personal data in each phase, so the data minimization strategies employed in each phase of processing should be different. They should also differ in each of the stages of the AI solution's lifecycle, whether it is the training stage, inference stage, or model evolution stage. All of this should be done without forgetting the limitations established by the legal basis itself.  
In particular, one aspect that must be justified when establishing the extent of data categories to be used in the AI component is the use of proxy variables. A proxy variable is a type of data that, by itself, seems to have no relation to the object of processing but could have a strong correlation with the inferred value. When proposing the processing of proxy variables, it is necessary to demonstrate the validity of that correlation to legitimize its processing.  
There are two important aspects to analyze concerning proxy variables. The first is that their use is not linked to any bias in the reasoning model. An example would be using the nationality of the data subject as an element.  

Cross-validation involves dividing the database into training and testing several times with different data selections in each subgroup, comparing the overall result of the training and testing of the algorithm in each iteration.  
Hyperparameters are those parameters that allow configuring the operation of a specific AI model. For example, in neural networks, hyperparameters would be the number of layers, the number of neurons per layer, the learning rate, etc.  
Federated Learning or FL is a distributed ML model that allows the training of the model on end devices (process over the edge). It is an approach to "bring the processing to the data instead of the data to the processing" and allows managing issues of privacy, ownership, and data localization.

Training Set Extension  
A specific aspect of the principle of minimization in AI solutions is related to the size of the population from which data will be collected to train the models, particularly when such processing is based on legitimate interest.  
In this case, the number of affected data subjects must be justified based on:  
• The accuracy that the AI solution must achieve in its inferences for the specific processing.  
• The convergence factor of the training algorithm towards the desired accuracy value based on the volume of examples provided.  
• That the volume of data used, either due to a lack of availability of data covering a balanced spectrum of the population (by age, gender, status, race, culture…), or due to the age of such data reflecting obsolete social contexts, introduces biases in the inference.  
The act of feeding an AI learning model with data without any prior control or analysis, in addition to being unjustified, especially in cases where the processing is based on legitimate interest, can cause the AI to lose accuracy and become a multiplier of biases. The dataset to be used must be carefully analyzed to avoid such risks and legitimize its use if, for example, the processing is based on legitimate interest.  
Furthermore, an excess of training data (overfitting) can cause the model to be overly tailored to easily re-identifiable cases, making it more vulnerable to privacy attacks (see in the section “F. Security” attacks by model inversion).  

Personal Data in the AI Solution  
In the event that the AI is distributed as a component of a system and that, as part of this, third-party data is included, a formal assessment must be conducted to determine which personal data of the affected data subjects communicated could become identifiable.  
Additionally, technical, organizational, or legal measures must be applied to minimize identification or the extent of identification to the smallest number of possible data points.  
On the other hand, during the operation or inference stage, it is advisable to use early data deletion strategies for the data subject in the AI solution, even when the domestic exception applies.

F.  
SECURITY  
The GDPR establishes in its Article 32 that both the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. These measures shall be proportionate to the costs of implementation, the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as to the risks of varying probability and severity. In other words, there is no standard solution for all types of processing, much less for those that include an AI component. The solution must be established through a risk analysis that, from a data protection perspective, must relate to the risks to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  
Specific threats in AI components  
The analysis of security measures common to any system is equally necessary to protect an AI solution. For example, those arising from components being developed by third parties or from data communications to third parties that may occur must be considered. Additionally, there is a need to implement specific measures aimed at preventing attacks that are specific to this type of application.  
There are studied typologies of attack and defense against AI components. Among the security measures, special attention is recommended for those that manage these types of threats:  
• Access and manipulation of the training dataset, prior to the model configuration, for example, through poisoning techniques with adversarial patterns.  
• Inclusion of trojans and backdoors during the AI development process, either in the code itself or in the development tools.  
• Manipulation of the user API to access the model, both at the black box and white box levels, for the manipulation of model parameters, filtering the model to third parties, attacks on the integrity or availability of the inferences.  
• Attacks by "adversarial machine learning," thus requiring an analysis of robustness and control of the data feeding to the model.  
• Imitation attacks of patterns that are known to be accepted by the system.

Activity logs or records  
The existence of log files or activity records, the performance of audits (whether automatic or manual), and the certification of the process are an inherent part of "accountability" or proactive responsibility strategies. They also arise from legal requirements specifically established in sectoral regulations.  
Log files will be necessary to support audit processes and security mechanisms. Regarding data protection, these log files must provide evidence to:  
• Determine who and under what circumstances accesses the personal data that may be included in the model.  
• Provide traceability regarding the updating of inference models, the communications of the user API with the model, and the detection of attempts at abuse or intrusion.  
• Provide traceability to enable governance in the communication of data among all participants in the AI solution concerning the obligations arising from Recital 66 of the GDPR.  
• Provide monitoring of the quality parameters of inference when the AI is used for decision-making or decision-support processes.  
The legitimate interest of the data controller, as a legitimizing basis for the processing of personal data from activity log files for security purposes, is developed in Recital 49127 of the GDPR, in "the strictly necessary and proportionate measure to ensure the security of the network and information." G.  
EVALUATION OF THE PROPORTIONALITY AND NECESSITY OF SUCH PROCESSING  
Article 35.7.b of the GDPR establishes that, when conducting a DPIA, an evaluation of the necessity and proportionality of the processing concerning its purpose must be carried out.  
The use of AI-based solutions places data controllers and processors in a scenario where the processing, due to its characteristics and, in particular, the chosen technology, may entail a high level of risk. Therefore, it should be assessed whether the objective of the processing can be achieved using another type of solution that achieves the same functionality, with an acceptable performance margin and a lower level of risk.  
That is to say, the availability or novelty of a technology does not justify, by itself, its use; rather, it must be subject to weighing, conducting an analysis of whether the processing, in the manner proposed, is balanced because it yields more benefits and specific advantages for the general interest and society as a whole than detriments, understood as the risks to the rights and freedoms of the subjects whose data is being processed.

H.  
AUDIT  
The audit process for a treatment that includes an AI component can vary in scope: auditing the development process of the component, the distribution of the model, specific aspects of the treatment, operation, security, and robustness against attacks on the model, etc. Additionally, the audit can be conducted internally or externally, and serve both a control purpose and as a transparency tool.  
As established by the principle of proactive responsibility, the guarantees set to manage the risk must be documented and include sufficient information to satisfactorily and demonstrably substantiate the actions taken. This documentation must allow for the traceability of the decisions and verifications made following the aforementioned minimization principles. Not only must the treatment be audited, but it must also be auditable throughout its lifecycle, including its withdrawal.  
It is necessary to conduct the audit to determine compliance with the requirements of the GDPR and to verify the validity of the treatment based on AI solutions. For the audit process to be effective, it must be conducted under the same conditions as a real operational environment, particularly to evaluate:  
• The existence of a documented process for analysis, development, and/or implementation including, where appropriate, the relevant evidence of traceability.  
• The existence or absence of personal data, profiling, or automatic decisions about data subjects without human intervention, as well as the analysis of the effectiveness of anonymization and pseudonymization methods.

V.  
INTERNATIONAL TRANSFERS  
The development or deployment of a cloud-based AI component, the communication of user data to third parties to evolve the AI model, or the distribution of the AI component in cases where there are personal data inherent to the model, may involve cross-border data flows to third countries. Data flows occurring within the framework of the European Economic Area (the EU Member States plus Iceland, Norway, and Liechtenstein) are not considered international data transfers.  
Such transfers must apply the guarantees established in Chapter V of the GDPR "Transfers of personal data to third countries or international organizations." It is especially important to establish mechanisms to ensure that contracts made in this context of international transfers are managed smoothly, while ensuring that the responsible client has sufficient information about contractors or potential contractors and retains the ability to make decisions. When international transfers occur, data subjects must be informed in accordance with the terms of Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR and this must be included in the record of processing activities.

VI.  
CONCLUSIONS  
The marketing of treatments that include solutions based on disruptive technologies, such as those based on AI components, for entities and consumers in general, requires the implementation of quality and safety guarantees. The availability of a technology or its novelty is not sufficient reason to market products that do not meet an adequate level of service quality, especially when these requirements are established in a standard. On the other hand, researchers and the AI-based industry need guidelines and support to assist them in regulatory compliance and provide legal certainty in their projects, products, and services.  
Regarding the protection of personal data, compliance with the provisions of the GDPR requires a certain level of maturity in AI solutions that allows for an objective determination of the adequacy of the treatments and the existence of guarantees and measures to manage their risks.  
For these risks, technology in general, and in particular AI technologies, can have a multiplying effect on the ethical shortcomings that are already present in society or those that have persisted from the past and are recorded in historical data. The application of the GDPR and the guarantees it incorporates allows for the minimization of such risks.  
The use of transparency strategies, risk management, and auditing and certification mechanisms will not only enable compliance with the provisions of the GDPR but will also enhance user trust in AI-based products and services, in addition to opening a new market in this sector: privacy engineers, auditors, certification schemes, accredited professionals, etc. These new development opportunities also extend to the creation of portability schemes.  
Moreover, AI applications can provide significant support for protecting privacy and implementing mechanisms that ensure data protection.  
This document aims to be a mere introduction to the adequacy of treatments that include AI components and does not cover all the possibilities and risks that may arise from the use of AI solutions in personal data processing.  
In conclusion, it should be emphasized that one of the main problems with AI solutions is not the AI itself, but how people will use AI technology and the new psychological biases that arise from its use. In particular, special attention must be paid to attributing responsibilities to AI components without oversight and without adopting a critical stance. The delegation of decision-making to machines is not new; it already occurs with deterministic algorithms, but the bias of attributing greater authority or weight to a result inferred by an AI solution can increase the risks arising from this delegation of responsibility.
