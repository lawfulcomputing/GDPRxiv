1. Introduction
Since it involves the processing of personal data for the purposes described, unless the so-called domestic exception applies, 
this processing must comply with the principles and obligations established by data protection regulations.
To facilitate this compliance, the AEPD website has a specific section dedicated to video surveillance, where various materials can be consulted, including practical sheets as well as legal reports that analyze specific cases of the use of cameras for both security and other purposes.
Introduction
The image of a person, to the extent that it identifies or can identify them, constitutes personal data, which may be subject to processing for various purposes. While the most common purpose is to use cameras to ensure the safety of individuals, property, and facilities, they can also be used for other purposes, such as research, healthcare assistance, or monitoring employee performance.

2. Image Processing for Security Purposes  
When processing images for security purposes through the various existing capture systems, the legitimacy for using such capture systems must first be assessed, as well as the principles of purpose limitation and data minimization set forth in Article 5 of the regulation.  
Additionally, in reference to the principle of proactive responsibility, a series of actions must also be taken to ensure that these processes comply with the content of the GDPR.  
2.1 Legitimacy  
Therefore, since the purpose of video surveillance is to guarantee the safety of people, property, and facilities, the public interest legitimizes such processing. Likewise, Recital 45 of the GDPR states that if the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest, this processing must have a basis in Union or Member State law.  
In this regard, it is worth mentioning the regulations applicable to specific sectors, such as Organic Law 4/1997, of August 4, which regulates the use of cameras by the Security Forces and Bodies, and its implementing regulation approved by Royal Decree 596/1999, of April 16, Law 5/2014, of April 4, on Private Security, or Law 19/2007, of July 11, against violence, racism, xenophobia, and intolerance in sports, as well as its implementing regulation approved by Royal Decree 203/2010, of February 26.  
The GDPR establishes several cases in its Article 6 that legitimize the processing of personal data, among which is allowing processing when it is necessary for the performance of a task in the public interest.

2.2.3. Data Minimization  
Another principle outlined in the GDPR in its Article 5 is the principle of data minimization, such that the data being processed is adequate, relevant, and limited in relation to the purposes for which it is processed.  
On the other hand, the aforementioned principle also extends to the number of cameras intended to be used as well as their type, since capturing images through a fixed camera is not the same as capturing images through so-called 'dome' cameras, which allow 360-degree recordings, or those that are mobile.  
Moreover, there are spaces where, due to their conditions, the use of video surveillance could be disproportionate, such as in changing rooms, lockers, and employee break areas.

2.3. Proactive Responsibility Measures  
This concept, as an essential principle in the processing of personal data, is established in Article 5 of the GDPR to which we have previously referred. Specifically, according to its paragraph 2, proactive responsibility is one of the obligations of the data controller in relation to the principles referred to in paragraph 1 of the same article. Therefore, it is one of the new obligations established in the GDPR to ensure compliance with these principles, which consists of the ability of the controller, that is, the organization, to demonstrate and provide evidence of such compliance.  
The GDPR establishes a catalog of measures that the controller and, in some cases, the processors must apply to ensure that the processing is compliant with European standards. However, it is necessary to clarify that not in all cases must these measures be applied mandatorily.

2.3.1 Data Protection Officer  
The GDPR mandates the designation of a Data Protection Officer (DPO) by controllers and processors in the cases regulated in its Article 37.1.  
These qualifications will be required in relation to the processing activities carried out, as well as the measures that must be adopted to ensure adequate processing of the personal data subject to those activities.  
In this regard, and concerning the appointment of the DPO in accordance with the provisions of the aforementioned Article 37.1, which may affect video surveillance, we must distinguish:  
·· Its mandatory nature for Public Administrations. This means that regardless of whether the Administrations use video surveillance, they are already required to appoint a DPO.  
·· When the main activities of the controller or processor consist of processing operations that, by their nature, scope, and/or purposes, require observation.

2.3.3 Risk analysis and security measures  
The GDPR requires that controllers carry out a risk assessment of the processing they perform, in order to establish the measures to be applied.  
This risk analysis will vary depending on:  
·· The types of processing  
·· The nature of the data  
·· The number of affected individuals  
·· The amount and variety of processing carried out by the same organization  
Through this risk analysis, the security measures to be applied in the data processing carried out will be determined.

The previous Title VIII of Royal Decree 1720/2007 established minimum controls that are mandatory to ensure the security of the data incorporated into the controls or security measures that must be taken into account in the GDPR within the risk analysis processes. Therefore, existing security measures must be maintained and reviewed within the framework of these processes. Under no circumstances should the GDPR be understood as the automatic elimination of all existing security measures.  
Thus, according to Article 32 of the GDPR, the appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk are defined based on the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the variable probability and severity risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals.  
In short, the first step in determining security measures will be the risk assessment referred to earlier, and once assessed, it will be necessary to determine the security measures aimed at reducing or eliminating risks to data processing.  
On the other hand, what is provided in the National Security Scheme is applicable to any information from Public Administrations, regardless of the medium in which it is found. Therefore, regarding security measures, this scheme aligns with the risk-based approach of the GDPR and serves as a valid tool for risk management and the adoption of security measures in the aforementioned Administrations.  
The GDPR does not establish static security measures; therefore, it is up to the data controller to determine those security measures that are necessary to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of personal data.

2.3.4 Notification of security breaches  
When a security breach occurs that affects the processing of cameras for security purposes, that is, the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data, the data controller suffering such a breach, provided there is a risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals, must notify:  
·· The AEPD, within a maximum period of 72 hours.  
Minimum content of the communication of the security breach to the AEPD  
·· nature of the security breach:  
·· categories of affected individuals (e.g., minors, disabled persons, employees, citizens)  
·· approximate number of affected individuals  
·· categories of compromised data (e.g., identifying, health, employment)  
·· number of records of affected personal data  
·· name and contact details of the Data Protection Officer  
·· possible consequences of the security breach suffered  
·· measures taken or proposed to remedy this breach  
·· Without prejudice to the above, for notification purposes and within the scope of Public Administrations, the obligations arising from the National Security Scheme and the applicable Technical Instructions will be taken into account.

The viewing of images captured by the camera can be done from any computer connected to the internet through the usual browser, simply by invoking the camera's IP address, provided that access controls have not been established. In practice, the DPIA allows determining the level of risk involved in a processing operation, with the aim of establishing the most appropriate control measures to reduce it to an acceptable level.  
The GDPR also states that when it is likely that a type of processing, particularly if it uses new technologies, by its nature, scope, context, or purposes, entails a high risk to rights and freedoms, the controller shall carry out an impact assessment prior to the processing. If it involves similar operations that pose similar risks, a single assessment may be conducted.  
Regarding operations that require an impact assessment according to the provisions of the previous paragraph, the Supervisory Authority will establish and publish a list in this regard.  
It may also publish another list regarding those processing operations that do not require such an impact assessment.  
Furthermore, the GDPR specifies the circumstances under which an impact assessment must be carried out:  
·· Systematic and comprehensive assessment of personal aspects of natural persons based on automated processing, such as profiling, and on the basis of which decisions are made that produce legal effects for the natural persons or that significantly affect them in a similar manner.  
·· Large-scale processing of special categories of data referred to in Article 9.1 or of personal data relating to criminal convictions and offenses under Article 10.  
·· Systematic large-scale observation of a publicly accessible area.  
In this regard, and applying the aforementioned circumstances to the use of video surveillance, there may be processing operations that require, prior to their implementation, the carrying out of a data protection impact assessment.  
To determine these two elements, we can refer to the Guidelines on Data Protection Impact Assessment from the Article 29 Working Party, which consider the following:  
·· Systematic observation: processing used to observe, monitor, and control data subjects. This type of observation represents a criterion because personal data may be collected in circumstances where data subjects may not be aware of who is collecting their data and how it will be used. Furthermore, it may be impossible for individuals to avoid being subject to such processing in public spaces (or publicly accessible spaces).

2.3.6 Privacy by design and by default  
The GDPR contains two principles for the effective implementation of proactive responsibility, namely data protection by design and data protection by default.

The principle of data protection by design implies that data protection must be present in the early stages of project conception and be part of the list of elements to consider before initiating the subsequent stages of development.  
Of course, these requirements will translate into technical and organizational measures aimed at effectively applying the principles of data protection and integrating the necessary safeguards in the processing.  
An example of such measures, which is expressly established in the GDPR, is that the processing itself incorporates measures for the pseudonymization of personal data or data minimization.  
On the other hand, data protection by default implies that appropriate technical and organizational measures must be adopted to ensure that, as the name suggests, by default, only the personal data necessary for each specific purpose of processing is subject to processing.  
In particular, it is highlighted as one of the principles of data protection by default that data should not be accessible to an indeterminate number of natural persons, without the intervention of the data subject.  
Furthermore, the following must be taken into account regarding data protection by default:  
·· Data collection: analyze the types of data collected with a minimization criterion based on the products and services selected by the user.  
·· Data processing: analyze the processes associated with such processing to ensure that only the minimum necessary personal data is accessed to carry them out.  
·· Retention: implement a data retention policy that allows, with a restrictive criterion, the deletion of data that is not strictly necessary.  
·· Accessibility: limit third-party access to such personal data.  
Regarding these elements applied to data processing in video surveillance, at the beginning of this Guide, we referred to the principle of minimization. The retention of images is analyzed later in another section.  
Also, regarding accessibility, it must be determined who can access the images and for what purposes. All of this must be properly documented.

2.3.7 Right to information  
Another obligation arising from the use of video surveillance for security purposes, in relation to data protection, is to comply with the right to information, through an informative notice.  
This notice must be displayed in a visible location, and at a minimum, at the entrances to the monitored areas, whether indoors or outdoors. If the monitored space has several access points, this notice must be present at each of them.  
The notice for the monitored area must inform about:  
·· The existence of the processing (video surveillance).  
·· The identity of the data controller or the video surveillance system, and its address.  
·· The possibility of exercising the rights recognized in Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR.  
·· Where to obtain more information about the processing of personal data.  
·· Additionally, the rest of the information that must be provided to the affected parties in compliance with the right to information regulated in the GDPR must also be made available to the interested parties.

2.3.8 Data Processor: contracting a third party for the processing of images  
Access to the images from the cameras on behalf of third parties other than the data controller must be regulated by the existence of a contract.  
For example, when an entity that has installed video surveillance cameras entrusts a third party with their management, providing access to the images, there must be a contract or another legal act in accordance with European Union law, which establishes the object, duration, nature, and purpose of the processing, the type of personal data and categories of data subjects, and the obligations and rights of the controller.  
Furthermore, the GDPR determines the minimum content of this contract or legal act, which must be adapted to the specific circumstances of the service provision and not be a mere copy of the content of the clauses of Article 28.3 of the GDPR, and whose objective will be to ensure compliance with the regulation according to the instructions of the controller.  
Access to the images from the cameras on behalf of third parties other than the data controller must be regulated by the existence of a contract.

2.3.10 Rights of individuals  
Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR regulate the rights that affected individuals can exercise against the controllers and processors (in the latter case, when so provided between the controller and the processor): access, rectification, erasure, restriction of processing, data portability, objection, and objection to automated individual decision-making.  
However, the exercise of these rights must be nuanced in the context of video surveillance.  
Firstly, the exercise of the right to rectification is not possible since, due to the nature of the data—images taken from reality that reflect an objective fact—it would involve the exercise of a right with impossible content.  
Secondly, the right to data portability would also not apply since, although it involves automated processing, the legitimization is not based on consent or the execution of a contract.  
Thirdly, part of the content of the right to restriction of processing would not apply, specifically the aspect of "precautionary cancellation" which is linked to the exercise of the rights of rectification and objection.  
On the other hand, the following rights would be applicable:  
·· The right of access, although it has specific characteristics, as it requires providing supplementary documentation in the form of an updated image that allows the controller to verify and contrast the presence of the affected individual in their records. It is practically impossible to access images without compromising the image of a third party. 4.4 Health and assistance centers  
Without prejudice to the installation of cameras in health centers and hospitals for the purpose of ensuring security, the taking and recording of images could be used to ensure appropriate health treatment, such as monitoring patients in intensive care units or telemedicine.  
In this case of using cameras for health purposes, first, the application of the GDPR is appropriate, although the legitimacy for processing the data, given that health data is considered special categories of data, would be provided in the following manner:  
- In the first case, by Article 9.2.c) of the GDPR, since the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or another natural person, in the event that the data subject is not physically or legally able to give consent.  
- In the second case, by Article 9.2.h) of the GDPR since the processing is necessary for preventive medicine, medical diagnosis, provision of health care or treatment based on European Union law or the laws of the member states or by virtue of a contract with a healthcare professional, without prejudice to the conditions and guarantees provided for in paragraph 3 of the aforementioned Article 9.  
4.5 Scientific research and related uses  
One of the possible purposes for capturing and recording images related to identified or identifiable individuals is research, whether it be scientific research, studying usage or consumption habits, or even in the context of recruitment processes.  
Although the purpose may be video surveillance,  
the use of cameras in patients' rooms  
for their relatives to view their health status  
via "streaming" is considered disproportionate.

4.6 Recordings of collegiate bodies of Public Administrations and assemblies  
In the realm of Public Administrations, Law 40/2015, of October 1, on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector, has provided that meetings held by collegiate bodies may be recorded, and the resulting file from the recording may accompany the minutes of the sessions. These recordings must be preserved in a manner that ensures the integrity and authenticity of the said file, as well as access to it by the members of the collegiate body.  
This recording of the sessions could be included in the operational regulations of the respective collegiate body, or through an instrument such as its internal regulations. Furthermore, it could also be stipulated whether the recording will be used as an aid for drafting the minutes or will form part of them as an annex.  
Regarding the recordings of assemblies (for example, in associations, homeowners' communities, or commercial companies), the recording would be legitimized by Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR based on legitimate interest.  
Therefore, in this case, the provisions of the GDPR will fully apply. In this regard, it should be noted the special importance of complying with the principles of purpose limitation and data minimization in data processing.  
In any case, the GDPR dedicates a specific article, Article 89, to processing for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes, indicating that it will be subject to adequate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, providing technical and organizational measures, particularly to ensure the principle of data minimization, which may include pseudonymization, as long as such purposes can be achieved in this manner.

5.1 "On-board" cameras  
This type of technology consists of installing a camera inside a vehicle or, on occasions, in the driver's helmet, and recording the entire journey made with it. There are even vehicles that already come equipped with such cameras.  
Regarding this type of recording, it is necessary to distinguish between two types of cases:  
·· Recordings for a "domestic" purpose, which would be exempt from the application of data protection regulations. For example, the use of these cameras in the helmets of a cyclist or motorcyclist, capturing scenic images. However, subsequent use, such as publishing the recordings on the internet, would be subject to data protection regulations.  
·· Recordings aimed at obtaining evidence to determine responsibilities associated with the occurrence of an event, that is, obtaining photographs or recordings of images as evidence in relation to possible accidents or traffic incidents.  
This second type of recording would be permitted under the application of the legitimate interest rule (art. 6.1.f of the GDPR), based on the right to effective judicial protection, a fundamental right enshrined in the Spanish Constitution of 1978.  
However, the fact that recordings can be made does not preclude the adoption of a series of precautions, such as ensuring that the recording is only activated in the event of a specific incident, or manual activation, or adhering to the principle of minimization, whereby the images captured from the outside are limited to the front of the vehicle. For more information, you may consult this legal report.

5.2 Drones  
Another technology that, along with "on-board" cameras, has become popular in recent years is remotely piloted aircraft, more commonly known as drones.  
Drones may or may not carry systems for processing information (data in general) of various types: image recording systems, detection systems (optical or electronic sensors, infrared, smoke), or radio frequency equipment (antennas to capture radio or Wi-Fi emissions). In this regard, and for the purposes of this Guide, we will refer to the possible capture and processing of images by drones, through the cameras they are equipped with.  
The Article 29 Working Group, of which the AEPD is a part, analyzed this type of recording using drones in Opinion 01/2015, dated June 16, 2015. Therefore, taking into account this Opinion, it should be noted, in summary, the following:  
·· The operation of the drone must comply with applicable regulations.  
·· Assess the possibility of conducting a Data Protection Impact Assessment, considering the type of drone and the data capture technology for processing.  
·· Avoid capturing or processing unnecessary data for the intended purpose.  
·· Inform in the most appropriate manner and prior to the affected individuals, including a clear indication of who is responsible and the purposes of the processing, as well as clear and specific indications for the exercise of rights.  
·· Establish appropriate security measures for the risks posed by the intended processing.

6. Cases of non-application of data protection regulations  
6.1 Processing of images in the personal and domestic sphere  
The GDPR does not apply to the processing of images carried out by a natural person in the exercise of activities that are exclusively personal or domestic.  
Previously, we have already referred to two specific cases, such as video doorbells and digital peepholes, as long as they do not reproduce and/or record images.  
Regarding the application of this domestic exception, those cases in which the processed information is made known to an indeterminate or indefinite number of people cannot be considered exempt.  
If a social media user acts on behalf of a company or an association or uses it as a platform for commercial, political, or social purposes, the personal or domestic exception does not apply.  
The exception also does not apply in cases where the processing of this data may harm the rights and interests of individuals. To this end, it should be noted that the use of information and communication technologies may lead to a situation where data processing initially linked to the private life of the individual performing it may involve access to information of a third party that they do not wish to be public.  
6.2 Processing of images by the media  
The publication of images in the media constitutes an exercise of the right to freedom of expression and information granted by Article 20 of the Spanish Constitution.

In the event that an individual considers their rights violated by the publication of images, they would have to resort to judicial proceedings under the provisions of Organic Law 1/1982, of May 5.  
In any case, the GDPR contains a mandate for Member States to reconcile by law the right to data protection under European regulations with the right to freedom of expression and information, including processing for journalistic purposes and for academic, artistic, or literary expression.  

6.3 Use of simulated cameras  
The GDPR applies when there is processing of personal data of natural persons.  
In the case of simulated cameras, this processing does not exist, and therefore the aforementioned regulation cannot be applied, based on the material impossibility of operating the cameras, as they lack all the technical elements necessary for their use.  
Notwithstanding the above, it is important to clarify the following:  
If they were real cameras that are deactivated or can be activated without excessive effort, the current principles regarding the protection of personal data and the applicable sectoral regulations must be applied.  

6.4 Tourism promotion and related purposes  
The dissemination of images for promotional purposes via the internet is an increasingly common practice, whether it involves promoting a specific corporate area—such as building facades or unique spaces in companies or institutions—or refers to places of tourist interest. Only when the capture and broadcasting of images do not affect identified or identifiable persons will the application of the GDPR be excluded.  
The GDPR would not apply when the images show a general view of a beach where it is not possible to identify individuals, as would be the case when the purpose of the images is to show the state of the waves to provide information to surfing enthusiasts about the conditions for practicing this sport.
