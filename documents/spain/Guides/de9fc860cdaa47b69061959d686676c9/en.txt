CONTRACTING ‘CLOUD COMPUTING’ SERVICES  
WHAT IS ‘CLOUD COMPUTING’?  
Cloud computing is a new way of providing information processing services, valid for both businesses and individuals, as well as for Public Administration.  
A cloud computing solution allows the user to optimize the allocation and cost of resources associated with their information processing needs. The user does not need to make investments in infrastructure but instead uses what is made available by the service provider, ensuring that situations of resource shortage or excess do not arise, as well as the additional costs associated with such situations.  
In a cloud computing environment, the management of information is virtually in the hands of the customer who contracts the cloud services, who processes it via the Internet accessing database solutions, email, or any type of applications according to their needs. Depending on the model used, the data may not actually be in the hands of the contractor, as the ownership, maintenance, and management of the physical support of the information, processes, and communications may be in the hands of third parties. The service provider can be located virtually anywhere in the world, and its ultimate goal will be to provide the aforementioned services while optimizing its own resources through, for example, practices of delocalization, resource sharing, and mobility or by engaging in additional outsourcing.
6  
In this way, cloud computing represents a new way of using information and communication technologies, based on employing existing techniques in an innovative manner and, above all, on a new scale. This last aspect is what truly makes it distinct, as it allows the use of hardware, software, storage, services, and communications resources that are geographically distributed and accessed through public networks, dynamically, as needed, while needed, and paying a fee (when it is not free) based on what is consumed; that is, providing its clients with on-demand information technology services.  
As a consequence of the above, the same contractor may be unaware of the precise location of their data and may not have direct control over access to it, its deletion, and its portability, since the information is not physically in their possession, although if that information contains personal data, it is indeed under their responsibility from the perspective of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, of April 27, 2016, General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).  
ACTORS IN THE ‘CLOUD COMPUTING’ MODEL  
We can say that cloud computing services are being utilized when we find companies, entities, departments, etc., which we will call users or clients, that share the same resources through the network to implement their information processing processes; resources provided by a different entity, called a cloud service provider or cloud provider.  
Apart from clients and providers, there are other actors in the world of cloud computing. Among them, the partners of cloud providers stand out. These create, offer, and support additional cloud services that they sell to the end user as usage licenses, for example, by creating a marketing application that runs in the cloud based on more basic services of the same. Partners are positioned between the client and the cloud provider, and can formalize their relationship with the latter through various contractual figures (reseller, independent seller, etc.).  
TYPES OF ‘CLOUD COMPUTING’  
Not all cloud computing services and providers are the same, nor are the possible relationships established between clients and providers. Clouds can be classified in many ways based on various criteria, and what is most relevant, from the perspective of Spanish data protection regulations, is how these implementation modalities affect the processing of personal data.  
PUBLIC CLOUD  
We speak of a Public Cloud service when the cloud service provider offers its resources openly to heterogeneous entities, with no further relationship among them than having entered into a contract with the same service provider. There are various and numerous solutions in the Public Cloud, serving everyone from individuals to large corporations, as anyone can contract with them.  
PRIVATE CLOUD  
On the other end, we can talk about a Private Cloud, which we find when an entity manages and administers its cloud services for the parties that comprise it, without external entities being able to participate and maintaining control over it. A Private Cloud is not necessarily implemented by the same entity that uses it, but can be contracted from a third party that will act under its supervision and according to its needs. Entities that opt for Private Clouds are those that are complex and need to centralize computing resources while also offering flexibility in their availability, for example, public administrations and large corporations, although there are examples of Private Clouds implemented in educational entities.  
OTHER MODELS  
Between both models, there are intermediate solutions that will take different names, such as Hybrid Clouds, where certain services are offered publicly and others privately; Community Clouds, when such services are shared within a closed community; or Virtual Private Clouds, when additional security guarantees are implemented over Public Clouds.  
SERVICE MODALITIES  
Cloud providers offer access to computing resources through the network and provide a series of additional value-added services that will bring the provider's offer closer to the needs of its client. Depending on how comprehensive that value-added service is, we can say that we have a solution of Infrastructure as a Service, Platform as a Service, or Software...
8  
as a Service. This division cannot be considered rigid, as there are providers that offer mixed solutions that combine features from all of them.  
SOFTWARE AS A SERVICE  
We can talk about a Software Cloud (Software as a Service or SaaS model) when the user finds in the cloud the final tools with which they can directly implement their company's processes: an accounting application, email, a workflow, a program for the document management of their company, etc.  
INFRASTRUCTURE AS A SERVICE  
If the added value is null, we can talk about an Infrastructure Cloud (IaaS). In that case, the provider offers raw storage and processing capabilities, on which the user must build the applications that their company needs practically from scratch. It could be said that this is the most primitive model of cloud, which began with websites that provided massive storage capacity over the network and web hosting servers.  
PLATFORM AS A SERVICE  
Between these two approaches, there are intermediate ones called PaaS (Platform as a Service), where utilities are provided to build applications, such as databases or programming environments on which the user can develop their own solutions.  
PORTABILITY OF INFORMATION  
The solutions offered by cloud computing providers can be classified as open to portability or closed to it. A solution will be considered open to portability the easier it is for a user to transfer all their data and applications from one cloud provider to another (or to the client's owned systems), ensuring data availability and service continuity.  
It should be noted that the end of the relationship with the cloud provider can occur not only in the case of contract termination by the client but also due to other circumstances beyond their control, such as the end of the provision of some type of service by the provider, changes in their commercial policy, or regulatory framework. This is, therefore, an important aspect to consider when using cloud services, especially public ones, as the more closed to portability the provider is, the greater the difficulty, or even impossibility, of making that transfer at a reasonable cost, which effectively makes the client captive to the provider.  
LOCATION OF THE PROCESS AND DATA  
The cloud computing provider, when implementing services to the end user, can be the sole provider of them when all the resources to provide them belong to the provider itself. That is, it has all the necessary infrastructure, which it manages directly, and does not subcontract third parties based on the different workload it has.  
SUBCONTRACTING  
On the contrary, it may not be a final provider when the service it offers directly to the user is built on subcontracting third parties for necessary elements to implement them (hardware, storage, communications, etc.), as is the case with partners. In turn, subcontractors may subcontract part of the service they provide to the final provider to third and successive companies. This is a model of a chain of subcontracting that theoretically could have no end, and whose purpose is to continuously resize cloud resources dynamically based on market conditions.  
LOCATION  
When deciding to use a cloud computing service, there are other factors to consider from the perspective of citizens' rights and the exercise of the responsibilities of the client of such services.  
It is important to identify which cloud providers are located within the European Economic Area or in countries that in one way or another guarantee an adequate level of protection of personal data. This location affects not only the headquarters of the cloud provider but also the location of each of the physical resources it uses to implement the service, whether directly or subcontracted. And it should be emphasized that the location of all resources must be taken into account because, by the very nature of the cloud service, data can be at any moment in any place, but the rights and obligations related to that data must always be guaranteed.
10  
TRANSPARENCY  
In relation to the control of the location of a user's data, a cloud service can be auditable or transparent (in the sense of the English word accountable) when the contractor can claim precise information about where, when, and who has stored or processed their data (within the provider's own resources or the subcontracting chain), and under what security conditions this has occurred. Otherwise, we will find ourselves with a service opaque to the user, in which they have no option to obtain precise information about what has happened to their data nor tools to audit the service being provided to them, and in which their own information escapes their control.  

CONTRACTUAL GUARANTEES  
The contracting of cloud computing services will be carried out through a service provision contract. It is essential that this contract incorporates the guarantees required by the GDPR (Article 28). For more information on these obligations, you can refer to the Guidelines for drafting contracts between controllers and processors.  
For these purposes, it is important to highlight, first of all, that the client contracting a cloud service provider has a legal duty of diligence to, according to Article 28.1 of the GDPR, choose “only a processor that offers sufficient guarantees to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures, so that the processing is in accordance with the requirements of this Regulation and ensures the protection of the rights of the data subject.” This duty of diligence will translate, given the specific characteristics of these services, into a range of information requirements directed at the service provider aimed at understanding the guarantees it offers for the protection of personal data for which it remains responsible. This information will be essential for deciding on the cloud modality and the type of services contracted and, specifically, for discerning which one or ones offer adequate guarantees and choosing among them. Compliance with this duty of diligence must be matched by a corresponding diligence on the part of the cloud service provider in providing information, particularly regarding the mechanisms that ensure compliance with the obligations arising from data protection regulations, in order to be considered a transparent provider, as established in Article 28.3(h): “will make available to the controller all necessary information to demonstrate compliance with the obligations established in this article, as well as to allow and contribute to audits, including inspections, by the controller or another auditor authorized by the controller,” as well as in the final paragraph of the same article “the processor shall immediately inform the controller if, in its opinion, an instruction infringes this Regulation or other provisions concerning data protection of the Union or of the Member States.”  
The options regarding diligence in the choice of the data processor must be documented to be accredited.  
Considering the contractual relationship established between the client and the cloud provider, this contract can also be classified as negotiated or adhesion. We can say that a contract between the client and the provider is negotiated if the former has, or is offered, the ability to set the contracting conditions based on the type of data to be processed, the required security measures, the subcontracting scheme, the location of the data, the portability of the same, and any other aspect of compliance with data protection regulations and the restrictions that this regulation implies.  
In most cases, however, what is offered are adhesion contracts, consisting of closed contractual clauses, in which the cloud provider sets the conditions with a standard contract equal for all its clients, without the user having any option to negotiate its terms. This latter case is the most common, especially when the client is in a situation of imbalance (e.g.: a small or medium-sized enterprise facing a large provider), although it should be noted that this does not exempt either party from the responsibilities determined by data protection regulations.  

RISKS OF CLOUD COMPUTING  
The use of cloud computing services offers a large number of advantages but also presents, due to its characteristics, specific risks that must be addressed with an adequate choice of provider. To this end, it must be analyzed that the service provision conditions take into account the elements that allow data processing to be carried out without compromising the guarantees that are applicable.
12  
We can group the risks into two main categories: lack of transparency regarding the conditions under which the service is provided and lack of control by the data controller over the use and management of personal data by the parties involved in the service.  
LACK OF TRANSPARENCY  
It is the service provider who knows all the details of the service they offer. Therefore, we face the need to understand the what, who, how, and where of the processing of the data provided to the provider for the provision of the service. If the latter does not provide clear, precise, and complete information about all the elements inherent to the service provision, the decision made by the data controller will not be able to adequately consider basic requirements such as the location of the data, the existence of subprocessors, access controls to the information, or security measures. In this way, it becomes difficult for the data controller to evaluate the risks and establish the appropriate controls, which is why compliance with the obligation of due diligence in the choice of the service provider, as previously noted, implies, first and foremost, obtaining information.  
LACK OF CONTROL  
As a consequence of the peculiarities of the cloud processing model and partly also due to the lack of transparency in the information, the lack of control by the data controller manifests itself, for example, in the difficulties of knowing at all times the location of the data, the challenges in accessing the data held by the provider or obtaining it in a valid and interoperable format, the obstacles to effective management of the processing, or, ultimately, the absence of effective control when defining the substantive elements of the processing concerning technical and organizational safeguards.  
A STRATEGY FOR THE CLOUD SERVICE CLIENT  
In light of the described risks, the data controller – e.g., companies or administrations – intending to incorporate all or part of the data processing into cloud services should consider an action strategy that could be as follows:  
EVALUATE THE PROCESSING AND DATA PROTECTION RISKS  
The client must carefully study which part or parts of the processing they perform are susceptible to being transferred to cloud computing services, considering not only the benefits but also the potential risks that will be assumed.  
The suggested action strategy entails, on one hand, studying the resources of the data controller that are susceptible to being transferred to the cloud or not, depending on the level of risk they present, which requires a detailed understanding of the data controller regarding the personal data processed. As suggested, a fundamental element will be knowing the processing of special categories of personal data (e.g., health data). The transfer of data to cloud services does not, in principle, exclude any type of data, provided there is a positive balance between the risks assumed and the safeguards, in the form of organizational and technical measures, provided by the supplier, in order to ensure and demonstrate that the processing is compliant with the GDPR.  
VERIFY THE CONDITIONS UNDER WHICH THE SERVICE IS PROVIDED  
The conditions under which the service is provided must be verified prior to contracting to determine if they offer an adequate level of compliance.  
The conditions offered by the providers should be contrasted with a checklist that includes, among others, elements related to the information provided, location of the processing, existence of subprocessors, security policies, user rights, and legal obligations of the service provider.  
It is advisable to compare the features offered by several providers, not only in economic terms but also in relation to the content of the service and the guarantees of quality and legal compliance that each provider offers, especially the adherence of the processor to approved codes of conduct or certification mechanisms, which can be used as elements to demonstrate the existence of guarantees.  
In any case, special attention must be paid to not contracting cloud services that do not meet the legally established requirements.  
Considering the exit procedures in case of a change of provider will facilitate that the data return processes are carried out without compromising the integrity of the data and with full control of the data controller over its subsequent destination. In this area, the systems that the provider offers to ensure, at all times, the portability of that data are of particular importance.

WHAT I NEED TO KNOW FOR THE HIRING OF ‘CLOUD COMPUTING’ SERVICES  
This guide aims to facilitate compliance with data protection regulations in the hiring of cloud computing services by providing practical information directed at SMEs, microenterprises, and professionals. It also informs about the specific characteristics of contracting in Public Administrations. To this end, the guide is structured around a questionnaire of questions and answers that addresses the essential aspects for the protection of personal data in these services.  

1.- What should I analyze and take into account before hiring ‘cloud computing’ services?  
–– You must evaluate the type of data being processed, considering its greater or lesser sensitivity (for example, merely identifying data is not sensitive data, while health-related data has the highest sensitivity).  
–– You should inform yourself about the types of cloud (private, public, hybrid) and the different service modalities (see the introduction of this guide).  
–– With this information, you must decide which personal data you will hire cloud computing services for and which you prefer to keep in your own information systems. This decision is important because it will delimit the purposes for which the cloud provider may process the data. Consequently, it must be expressly guaranteed that the data will not be used for any purpose unrelated to the contracted services.  

2.- From the perspective of data protection regulations, what is my role as a client of a ‘cloud’ service?  
–– The client hiring cloud computing services remains responsible for the processing of personal data. Even if contracted with a large multinational company, the responsibility does not shift to the service provider, not even by incorporating a clause in the contract for this purpose.  
–– The entity offering cloud computing services is a service provider that, under data protection law, is classified as a ‘data processor’.  

3.- What is the applicable legislation?  
The cloud computing model makes it possible for both service providers and the data stored in the cloud to be located anywhere on the planet. However, in any case:  
–– The client hiring cloud computing services remains responsible for the processing of the data, so the applicable regulations for the client and the service provider are the GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, of April 27, 2016, General Data Protection Regulation) and any other applicable data protection regulations.  
–– The application of this regulation cannot be modified contractually.  
–– Even if you are informed that personal data is dissociated, this does not change the applicable law or the responsibility of the client and the service provider.  

4.- What are my obligations as a client?  
–– You must be diligent in choosing the service provider to ensure they offer sufficient guarantees in compliance with the GDPR and in protecting the rights of the data subjects.  
–– You must formalize a contract with the guarantees indicated in the section “contractual guarantees.”  
–– In particular, you must request and obtain information on whether or not third parties (subcontractors) are involved in the provision of cloud computing services.  
It is common for third-party companies to be involved. If so:  
• You must give your prior written authorization for the participation of third-party companies, at least generically delimiting the services in which they will participate (e.g., in data hosting). For this, the cloud computing service provider must inform you about the types of services that can be subcontracted to third parties.  
• You must be able to know the third-party companies involved (e.g., by being able to access a webpage or through other options provided by the service provider).  
• The cloud provider must assume in the contract that the subcontractors offer legal guarantees for the processing of the data equivalent to those that they themselves assume.
16  
–– The contract you sign must incorporate contractual clauses for the protection of personal data as detailed in the following questions.  
5.- Where may personal data be located? Is its location relevant?  
–– The location of the data is important because the guarantees required for its protection differ depending on the countries in which they are located.  
–– The countries of the European Economic Area provide sufficient guarantees, and it is not legally considered an international transfer of data. The European Economic Area consists of the countries of the European Union and Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway.  
–– If the data is located in countries that do not belong to the European Economic Area, there would be an international transfer of data, in which case, depending on the country in which they are located, adequate legal guarantees must be provided.  
6.- What guarantees are considered adequate for international data transfers?  
–– If they are countries, territories, or specific sectors of activity that have been declared to have an adequate level of protection by the European Commission, international data transfers may be carried out with the only obligation to include them in the register of processing activities (art. 30 GDPR). This includes companies established in the United States that are certified under the EU-U.S. Privacy Shield framework (to see the list of countries with an adequate level of protection, click here).  
–– Otherwise, adequate guarantees must be provided, which can be done through various instruments (click here to learn about them). For these transfers, authorization from the Spanish Data Protection Agency is not required, except for those using contract templates not approved by the Commission or the Agency, which are infrequent, or administrative agreements between authorities or public bodies.  
–– Ask the cloud computing service provider if there are international data transfers and, if so, what guarantees are in place.  

17  
–– When data is located in third countries, it may happen that a competent authority can request and obtain information about the personal data for which the client is responsible. In this case, the client should be informed by the provider of this circumstance (unless prohibited by the law of the third country).  
7.- What security measures are required?  
–– Security measures are essential to ensure the integrity of personal data, prevent unauthorized access, and recover information in the event of security incidents.  
–– The guarantees, security measures, and mechanisms that ensure the protection of personal data will result from conducting a Risk Analysis, and where applicable, from the Impact Assessment provided for in Article 35 of the GDPR.  
–– Furthermore, access to information through communication networks must include a level of security measures equivalent to that of local access.  
–– Ask the cloud computing provider about the security measures they offer and guarantee.  
8.- How can I ensure or verify that security measures are being met?  
–– As a client, you should have the option to verify the security measures, including records that allow you to know who has accessed the data for which you are responsible. The provider must provide you with all the necessary information to demonstrate compliance with the security measures.  
–– The cloud computing provider certifies that they have adequate security certification.  
–– It may be agreed that an independent third party audits the security. In this case, the auditing entity and the recognized standards it will apply must be known.  
–– Request information from the cloud provider on how the security measures will be audited.
18  
–– The client must be informed without undue delay by the cloud provider about security incidents affecting the data for which the client is responsible, as well as the measures taken to resolve them or the measures that the client must take to prevent any damage that may occur (e.g., informing their own clients on how to protect their personal information). Do not forget that the GDPR requires the data controller to notify these security breaches to the supervisory authority, as well as to the data subjects when they pose a high risk to their rights and freedoms.  
–– The encryption of personal data is a measure that should be viewed positively. Request information from the cloud provider regarding the encryption of the data.  

9.- What commitments regarding the confidentiality of personal data should I require?  
–– The cloud service provider must commit to ensuring confidentiality by using the data only for the contracted services.  
–– It must also ensure that the personnel authorized to process personal data have signed confidentiality commitments or are subject to statutory confidentiality obligations.  

10.- How do I ensure that I can recover the personal data for which I am responsible (portability)?  
–– Portability means that the provider must commit, when the contract can be resolved or upon termination of the service, to deliver all information to the client in the agreed format, so that the client can store it in their own systems or choose to transfer it to those of a new provider in a format that allows its use, within the shortest possible time, with full guarantee of the integrity of the information and without incurring additional costs.  
–– In particular, the client must have the option to demand the portability of the information to their own information systems or to a new cloud provider when they consider the intervention of any subcontractor or the transfer of data to countries that they deem do not provide adequate guarantees to be inappropriate.  
–– It is also particularly important in cases where the cloud provider unilaterally modifies the conditions of service provision given its negotiating power over the client.  

19  
–– Request information and guarantees from the provider regarding the portability of personal data.  
–– Note that this portability is independent of the right to data portability that the data subject has, as specified in Article 20 of the GDPR.  

11.- How can I ensure that the cloud provider does not retain personal data if the contract is terminated?  
–– Mechanisms must be established to guarantee the secure deletion of data when requested by the client and, in any case, upon termination of the contract. (An appropriate mechanism is to require a destruction certification issued by the cloud computing provider or by a third party).  

12.- How can I guarantee the exercise of the rights recognized in Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR (access, rectification, erasure, restriction of processing, portability, objection, and automated individual decision-making)?  
The cloud computing client, as the data controller, must allow the exercise of the aforementioned rights by the data subjects.  
–– To this end, the cloud provider must ensure its assistance to the controller and the appropriate tools to facilitate the exercise of these rights.  
–– Inquire about the conditions that the provider offers to comply with this duty of cooperation to ensure the exercise of these rights.  

CLOUD COMPUTING IN PUBLIC ADMINISTRATIONS  
What specific features does cloud computing present in Public Administrations?  
The volume and sensitivity of the data managed by Public Administrations entail specific risks that must be subject to rigorous analysis in each scenario where its use is considered. These specific risks advise the adoption of additional precautions in its implementation, so that the rights and security of citizens are not compromised.
20  
The possibility of processing data outside the national territory, a characteristic of cloud computing, constitutes an element of special relevance in the case of Public Administrations. In this regard, it should be noted that the regulations governing international data transfers apply to both public and private entities (more information can be found in question number 6 of this guide).  
In particular, it must be kept in mind that competent authorities from third countries where personal data is processed within the framework of cloud computing services could request and access information for which public administrations are responsible, in some cases, without being informed of this circumstance. Therefore, it is essential to obtain information from the cloud computing service provider regarding whether this possibility exists in any of the countries where the data will be processed, whether the contracting Administration can know such requests, as well as the decisions it can make in this regard. The significance of these potential accesses is a very relevant factor in deciding on the contracting of these services and the provider that will offer them.  
In addition to data protection regulations, Public Administrations are subject to a specific legal framework to which the provision of cloud computing-based services must be adapted:  
–– Law 9/2017, of November 8, on Public Sector Contracts (Twenty-fifth additional provision), although references to Organic Law 15/1999 should be understood as referring to the GDPR.  
–– Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations, which incorporates, with the necessary adaptations, the rules previously contained in Law 11/2007, of June 22, on Electronic Access of Citizens to Public Services, and some of those provided for in Royal Decree 1671/2009, of November 6, which partially developed this law.  
–– The National Security Framework (ENS) and the National Interoperability Framework (ENI) (Royal Decrees 3/2010 and 4/2010, of January 8).  
What aspects should be considered when contracting ‘cloud computing’ services?  
–– The obligation of diligence in the selection of a data processor referred to in the section on “contractual guarantees” must be particularly reiterated, incorporating the corresponding provisions in the specifications.  

21  
–– The contracting by Public Administrations of cloud computing services that involve the processing of personal data requires the formalization of a written contract under the terms provided in Article 28 of the GDPR.  
–– These requirements can be summarized as follows:  
• The cloud computing service provider will be considered a data processor and will only process the data following the documented instructions of the data controller.  
• The processing will be governed by a contract that establishes the object, nature, and purpose of the processing, as well as the type of personal data, categories of data subjects, and rights and obligations of the data controller.  
• At the end of the contractual provision, the personal data must be destroyed or returned to the contracting entity responsible, or to the data processor designated by it. (More information on the return and destruction of data can be found in questions number 8 and 9).  
–– Regarding the possible subcontracting of services by the data processor (the cloud computing service provider), this will only be possible if the following requirements are met:  
• That such processing has been specified in the contract signed by the contracting administration and the cloud computing service provider. (For information on how to comply with this obligation, refer to question number 4).  
• That the processing of personal data complies with the instructions of the administration acting as the data controller. (For information on how to comply with this obligation, refer to questions number 5 and 6).  
• That the service provider acting as the data processor and the third party formalize the contract under the terms provided in Article 28 of the GDPR. In this contract, the same data protection obligations stipulated in the contract between the responsible administration and the processor will be imposed on the third party.  
–– Contracts for the provision of cloud computing services must specify the technical and organizational measures that the service provider intends to implement to ensure an appropriate level of data security relative to the risk. Likewise, the special requirements for availability, confidentiality, and integrity that certain
22  
Electronic services provided by Public Administrations (for example, in the case of electronic headquarters) must be reflected in the contract through a Service Level Agreement (SLA) that specifies the quality of service indicators to be measured and the minimum acceptable values for them. (For information on how to comply with this obligation, refer to questions number 7 and 8).  
–– In accordance with Article 62 of Law 9/2017, of November 8, on Public Sector Contracts, contracting authorities must designate a contract manager to ensure the proper execution of the agreed service. However, the designation of this figure does not modify the regime of obligations and responsibilities to which the data controller is subject in terms of personal data protection.  
What security elements are particularly relevant in a 'cloud' environment?  
–– Data controllers must conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) prior to the implementation of those processing activities that are likely to pose a high risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  
–– In particular, a DPIA will be required when the processing involves the creation of profiles on which decisions are made with legal effects, when sensitive data is processed on a large scale, or when systematic large-scale observation of a publicly accessible area is conducted.  
–– For its part, the implementation of cloud computing services particularly affects some elements of the National Security Framework as follows:  
•  
• Risk analysis and management. The migration of services and applications to cloud computing environments must be subject to a risk analysis that, depending on the sensitivity of the data and the level of threats, first determines the appropriateness of this solution and, if affirmative, the controls and safeguards that must be implemented to mitigate the risks to a level that can be considered acceptable.  
•  
• Professionalism. This entails the need for, in any modality of cloud service provision, security to be attended to, reviewed, and audited by qualified personnel.  
•  
• Protection of stored and in-transit information. Due to the special sensitivity of the data processed by Public Administrations, the application of robust encryption techniques to both in-transit and stored data is a necessary measure to ensure its confidentiality. Furthermore, the cloud service provision contract must include the performance of backup copies of the information in such a way as to guarantee the full availability and integrity of the stored data.  
•  
• Security incidents and business continuity. The adoption of service models based on cloud computing must contemplate adequate management of security incidents and mechanisms that ensure the continuity of operations in the event of disasters or severe incidents.  
•  
• Security audit. The contracting of cloud computing services requires ensuring the performance of the ordinary and extraordinary security audits provided for in Article 34 of the ENS.  
This article requires specific requirements regarding the criteria, working methods, and conduct used; the aspects that the audit must determine; and the methodological criteria employed.  
It also addresses the recipients of the audit and the conclusions that must be drawn from it.  
Therefore, the Administration contracting cloud computing services must obtain information on how to comply with these obligations. (Basic information on this obligation can be found in question number 7).

GLOSSARY  
IaaS: Infrastructure as a Service. When the cloud provider supplies its clients with processing and storage resources over the network, without any other added value.  
SaaS: Software as a Service. When the cloud provider supplies the client with applications that implement the processes of their business.  
PaaS: Platform as a Service. When the cloud provider supplies the client with the necessary tools to develop their applications in the cloud.  
Portability: the ability for a contractor's data that is on the cloud provider's servers to be transferred to another provider (or to local systems) at the contractor's choice and without loss of data or service. It should not be confused with the right to data portability that data subjects may exercise under Article 20 of the GDPR.  
Location: the specific geographical point where the data is located or where the process is carried out in a cloud computing service.
