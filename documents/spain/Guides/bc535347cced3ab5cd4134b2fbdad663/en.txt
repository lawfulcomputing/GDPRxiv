1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 

This inspection plan originates from the action numbered 1.3.2 of the Strategic Plan 2015-2019 of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, titled “official plan for the social and health sector,” and its main objective is to analyze the data processing carried out in this area and investigate its compliance with data protection regulations. 

The plan began in June 2018, initially perceiving as a fundamental issue to resolve the determination of the scope of the social and health space to be studied, as well as the definition of the inspection actions to be undertaken and their planning. 

It should be noted that, after a preliminary analysis phase, it was concluded that the social and health sector as such does not exist in Spain, as this would require the existence of regulatory legislation as well as governance structures that promote and exercise its direction, administration, and control. 

Undoubtedly, the social and health sectors do exist, with the intersection of both being the existence of a social and health space where curative assistance is coordinated with social assistance, providing comprehensive care to users who simultaneously present chronic illnesses and social deficiencies. 

This social and health care is materialized in social and health centers that provide transversal and multidisciplinary services, both social and health-related, attempting to serve certain previously defined groups in a more effective and personalized manner, according to their specific needs. 

In this report, after the introduction and the description of objectives (chapters 2 and 3), the concept and the state of the social and health space in Spain are developed, presenting the findings in this regard, which are the subject of chapters 4 and 5 (4. Concept of Social and Health Care, and 5. Situation of the Social and Health Space in Spain – Scenario). 

Chapter 6 provides an overview of the methodology used and the actions carried out in this plan: 

• During the planning phase, the involved actors were identified, and a preliminary study was conducted consisting of a documentary investigation through the Internet, in which reports and data related to the social and health space were analyzed, as well as other technical information about social and health services, initiatives undertaken, studies conducted by other organizations, and data from social and health centers. 

Additionally, in this preliminary analysis phase, a meeting was held with IMSERSO, and a workshop was developed in collaboration with a private association, during which concerns regarding the specific issues of personal data processing carried out in nursing homes and social and health centers were collected.
4

• In a second phase, information requests were sent to Autonomous Communities and the Ministry of Health, Consumption and Social Welfare, with the aim of gathering information on the situation of social and health services as well as the data protection guarantees implemented.

• In September 2018, on-site inspections began in selected centers where social and health services are provided with direct attention to users. State public centers, medium-stay hospitals in an autonomous community, specialized Alzheimer residences from municipalities, and private social and health centers for mental health patients were chosen.

With these specifically selected inspections, different social and health centers have been audited, both for the different services they provide and for their state, regional, and local scope. Additionally, there are public and private centers, including large, medium-sized, and more modest ones.

These on-site inspections concluded at the end of December 2018.

It is noteworthy that the plan began one month after the full implementation of the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), but not the new Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD), which was published a few days before the end of the last inspection carried out.

However, the conclusions developed in Chapter 7 of this report reflect the results of the analyses carried out, both regarding compliance with the GDPR and the new LOPDGDD, and recommend improvement actions, always with a view to raising awareness and advising both public bodies and the companies and institutions that own social and health centers, emphasizing actions that may be necessary for the correct application of both regulations.

Given the large number of recommendations obtained, it is estimated that this plan has been very cost-effective, considering the small team that participated in it and the time consumed. Precisely because of the extensive number of recommendations, a ten-point summary has also been drafted, compiling the most relevant ones, in order to facilitate reading and increase the accessibility of the results in social and health care.

Also, for the same reasons and in order to make the plan more user-friendly, a group of frequently asked questions has been developed with the doubts collected and raised throughout its execution.

It should be noted that in the context of this Plan and the pandemic caused by COVID-19, no complaints or inquiries have been received, but given the importance of the pandemic, it has been deemed appropriate to include in this report an annex on COVID-19, which includes links to the resources generated by the Agency in relation to it. Although elderly residences have not been studied generically in this plan, centers with vulnerable users due to COVID-19, including the elderly, have been inspected.

Finally, this report addresses a section on future challenges that outlines the possible next steps to be taken and proposals for actions, both due to the time limitations of this plan and the trends detected during its execution.

2. INTRODUCTION

The increase in life expectancy and low birth rates have caused the accelerated growth of the percentage of elderly people in Spain, with the number of citizens over 65 years old doubling in less than 30 years. This has led to an increase in the chronicity of pathologies and a decrease in the functional capacity of older individuals, generating a growing demand for social and healthcare services.

On the other hand, social changes have created more complicated situations for the family care of elderly individuals, as well as increasing expectations for support to fall more on social and healthcare services.

These new needs arising from social, demographic, and epidemiological changes are being addressed by administrations through the design of socio-health spaces that particularly focus on the comprehensive and multidisciplinary care of the needs of certain groups such as elderly patients, as well as the disabled, both typically in a state of dependency, or certain chronic patients.

The emerging charter of socio-health services offered and the special characteristics of the data processed (socio-economic and health-related) led to the consideration, within the Strategic Plan 2015-2019 of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, of a plan regarding the socio-health space, within the legally attributed competencies, in compliance with its preventive function, promoting awareness among those responsible for and in charge of data processing about the obligations that fall upon them under data protection regulations.

It should not be forgotten that the processing of this type of data is protected by the safeguards that the new European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) grants to "special categories of data." Therefore, the role of those responsible for and in charge of data processing will be very relevant in fulfilling their duties.

3. OBJECTIVES

The recent implementation of the GDPR has resulted in a profound transformation of data protection regulations, significantly affecting the policies of organizations, which must increase their proactivity and base their actions more on risks. This plan aims to assess the exposure to risk of data processing activities in socio-health services, in relation to the rights and freedoms of individuals, as well as the guarantees that must be implemented regarding the

protection of personal data, collecting evidence and weighing its impact to determine compliance and maintenance of these guarantees.

The first objective has been to detect the processing and flows of personal data carried out within the framework of social and health services, as well as their possible risks and the guarantees established to mitigate them, analyzing the level of compliance with the new data protection regulations. The identification of deficiencies and possible improvement actions has the ultimate goal of issuing recommendations to the involved actors that contribute to raising the level of compliance and thus improving citizens' trust.

Finally, it is also the intention of the conclusions and recommendations presented to allow actors a clearer understanding and application of the new regulations, attempting to resolve doubts and errors regarding the non-compliances that have been detected, always within the temporal limitations under which this plan has necessarily been carried out.

4. CONCEPT OF SOCIAL AND HEALTHCARE ATTENTION

Article 14 of Law 16/2003, of May 28, on Cohesion and Quality of the National Health System defines social and healthcare attention as follows:
“Article 14. Provision of social and healthcare attention.
1. Social and healthcare attention encompasses the set of care aimed at those patients, generally chronic, who due to their special characteristics can benefit from the simultaneous and synergistic action of health and social services to increase their autonomy, alleviate their limitations or suffering, and facilitate their social reintegration.
2. In the health field, social and healthcare attention will be carried out at the levels of care determined by each autonomous community and in any case will include:
a) Long-term health care.
b) Healthcare during convalescence.
c) Rehabilitation in patients with recoverable functional deficits.
3. The continuity of service will be guaranteed by health and social services through adequate coordination among the corresponding public administrations.”

Although this concept has evolved over time, it can be stated that social and healthcare attention is that which coordinates health and social assistance focusing on particularly vulnerable groups such as, among others, the elderly, chronic patients, people with some physical, mental, or sensory disability, and those at risk of social exclusion. 
Social and healthcare care involves comprehensive and interdisciplinary attention that must be directed to groups of people where a simultaneous need for health and social care is identified. Therefore, social and healthcare attention is provided to groups that combine the presence of illness with various situations of dependency and social deficiencies, with the prior identification of the profiles of the target individuals of the services being of special importance in the design of social and healthcare attention.

In the social and healthcare space, typical services include:
• healthcare during convalescence after an acute illness treatment, and palliative care,
• rehabilitation (physiotherapy and occupational therapy) for patients with functional deficits,
• care for elderly individuals with some illness, dependency, or social risk,
• attention to advanced mental or neurological illnesses,
• long-term health care, both at the primary and specialized levels, with continuous medical and nursing supervision.

These services are primarily provided in:
• medium-stay hospitals specialized in rehabilitation and/or geriatric patients,
• centers or care complexes for patients with psychiatric conditions,
• geriatric residences, especially those specialized in psychogeriatrics or diseases such as Alzheimer’s.

Social and healthcare centers, when necessary, utilize the health services of the Health System, specifically in cases of medical emergencies and acute processes that require the means of a hospital center, with the necessary mechanisms for interconsultations and hospital referrals being articulated from the social and healthcare service. 
It should be noted that health promotion programs, quality of life improvement, or disease or functional deterioration prevention are also important within the social and healthcare space. 
What is undeniable is that the concept of social and healthcare attention entails a new paradigm in the provision of services, which seeks to comprehensively and transversally address the growing expectations of patients regarding quality and effectiveness, focusing more on people and their needs. 
The greater effectiveness of services also involves the deinstitutionalization of users, keeping them in their environment when possible, enhancing home care as well as medical and pharmaceutical attention in the environments.

5. SITUATION OF THE SOCIO-SANITARY SPACE IN SPAIN - SCENARIO

The System for Autonomy and Attention to Dependency (SAAD) is the set of services and economic benefits aimed at promoting personal autonomy, attention, and protection for people in a situation of dependency, through duly accredited public and private contracted services.

It is articulated in Law 39/2006 of December 14, on the Promotion of Personal Autonomy and Attention to Persons in a Situation of Dependency, which in its article 1.2 establishes that “the System for Autonomy and Attention to Dependency will respond to a coordinated and cooperative action of the General Administration of the State and the Autonomous Communities, which will contemplate measures in all areas affecting persons in a situation of dependency, with the participation, where appropriate, of Local Entities.” Thus, the social service centers of the Autonomous Communities and Local Entities are part of the SAAD, for example, residences for the elderly or day centers, and the State Reference Centers dependent on IMSERSO. Private centers, duly accredited according to article 16.1 of the aforementioned Law, are also integrated into the SAAD. 

The social and health competencies belong to the Autonomous Communities, making them the main actors. However, Law 7/1985, of April 2, Regulating the Bases of Local Regime, which regulates the competencies of municipalities, recognizes the competence of Municipalities to provide social services, promotion, and social reintegration, establishing that municipalities with more than 20,000 inhabitants are obliged to provide social services, without specifying which ones, nor the beneficiaries or recipients of these services. 

To channel coordination in social services between the Autonomous Communities and the Municipalities, mechanisms such as Interadministrative Councils are implemented, and collaboration agreements are signed between Autonomous Communities and Municipalities to articulate the provided attention.

Currently, it can be stated that there are notable contrasts in the number of socio-health services provided by Autonomous Communities. There are also differences even in their definition, due to the various interpretations of the term socio-health adopted in each case.  
Several Autonomous Communities have defined socio-health care plans, through which some health and social policies are directed towards comprehensive socio-health care, although these plans are usually aimed at very specific groups, with certain conditions, and typically establish a specific and limited number of places.  
In some cases, joint action protocols have been signed between health and social administrations, with some pilot tests underway, or protocols regarding socio-health coordination between the corresponding Departments of health and social services.  
Occasionally, specific socio-health care agreements are signed for certain services, between the heads of social services and health services of the autonomous community, with the Department of Health responsible for the monitoring, evaluation, and control of the health activities carried out in socio-health services, and the Department of Social Affairs responsible for the monitoring, evaluation, and control of the corresponding social activities.  
Other Autonomous Communities have indicated that they continue to independently manage matters related to social services separate from health competencies, lacking a socio-health space as such for providing services to citizens.  
Specifically, some Departments of Social Affairs have informed this Agency that the concept "socio-health" does not exist in the realm of social services in their Community: the Centers and Social Action Services must attend to "appropriate social and health care," but if a user requires, in addition to social care, health care, the entity responsible for the Center must guarantee it through its own means, if it has health professionals on its staff, or through external means by referring the user to the corresponding health center or local hospital, without the Social Administration being competent to evaluate or inspect such health services.  
That is to say, some Departments report that there are social devices, with varying degrees of health care, and health services, with varying degrees of attention to dependency. Thus, centers for disability or dependency care, regardless of the origin (age, mental disorder, autism, degenerative disease, etc.), whose main objective is to cover the need for assistance or social or labor integration, are social centers. If the objective is rehabilitation or ongoing health care, they are health centers.  
However, in general, they also report the existence of experimental and pilot initiatives, such as projects for tenders for a certain number of residential public service places aimed at dependent elderly individuals with a socio-health profile.  
Additionally, former psychiatric centers, where health and social-type services coexist, the latter aimed at the care of individuals with long years of hospitalization, are usually considered socio-health centers.
10

Ultimately, the existing models of social and health care tend to be based on the expansion of coverage of the benefits of the current social and health systems, with the aim of addressing all the needs of individuals in these specific groups who require synergistic intervention of social and health care, but without the creation of a social health system as such, rather by implementing mechanisms for coordination and relationship between the social and health systems. In conclusion, within the social health space of an Autonomous Community, the following types of services could be considered social health services:
• Social-origin social health centers, with health care based on the needs of dependent individuals,
• Health-origin social health centers, with attention to the dependency of patients,
• Psychiatric centers,
• Social health places integrated within pure social or health centers, as a specific modality of "beds." Typically, this involves a limited number of places with comprehensive social health care that are offered through public tender with specific objectives and sometimes as a pilot project. These centers must meet the operational authorization criteria for health centers and social centers, the former issued by the corresponding Health Department and the latter by the Department of Social Affairs.

LOCAL ADMINISTRATIONS. At the local level, some local corporations have created autonomous local bodies in the form of Institutes for the implementation, within their area of action, of activities for the promotion, provision, and management of social health resources and services, teaching, research, and promotion of social health care. There are also social health centers owned by local corporations. Elderly residences have been found that, as social centers, also provide specialized health care for specific ailments such as Alzheimer's, based on the needs of individuals, who benefit from the synergistic action of both types of care. The management of social health services by local entities is carried out through the signing of collaboration agreements with the Autonomous Communities.

AT THE STATE LEVEL. The Ministry of Health, Social Policy, and Equality published in December 2011 the White Paper on Social Health Coordination in Spain, which concluded that, at that time, there were numerous plans and programs, although very rarely did they translate into organizational structural changes or the creation of shared mixed structures in the intermediate space between both sectors. IMSERSO, the managing entity of Social Security attached to the Ministry of Health, Social Services, and Equality, has been assigned management competencies for plans,

State-level programs and services for elderly people and individuals with dependency, in addition to the social benefits and programs concerning Ceuta and Melilla. The following State Reference Centers are owned by IMSERSO:
• Centers for Recovery for People with Physical Disabilities (CRMF).
• Centers for Attention for People with Physical Disabilities (CAMF).
• State Reference Centers (CREs), integrated into the network of Services of the System for Autonomy and Attention to Dependency (SAAD), with the objectives, among others, of ensuring inter-territorial balance and promoting equalization policies.

IMSERSO has a total of 16 Centers of these characteristics in Spanish territory, with six CRMF, five CAMF, two CRE, and one CEAPAT, CEADAC, and CREER.
Some of these centers provide socio-health services to users, within the framework of the competencies reserved at the state level for IMSERSO following the transfer of social competencies to the Autonomous Communities: while the competencies for direct attention to users correspond to the Autonomous Communities, some IMSERSO Reference Centers also provide direct socio-health care to users to properly fulfill their designated reference mission, in order to technically assist, advise, inform, train, research, and evaluate in relation to their specialty, based on the experiences acquired during user care.

PRIVATE CENTERS. The private initiative should not be overlooked. There are private socio-health centers owned by Associations, Foundations, and Religious Congregations, which offer specialized services according to the individual needs of users, ranging from psycho-geriatric care or attention to neurological diseases in general, to rehabilitation, convalescence, and palliative care.
The management of socio-health services by private entities can be purely private, but it is common to find the subscription of public service management contracts, under the modality of agreements and concessions, regulated in the public sector contracting regulations. In this indirect management, the Public Administration will be responsible for guaranteeing the right to benefits and for exercising the inspection authority to ensure proper compliance.
In indirect management, the Public Administration will generally be responsible for the treatments, governing the relationship by a contract or other legal act in accordance with Union Law or the laws of the Member States that binds the processor with the controller, which establishes the object and duration of the treatment, the nature and purposes of the treatment, the type of personal data, and the categories of data subjects, among other aspects.

CONCLUSION
In summary, it is observed that in Spain there are disparate socio-health experiences, emerging, sometimes very local and particular, and generally with little visibility, although they constitute a trend.

To be able to speak of the existence of a socio-health sector in Spain as such, it would be necessary to develop regulatory legislation, governance structures, consensus technical documents, assistance programs, and resources. This is even inferred from the aforementioned white paper on socio-health coordination in Spain by the Ministry of Health, Social Policy, and Equality.
Therefore, it is preferable to speak, rather than of a socio-health sector, of the so-called socio-health space, where social and health services are coordinated to provide comprehensive - not fragmented - care to users who simultaneously present chronic diseases and dependency, with transversal and multidisciplinary services that attend to the individual more effectively and according to their specific needs.
It should be noted that there is an Observatory of Integrated Health Models (OMIS http://omis.newhealthfoundation.org/) that has been established to give visibility and dissemination to all initiatives being promoted in our country regarding health, social care, and family and community support to improve quality in processes of advanced chronic illness, high dependency, and the final stages of life. It is part of the NewHealth Foundation and is a non-profit technical observatory that seeks, among other things, to identify experiences of coordination between the social and health sectors, as well as to promote synergies between both sectors.

6. METHODOLOGY AND ACTIONS CARRIED OUT IN THE PRESENT PLAN

The working methodology has been structured around the inspection powers of the Agency, which specifically allow for investigative mechanisms such as information requests and on-site inspections.

DEVELOPMENT OF THE PLAN:

1. Audit planning: in this phase, the objectives, content, scope, phases, and planned schedule have been determined; during this phase, the involved actors and the preliminary study have also been identified. This preliminary study began in June 2018.

The preliminary study phase has consisted of the search and analysis of existing documentation on the Internet, with the basic objective of studying the concept of social and health care, as well as the state of social and health services in Spain. 7. CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

The following conclusions are grouped by significant sections, detailing both the deficiencies detected and aspects that are susceptible to improvement. In each section, the proposed recommendations for correction or improvement are included.

7.1. TYPOLOGY OF THE DATA PROCESSED

• It has been confirmed that the following types of personal data are processed in the audited centers:
o Basic personal data and banking data.
o Health data, including Clinical History, diagnoses, treatments, analyses, assessment scales, diagnostic tests, and clinical evolution, medical-pharmaceutical prescriptions, etc.
o Social History: social reports, socio-family information and environment, personal economic situation, support networks (family, neighborhood, public professionals, institutions), social intervention programs.
o Psychopedagogical reports.
o Autonomy assessment data, diagnoses, tests used, skills inventory, assessment scales, therapeutic intervention programs.
o Incident records (falls, daily events of special relevance, behavioral alterations, etc.) occurring during the provision of the service.
o Coexistence contract signed by the user or their legal representative and by the Center.
o Personalized/Individualized Care Plan (PAP/PAI) of the user.
o Contact details of family members or responsible persons for the user.

RECOMMENDATIONS:

• It should be noted that most of this information constitutes personal data of special categories (medical diagnosis, provision of assistance or treatment of a health or social nature, or management of health and social care systems and services), so specific guarantees must be followed, establishing appropriate measures to protect the rights of users and, in particular, ensuring that treatments are carried out by professionals subject to professional secrecy or under their responsibility.

• In this area, the processing of data must observe the principle of data minimization, ensuring that they are adequate, relevant, and limited to the intended purpose, applying the technical and organizational measures that guarantee this.

7.2. SOCIAL AND HEALTH HISTORY AND DOCUMENTATION

• Despite the announced initiatives regarding the creation of a social and health history that integrates, at the state or Autonomous Community level, the social and health histories for each citizen, it can currently only be stated that the social and health history is integrated at the level of the social and health care center. Integrated social and health histories have been found in all audited centers, with each center being responsible for maintaining the data of the users to whom it provides services.

Partial integration of the social and health history at the level of the Autonomous Community has also been found in some cases.

• It has been found that all centers, to a greater or lesser extent, continue to use paper files or paper social and health histories, in addition to the electronic history in the information system.

It is common to store in paper format medical reports provided by the patient or by family members, in addition to other documents such as admission requests, the health card or its copy, the ID card, among others.
16

• Occasionally, paper records are found fragmented, with each part dispersed across different departments of the center. Some centers allow doctors the freedom to decide which documents to keep for each patient, which they store in their own files, while other fragments of the record are kept in nursing, administration, rehabilitation, or even temporarily at the reception of the center.

It is also common practice to maintain multiple paper records for the same user, one in the unit or floor where the patient is treated and another in admissions, containing different documentation. Additionally, there are exclusive nursing records, usually kept in the various nursing stations of the center, which contain information such as the medication to be administered or a record of nursing actions.

• It has been found that the admissions or administration department of the centers typically handles and safeguards medical reports due to the need to send them to courts or other agencies.

• In all cases, the fragments of the paper socio-health history are gathered when the patient leaves the center, being compiled into a single record for safekeeping.

• In some cases, it was noted that the distribution of paper records and the various documents that comprise them was done in open envelopes. It should be noted that all centers store the records in open envelopes due to the need for continuous consultation of the same, although they are kept in places such as nursing stations, which are restricted access areas for professionals.

• Regarding the general documentation files of the inspected centers, where the histories of users who have left the center are stored, they have always been found in locked rooms. Not all of these files had an access log for the documents, and only some were equipped with an alarm system.

RECOMMENDATIONS:

• The dispersion of documents across different departments, with the existence of fragmented or duplicated records, poses risks to the integrity of the documentation, making it more difficult to update information, as well as to combine data from different sources.

In the event of modification or annotation of specific information in one location, it may happen that it is not updated in the other locations, resulting in data inconsistency. Also, since not all information is found in a single place, there may be availability issues when trying to locate documents, creating situations such as, for example, not even knowing if a particular medical report exists and, therefore, not taking it into consideration.
17

On the other hand, a greater number of locations implies higher security risks, which generates vulnerabilities in custody. Having multiple locations increases the risk of unauthorized access. It also complicates security controls, which must be multiplied. It should be noted that, while it is easy to collect the footprint of access to the information system, recording that same footprint in a paper file is more complicated to implement and control.

• These islands of information are therefore considered inadvisable from the perspective of personal data protection. The use of different locations should ensure the integrity and security of the information, so that additional locations are guaranteed a level of security equivalent to that of the main location.

Regarding this issue, it should be indicated that a fragmented medical record may not be considered unique by institution as advocated in Law 41/2002, of November 14, which regulates the autonomy of the patient and rights and obligations in the field of information and clinical documentation (hereinafter LAP), which states that "The medical record shall be maintained with criteria of unity and integration, in each healthcare institution at a minimum," meaning there is one per center, not per department.

A possible solution, depending on the available resources, involves the total digitization of the socio-health history of the center, in such a way as to avoid temporary paper storage, accessing the digitized records according to the needs of the different professionals. While the electronic socio-health record has been found to be operational in all inspected centers, there has also been detected an inertia with the use of paper that may be justified in specific and concrete cases, but never in a generalized manner, as it leads to the problems outlined.

• As long as the entire socio-health history is not maintained in a digitized format, a procedure for managing paper documentation must be established that includes the registration of accesses, so that it can be known which documents are accessed or loaned and by whom.

In addition, the procedure will contemplate the adoption of secure storage systems, with locks, and effective access controls to the rooms containing socio-health documentation. If it is impossible to lock those rooms or containers, continuous staff presence or video surveillance or alarm systems should be implemented, which constitute alternative and reinforcing measures.

• Secure procedures must also be created for the transfer of socio-health documentation in such a way as to prevent unauthorized persons from accessing documents in transit.

• The documents that are in the possession of professionals, while they are not permanently stored due to treating the user, must be safeguarded by those professionals, for which the center must inform the workers of this obligation, including the need to prevent unauthorized access by third parties, being advisable that such duty is specifically included in the confidentiality commitments signed by the employees.

Likewise, mechanisms for verifying the integrity of the paper socio-health history must be established.

• The establishment of a clean desk policy is recommended to prevent unauthorized persons from accessing documents with personal data. If this policy is impossible, other types of measures should be adopted to prevent third parties from accessing personal information.

• There must be a secure destruction procedure for documentation, using closed containers or paper shredders.

7.3. DATA SHARING AMONG DIFFERENT PROFESSIONALS. DATA ACCESS PROFILES

• In socio-health centers, various profiles of healthcare professionals provide services: doctors, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, psychologists, psychopedagogues, non-graduate care staff (assistants), as well as other management or support staff, and even spiritual advisors (in religious centers). All of them coordinate their efforts to try to provide comprehensive and personalized care to the patient.

To achieve this socio-health coordination, it is necessary for the different professionals to share certain patient data to assess their condition and outline personalized treatment plans. Each type of professional attending to the patient makes their assessments, generating or processing data related to disabilities or health problems (clinical assessments), functional problems (functional assessments), psycho-affective issues (mental assessments), and socio-family issues (social assessments).

These particularly sensitive data, as indicated, are considered special category personal data.

• In the inspections carried out, it has been found that normally each type of professional fills out a specific form or report independently from the other professionals, generally in the Information System, which gathers them. However, it has been found that in some centers, each professional can decide which data is shared with the other professionals. In contrast, in other centers, all data is shared by all professionals, or the Information System itself decides which data is shared, with access configurations typically being very broad.
19

• On the other hand, in all the audited socio-healthcare centers, interdisciplinary meetings are held to coordinate care and follow up on patients. These meetings involve the various professionals who attend to the patients, and they openly discuss each case, sharing all necessary health and social data. Given the complexity of the cases addressed (socio-healthcare services are directed at patients with significant needs), it is essential to approach them from a comprehensive perspective.

As an illustrative example of the need to share data, if a patient with Alzheimer’s or a mental illness behaves strangely, for instance, shouting without apparent reason or known cause, the source of their discomfort may be physical, psychological, or social. Therefore, to provide the necessary attention, it is crucial to consider all possible information.

In the interdisciplinary meetings, personalized objectives for the patients are set, and specific tasks are assigned to be carried out by the different professionals at the center, with follow-ups on these activities also being conducted.

• The individualized care plans that contain the personalized objectives are also communicated and agreed upon with the patients themselves and/or authorized family members, as they must participate in some activities. For this purpose, interviews with the patient's family are arranged.

• While it is common to find differentiated access profiles to the Information System for each professional group, data sharing is general and very broad, revealing an insufficient application of technical and organizational measures that integrate data protection guarantees from the design stage and by default. There tends to be a tendency to grant all professionals broad access privileges to the data to avoid specific access problems to the information of patients that is needed to provide them with the necessary socio-healthcare attention.

Thus, centers have been found that allow total access, in read mode, to all data of all patients, although as a general rule, each system user can only modify the data they themselves have entered or those corresponding to their professional category. Usually, except in a few cases, access is not limited by floors or care groups, with all workers having access to the data of all users at the center.

Moreover, during the inspections carried out, it was evident in several centers that the representatives of the centers were not fully aware of the scope of the access profiles of the different professionals, discovering during the inspection the broad profiles enjoyed by the various professionals.

In some cases, the reason given for this was the complexity of the application. In other cases, it was simply unknown that almost all professionals had complete access to all user information.
20

Also, in the centers where there are differentiated access profiles for different professionals, the differences tend to be limited to a few categories of data, with all or nearly all patient information being generally shared. In some cases, more elaborate profiles were found, where there are access limitations such as:
- Doctors have almost total access.
- Therapists do not access the medical reports provided by the center's users. They do have access to those prepared by the coordinators, to the treatment guidelines recorded, and to the follow-ups they maintain on their own actions.
- Nurses have more access at the follow-up level and to data regarding pharmacological treatment. They can raise alerts to the doctor, who must give an “OK” upon receipt.
- Nursing assistants have much more limited access compared to the nurse profile. Assistants are provided with information related to the care that must be given to the user.
- Social workers access basic data but not health data, with coordination between healthcare personnel and social workers established through alerts containing strictly necessary information transmitted by the Information System. Conversely, notes from social workers can be seen by doctors, psychologists, and coordinators, but not by therapists as they do not need them for their work.
- Dining room staff: consultation access (without the possibility of modifying data) exclusively to certain information about users necessary to administer the correct diet in the correct manner. This information includes data related to allergies, dysphagia, diabetes, etc.
- Quality department staff: only sees the main records of users, without medical data.
- In addition to the social worker profile, some other profiles such as doctor, therapist, and nurse can generate alerts or information about a patient, such as instructions directed to other professionals for the purpose of coordination in the care that must be provided.

• The use of generic users has been detected on occasions, such as a generic user shared by several people working in the dining room, in order to consult data related to the specific needs of a patient in the dining room (including allergies, dysphagias, etc.), or a generic user shared by several employees in positions for viewing images from video surveillance.

RECOMMENDATIONS:

• From all that has been presented, it is evident that it is impossible to provide socio-health services without sharing certain information, which includes special categories of personal data. Therefore, sufficient guarantees must be established regarding the processing of these personal data that allow for appropriate handling, including:
- An effort must be made to create more restrictive and differentiated access profiles for each type of professional, addressing their information needs for the correct development of their work. A clear relationship of users and their profiles with access privileges for socio-health data must be documented, with a review of the mechanisms for granting permissions.
- Profiles must be developed considering privacy by design and by default, structuring the information in such a way that allows for the definition of different accesses and initially granting the minimum necessary access privileges to provide socio-health care, subsequently refining and adjusting other needs based on new requirements that arise.
- The categories of data and the specific data that must be shared between different professional groups must be studied meticulously, allowing access only to necessary information, avoiding the sharing of unnecessary data for each type of professional involved in patient care, and preventing indiscriminate sharing of all data in any case. It would be advisable for the centers, with the involvement of different professionals, to decide what information is relevant to share, all while considering the recipient's profile and their information needs. 
Additionally, the generation of specific alerts or notifications is recommended, which avoids granting total access to information, potentially providing an agile solution by establishing a dynamic flow of data, allowing the recipient's attention to focus on the necessary and most important data. 
In any case, general guidelines for data sharing that are mandatory for all staff should be established, which should preferably be implemented through restrictions or rules embedded in the Information System.
- These guarantees should be reinforced by including in the confidentiality commitment signed by employees, an express prohibition against accessing personal data of users that is not necessary for the performance of their professional duties.
- The use of generic users shared by several people for access to personal data should not be allowed, as it prevents traceability of accesses, diluting responsibilities. This practice increases risks related to unauthorized access and breaches of the duty of confidentiality.

7.4. CONFIDENTIALITY AND OBLIGATIONS OF STAFF

• In all audited centers, employees sign a confidentiality commitment, which is also extended to other groups, such as interns, trainees, and volunteers.
22

• In some cases, the confidentiality commitment specifies the obligation to comply with security measures, which are included in an informational circular that is also signed. The commitment to access users' personal data only when necessary for the provision of the service (without defining the scope of access) and the indefinite nature of the commitment are also stated.

None of the other commitments mention the indefinite nature or the non-cessation of the commitment upon the termination of the contractual relationship. Occasionally, there is a warning about possible liabilities in case of non-compliance.

In some cases, the mandatory observance of security measures and the obligations contained in data protection legislation is mentioned, although they are not signed; instead, these obligations are published for staff access on the center's intranet, leaving it to the employee to access them.

Occasionally, it is claimed that, as an additional measure, training is provided to employees, as well as verbal information on data protection.

RECOMMENDATIONS:

• Given that the processing operations in social health centers involve widespread access to special category data, confidentiality guarantees must be maximized to mitigate risks.

• As already pointed out in section 7.1, according to the provisions of Article 9.3 of the GDPR, the processing referred to in Article 9.2.h) of the GDPR (medical diagnosis, provision of healthcare or social assistance, or management of healthcare and social assistance systems and services) must be carried out by professionals subject to professional secrecy or under their responsibility.

While most professional groups working in social health centers are subject to professional secrecy, this must be complemented by a written confidentiality commitment that must be signed by all employees, interns, students in training, external company staff, and, in general, anyone with access to users' personal data.

This commitment must be communicated to employees by outlining all specific obligations regarding confidentiality, and it is advisable that the document includes:
o The confidentiality commitment itself, which includes the express prohibition of disseminating, communicating, or revealing any personal data of the center's users to third parties.
o The indefinite nature of the commitment, even after the termination of the worker's relationship with the center.
o As mentioned in point 7.3, the express prohibition of accessing users' personal data that is not necessary for the performance of their duties at the center, as well as not using such data for personal interest or that of other third parties.
o The prohibition of making copies of data on any media on their own initiative or reproducing it in any format.
o Information about the consequences and responsibilities arising from the breach of the signed commitment.
Employees without access to personal data, whether internal or external, must also sign a similar document that expressly prohibits access to users' personal data and includes the confidentiality commitment, in case of accidental or occasional access to data due to unforeseen circumstances. When such an incident occurs, necessary measures must be taken to inform the responsible party of the incident.

• It is recommended to provide training and information on the other obligations regarding personal data protection, and it is advisable to deliver, with acknowledgment of receipt, a summary informational document, in clear and understandable language, of the data protection regulations to all mentioned groups (employees, students in training, interns, and even external workers). This can be a document parallel to the confidentiality commitment or integrated into it.

7.5. INFORMATION PROVIDED TO THE USER

• Most of the audited centers lack informational posters in reception areas or other patient access areas.

Outdated posters have been detected, with references to old regulations, and located in interior areas such as the admissions office (out of the users' sight under normal conditions).

Visible and updated informational posters with the GDPR have been found, which constituted a first layer of information along with the informational clauses of the forms used for data collection. However, not all forms from this center included data protection information. They also had brochures available to interested parties upon request, which constituted the second, more detailed layer of information.

Occasionally, unclear information has been found, which is very extensive and complicated to understand, without the use of layers, and without adaptation to the characteristics of the users.

• Admission requests and other data collection documents, such as complaint forms, have been found that do not include informational clauses, or that are incomplete or outdated with respect to the new regulations.
24

While in all cases updated and complete information is provided separately during the user's admission, in the case of admission requests, since the admission occurs after data collection, the information is not provided at the time of data collection, but rather subsequently. Moreover, when the request is denied or archived (if the user does not end up being admitted), the applicant is never properly informed, and yet their data has been processed.

• It has been found as a common practice in most centers to provide detailed information at the time of admission, which is offered for reading and signing by the user or family member in order to be able to certify the information provided. This information is usually extensive, with the signed document remaining at the center.

Additionally, generally, patients in these types of centers, either due to their advanced age or their condition, are admitted directly to the ward, with a family member or companion handling the procedures in the admissions department of the center, and to whom information about the protection of personal data is provided.

On the other hand, this information is specific to the user. However, the family member or guardian is usually not informed about the treatments that are carried out on their own data, which are also collected.

• Outdated informational legends in relation to the GDPR have been found. Different documents with the collection of personal data from the same center have been found, some with updated legends and others not.

Also, in some admission requests, information was provided about treatments that were not carried out, as these were common documents for several centers owned by the same entity where different treatments are performed, so some information did not apply to all centers.

In this same regard, in the case of dependencies of public bodies, as well as service provision contracts, a lack of coordination has been evidenced between the data controller and the data processor (and between the entity owning the center and the center itself) when providing information. For example, inconsistency was detected between the information provided in the informational clauses of the center and that offered in the Treatment Activities Register of the controller.

• In some cases where clauses updated in accordance with the GDPR are found, there is some confusion when informing about the legal basis or lawfulness of the treatments, in cases where this is mentioned, as sometimes it is not mentioned, nor is the existence and contact details of the Data Protection Officer (DPO) provided in most cases.

For example, information regarding legitimacy has been found stating “the fulfillment of a mission carried out in the exercise of public powers conferred to the data controller” according to art. 61.e) GDPR.

There have also been cases where the user's consent is specified as the legal basis, when there is a contract that regulates the relationship between the user and the center.
25

• It has been found, in some cases, that the email address provided to users for exercising their rights does not work, despite this address being published on various sites, including the website. Additionally, in another center, an outdated postal address has been found published for the same purposes, as the responsible entity has not been located at that address for over five years.

• Lastly, as a detail, there is information that does not enumerate the data protection rights available to users, but does outline the procedure for exercising the rights it refers to as "rights."

RECOMMENDATIONS:

• It is recommended to place simple, updated, and easily readable informational posters for users in access areas of the center, such as the reception.

Informative legends should also be included in all data collection forms, so that users are informed at the moment they provide their data. It is recommended that these clauses, along with the posters, constitute a first layer of concise and clear language information, including references or links to more detailed information.

The AEPD's website has available the Guide for Compliance with the Duty to Inform published to assist those responsible in fulfilling the obligation to inform the interested parties.

• Users must always be informed at the moment of data collection and not at the time of admission or any later time, to avoid situations where applicants who ultimately do not occupy or obtain a place remain uninformed.

• The second layer of more detailed information must be accessible to the user at that moment, in a simple and immediate manner, either upon request or through online consultation, with references to information provided later being invalid.

This second layer of information should be able to be consulted by the user immediately following the information of the first layer, so it should be located in close and accessible places from where the first layer information is displayed or provided.

• The use of a single informational document that is signed by the user can accurately and precisely document the information provided, but a copy should always be given to the user for later detailed consultation, as it is unlikely that a detailed and comprehensive reading of the provided text will occur at the time of admission.

• The special characteristics of users in social health centers mean that they may be less aware of the risks, consequences, guarantees, and rights related to the processing of their personal data.

26

Therefore, an effort must be made to ensure that the information provided is accessible to users who, due to their special characteristics, may have their capacities partially or temporarily limited. Information can also be provided through intermediaries such as family members.

For this reason, it is recommended to develop specific clauses for such users, through which the understanding of information regarding the protection of their personal data is facilitated.

In cases of legal incapacitation, their legal representatives will be informed with the assistance of authorized family members or voluntary representatives.

• The clauses of the different documents must be unified, offering correct, updated information in accordance with the GDPR and uniform.

Likewise, only the information applicable to the treatments of each center should be provided, in the event that the responsible entity owns several, making an effort to adapt the clauses to the specific treatments carried out by each center.

Similarly, when there is a data processor, the responsible party must ensure, with the collaboration of the processor, that all data protection information provided to users by both parties is scrupulously correct and consistent with each other.

• Users must be correctly informed about the means to exercise their rights and the designated address for this purpose, conducting tests or controls to ensure the proper functioning of the channels for receiving rights exercises.

• Legal basis  
When analyzing the lawfulness of the treatments and informing the user of the legal bases, the responsible party must consider which legal basis or bases provided for in Article 6.1 of the GDPR support the intended treatment.

When it comes to special categories of data, including health-related data, it must be taken into account that Article 9.1 of the GDPR prohibits their processing, unless one of the exceptions to this prohibition listed in paragraph 2 applies.

Among the circumstances listed in Article 9.2 of the GDPR that exclude the prohibition of processing health data for social health purposes, the following should be highlighted (letters a, c, and h):
o a) the data subject has given explicit consent for the processing of such personal data for one or more specified purposes, except where Union or Member State law provides that the prohibition referred to in paragraph 1 may not be lifted by the data subject;
o c) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or another natural person, in the event that the data subject is not physically or legally able to give consent;
o h) the processing is necessary for purposes of preventive or occupational medicine, assessment of the working capacity of the employee, medical diagnosis,
27

provision of assistance or treatment of a health or social nature, or management of health and social assistance systems and services, based on Union Law or the laws of the Member States or by virtue of a contract with a healthcare professional and without prejudice to the conditions and guarantees set forth in paragraph 3.

The rule that enables treatments in national law must be a rule with the rank of law.

The most relevant laws at the state level that support the treatments are Law 14/1986, of April 25, General Health Law (LGS), Law 41/2002, of November 14, basic regulatory law on patient autonomy and rights and obligations regarding information and clinical documentation (LAP), and Law 39/2006, of December 14, on the Promotion of Personal Autonomy and Care for Persons in a Situation of Dependency (Dependency Law), in conjunction with the corresponding regional laws that regulate health and social matters.

The determination of the legal basis that legitimizes each treatment must be made by examining which of those contained in Articles 9 and 6 of the GDPR are applicable in each case and always taking into account that when it concerns special categories of data, one of the circumstances listed in Article 9 must previously exist to lift the prohibition on the processing of such data.

Article 6.1 of the GDPR states that:
“The processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:
 a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;
 b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;
 c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;
 d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;
 e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;
 f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.
The provision in point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”
28

It should also be noted that, in accordance with Article 8 of the LOPDGDD, data processing can only be considered based on a legal obligation when this is established in EU law or in a norm with the rank of Law. Similarly, data processing can only be considered legitimate in the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers when it is established in a norm with the rank of Law.

For the purposes of determining the legal basis, we can mention the following examples:
- In those cases where, according to the provisions of the LAP, healthcare centers must create a medical history, the legal basis will be compliance with a legal obligation, Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR established by the aforementioned law.
- Some autonomous communities regulate in their social services laws the "social history," the creation of which by the public authorities to whom it is entrusted will constitute a legal obligation in such cases, in accordance with Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR.
- In other cases, the legal basis for the processing of users of the center may be found in the fulfillment of a task carried out in the public interest or the exercise of public powers (Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR), depending on what is provided by the different social services laws of the autonomous communities. In this way, the processing of data necessary for the proper care of individuals admitted to a senior center, distinct from treatments related to medical or social history, could be found in the exercise of public powers or a mission carried out in the public interest when it concerns public centers, or in the fulfillment of a mission of public interest in contracted private centers (for example, Law 16/2010, of December 20, on social services of Castilla y León provides for the participation of private initiative entities in the provision of benefits from the public responsibility system; such entities, when financed wholly or partially with public funds, are subject to the regional planning of social services; these cases can be considered as fulfilling a mission of public interest). In other cases, however, it will derive from the fulfillment of a contract (Article 6.1.b) entered into between the data subject and the corresponding private center.
- When, for reasons of urgency, immediate admission to a socio-health center is necessary, Article 6.1.d) of the GDPR ("necessity of protecting vital interests of the data subject or another natural person") could be considered as the legal basis, until the normalization of the situation. This legal basis requires, taking into account that it will normally involve the processing of health data, that the data subject is physically or legally incapacitated to give their consent as provided for in Article 9.c of the GDPR.

Finally, consent (which must also be explicit when it comes to health data) of the user should not be relied upon as the legal basis for the processing of data corresponding to the provision of socio-health care. Consent may be obtained for ancillary processing operations to the main one, which are detailed in the following section.

29

7.6. CONSENTS

• As mentioned, the legal basis for the main data processing in socio-health services will be compliance with a legal obligation or the exercise of public powers and the contractual relationship.

However, other treatments have been identified, such as:
- the publication of images of users,
- advertising or promotional treatments,
- ancillary or optional activities such as outings or excursions, or
whose legal basis may be different (consent, legitimate interest, etc.).

• Thus, it has been found that in the different audited centers, the following written consents are requested from users or their representatives:
- It has been detected that in some cases consent is requested for the use of images of the user (audio and video recordings), medical research, and excursions. Always with checkboxes to mark independently for each consent. For example, they have made a promotional music video with users and employees for which they requested consent.
- In other cases, consent is only requested to provide information about the user to family members or interested parties, differentiating between location information and care information.
- It has also been detected that consent is requested for the use of images of the user, research, and commercial and promotional treatments, as well as "the improvement of our services," without specifying for this last purpose anything further, specifically what it consists of and what the scope of the processing is.
- In other cases, consent is requested solely for the use of images.
- In some of the centers, informed consents are also obtained for medical actions that affect their health, as stipulated in the LAP.

• Regarding these consents, the following particularities have been found:
- Due to the characteristics of the patients, in general, the consent documents are signed by the person (or Institution, in the case of Guardianship Agencies) who exercises guardianship over the patient, or by the patient themselves if they are not legally incapacitated.
- While all consent documents collect personal data (both from the user and from the family member or guardian who signs on behalf of the patient), not all include informative legends on data protection with the information stipulated by Article 13 of the GDPR.
30

It is important to highlight that when consent documents are signed by a representative (family member or guardian) of the user, the consent for the processing of the representative's data is neither informed nor requested, nor is there any reference to such processing, as if the representative's data were not being processed, when in most cases, their data is also expressly collected, not only their name and ID for signature purposes, but also other types of data such as their contact information.

o In some cases where users are not usually legally incapacitated, it has been found that the consent documents are signed by both (the user and the representative, if applicable), regardless of whether the user is incapacitated or not. According to allegations from the center's representatives, this is done as a precautionary measure.  
It should be noted that, in general, when the user's signature is not possible and no family members are present or it is a protected user, the centers inform the Court once the admission has been made and investigate the possibility of guardianship by a family member or institution.  
o Consent documents have been found in which the boxes were pre-checked.  
o Also, in some cases, the consent form was not updated according to the new regulations (GDPR), referring to the old Data Protection Law (LOPD).

RECOMMENDATIONS:

• While a user is not legally incapacitated, only their consent is considered valid for the processing of their personal data for ancillary purposes, such as image processing and advertising.

• To provide information about a user to their family members or third parties, the consent of that user is generally required, except in cases provided for by the LAP for clinical information where it is permitted or required to provide such information, such as when the patient, in the opinion of the attending physician, lacks the capacity to understand the information due to their physical or mental state, in which case the information will be made known to those related to them for family or factual reasons.

Another exception to consent occurs when the right to health information is limited for users due to the accredited existence of a state of therapeutic necessity, in which case the LAP provides for the communication of clinical information to persons related to the user for family or factual reasons.

• As indicated in the information section, the special characteristics of users in social health centers require that information about the user's characteristics be provided in an accessible manner to authorized family members, so that consent is considered valid.

• In any case, if the data of the representative and/or additional family members for contact purposes is collected, they must be informed about the processing of their personal data in all terms required by data protection regulations (Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR and Article 11 of the LOPDGDD).

• The purposes for which consent is collected must be clear and specific, and generic statements about the purpose of processing such as "the improvement of our services" are not valid.

• Consent must be obtained through a declaration or clear affirmative action, so pre-checked boxes for collecting consent are not permitted.

• Consent documents and their informative clauses must be updated to the new regulations (GDPR and LOPDGDD).

• For the obtaining and processing of images of users, two distinct scenarios should be considered:  
o When the user voluntarily poses in front of a camera, it is considered that consent does not need to be requested, as being in front of a visibly clear camera can be considered a clear affirmative action. It should be noted that, since this is informed consent, information about data protection must always be provided before proceeding to take the images, and there must be real possibilities to avoid being recorded if desired.  
o In other types of situations, such as events, parties, health recordings, and general recordings or photography, when the taking of these images does not depend on the user's will, consent must be requested, except when the capturing of images is merely incidental or accessory, such as in the case of people passing in the background of the image not being the object of the capture.

When the person in front of the camera is unable to give their consent due to being judicially incapacitated, consent must be obtained from the user's guardians or legal representatives.

• Informed consents for medical procedures are regulated by specific health regulations without prejudice to the general considerations regarding the provision of consent itself and the legal bases for data processing that can be derived from the GDPR.

However, it should be emphasized that consents for medical procedures should not be confused with the legal bases for the corresponding data processing.

• Data processing for purposes related to medical research presents substantially different characteristics that require consent to be requested separately.

7.7. INFORMATION TO FAMILIES AND OTHER THIRD PARTIES

• Some of the audited centers did not have a written procedure with instructions for staff on how to proceed with requests for information about
32

the users of the center. The user was also not asked upon admission if they authorize their data to be shared, or if they have any objections in this regard.

• Other centers had implemented a password mechanism, which is provided to the user at the time of admission, and which family members need in order to obtain information about the user via telephone.

In the centers, prior authorization from the user, tutor, or legal representative is also requested.

In some cases, different information was offered in two of the documents provided to the user or representative during admission: in the center's welcome guide, it states “if you do not want to authorize, you must indicate it,” and in the corresponding Ministry guide, which is also provided, it states that “information will be provided to the persons indicated by us.”

The information thus provided is confusing and not consistent with the password mechanism established. The first phrase “if you do not want to authorize, you must indicate it” would imply tacit consent. The second does not, but in the consent form, where authorization to provide information is collected, the identification of the persons authorized to obtain information is not requested. The mechanism is through a password, such that those who possess it will be able to gather information, regardless of what is stated in the aforementioned guides.

• In day centers, a common communication mechanism with families has been a diary in which possible incidents and other basic daily information about the center are noted, to be taken into account by caregivers when the user returns home.

• Another common information mechanism in socio-health centers is face-to-face interviews with family members, during which the center informs about the user's condition and requests the family's collaboration and commitment to the objectives to be achieved with the user, as this is a fundamental aspect of comprehensive socio-health care, which even appears in contracts or agreements between the center and the administration as an obligation: to inform the user, tutor, or authorized family member.

RECOMMENDATIONS:

• A procedure should be established to obtain the user's consent regarding whether they wish or do not wish for information about their presence and location in the center to be provided to persons or family members who inquire about it.

The consent must have the characteristics provided by the GDPR, which requires it to be informed, free, specific, and granted by the affected individuals through a manifestation that shows their willingness to consent or through a clear affirmative action. The responsible party must be able to prove at all times that consent has been obtained.

As long as the patient has not opposed the provision of such information, in cases of vital urgency or when the presence of related persons is involved...
33

For family reasons or in fact, it may be necessary for the proper attention of the user, the center may inform whether the person is admitted and their location, without indicating special category data or information about the care provided.

• For socio-health information, such as health status or medical or social actions performed or to be performed with the user, it should be noted that Article 5 of the LAP specifies the following regarding health information:  
“1. The holder of the right to information is the patient. Persons linked to them, for family or factual reasons, will also be informed, to the extent that the patient expressly or tacitly allows it.”

However, since the application of the GDPR, tacit consent is not possible for the processing of personal data, so this legal reference is superseded, and explicit consent from the user must be obtained as indicated in Article 9.2.a) of said Regulation:  
“a) the data subject has given explicit consent to the processing of such personal data for one or more specified purposes, except where Union or Member State law provides that the prohibition referred to in paragraph 1 may not be lifted by the data subject”;

Therefore, for the processing operation consisting of the communication of socio-health data to persons linked to the user for family or factual reasons, explicit consent from the user of the center, their legal guardian in case of incapacitation, or legal representative must be obtained, except in the cases provided for by the LAP for clinical information where the provision of such information is allowed or required, as previously discussed in the preceding section (such as when the patient lacks the capacity to understand the information due to their physical or mental condition, or when the right to health information is limited for users due to the accredited existence of a state of therapeutic necessity). There may also be other exceptions in cases where other legal bases of the GDPR apply that are different from consent, such as a mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers, and in particular the vital interest of the data subject.

• As a preventive instrument, the Spanish Agency for Data Protection has developed a guide to raise awareness and promote compliance with the rights of patients and users of healthcare. Consulting this guide by the heads of the centers can be useful when implementing mechanisms to address the rights of users. The guide for patients and users of healthcare can be accessed through the following link:  
https://www.aepd.es/media/guias/guia-pacientes-usuarios-sanidad.pdf

7.8. DATA FLOWS BETWEEN ADMINISTRATIONS AND OTHER PUBLIC/PRIVATE ACTORS

• It has been detected that the Councils, Municipalities, Mutuals, and private insurers sometimes send, via communication means such as fax and unencrypted email, documentation related to the patient's admission, which usually contains specially protected data.
34

On the other hand, the courts urge the centers to use fax as a means of communication, as it is common for health data such as medical reports of patients to be included, as well as other information that may be considered special category data. This is the case for involuntary admissions, which are always carried out by court order. In the prior judicial authorization itself, the Center is urged to keep the Court informed about the maintenance of the measure and the evolution of the admitted person.

• 
It has also been detected that, on occasions, some Public Bodies send emails to the Centers where mentally ill patients are admitted, asking whether a certain person is admitted, indicating data about that person, such as having allegedly committed a crime of sexual assault, for example.

Such emails are usually addressed to several directors of social health centers, revealing data that may be considered sensitive regarding the person in question.

• 
On the other hand, Article 3, section 2 of the new Law 40/2015, of October 1, on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector states: “Public Administrations shall relate to each other and with their bodies, public organizations, and entities linked or dependent on them through electronic means that ensure the interoperability and security of the systems and solutions adopted by each of them, shall guarantee the protection of personal data, and shall preferably facilitate the joint provision of services to the interested parties.” The use of data intermediation platforms of the Administration available for the interconnection and collection of user data, which are already in the possession of the Administration, has been found, with the aim of avoiding the user having to provide them and for the verification of the origin of the benefits being requested.

For example, it can be determined through the IMSERSO platform whether a valuation applicant in one Autonomous Community has a file in another Autonomous Community, returning the available queries with the minimum necessary information for the intended purpose.

RECOMMENDATIONS:

• 
From a data protection perspective, fax and unencrypted email should not be used for sending documentation that contains personal data of special categories such as health data or other data such as social, psychosocial, or evaluation reports.

For the transmission of these types of data over public networks, secure communication channels should be established, such as encrypted or protected email.

• 
Information that is not necessary to achieve the objective of the communication, such as details of the alleged crimes committed by a particular person being sought, should not be disclosed in communications of any kind.
35

• Regarding the inquiries of personal data between public bodies in order to allow the interested party not to provide documents that are already in the possession of the Administration, the eighth additional provision of the LOPDGDD establishes that:

“When requests are made by any means in which the interested party declares personal data held by Public Administrations, the body receiving the request may carry out the necessary verifications in the exercise of its competencies to verify the accuracy of the data.”
On the other hand, Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations, in its article 28.2 (amended by the LOPDGDD) stipulates that “…The acting administration may consult or request such documents unless the interested party opposes it,” so operational protocols must be established that include, among the most important aspects, the following:
o The information to be provided to the data subject regarding the inquiry to be made in the application forms, allowing the possibility of opposition.
o The creation of specific inquiries for each procedure, which yield the necessary data for the intended purpose.
o The security guarantees that include, among other aspects, the creation of specific access profiles and the traceability of the inquiries made.

7.9. ANALYSIS OF CONTRACTS, TERMS OF REFERENCE, AND AGREEMENTS

CONTRACTS AND AGREEMENTS FOR THE PROVISION OF SOCIAL HEALTH SERVICES BY THE CENTER

• During the audits conducted on private centers but with contracted places with the Department, and therefore corresponding to a treatment assignment contract, it was found that in the examined contractual documentation there are cases where the data protection clauses cannot be located, as they do not appear in the annual extension documents they maintain, with the duration of the agreement reaching almost 20 years. In one case, the representatives of the center were unaware of their existence or, if applicable, in which document they were found, having to review the documentation thoroughly to ultimately find incomplete clauses referring to the old LOPD in a technical specifications document that is 13 years old.

It follows that, in any case, such incomplete and obsolete clauses were not taken into account, as they were unaware of their existence until the audit was conducted.

• Confidentiality annexes, personal data protection, and information security have also been detected, with all clauses referring to the old LOPD, in cases of centers managed by private companies under public service contracts subject to administrative clauses and technical specifications.

• In other audited cases, corresponding to public centers with direct management, there are therefore no contracts for the provision of social health services.

CONTRACTS FOR THE PROVISION OF SERVICES ENTERED INTO BY THE CENTER WITH THIRD PARTIES

• In general, from the examination of the contracts entered into with third-party service providers, it appears that efforts are being made to adapt them to the GDPR.

New clauses reflecting the requirements of this new regulation have been found, both in contracts signed after May 25, 2018, and in contract models prepared for future use.

Specific deficiencies have been found in the new contracts regarding the lack of express specification of the nature and purpose of the processing, although in some cases it can be inferred from the object of the contract, as well as from the type of data and the categories of the interested parties. Article 28 of the GDPR requires that these aspects be established in the contract.

This, along with the analysis of the rest of the clauses in the contracts, shows that the majority of the reviewed contracts, while very comprehensive, are very general, designed for validity within the framework of any contracting. Identical data protection clauses have been detected in the same center for completely different contracts. In fact, for example, it is typical to find the same generic clauses, or even lists of very general security measures valid for any situation without conducting a minimum Risk Analysis.

• In some cases, a form has been developed to be completed by candidates for suppliers or service providers acting as data processors or having access to personal data under the responsibility of the center. The form contains a series of questions that help the center choose the data processors according to specific objectives.

• It should be noted that some isolated contracts, signed before May 25, 2018, were found that did not comply with the regulations in force at the time of signing, the old LOPD and its implementing regulations, without reference to basic guarantees, such as what happens to the data at the end of the service provision, the conditions for subcontracting, or the security measures.

• Regarding suppliers or service providers who, by the nature of the service, do not have access to personal data under the responsibility of the center, it is common to require the signing of a confidentiality contract or commitment to cover possible accidental and eventual access to personal data. In some of these documents, there is an obligation not to access the information under the responsibility of the center and, especially, to the personal data, in addition to maintaining due confidentiality regarding them in case of accidental access.

RECOMMENDATIONS:
37

• The new LOPDGDD establishes that contracts for data processing signed prior to May 25, 2018, under the provisions of Article 12 of the former LOPD, will remain in effect until the expiration date indicated in them and, in the case of being agreed upon indefinitely, until May 25, 2022.

However, new data processing contracts signed from May 25, 2018, must be adapted to the GDPR, and it is mandatory that they specify:
o the object, duration, nature, and purpose of the processing,
o the type of personal data and categories of data subjects,
o the obligations and rights of the data controller,
o that the data will be processed following the documented instructions of the data controller,
o the obligation of confidentiality on the part of employees,
o the establishment of necessary security measures,
o compliance with conditions for possible subcontracting,
o assistance to the data controller regarding the exercise of users' rights,
o the deletion or return of personal data upon termination.

Detailed information on this can be found in the guidelines for drafting contracts between data controllers and data processors published by this Agency.

• All data processing contracts made by the center with third parties must be reviewed, ensuring that the data protection clauses are correct according to the date of the contract. Such clauses must be observed during the contracting to which they refer, implementing the necessary mechanisms to take them into account.

It is recommended to gradually renew contracts signed prior to May 25, 2018, including new clauses adapted to the GDPR.

• To comply with the obligation of confidentiality, it is recommended to include in the contracts the requirement for employees of the service provider to sign a confidentiality commitment regarding personal data, with a model that preferably will be attached to the contract.

It is also recommended to include, in contracts with service providers without access to personal data, the obligation not to access such data and to inform the workers participating in the provision of the service about this obligation, preferably through the signing of a document that records the notice and also covers confidentiality regarding possible accidental or incidental access to personal data.

• In drafting contracts, it is advisable to avoid very general formulas, valid for all types of situations, such as “in case it is legally required, conduct an impact assessment on the protection of personal data of the processing operations...”. It should be determined based on the object, duration, nature, scope, and purpose of the processing, as well as the type of personal data, categories of data subjects, and other circumstances of the context of the processing to be carried out, whether each measure in question is appropriate or not, specifying for each contract.

• The data controller has a special diligence obligation when selecting data processors, and this diligence must be documented. Data processors should be chosen based on criteria of trust, security, and responsibility, to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is carried out in accordance with the GDPR (principle of proactive responsibility).

7.10. OTHER ADAPTATIONS TO THE GDPR

• Upon examining the Records of Processing Activities (RAT) published by the data controllers of the audited centers, no notable non-compliance was observed. The analysis of these reveals the following:
o All data controllers of the audited centers have prepared a RAT that includes the data of the controller, the purposes of the processing, the categories of data subjects, and the categories of personal data.
o In some RATs, the data of the Data Protection Officer (DPO) are not published. On one occasion, a Data Protection Coordinator is mentioned instead of the term DPO.
o Occasionally, the legal bases for processing are cited, although it is not mandatory, with confusing expressions such as “legitimate interest is determined by the consent of the data subject.” It has been detected that in public centers, the user's consent is mentioned as the legal basis for the main processing (social health care).
o Sometimes, technical measures are not mentioned, although the GDPR indicates that they should be described when possible.

• All data controllers of the audited centers have designated a DPO.

• In few cases did the center have its procedures for addressing the rights of data subjects fully adapted to the new rights contained in the GDPR.

In some cases, users are informed of the new rights, but their policies only cover the ARCO rights of the former LOPD. Conversely, in other cases, there is a procedure for addressing all rights contained in the GDPR, but users are not correctly informed about them.

• In few cases had a procedure been established to identify and manage security breaches.

• In some cases, a Risk Analysis has been conducted to determine the appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of the processing.
39

In other cases, the centers continue with their security policies based on the development regulation of the former LOPD, which determined the security measures that should be applied according to the type of data being processed.

• In few cases has a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) been conducted. In some cases, it has been determined, from the Risk Analysis carried out, that the DPIA is not necessary.

• Notwithstanding the above, an effort has been perceived on the part of organizations to change their policies in accordance with the new regulations, with most cases having adaptation plans for this purpose: having already made the most immediate changes, they are immersed in the adaptation process, which, in many cases, they have reported as complicated, especially regarding Risk Analysis and the DPIA.

RECOMMENDATIONS:

The GDPR came into force on May 25, 2016, and has been fully applicable since May 25, 2018. Therefore, the transitional period has ended, and the data controllers, as well as the processors, must have already adopted the necessary measures for full compliance with this regulation.

According to the principle of proactive responsibility, controllers must adapt their policies to these new obligations, applying the necessary technical and organizational measures to ensure, and be in a position to demonstrate, their compliance. It should be taken into consideration that:

• A Record of Processing Activities (RPA) must be maintained, containing the information established in Article 30 of the GDPR. It should be noted that the RPA should not be understood as a mere formality, as it is considered an essential tool that can serve as a starting point when addressing adaptation to data protection regulations and ensuring compliance.

• The procedures for addressing the rights of data subjects must be adapted, considering the rights contained in the GDPR, as well as the new conditions to be followed for their attention.

• A procedure must be established to identify and manage security breaches, considered as such those incidents that cause the destruction, loss, or accidental or unlawful alteration of personal data, or their unauthorized communication or access.

Such procedures must ensure that:
o the breach is documented,
o notification is made to the AEPD, unless it is unlikely that the breach poses a risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects,
o notification is made to the affected individuals if it poses a high risk to their rights or freedoms, informing them of the measures they can take to eliminate or mitigate the consequences.

40

• A Risk Analysis must be conducted to determine the appropriate technical and organizational measures according to the level of risks detected, in order to ensure the security of the processing activities. For this, the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals, must be taken into account.

• The GDPR establishes that the performance of the DPIA is mandatory prior to the implementation of new processing activities that entail a high risk to the rights and freedoms. It is considered that the DPIA is mandatory for a socio-health center, given that it involves processing data of special categories, an association or combination of social and health data, considering the vulnerability of its users and understanding that it poses a high risk to their rights and freedoms.

However, regarding pre-existing processing activities as of May 2018 that present a high risk, such as those already being carried out in socio-health centers, the obligation to conduct the DPIA is limited to when relevant changes occur regarding technology, purposes, data volume, or other similar circumstances.

The AEPD has made available the GESTIONA_EIPD tool in order to provide an initial basis for the analysis of high-risk processing activities that require the performance of DPIAs and risk analyses in personal data protection.

This tool can be accessed through the following link: https://www.aepd.es/herramientas/gestiona.html

This tool generates the basic documentation on which a risk analysis and management must be carried out by controllers and processors to comply with the provisions of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD. This basic documentation must be completed and analyzed by the data controller and, where applicable, the data processor, following the guidelines established in the Practical Guide for Data Protection Impact Assessments.

A model DPIA report for Public Administrations is also available on the AEPD website.

8. DECALOGUE OF RECOMMENDATIONS

1. Information should be provided to the user preferably in layers, concise and with clear language, according to the comprehension capacity of the information recipient.

The first layer should consist of simple informational posters located in access areas to the centers, along with the informational legends inserted in all personal data collection forms. This first layer may include references to other layers of more detailed information, to which the user should have immediate accessibility.
41

2. For each processing activity carried out, its legal basis must be identified, which will be one or more of those set forth in Article 6 of the GDPR in conjunction with the processing conditions provided in Article 9.2.h) of the GDPR.

3. The sharing of personal data among professionals must be minimized to what is strictly necessary, without compromising healthcare attention. It is not valid to allow total access to user data for all professionals involved in the care.

Access profiles must be created that consider the information needs of each professional, from the design of the applications, applying by default the minimum necessary access privileges to provide care to the user. An audit of accesses must be conducted.

4. All employees who process personal data of users must sign a confidentiality commitment.

In all contracts with data processors, if any, it must be established as mandatory for the employees of the processor to sign the commitment, and it is desirable that the specific commitment model is annexed to the contract.

5. It must be avoided that paper documents are dispersed in different locations within the center, and their transfer must be carried out with sufficient security measures (guarded at all times and in a sealed envelope).

Secure procedures must be created for the transfer of documentation and its storage, with mechanisms that prevent access by unauthorized persons.

6. Generic users, whose use is shared among several employees, must not be used to access personal data, whether basic or of special categories, as this poses a security vulnerability.

7. User consent must be obtained to provide information to relatives about their stay or location in the center, as well as their health status.

In cases of vital urgency or if the presence of individuals related to the user for family or de facto reasons could be essential for the proper care of the user, as long as the patient has not opposed the provision of such information, the center may inform whether the person is hospitalized and their location, without indicating special category data or details about the care provided.

8. From a data protection perspective, fax and unencrypted email should not be used to send documentation containing special category personal data, such as health data or other data such as social, psychosocial, or assessment reports.

42

9. Data processing contracts must specify all obligations stipulated by the GDPR, which must be considered and fulfilled during the contracting process, establishing the necessary mechanisms for this.

In service provision contracts without access to personal data, access to such data must be expressly prohibited, and workers must be informed of this prohibition. The contract must also consider possible accidental or fortuitous accesses, with a confidentiality commitment.

10. Security policies must be based on a Risk Analysis. It is considered that the Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is mandatory for new processing activities in social health centers, given that special category data is presented in sufficient numbers, also considering the vulnerability of their users and understanding that it poses a high risk to their rights and freedoms.

9. FREQUENT QUESTIONS

1. In a social health center, is it possible to cancel certain data of a user at their request? For example, one might present the case of users who do not want certain social data, such as that their children do not visit them, which is considered essential for providing due attention by reflecting family support.

A: Cancellation is not possible. Since consent is not the legal basis for processing, neither the revocation of consent nor opposition to processing can be considered without weighing this right against the legal basis that supports the processing. Generally, the legal basis will be found in compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller, as it is necessary to maintain the authenticity of the social health history.

The request must be answered by explaining the reasons why the data cannot be canceled.

An appropriate caution could be to mark this data as confidential, establishing mechanisms so that it is only consulted when strictly necessary and warning of its particularly sensitive nature in each access made through a message to the accessing professional.

2. Are treatments for medical research purposes possible in a social health center?

A: Article 9.2.j) of the GDPR lifts the prohibition on processing special categories of data for scientific research purposes, based on Union law or the laws of Member States, provided that:
- the processing is proportional to the intended objective,
- the essential data protection is respected,
43

o establish appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject,  
o and the processing shall be subject to adequate safeguards, to the health sector regulations, to the LAP, and in particular to the seventeenth additional provision of the LOPDGDD, providing adequate technical and organizational measures, particularly to ensure data minimization.  
3. In the case of individuals who are not legally incapacitated, but who, for example, are in an advanced stage of cognitive decline and refuse to receive social care, can their data be processed without their consent?  

A: Yes, it is possible, as the legal basis for processing their personal data is not their consent, but a legal obligation applicable to the data controller, or the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public authority.  

Depending on the cases and circumstances, it may also be applicable that the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject.  

In addition to these bases, some of the exceptions in Article 9.2 of the GDPR or a combination of them may apply.  

4. For functions and events at the center, with the public or family members, what can the center do if a user or their guardians have not given consent for the processing of images and the user in question is recorded by relatives of other users? And for the images captured by the center?  

A: It is recommended that the center inform at events about the possibility that relatives and friends may take images of the event for domestic use. This domestic use would be outside the scope of the GDPR according to Article 2.2.c), which establishes that this regulation does not apply to the processing of personal data carried out by an individual in the course of activities that are exclusively personal or domestic.  

It would also constitute good practice to inform before starting the event (for example, through posters) that recording is only feasible for domestic use (private, family, and friendship activities), not for public dissemination.  

In the case of image capture by the center, it must inform users, legal representatives, or guardians in the case of legal incapacitation, of the purpose of recording the images and the intended dissemination, whether they will be published on websites, social media, or any other type of publication, and request their unequivocal consent (explicit if special category data). If consent is not obtained or is negative, it must publish these in a manner that does not allow identification of those appearing in the recordings, for example, by pixelating the images.  

5. In the case of restraining orders related to a user of the center, whether due to mistreatment, harassment, gender violence, etc., is it possible to inform everyone about it?
44

Can the employees of the center, including those who do not have access to personal data, such as cleaning contract employees, be informed?

A: Information must be communicated to all staff who have the function of identifying visitors and making decisions about them.

It should be noted that, as recommended in point 7.4 of this report, all staff with and without access to personal data should have signed a confidentiality agreement.

6. In the case of users under guardianship by Adult Guardianship Agencies, can social health centers provide family members with information about their health or social status?

A: In principle, no, since the user is under guardianship, and therefore the communication of any data, whether social health-related or of any other nature, must always be directed to the legal guardian or through them.

The only cases in which data could be provided to family members would be those in which, upon examining the incapacitation ruling, it is specifically determined therein, or as a consequence of its scope, such possibility.

7. When law enforcement requests it, is it mandatory to provide them with the personal data of the center's users?

A: The files of the Law Enforcement and Security Forces are not within the scope of the application of the GDPR or the LOPDGDD, being subject to the provisions of Article 22 of the former LOPD, which stipulates that the collection of personal data by these Forces and Bodies without the consent of the affected individuals is limited to those cases and categories of data that are necessary for the prevention of a real danger to public safety or for the repression of criminal offenses. In the specific case of health data, collection may only be carried out in cases where it is absolutely necessary for the purposes of a specific investigation.

In cases where a professional, in the course of the healthcare provided, has evidence that the user suffers injuries that may have been caused by a third party and has informed the judicial authority through the corresponding judicial report of injuries, the acting law enforcement officers, in their role as judicial police, may not access the user's health data other than those related to the injury suffered, as stated in the aforementioned judicial report, without a judicial order or request from the Public Prosecutor's Office.

Additionally, any access to the medical history associated with the personal identification data of users, for judicial purposes, is subject to what judges and courts determine in each specific case, and such access is not lawful without the corresponding judicial order, in accordance with Article 16.3 of the LAP.

8. Can the professional registration of workers at a center be verified through their Professional Association?

A: Yes, as long as registration is mandatory for the performance of the activity, with the processing being covered by Law 2/1974 on Professional Associations, along with its successive amendments.

9. Can data of users from the center be provided to regional deputies who request it?

A: Regional deputies, within their oversight functions, can obtain personal information for which the Autonomous Community is responsible, and requests for information from deputies must be processed in the terms and with the limitations established in the regulations of the Chambers as a special rule applicable in a prevailing manner.

Requests for information from deputies should not be directed to the centers but to the competent Departments of the Autonomous Community.

The Departments, taking into account the sensitivity of the information, may reasonably communicate the reasons that may prevent or restrict the information to be provided to the deputies. The centers, if applicable, may express these limitations to the competent Departments.

10. If an entity such as a Court requests special category data from a user of the center, such as health data, via a communication method that can be considered insecure, such as fax or unencrypted email, should the request be processed?

A: From a data protection perspective, fax should not be used to send documentation containing special category data. It should be pointed out to the entity in question that there are currently other methods, simple, equally fast, and more secure, such as encrypted email, agreeing with the entity on the secure sending of the aforementioned documentation.

11. In guard posts, or positions where medical tests are conducted, where shifts are established and there are teams operating 24 hours due to service needs, is it possible to share the system user to avoid logging in and out of the system?

A: The use of the user should not be shared, nor should the access password associated with any employee, colleague, or third party be disclosed. Generic shared users should also not be used.

Each employee, when starting their shift, must log in with their user and log out at the end.

12. Is it possible to place signs with the names and surnames of the users of a center on the doors of their rooms to prevent them from getting lost or entering another room by mistake, as well as to facilitate visits?

Is it possible to place signs on the dining tables with the identification (name and surname or photograph) of the user and their allergies, dysphagia, or other characteristics of special relevance during meals?
46

R: The necessity of identifying patients or disclosing their data must be assessed at different moments of service provision, always considering other less intrusive measures for privacy that may exist and that offer equivalent security guarantees. Thus, for example, the alternative of using the room number on a card or badge carried by the user, instead of placing a sign with the user's full name on the door of their room, could be evaluated.

Regarding the information to be displayed in the dining area about considerations to be taken into account for each user, it should be the minimum necessary to allow for the safe administration of food or medication, positively evaluating other alternatives that ensure the information is exclusively available to the dining staff who need it, without being exposed to all attendees.

10. FUTURE CHALLENGES

Throughout the history of this Agency, multiple official plans have been made, some related to the healthcare sector. However, this plan is the first that addresses social services, and it does so only at the points where they intersect with healthcare services. It should be noted that during this plan, the enormous number of social services currently provided in Spain has become apparent, ranging from primary care services that offer information, guidance, and advice, assistance with processing, etc., to more specialized services where the list becomes endless, including not only care for dependency and disability but also prevention services, family support services, services related to gender-based violence, substance abuse and alcoholism, juvenile offenders, and generally groups in situations of neglect and at risk of social exclusion. 

The service portfolio includes Residences and Day Centers, Child Care Centers and minor protection services, Family Support Centers, Social Education Services, Social Emergency Services, Early Attention Services, Home Teleassistance Services and Home Help, Shelters, Accommodations for single-parent families, Shelters for Women in situations of prostitution, Shelters for pregnant women, Accommodations for immigrant populations, and a long etcetera. 

Observing the number of existing services and the categories of data that are necessarily processed during their planning, administration, provision, and control, one can deduce the appropriateness of carrying out official actions aimed at verifying the data protection guarantees implemented to minimize risks concerning the rights and freedoms of the interested parties. 

Due to the breadth of the topic to be addressed and the complexity of such actions, which would involve actions with various administrations—central, regional, and local—as well as private entities, it would be necessary to delimit the spatiotemporal scope of the investigations, their scope and objectives, selecting the most relevant services from the perspective of the treatments and types of data involved. To this end, we should ask questions such as: What special categories of data are processed? Where is the data...
47

Where are the greatest risks found? And, ultimately, what actions would yield the maximum return?
