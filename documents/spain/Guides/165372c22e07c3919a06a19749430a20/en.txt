EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  
In the last twenty years, we have experienced a steady increase in the amount of data that is generated and subsequently processed in some way. Data has transitioned from being a scarce resource, difficult to collect and manage centrally, to becoming an abundant resource created in a decentralized manner and easy to reproduce and communicate. There seems to be a natural tendency to "extract data from devices or organizations" and share it among different parties to create new value for our society, or simply to reduce operational costs.  
Data sharing can be considered as the disclosure of data to third parties outside the organization for the purpose of achieving a specific objective. This sharing can take place as part of a processing operation or while attempting to provide additional utility to existing data. Recent EU legislative initiatives promoting data sharing are sectoral or cross-sectoral instruments aimed at making data available by regulating the reuse of publicly and privately owned data, including personal data. They also facilitate data sharing by creating intermediaries and novel exchange environments in which the involved parties can share data and facilities reliably and securely.  
This report aims to analyze in detail specific use cases related to personal data sharing, primarily in the healthcare sector, and examines how specific technologies and implementation considerations can support compliance with data protection specifications. After analyzing some of the challenges of data (personal) sharing, this report demonstrates how to design specific technologies and techniques that enable data sharing while preserving privacy.  
More specifically, it analyzes concrete cases of data exchange in the healthcare sector, with the objective of demonstrating how data protection principles can be fulfilled through the proper use of technological solutions based on advanced cryptographic techniques. It then examines data sharing that occurs as part of another process or service, in which data is processed through some secondary channel or entity before reaching its primary recipient. Finally, it identifies challenges, considerations, and possible architectures as solutions to aspects of control by stakeholders (such as the right to erasure and the right to rectification when sharing data).

1. INTRODUCTION  
Data is at the center of our daily lives and our economy. Over the past twenty years, we have experienced a steady increase in the amount of data generated and subsequently processed in some way. Data has transitioned from being a scarce resource, difficult to collect and managed in a centralized manner, and costly to store, transmit, and process, to becoming an abundant resource created in a decentralized manner (by individuals or sensors), easy to replicate and communicate or disseminate at scale. This is evident in the fact that in the last 20 years, the capacity of the backbone fiber optic of the Internet has multiplied almost 100 times (from 10 Gbps to nearly 1 Tbps), while the cost of transmitting a single Gbps has decreased at the same rate, currently around 1% of the cost incurred in the early 2000s [1].  
Data is considered the new currency, and organizations exchange information about their customers with their partners, analytical platforms, public administrations, and other stakeholders in the data ecosystem in order to leverage new technologies or the additional information they can obtain from sharing and correlation. There seems to be a natural tendency to "extract data from devices or organizations" and share it among different parties to create new value for our society, or simply to reduce operational costs. Sharing data is already becoming the norm rather than an exception in data processing. To harness the value of data, service providers must be able to use it, including data they do not own.  
In an effort to provide an accurate description, and based on the definitions provided by the Data Protection Commission (DPC) in 2019 [2] and the ICO in 2020 [3], data sharing can be considered as the disclosure of data to third parties outside the organization for a specific purpose. Such sharing can be carried out as part of a processing operation or to attempt to provide additional utility to existing data. Data sharing can be done routinely or in response to specific or emergency situations. According to Gartner [4], sharing data is a business necessity, as it can enhance digital transformation and innovation.  

1.1 RELEVANT EU LEGISLATIVE INITIATIVES  
Currently, European legislators have a strong interest in data sharing. One of the fundamental pillars of the European Data Strategy [5] is to increase the availability of data and facilitate its sharing across sectors and countries in the EU to leverage the potential of data for the benefit of European citizens and businesses. Considering only the EU 27 area, the projected value of data for the economy by 2025 will be €829 billion, compared to €301 billion (2.4% of EU GDP) in 2018.2 It is expected that facilitating access to data and its sharing will bring significant and concrete benefits in various fields, such as personalized diagnostics and telemedicine, transportation, policy-making, and public administration.  
The European Data Governance Regulation [6] provides mechanisms to increase the availability of data in the public sector and overcome technical obstacles to the reuse of public interest data. These mechanisms are supported by a set of concrete measures that facilitate data sharing. The measures include the establishment of data intermediaries that act as reliable data organizers and technologies.

1.2 THE ROLE OF DATA PROTECTION ENGINEERING  
The protection of personal data is an integral element of the trust that individuals and organizations must have implemented in the development of data sharing ecosystems. As highlighted in the joint opinion of the EDPB and the EDPS [9], success will also depend on the establishment of robust data governance and effective safeguards for the rights and interests of natural persons that are fully compliant with the GDPR. This is where data protection engineering has a very important role to play. Legislative initiatives that promote data sharing are sectoral and cross-sectoral instruments aimed at making data available by regulating the reuse of public and private ownership data, including personal data. They also facilitate data exchange by creating intermediaries and novel exchange environments where the involved parties can share data and facilities reliably and securely.  

Data protection engineering, as described in [10], can be a key factor in building a trusted sharing environment, where organizations can present data without revealing personal data or sensitive business information, or by revealing personal data with an adequate level of protection. This aligns with the spirit of the data protection by design concept prescribed in Article 25 of the GDPR; safeguards must be integrated and designed into the processing. Data protection engineering offers the possibility of addressing the growing capabilities of transmission, storage, and processing technologies without diminishing their innovation potential, while simultaneously mitigating emerging risks to individuals' privacy and economic risks to businesses.  

As noted by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) in its guidelines on data protection by design and by default [11] in an increasingly digital world, adherence to data protection by design plays a crucial role in promoting privacy and data protection. It is crucial that data holders understand the principles of data protection and the rights and freedoms of data subjects, and implement appropriate measures and necessary safeguards to reinforce these principles and enable the exercise of these rights. Each technical and organizational measure must produce the expected results in the specific context in which the processing takes place.  

Therefore, special attention must be paid to identifying the main paradigms of data protection engineering in data sharing and to understanding the types of safeguards that must be implemented in all possible scenarios. The following chapters of this document will exemplify these paradigms and the relevant technical safeguards in more detail through practical use cases. These use cases primarily focus on the healthcare sector; however, the technologies and techniques presented are equally applicable to other areas of application.  

Data protection engineering can be a key factor in building a shared trusted environment with an adequate level of protection.

2. DATA SHARING PRACTICES IN THE HEALTHCARE SECTOR  
One area where data sharing presents an opportunity is undoubtedly the healthcare sector. Sharing health data can enhance coordination and collaboration between public and private healthcare entities to provide effective personalized healthcare and achieve public health objectives, as well as to conduct scientific research (including clinical trials) [12]. Data sharing in the healthcare sector also has cross-border dimensions, as noted in the Directive on Cross-Border Healthcare and currently in the proposed EU Health Data Spaces [7]. However, there are several risks to the protection of personal data arising from the sensitive nature of health data under Article 95 of the GDPR and the fact that ensuring compliance with data protection principles such as transparency and data minimization requires a very thorough assessment and a cautious "by design" implementation approach [12].  
Health data includes biomedical data, electronic medical records (e.g., medical histories stored and processed in a hospital), and data generated by individuals themselves, e.g., data from wearable devices [14]. In the context of sharing health data for various purposes, it is necessary to efficiently address the following properties or requirements [15].  
• Data for the diagnosis and treatment of individual patients must be identifiable.  
• The (same) data for medical research (possibly on a large scale) must be duly pseudonymized, in order to ensure that it is unlikely that a researcher can re-identify them (unless the user gives explicit consent for processing without pseudonymization, which can be revoked at any time)6, as well as the existence of the ability to eliminate the link [16] between two different datasets for different purposes.  
• There must be the capability to manage multiple sources of patient data, including wearable devices and applications.  

It is noteworthy that the need to minimize data encompasses these three requirements horizontally.  
Other data protection requirements that must also be met include transparency for the data subject and data security.  
2.1 USER-CONTROLLED PERSONAL DATA SHARING  
A basic approach to ensuring transparency for the user consists of allowing them to control who will have access to their data, as well as for how long and to which part of their data.

However, this approach has some limitations, primarily in terms of practicality and efficiency. If the same data needs to be shared with multiple entities, the user must share the same data multiple times, each time encrypted with the public key of the relevant entity. This inevitably leads to redundancy, which becomes a predominant problem, especially in the case of large volumes of data that are constantly being produced. Additionally, potential recipients may not necessarily be known in advance, and consequently, for each new access that is to be granted, a new encryption would be necessary.  
2.1.1 Attribute-based encryption  
A cryptographic technique to overcome the aforementioned limitations is Attribute-Based Encryption (ABE), which was first introduced in 2004 under the term Fuzzy Identity-Based Encryption. ABE is a special case of asymmetric encryption, in which data can be encrypted with an ABE public key, but, unlike "classic" public key encryption, there can be more than one decryption key, each linked to small pieces of additional information related to the data, referred to as attributes. In practice, the decryption keys are generated using a generic ABE master secret key, which must remain private.  
Reviewing the previously analyzed use case, we will now describe how ABE can be used to implement user-controlled data sharing through a cloud service. Our scenario is largely based on the implementation described in [19]. However, it should be noted that today’s cloud infrastructures and services offer a wide range of possibilities and can carry out part of the processing operations, in addition to the transmission of the data. However, these applications fall outside the scope of the use cases described in this report.  
Figure 3: Cloud-stored encoded objects  

The user's data is generated by their device and assigned to relevant objects, describing specific attributes related to them, such as the date of origin, the type of object, etc. These attributes will later be used to define the access control mechanism for this data. The data is then encrypted with the ABE master key and uploaded to the cloud.

When a third party requests access to a user's data, User A creates an access policy for that third party. This policy specifies which exact properties, based on the already defined attributes, the data must meet to grant access. Next, the user's device "translates" the policy, for example file="blood pressure" and year>=2021 AND recipient="Doctor D", into a corresponding ABE decryption key and sends the key to the other party (the cloud provider does not have access to this decryption key). Once Doctor D receives this decryption key, he will only be able to locally decrypt the corresponding data that satisfies the access policy defined by the user, as the ABE decryption key can only decrypt a subset of the dataset.  
However, this approach allows for "tagging" the data at an early stage, which is not always a straightforward task, whether due to the capabilities of the device or the interaction required by the user. Additionally, an appropriate selection of attributes in relation to subsequent access policies may not always be obvious. However, in a user-controlled data exchange model, it is inevitable that the user can make decisions about their data. Furthermore, the cloud provider continues to collect information related to the metadata of each communication between the user and each of the parties.  

2.1.2 Proxy Re-Encryption  
Another advanced cryptographic technology that enables user-controlled data sharing is Proxy Re-Encryption (PRE) [20]. This is a specific type of asymmetric encryption that allows re-encrypting a set of already encrypted data from one public key to another, without the proxy having access to the unencrypted dataset. It is considered a very good approach when the entity with which the data will be shared is not known at the time of the initial encryption or when sharing will be carried out through unreliable infrastructures.  
Delving into the previously described use case, User A encrypts their data with their own public key and uploads it to the cloud. When a third party, for example, Doctor D, requests access to the data, the user generates a re-encryption key, using their own private key and Doctor D's public key. The re-encryption key can be sent to the cloud provider, which can now transform the encrypted dataset corresponding to the encryption using Doctor D's public key, as shown in Figure 5 below. In this way, only Doctor D can decrypt the data using his private key.

PRE allows for cryptographically strengthened access control in a user-controlled sharing model. Additionally, it enables the data owner to delegate access once encrypted, which is important because in a typical sharing scenario, it is not always possible to identify the recipient entities in advance.  
In general, PRE can be considered a good solution when sharing occurs through unreliable infrastructures, such as cloud infrastructure. Furthermore, since re-encryption can only be performed on the initially encrypted dataset, the user must have a good understanding during the initial encryption of the data that would be shared later. It is worth noting, however, that ABE and PRE can be used in combination [22], allowing for a more refined user-controlled data sharing.  

2.2 EXCHANGE OF HEALTH DATA FOR MEDICAL AND RESEARCH PURPOSES BY HEALTHCARE PROVIDERS  
Another typical scenario of data exchange in the healthcare sector is the management of Electronic Health Records (EHR) by healthcare providers. Broadly speaking, EHRs are an electronic version of a patient's medical history that contains all key medical data related to that person's conditions, medical test results, treatments, medications, etc. EHRs are usually managed with a central repository at the national level, and users can authorize access to their data to treating physicians or medical institutions. During the pandemic, the need for large-scale data collection projects became even more apparent, in an attempt to support not only the treatment of patients but also scientific research and forecasting.

2.2.1 Polymorphic Encoding and Pseudonymization  
Leveraging the advantages of polymorphic encoding, Polymorphic Encoding and Pseudonymization (PEP) [15] has been proposed as a means to address the challenges described above. The main property of polymorphic encoding is that personal data can be encrypted in such a way that it is not necessary to determine in advance who can decrypt it. This can be decided later through transformations of the encrypted text that allow decryption using different cryptographic keys. This transformation can be performed blindly, so that the party carrying it out, the transcriber, cannot access the unencrypted dataset. Therefore, the transcriber "transforms" the encrypted dataset into another version that only the recipient can decrypt [15]. The transcriber can be an entity within the organization (for example, the medical center or the hospital) or outside the organization, such as a cloud provider. Regarding pseudonymization, PEP also uses the transcriber as the pseudonymization entity, as described in [23]. Each individual is assigned different pseudonyms for each third party requesting access to their data, thus avoiding the linking of pseudonyms among multiple third parties.  

Returning to the use case of large-scale data collection projects in the healthcare sector mentioned earlier, each patient has a unique identifier. The transcriber transforms this identifier into several pseudonyms depending on the recipient and the context or purpose of sharing the data. Each pseudonym is communicated to each recipient along with the polymorphic encrypted data. Since a new pseudonym is generated for each recipient, the pseudonyms used for the same patient cannot be linked, making them unlinkable and preserving the confidentiality of the patient's data. As shown in Figure 7, when the recipient is a doctor, the transcriber re-encrypts the patient's health data and transmits it to the corresponding doctor.

3. DATA SHARING  
THROUGH THIRD-PARTY SERVICES  
In addition to the use cases of data sharing, where one entity shares data directly with another entity, which is the final recipient of the data, there are also cases of secondary, indirect data sharing that may require a user to perform specific actions. In this type of data sharing, the sharing occurs as part of another process or service, where the data is processed through some secondary channel or entity before reaching its primary recipient. Often, this data sharing is not transparent to users. Therefore, this issue must be addressed by engineers, architects, and developers specialized in privacy. For example, this data sharing occurs when an application integrates a third-party service, which is also managed by an entity different from the primary recipient or sender of the data [26]. Although this is an established practice in software development, there are cases where it is detrimental to user privacy, e.g., a component or service used may share data with a third party—sometimes without the system architects and developers being aware of it.

3.1 PUSH NOTIFICATIONS FOR MOBILE DEVICES  
Push notifications for mobile devices are an important communication channel between mobile applications and their users. Through push messages, a mobile application provider can instantly send a message to its users. Push notifications can provide timely information from the application provider to the user or users and, potentially, provoke a reaction from them.  
Push notifications for mobile devices can convey different types of content, such as text, images, external links, and in-app links, etc. In addition to marketing purposes, push notifications can also be used to provoke a reaction from the user or users, or to indicate to users that they should provide certain information, carry out a specific process, or offer a functionality. Hence, the timeliness of the push notification is often important and generally its main advantage over other communication channels, such as email or text messages. Push messages for mobile devices can be sent in bulk or individually (personalized).  
In the case of personalized notifications, the information transmitted may very well include personal data or pseudonyms as user application identifiers. Therefore, it is necessary to integrate additional technical and organizational measures to address the privacy threats described below. Currently, one of the most widely used platforms for push notifications is the cross-platform messaging Firebase Cloud Messaging (FCM),9 compatible with the two main mobile operating systems (iOS and Android).  
From an engineering perspective, developers often integrate code provided by third parties into their application. Although it may not be transparent to users, the infrastructure of push notifications for mobile devices involves, at least, two additional entities that are fundamental to its architecture in the mobile world. • Linkability: observation of the interaction between the two entities, including the frequency of interaction, types of messages exchanged, etc.;  
• Identifiability: messages that identify the user;  
• Disclosure: the content of the messages being sent may be disclosed, thus violating the confidentiality of the notification, as in many cases these messages are sent through the third party in clear text.  
3.1.1 Anonymous Notification Protocols (via Proxies)  
An approach to address the aforementioned privacy threats could be implemented through the use of private notification protocols, which allow the delivery of notification messages using multiple layers of anonymization or proxies, as also described in [10]. This approach requires the use of a chain of proxies, through which the notification messages could be mixed before reaching the end user. This type of protocol allows mobile push notification services to be sent to users without the intermediary nodes knowing either the original sender or the final recipient.

Based on the example described above, a notification message in this approach would be sent through a chain of proxies. The application provider would prepare the message and choose a random selection of intermediary nodes (proxies) through which the message would be routed. In this case, there is not one but a mix of notification mediation servers. Each of them only knows the address of the next mediation server, thus concealing information about the two end entities (application server and user). Furthermore, the communication between the notification servers (proxies) is encrypted with their corresponding public keys, thereby protecting against inadvertent disclosure. An example of this type of protocol is AnNotify [27], which supports the decoupling of the notification between the subscriber and the publisher, the decoupling of push notifications to a subscriber, and the privacy of the broadcast, concealing whether a subscriber is subscribed to a notification or not.  
Regardless of the chosen protocol, it is necessary to consider the potential implications for both the development and operation of the service that uses such a protocol, in order to provide practical viability and be adopted by developers. In this regard, various criteria can be used to evaluate the different options, such as ease of integration into the application, ease of maintenance, scalability, etc.  

3.1.2 End-to-End Encryption  
Encrypting notification messages is the most direct measure to address at least some of the privacy threats mentioned above (especially the threat of disclosure). Currently, the delivery of push notifications is not typically carried out using end-to-end encryption. Either no encoding is used at all, or only part of the communication is encrypted. In some cases, the developer or developers may decide to deliver the notification message between the application server and the notification agent using transport layer encryption (e.g., TLS), which provides encryption between these two entities. Additionally, a notification agent may choose to encrypt communication with the user (device). However, the encoding is partial and does not fully address the threat of disclosure through each of these entities. The notification agent decrypts the message before sending it to the user. A more suitable solution for this would be the implementation of end-to-end encryption at a minimum.  
The details of this approach are shown below in Figure 11. The application provider uses the user's public key and encrypts the content of the message being sent. Although the application server still uses the notification agent to send the message, it cannot decrypt it. It simply delivers the notification messages without being able to read the sent message. Then, upon receiving the notification message on their phone, the user can use their private key to decrypt it and read the sent message.

3.1.3 Design Strategies  
In addition to the technical options described above, decisions regarding the design of the architecture that integrate privacy by design could be used to address some of the threats discussed earlier. One of these decisions is, for example, to follow a two-step strategy. In the first step, the architecture must require that the use of push notifications in the application be limited. This can be achieved, for example, by using a “pull by default” strategy, whereby the functionality of push notifications is minimized and only used to transfer non-personal data. In these cases, it is sufficient to notify the user that there is an update or that interaction is required. Then, in the next step, the notification message, which includes personalized content, can be obtained directly from the application through direct communication with the application server.  

Following the previous example, the electronic health application could notify the user that a specific action is required or that there is a new message for them, but without displaying the message itself. The user can then proactively react by clicking on the application. However, it is clear that this is complementary to the other measures presented earlier, as it can reduce, for example, the threat of disclosure to third parties, but not eliminate it entirely. Furthermore, this approach poses a challenge, as it requires covering the full scope of the notification functions of an application. Therefore, its use is recommended as a complementary measure but not as the primary one, or when other measures are not feasible.

3.2.1 Relevance of attribute-based access to online platforms  
In 2022, the French data protection authority (CNIL) highlighted in [31] that it is not possible to aspire to absolute effectiveness in online age control, especially in the case of minors. Given the increasing requirements for age verification of minors in online services, the CNIL recommends the use of a trusted independent third party to promote data minimization and the unnecessary collection of personal data from service providers. The CNIL provides an overview of six possible existing age verification solutions but concludes that none of them meet all the required properties in terms of reliability, data protection, and security. In addition to this analysis, the CNIL's Digital Innovation Lab (LINC) has developed a privacy-preserving age verification system based on zero-knowledge proof.  
The CNIL's recommendation for a trusted third party and attribute-based authentication, which is closely related to the concept of zero-knowledge proof, seems to be a quite promising concept for the Digital Services Regulation [32] Art. 28.2 provisions on the online protection of minors. According to this provision, online platform providers shall not display ads on their interface based on profiling, as defined in Article 4, point 4, of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, using personal data of the service recipient when they are reasonably aware that the service recipient is a minor.  
Based on the previously discussed use case of trusted third parties, the guardian or parent of the minor could support the creation of an attribute-based identity for the minor using such a third-party service. Then the minor could navigate online platforms and utilize the functionalities of the attribute-based identity without having to reveal their full identity, date of birth, etc. This approach would satisfy the requirements of reliability, data protection, and security of the minor's data.

A key element of the GDPR is the right of data subjects to be properly informed and to control the use or other types of processing of their personal data. These rights of data subjects include, among others, aspects related to the principles of data protection of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency (i.e., right to information and access), non-linkability (i.e., data minimization, purpose limitation, and storage limitation, integrity, and confidentiality), and control by the data subject (intervenability) (i.e., right to erasure, right to rectification, right to object, as well as the right to lodge complaints with data protection authorities and to seek effective judicial remedies against data protection authorities; or the data controllers or processors).  
These fundamental rights of data subjects require closer examination regarding their application in data-sharing environments. While the previous sections cover multiple examples of data minimization techniques, removing information from data or restricting access to information to a smaller subset of data subjects, thus addressing the goal of protecting unlinkability, the other two protection goals require a different engineering approach. This chapter will address these aspects.

4.1.1 Purpose Limitations  
A key challenge in this landscape is the aspect of purpose limitation. Given that a data subject can arbitrarily choose to restrict the processing of their own data for certain purposes, it becomes necessary to closely examine the scope of purpose compatibility. How can an information system help determine whether the consent for the processing of personal data given for one purpose is compatible with the exact purpose of processing by the data user? What can be done to provide data intermediaries with the necessary certainty and potential guarantees for data sharing that complies with data protection?  
In the current environment of data production and usage, there exists a plethora of different purposes, which may or may not be compatible, overlap, or include one another. Furthermore, there is significant potential for conflict if the interpretation of the purposes of these permissions differs among the various parties involved. It should be anticipated that some time and space will be needed to arrive at a somewhat consolidated and generally accepted approach to address these issues of consent and binding purpose in data intermediary scenarios.  

4.1.2 Implementation Aspects  
In the technical aspect of engineering, the interaction between data subjects and data intermediaries can occur in multiple ways. When cookie policy banners and similar consent management systems are commonplace, it is possible to give and withdraw consent, with more or less functional technical interfaces to use, for example, in [36] and [37]. However, these systems typically form part of a website with which the data subject interacts, and only cover the services offered through this website or its service provider. Therefore, this does not resolve the issues posed by collecting consent in different modes of interaction, e.g., in smart cars, for wearable devices or medical implants, or for other IoT systems that do not provide a reasonable user interface for the data subject [38] and [39].  
Moreover, most consent management systems only cover a single website or service of a specific scope. They do not function, or do not do so explicitly, for cases where the initial interaction is not triggered by the data subject, but simply by a data user. Clearly, it is not possible to actively display a consent banner based on a website to a user who is not currently interacting with the website of a data user. Therefore, if the initiation of a new data processing activity is not carried out by the data subject but by the data intermediary, a different mode of interaction between the data subject and the data intermediary is necessary, in which the data intermediary can proactively contact the data subject to obtain new consent for a new case of data processing, e.g., for a new data user, or for a new purpose of data processing.  
Withdrawing consent and communicating restrictions on data processing are two other challenges of this interaction. Each of these processing operations is initially triggered by the data subject. The common approach in this case is for the data to be deleted by the intermediary or the user, along with an active notification directed to the data subject. Therefore, in engineering terms, the withdrawal of consent or the rights of rectification, deletion, or expression of restrictions on processing can be managed similarly by providing a communication interface (such as a website), directed at the data subject, that offers these interactions as services.  
However, a challenge for a data intermediary in these cases is to transmit such requests to all affected data users. This requires some management within the data intermediary and some interactions with the data user. Let’s start with the latter.

4.3.1 Coverage of Consent and Limitation of Purpose  
A key issue in the process of selecting data from a data subject that may or may not meet the criteria established by the data user is compliance with the purposes of data processing. If the legal basis for data processing is consent, does this consent cover the scope of the processing intended by the data user? Clearly, if the data subject has explicitly blacklisted the data user, the viability test is easy: the data of that data subject cannot be part of the response to the data user. However, there may be cases where the data intermediary must determine—and decide—whether to include these data in the response; e.g., to determine whether that transfer is compatible with the initial purpose of data processing.  
Sometimes, it may involve a comparison of attributes in an explicit list of restrictions, such as the identities of the data users or the processing countries, but sometimes, especially for the different purposes of processing, such a decision is not so trivial. When is the purpose defined by a data user for a specific case of data processing sufficiently covered by the purposes granted by a data subject in their consent? Depending on the exact conditions, the data intermediary must decide on one of several different options on how to proceed:  
• include the data, assuming the purposes are clearly compatible,  
• exclude the data, assuming the purposes are clearly incompatible,  
• contact the data subject to clarify whether the purposes are compatible or not, or to determine if the data subject would consent to the specific purpose of the processing in question,  
• consult relevant third parties, such as data protection authorities, for guidance on whether the purposes are compatible or not.

4.4 DATA ALTRUISM  
The concept of altruistic data sharing, also introduced in the Data Governance Regulation, refers to interested parties who agree to the use of their data for public interest purposes, such as scientific research or the improvement of public services. For example, data altruism may occur when a patient decides to allow the processing of medical data collected about them not only in the hospital but also by research institutions developing treatments. What is interesting about altruism is that typically no compensation is given to the interested party. However, this does not imply a lifetime waiver of fundamental rights to privacy and data protection, both for the interested party and for the organization processing the data.  
Regarding the management of this data in a data intermediary, consideration could be given to marking the data as disclosed under a “license” for the management of altruistically shared data, in order to properly address subsequent demands from data users regarding this data. Normally, permissions for processing this data are always granted, but data sharing must be documented to demonstrate the reason for the data disclosure in case the interested party files a complaint, for example, with a data protection authority regarding the rights of the interested parties. In that case, the data intermediary must be able to demonstrate the origin of the data and the initial permissions granted by the interested party at that time.

5. CONCLUSIONS  
When two or more parties decide to share their data, they become part of a broader data ecosystem in which they can leverage the combined dataset that allows for the discovery, through computation, of new information or trends related to individuals, groups of individuals, or society as a whole. The easiest and most direct way to achieve this goal would be to share the raw data held by each actor through technical interfaces, placing them in a common table (i.e., a single database), but this hypothetical option is not really feasible. In reality, what we seek are trusted shared environments that maximize the potential offered by secure sharing and use of personal data, while simultaneously respecting data protection principles.  
This report aimed to analyze specific cases of personal data sharing, primarily in the healthcare sector, and discuss how specific technologies and implementation considerations can support the engineering of personal data sharing in practice. The analysis covered everything from user-controlled data sharing to large-scale personal data collection and data sharing through third-party services.  
Despite the potential of the concept of data sharing and the policy and legislation of the Union in this area, there are still considerations regarding what the appropriate technical and organizational measures are and how to implement them in practice. The European legislative initiatives on data sharing described in Section 1.1 involve the processing of large amounts of data that will also include personal data. Therefore, in addition to the consistency of their provisions with the GDPR, it is important to eliminate any legal uncertainty regarding the roles and obligations, not only for natural persons, as highlighted by the EDPB and the SEPD in [9], but also for the entities participating in data sharing. To harness the potential of data sharing across the EU, professionals could receive guidance on what technologies and techniques can be considered, under what circumstances, and what data protection principles can be complied with.  
There are several commonly used (cryptographic) techniques (namely, asymmetric encryption, pseudonyms, access control, etc.) that are already recognized as capable of mitigating data protection risks. Some of these have been analyzed in Section 2, Section 3, and Section Error! Reference source not found. However, in emerging concepts such as data spaces and data intermediaries, the risks posed cannot always be adequately addressed solely with such techniques. This is due to the fact that stakeholders wish to preserve the confidentiality of the data they share, may not know in advance with whom they will share data, or may want to share accumulated datasets. Although advanced techniques exist that are still evolving, they should not be considered of purely academic interest, as there are practical implementations in real use case scenarios.  
Finally, since most of the technologies described above and in previous ENISA reports [10] and [40] are based on asymmetric cryptography, the arrival of quantum computing and its impact on the security of currently used asymmetric encryptions must be anticipated. After the deployment of data exchange infrastructures and services, we cannot expect them to cease functioning due to the possible inadequacy of asymmetric encryptions. This is where crypto-agility becomes relevant, as it allows for switching between algorithms, cryptographic primitives, and other encryption mechanisms without significant changes to the overall computing system or process.
