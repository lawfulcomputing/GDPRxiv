EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  
As the healthcare sector evolves into one that maximizes the benefits of technical advancements and adapts services to timely meet the growing needs of patients, additional challenges arise in terms of cybersecurity and data protection. Integrating new technologies into already complex IT infrastructures presents new challenges regarding data protection and cybersecurity.  
This is due to the increasing need to exchange and share information related to patients' health among various stakeholders. Therefore, it is important that, on one hand, entities that process personal data collect and subsequently process only the data they need, and on the other hand, that they adopt appropriate organizational and technical measures to protect such personal data.  
Pseudonymization is becoming an important security technique that provides a means to facilitate the processing of personal data while simultaneously offering robust guarantees to protect personal data and, therefore, safeguard the rights and freedoms of individuals.  
This report, which complements previous relevant work by ENISA, demonstrates how pseudonymization can be practically adopted to better protect health data during processing. There is no doubt that there is no one-size-fits-all solution regarding how and when to apply this technique; in fact, equally good results could be achieved with other solutions in specific cases, depending on protection requirements, utility, scalability, etc.  
Pseudonymization can range from a "simple" option to a very complex process, both technically and organizationally. For this reason, it is truly important to define both the goals and objectives of pseudonymization in each particular case as well as the processing operation.  
This report highlights the added value that pseudonymization has for the healthcare sector and demonstrates its applicability through simple yet concrete use cases. As a complement to ENISA's publications in this area, this report shows how these techniques can better protect personal data processed in the healthcare sector and, ultimately, promote such technical measures and raise awareness of their ease of use and application.

1. INTRODUCTION  
In recent decades, we have witnessed rapid advances in the development and adoption of new technologies. These swift technological changes have also reached the healthcare sector, which is digitalizing and continuously adopting new technologies to improve patient care, offer new services focused on home care for patients, and even in prevention plans.  

Integrating new technologies into already complex IT infrastructures poses new challenges regarding data protection and cybersecurity, as there is a growing need to exchange and share health-related information among various stakeholders, in some cases across countries, in order to provide better healthcare services. Therefore, it is important that entities processing personal data collect and subsequently process only the data they need and, furthermore, adopt appropriate organizational and technical measures to protect such data. Pseudonymization is a well-known measure that can significantly contribute to this end.  
In general terms, pseudonymization aims to protect personal data by concealing the identity of individuals in a dataset, for example, by replacing one or several personal identifiers with so-called pseudonyms (and adequately protecting the link between the pseudonyms and the initial identifiers).  
This is by no means a new process in the design of information systems, but it has received special attention following the adoption of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [1], in which pseudonymization is explicitly mentioned as a technique that can promote data protection by design (Article 25 of the GDPR), as well as the security of personal data processing (Article 32 of the GDPR).  

1.1 THE DIGITAL TRANSFORMATION OF THE HEALTHCARE SECTOR  
Health data has always been a valuable source of knowledge in the field of healthcare. Historically, this field has generated enormous amounts of data, both for patient treatment and for subsequent research and analysis. Data was primarily processed on paper, but in recent decades, the accessibility and quantity of digitized data have increased significantly.  
More recently, numerous new sources of health data have emerged as a result of the widespread use of electronic health records, health applications, and wearable electronic devices [2]. Additionally, advancements in computing power have facilitated the development of new data analysis techniques and machine learning that assist in diagnosis, treatment, and management in healthcare.  
As a result, approaches are changing, and hospitalizations are losing ground in favor of a more distributed healthcare system that is managed by both public and private operators, all in an environment closer to the patient's home, as shown in Chart 1.  

Chart 1: Shift in value in healthcare as a result of digital transformation [3]  

These technological advances have increased the demand for medical big data banks to provide solutions, for example, for disease diagnosis and phenotyping, modeling clinical outcomes, predicting early intervention strategies, precision medicine, etc. However, the growing processing of digitized medical data has also increased risks in terms of cybersecurity, data protection, and the likelihood of data security breaches.  
These risks and related threats have already been identified in relevant publications from ENISA in the field of e-health security [4], [5], and [6]. Relevant EU legal instruments, such as the SRI Directive [7], the General Data Protection Regulation [1], the Medical Devices Regulation [8], the Directive on the application of patients' rights in cross-border healthcare [9], etc., have imposed obligations on healthcare providers and manufacturers of medical products to ensure an adequate and uniform level of protection for medical data and the products and services that utilize them.

2. PSEUDONYMIZATION  
2.1 CONTEXT  
The GDPR defines pseudonymization in Article 4, paragraph 5, as: "the processing of personal data in such a manner that the data can no longer be attributed to a specific data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such additional information is kept separately and is subject to technical and organizational measures to ensure that the personal data cannot be attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person."  

In general terms, pseudonymization aims to protect personal data by concealing the identity of individuals (data subjects) in a dataset, for example, by replacing one or more identifiers of personal data with so-called pseudonyms and adequately protecting the link between the pseudonyms and the original identifiers. This link, often referred to as the pseudonymization secret, is typically stored in a mapping table and can be used to re-identify the person by associating the pseudonyms with the original data, as shown in Figure 2. Only the entity that initially performed the pseudonymization, often referred to as the "pseudonymization entity," can access the mapping table.  
Figure 2: Example of Pseudonymization  

Pseudonymization is one of the various "de-identification" techniques (such as aggregation, obfuscation, masking, etc.) aimed at eliminating the association between a set of identifying data and the data subject. Other definitions of pseudonymization, such as those from ISO and specifically ISO 25237:2017, "Health Informatics — Pseudonymization" [13], are based on this principle and are similar to the aforementioned GDPR definition.  

2.2 THE IMPORTANCE OF PSEUDONYMIZATION  
The main advantage of pseudonymization, when applied correctly, is that it allows for the concealment of a person's identity in the context of a specific dataset, making it impossible to associate the data with the individual in question. Therefore, it also helps to reduce the risk of linking personal data of a specific individual across different data processing contexts.  

Thus, for example, if a personal data breach occurs, pseudonymization makes it more difficult for those who are not the data controller to associate the data.

2.3 BASIC PSEUDONYMIZATION TECHNIQUES  
As explained in [10], [11], and [12], there are several pseudonymization techniques. The main differences between them lie in how the pseudonym is generated. The following table 1 provides a comprehensive summary of the most common techniques.  

Table 1: Summary of Basic Pseudonymization Techniques  
Technique  
Pseudonym Generator  
Counter  
Monotonic counter that starts with a certain value that increments when a new pseudonym is needed  
Random Number  
Random value extracted between a minimum limit and a maximum limit when a new pseudonym is needed  
Hash Function  
Cryptographic one-way function (non-reversible) that transforms the input personal data into fixed-length values  
Hashed Message Authentication Code (HMAC)  
Cryptographic one-way function (non-reversible) that adds a key making it less predictable than a hash function  
Encryption  
Bidirectional cryptographic function (reversible) that transforms the input personal data into values that can be transformed back into their original format using a key  

As mentioned in [10], although the hash function can significantly contribute to ensuring data integrity, it is generally considered a weak pseudonymization technique, as it is vulnerable to brute force and dictionary attacks. Similarly, counters are also considered a weak pseudonymization technique, as they cannot be scaled. A robust approach to generating pseudonyms may consist of using keyed hash functions as shown in chart 8, that is, hash functions whose output value depends not only on the input value but also on a secret key (salt). 2.4 CONSIDERATIONS ON PSEUDONYMIZATION  
Data controllers and processors can use pseudonymization techniques and related policies either jointly—using the same criteria—or separately. The first case usually occurs when the data controller needs the data processor to adopt the same criteria. Apart from the use of criteria, the most important difference would be the sharing of the correspondence table shown in Graph 2.  
If this table is not shared, the entity receiving pseudonymized data—provided that all mechanisms are applied correctly—would have no way to recover the original personal data. If the table is shared, it must be done with the same care as is given to the exchange of encryption keys, except for possible differences due to the larger volume of data.  
A data controller may ask processors to pseudonymize personal data or even instruct them on how to do so, especially if the pseudonymized data needs to be exchanged between different parties. Such specifications must include at least the following elements and always be based on the results of prior risk or impact assessments:  
▪ the target personal data (for example, a set of identifiers);  
▪ the technique to be used;  
▪ the parameters applicable to the technique (for example, counter logic, randomness management, employed algorithms, key lengths);  
▪ the policy to be used.  
Depending on the applicable requirements, which will likely include regulations, speed, simplicity, predictability, and budget, the technique and related parameters may vary.

3. USE CASES  
In an attempt to demonstrate the added value of pseudonymization in the healthcare sector, this section presents three use cases in which personal medical data subject to processing has been pseudonymized. Although specific pseudonymization techniques are not analyzed in depth, the different use cases aim to provide an overview of the possibilities and key aspects of their application, regarding the higher level of protection of the personal data processed due to the removal of direct personal identifiers.  
It should be noted that these use cases serve to demonstrate the application of pseudonymization and do not intend to cover operational use cases in their entirety. In such cases, a more in-depth analysis of the context, the data processors, and the data processing involved, as well as all relevant processing operations, would be necessary.  
3.1 THE EXCHANGE OF PATIENT HEALTH DATA  

In current medical practice, the exchange of data between organizations is a common practice and is primarily used for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. This includes exchanges between different units of the same entity (for example, a hospital) and the exchange between specific recipients (for example, medical professionals, laboratories, etc.).  

For example, Anne undergoes a series of medical tests at the medical laboratory Mlabs that must subsequently be reviewed by her doctor. When Anne visits Mlabs for the first time, she is asked to provide contextual health information (medication, medical history, etc.) and personal data (name, contact details, social security number), and Mlabs assigns her a Patient_ID. From this moment on, all data, such as results, etc., related to a medical check-up are associated with this Patient_ID and not with her personal data, as shown in Graph 6. The patient identifier, which can even be a simple progressive record counter, is used to dissociate Anne's personal data from the results and acts as a simple yet effective pseudonym.  

The data transferred from Mlabs to the treating physician does not contain Anne's personal identifiers, but only the pseudonym and the results of the medical tests.

Once the medical tests are completed, Mlabs will associate the laboratory results with the ID_Patient and not with personal data. When Anne requests a copy of the results, Mlabs will have to look for the ID_Patient assigned to her and then perform the matching, as the ID_Patient and personal data are stored separately. If the patient requests that the results be sent directly to her treating physician, who will be responsible for analyzing them and deciding if further tests or medication are needed, Mlabs can inform the physician of the results of the ID_Patient assigned to Anne. The association between Anne and ID_Patient must be communicated to the treating physician only once; thereafter, the physician will already know that this specific ID_Patient refers to Anne. However, at this point, the physician gains access to the pseudonymization secret and can directly identify Anne, which ends the level of protection of personal data that the previously performed pseudonymization process offers. This level of protection would remain in effect if Anne only shared the results of the medical tests and not the pseudonymization secret.  
The same principle can be extended to an entity, such as a hospital, where a patient undergoes different tests in various clinical units. Once again, the units conducting the tests will process the data using the ID_Patient, and the treating physician will be able to relate that identifier to Anne as shown below. Access to medical data among the practitioners of the same medical center (for example, a hospital) must be based on authentication and relevant user rights. Depending on the processing operation, physicians may have access to the pseudonymization secret, allowing them to directly identify Anne, or have access only to Anne's pseudonymized identifier, meaning they could not directly identify her.

3.2 CLINICAL TRIALS  

Clinical trials study new procedures and medical treatments and evaluate their effects and possible adverse effects. They are considered a prerequisite for obtaining the necessary authorizations from the competent authorities. Generally, they are not conducted by the manufacturer (for example, the pharmaceutical company), but by independent organizations known as "clinical research organizations."  
A typical case is the so-called double-blind study, in which a cohort of individuals with similar characteristics (for example, patients with the same disease) is divided into two subgroups. Members of one group receive the medication being tested, while members of the other group receive a placebo. In a double-blind study, neither the participants nor the researcher knows who belongs to each group. During the trial, the same medical data is collected for both cohorts. Once all the data is obtained, researchers can compare the results of each group and determine if the new medical mediation (independent variable) has had any impact on the treatment (dependent variable).  
Beyond merely comparing patient data records, another common use of this medical data is to detect associations and patterns between different variables (for example, age, sex, occupation), with a view to identifying the upper limits of efficiency and effectiveness, as well as additional safety information, for example, regarding dosage. Therefore, in these research organizations, data from all patients must be analyzed to establish common patterns with medical relevance.  
For these analyses, the identity of the participants does not have direct importance, and researchers typically do not need to access the actual identity of the participants in the research whose data the clinical research organization analyzes. However, the

3.3 MONITORING OF PATIENT-GENERATED HEALTH DATA  

Currently, smart wearable devices monitor vital signs such as heart rate, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure. Regular monitoring of vital signs is a common practice in patient care and helps to early recognize abnormal physiological parameters. However, it is common for only the patient themselves to see the measurements and contact the doctor when they observe an abnormal value.  
The normal ranges for a person's vital signs usually vary based on age, weight, sex, and overall health status, making it difficult to set predefined values. This use case contemplates the provision of a health monitoring system (HMS) in which the doctor can access medical information and monitor vital signs and also receive notifications of their abnormal values, through appropriately predefined value ranges.

For example, John has been diagnosed with cardiovascular disease and arrhythmia; bradycardia or tachycardia can increase the risk of stroke. John's doctor has defined three sets of values concerning his resting heart: low, normal, and high. John's wearable device regularly monitors his heart rate and physical activity, and alerts him when his resting heart rate exceeds the low or high thresholds. At the same time, this notification can also be sent to the treating physician, who can assist John immediately or contact him later to advise him on what to do next.  
Instead of transmitting John's heart values and data, the wearable device can perform the pseudonymization process and transmit a pseudonym for John and the range of parameters of his resting heart rate. The doctor will receive the pseudonym and will be able to access the corresponding patient information and the patient's resting heart rate status. Just as in the use case analyzed in section 3.1, if the doctor has access to the pseudonymization secret and can directly identify John, the pseudonymization process will primarily be considered a security measure in favor of confidentiality during transmission, rather than a data protection measure.

4. CONCLUSIONS  
As the healthcare sector evolves towards one that maximizes the benefits of technical advancements and adapts services to timely meet the growing needs of patients of all ages and cultures worldwide, additional challenges arise in terms of cybersecurity and data protection.  
Pseudonymization is becoming an important security technique that provides a means to facilitate the processing of personal data while simultaneously offering robust guarantees to protect personal data and, therefore, safeguard the rights and freedoms of individuals.  
This report, which complements previous relevant work by ENISA, demonstrates how pseudonymization can be practically adopted to better protect the exchange of health data.  
There is no doubt that there is no one-size-fits-all solution regarding how and when to apply pseudonymization; in fact, equally good results could be achieved with other solutions in specific cases, depending on protection requirements, utility, scalability, etc.  
Starting from a simple token, pseudonymization can range from a "simple" option to a very complex process, both technically and organizationally. For this reason, it is truly important to define the goals and objectives of pseudonymization in each particular case and each processing operation. To this end, good practices and relevant examples of pseudonymization in the context of the GDPR can be very valuable for healthcare providers and developers of health applications.  
Developers and national and European regulators should promote the exchange of good practices and provide practical guidance on the use of pseudonymization.  
Advancements in technology and the types of healthcare services offered to the population could compromise the effectiveness and applicability of current pseudonymization solutions. This is relevant not only for the choice of technique but also for the overall design of the pseudonymization process, which includes, in particular, the protection of additional information (i.e., information that allows associating pseudonyms with the initial identifiers).  
The proposed pseudonymization solutions largely depend on technological advancements, and over time, problems or application limitations may arise for each technique.  
The research community must continue to work on data protection and security engineering, including more advanced pseudonymization techniques and their possible applications, with the support of EU institutions in terms of policy guidance and research funding.

ABOUT ENISA  
The European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) is the Union's agency whose aim is to achieve a high common level of cybersecurity across Europe. Established in 2004 and strengthened by the EU Cybersecurity Regulation, the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity contributes to the EU's cybersecurity policy, enhances the reliability of ICT products, services, and processes through cybersecurity certification programs, cooperates with Member States and EU bodies, and helps Europe prepare for tomorrow's cybersecurity challenges. Through knowledge sharing, capacity building, and awareness-raising, the Agency collaborates with its key stakeholders to strengthen trust in the connected economy, boost the resilience of the Union's infrastructure, and ultimately protect society and citizens in Europe digitally. For more information about ENISA and its work, you can visit: www.enisa.europa.eu.
