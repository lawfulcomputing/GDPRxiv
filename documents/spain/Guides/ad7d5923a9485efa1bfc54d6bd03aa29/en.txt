INTRODUCTION  
Information is a fundamental resource in today's society for decision-making in all aspects and sectors. The processing and exploitation of large volumes of information can offer multiple benefits to society, provided that respect for individuals' rights, their privacy, and the protection of their personal data is maintained. In this context, the anonymization of personal data acquires special value as a formula that can ensure the advancement of the information society without undermining respect for data protection.  
To achieve this objective, it is necessary to guarantee the irreversibility of anonymization, and for this, both the available information sources in different media, particularly through the internet, and the applicable technology in the processes of anonymization and reidentification must be assessed.  
The anonymization of data should be considered as a way to eliminate the possibilities of identifying individuals. The advancement of technology and the available information makes it difficult to guarantee absolute anonymity, especially over time; however, in any case, anonymization will offer greater privacy guarantees to individuals.  
The techniques that can be used must be evaluated both for the processes of anonymization and for possible attempts at reidentification of the affected individuals, so that the effort of reidentifying the subjects entails a sufficiently high cost that it cannot be approached in terms of a cost-benefit relationship. In other words, since anonymization would never be absolute, the reidentification of subjects must involve a considerable effort, making any potential benefit negligible or making such effort unfeasible for the person or entity with access to the anonymized information.  
Therefore, in an anonymization process, it is essential to assess the risks of reidentification a posteriori and how the confidentiality of the anonymized personal information will be guaranteed. The risks of reidentification of subjects should be addressed as a residual risk that must be managed and not as a failure to comply with the security measures for the protection of personal data. In this sense, the use of measures such as, for example, mechanisms and protocols for anonymization that involve defining the work team, organizational measures, staff training, confidentiality measures, the use of possible standards, the use of codes of good practices, etc., explicitly define the intention and diligence of the data controller in the processes of anonymizing personal data.  
The Agency considers that the development of data anonymization techniques acquires vital importance to guarantee the protection of personal data in the development of studies and research of social, scientific, and economic interest, and with these guidelines, it aims to provide useful guidelines for implementing these techniques, while also promoting their knowledge and encouraging their development and dissemination.  
In this document, the term "chain" is used to refer to a set of technical measures aimed at the concealment, masking, or dissociation of personal data. These measures constitute what has been termed the "anonymization chain" or "confidentiality chain," the breaking of which implies the possibility of reidentifying the interested parties.  
1
The design of the confidentiality chain involves specific study and analysis for each block of personal data, which must be adapted to each specific situation and each block of data intended for anonymization. This document provides some guidelines that can be considered by those responsible for the processing of personal data, encouraging them to implement anonymization procedures. 

The processes and techniques described in these guidelines may be useful both for the anonymization processes themselves and for reinforcing the security of the information, providing greater guarantees in the processing and preservation of personal data. 

In this regard, the measures proposed can be applied to data of natural persons, as well as to data of legal entities such as administrations, companies, educational centers, associations, etc., from which information is collected and compiled and that, for various reasons, do not wish to be identified or have made anonymity a condition for providing that data for publication. 

The purpose of the anonymization process is to eliminate or minimize the risks of reidentification of the anonymized data while maintaining the accuracy of the results of the processing of the same; that is, in addition to avoiding the identification of individuals, the anonymized data must ensure that any operation or processing that may be carried out after anonymization does not lead to a distortion of the actual data. A massive analysis of the data or macrodata that may derive from the anonymized data should not differ from the analysis that could be obtained if it had been conducted with non-anonymized data. 

In the anonymization process, the break in the identification chain of individuals must occur. This chain consists of microdata or direct identification data and indirect identification data. Microdata allows for the direct identification of individuals, while indirect identification data are cross-referenced data from the same or different sources that may allow for the reidentification of individuals, such as information from other databases of the same or another responsible party, social networks, search engines, blogs, etc. 

In designing the anonymization process, it will be necessary to foresee the consequences of a potential reidentification of individuals that could cause harm or diminish their rights. It will also be necessary to anticipate a hypothetical loss of information due to negligence of the involved personnel, due to a lack of an adequate anonymization policy, or due to an intentional disclosure of secrets that could lead to the loss of the identification variables or identification keys of individuals.

INDIRECT IDENTIFICATION: the identification that may occur as a result of information from one or several sources that, by itself or in combination with other factors, may allow for the reidentification of individuals when their data has been anonymized. For example, the combination of sex, age, place of birth, and suffering from a particular disease may allow for the indirect identification of a specific individual. 

1. ANONYMIZATION
Below are some principles to consider in an anonymization process.  
Anonymization processes should be approached from the concept of data protection by design, which means that privacy requirements will be taken into account from the initial stages of the design of the information system or the product used for the anonymization process and throughout the entire lifecycle of that product or information system. The concept of privacy by design in anonymization processes can be summarized in the application of the following principles:  
1. Proactive Principle. The protection of privacy is the primary objective of anonymization, and its management must be carried out proactively rather than reactively. From the conceptual inception of the design of the information system or product to be used in the anonymization process, necessary measures will be taken to ensure the privacy of individuals. Privacy cannot be guaranteed a posteriori as a result of remedying existing gaps in the anonymization process or harms caused to the data subjects, so it is essential to ensure the non-existence of potential re-identification chains of the data subjects in the anonymized data.  
An important measure at the initial state of the concept of an information system or product used in anonymization processes is to carry out an initial classification of the data and have a scale or gradient of sensitivity of the information. This classification can be qualitative or quantitative and will serve as a reference within an organization.  
For example, a classification scheme can be developed consisting of a scheme based on at least three levels of identification of individuals (microdata, indirect identification data, and sensitive data), where a quantitative value is assigned to each of the identification variables. The scale will be known to all personnel involved in the anonymization process and will be a fundamental key to consider in the risk analysis or Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) of the anonymization process.  
2. Privacy by Default Principle. The first conceptual requirement in the design of an information system will be to guarantee the confidentiality of the data subjects. Therefore, it is advisable to safeguard privacy from the outset, taking into account the granularity or degree of final detail that the anonymized data should have. In this sense, maintaining a qualitative or quantitative scale as referred to in the previous point is an undoubtedly useful tool for the elimination of variables according to pre-established granularity criteria.

3. Objective Privacy Principle. As a result of the DPIA, there will be a threshold of risk or residual risk index of re-identification. This risk index will be assumed by the data controller as an acceptable risk and will be taken into consideration for the design of the anonymization process. Finally, the residual risk threshold of re-identification will be known by the recipient of the anonymized information and, when the anonymized data is for public use, it will also be made publicly known, informing the individuals or entities that use the information about this risk.  
4. Full Functionality Principle. From the outset of the design of the information system, the final utility of the anonymized data will be taken into account, ensuring, as far as possible, the non-existence of distortion in relation to the non-anonymized data. In this way, the utility of the anonymized data will be guaranteed.  
In some cases, in order to ensure the privacy of individuals, it may be necessary to use geographic range distortions, as in the case of individuals with extremely rare pathologies. In these cases, the recipient of the information will be informed of the reason for the use of geographic range variances or any other type of variances that may have been used in the anonymization process (temporal, length, etc.).  
5. Privacy in the Information Lifecycle Principle. The measures that guarantee the privacy of the data subjects are applicable throughout the entire lifecycle of the information starting from the non-anonymized information. For example, at the beginning of the anonymization process, the identification variables that are not deemed necessary or that cannot be anonymized will be eliminated, and once the exploitation of the anonymized information begins, measures will continue to be taken to ensure the privacy of the data subjects, such as audits to verify the use of the information, procedures for the destruction of the information, etc.  
6. Information and Training Principle. One of the keys to ensuring the privacy of the data subjects is the training and information provided to the personnel involved in the anonymization process and in the exploitation of the anonymized information. Throughout the lifecycle of the information, all personnel with access to the anonymized or non-anonymized data will be appropriately trained and informed about their obligations. The design of the information system or product used for the anonymization process will take this need into account from its conceptual design, considering the profiles and needs of each of the actors involved in the anonymization process.
In an anonymization process, it is advisable to define a protocol of action. The elements described in this section can serve as guidance when defining this protocol, but it is in no case intended to be a closed conceptual or operational scheme; rather, it reflects the proposal of a structure that can be taken into account in anonymization processes. In the development of an anonymization process, it would be possible to segregate functions according to profiles or roles. Some of the profiles that may be considered within the anonymization process are as follows:

–  
Recipient or data controller of the anonymized personal information.

Risk assessment team.

Pre-anonymization team and anonymization team.

It is advisable that the necessary functions and the details of the scope of each of the profiles that may ultimately be designated in relation to them are defined in the anonymization processes. It is recommended to ensure, as far as possible, that each of the actors will generally act within the scope of their competence, independently of the other actors. Logically, the number of actors involved and their independence will be proportional to the volume of data processing and the complexity of the anonymization process, making it possible and sometimes necessary for some of the involved actors to hold several of the functions that have been defined.

3. PHASES OF ANONYMIZATION  
3.1. DEFINITION OF THE WORKING TEAM  

Anonymization:  
–  
Data Controller: decide on the purpose and objectives that the information must respond to.  
–  
Data Protection Officer or, if applicable, DPD: promote the conduct of prior impact assessments on privacy in anonymization processes, verify the execution of anonymization processes, ensure the independence of roles and functions, report to the information and data processing controller on the anonymization processes, promote compliance audits of the anonymization processes, or respond to citizens' requests for information regarding their anonymized or non-anonymized personal data, among other functions.  
–  
Recipient of the anonymized information and data controller of anonymized data: both are the final recipients of the anonymized information and decide on the requirements of the information according to the final objectives for which it is intended.  
–  
Risk assessment team: responsible for conducting the initial risk assessment, evaluating the results of the anonymization process, auditing the anonymization procedure, auditing the use of anonymized information, and ultimately responsible for ensuring that the anonymized file meets the requirements related to the residual risk of re-identification, conducting ethical hacking tests aimed at assessing the strength of the procedures that must guarantee the irreversibility of the anonymization, or conducting tests in which the possible participation of the systems team must be taken into account, among others.  
–  
Pre-anonymization and anonymization team: responsible for determining the variables that need to be anonymized and proposing anonymization techniques that will be selected or validated by the risk assessment team. They will also be responsible for eliminating those variables whose anonymization is not viable or does not fit the purpose of the anonymized data, facilitating the final work of the anonymization team and ensuring the value of the anonymized data. The anonymization team may consist of individuals who have participated in the pre-anonymization of personal data, although it is also possible to segregate their function and keep it separate from the functions of the pre-anonymization team. The anonymization team will be responsible for choosing the necessary anonymization techniques and their application.  
–  
Information security and process team: this team will be responsible for ensuring the security measures that may be necessary during the life cycle of the anonymized information and during the anonymization processes, in addition to evaluating the results of the DPIA and implementing measures aimed at mitigating risks to the anonymized personal information.  
Another possibility would be to establish a multidisciplinary team that would be responsible for the prior study of the feasibility of the anonymization process and the use of the anonymized information.
This team would be responsible for defining the process and the involved agents as well as the functions of each of them, ensuring that there is no task that, being necessary to guarantee confidentiality, has not been assigned to a specific responsible party. This possible scheme of segregation of roles and tasks ultimately presents a potential functional and organizational inventory that could serve as guidance when planning the anonymization processes. 

As already indicated, it would be advisable for the definition of the working team and the different involved actors to be carried out according to the principle of professional independence, so that each of them acts within the scope of the functions assigned to them and, whenever possible, efforts are made to avoid having the same person involved in several different profiles or functions. 

One of the objectives of the principle of independence of functions is to prevent an error that occurs at a certain level from being supervised and approved at another distinct level by the same person. However, the conditions and resources available to address an anonymization process may not allow for a thorough segregation of functions, and it may be necessary for some members of the working team to perform several different roles. 

It is essential that there is a document defining the working team in which each of the functions of the involved persons is expressly delineated. This document must be approved by the information responsible party and updated whenever necessary by the responsible party for anonymization. In cases where segregation and independence of functions are not possible, the reasons or situations that condition the independence or segregation of functions must be noted in the document. The objective of this document is to ensure that each task aimed at the anonymization or definitive dissociation of personal data has an associated responsible party. 

The processes of data anonymization have a direct impact on the resources of an organization. This effort associated with the anonymization processes (economic, technological, human, etc.) must be adequate to the objectives and requirements of the anonymization process. The effort required will depend on the measures that need to be implemented to guarantee the privacy of personal data, adapting the necessary measures to the purpose of the anonymized data and to the possible risks of reidentification of individuals.

3. IDENTIFICATION OF RISKS: The first aspect to consider in the risk analysis is the initial cataloging of risks according to three categories:  
a. Known existing re-identification risks  
b. Potential re-identification risks  
c. Unknown risks  
Each identified risk in the aforementioned categories will be assigned a specific value from a quantitative or qualitative scale based on the probability of occurrence. The set of all risks will result in the risk catalog of a block of information intended for anonymization.  
Some of the risks that may be taken into account are: re-identification risks due to correlation with other data sets, risks of breaching the duty of confidentiality due to unauthorized access to non-anonymized information, risks of disclosure of anonymization keys for the information, the existence of a potential attacker or adversary, or what may be considered the role of the "pursuer," or the existence of a subject who knows the identity of a person in a block of information and seeks to obtain more information, etc.  

4. ASSESSMENT OF EXISTING RISKS: based on the set of assets and the catalog of existing risks, a categorization of each of the re-identification risks that have been detected will be carried out. This categorization will be taken into account in the phases of the anonymization process and especially when eliminating variables.  

5. SAFEGUARDS: for each of the identified risks, one or more safeguards must be proposed aimed at preventing a specific risk from materializing. Finally, a catalog of risks and a catalog of safeguards will be obtained, categorized based on the criticality of the assets that need to be protected.  

6. QUANTIFYING THE IMPACT: it is advisable to conduct an accumulated or compounded risk analysis in which each asset inherits the risks of the assets it depends on. In this sense, it may be useful to create diagrams that represent the flows of risks and from which a graphical image of the critical areas can be obtained, along with the safeguards that have been assigned and the asset on which the safeguards are implemented.  
Once the risks and safeguards have been identified, it is time to assess or quantify the impact of the possible materialization of a risk. It should be noted that the impact can be tangible (for example, material damages, possible compensations, etc.) or intangible (loss of trust, deterioration of the image of the data controller, stigmatization of the data subjects, etc.), but in both cases, we must assign a quantitative or qualitative value to the possible impact.  

7. RISK REPORT: the report of the resulting risks will be executive in nature and will clearly reflect the existing risks and their level of criticality according to the scale that has been used for their identification. The working team conducting the risk assessment will present to the data controller and the security team a proposal with the acceptable risk threshold for each anonymization process, so that each of them can issue their opinion.  

8. DETERMINATION OF THE ACCEPTABLE RISK THRESHOLD: the data controller, at the proposal of the risk assessment team, the anonymization team, and the information security team, will ultimately decide on the acceptable risks resulting from the anonymization process. All personnel involved in the anonymization processes will be aware of the acceptable risk threshold, and the recipient of the anonymized information will have access to the risk catalog prepared by the data controller in order to adopt the appropriate measures to mitigate the possible consequences that may arise from the identification of individuals in the anonymized data.  

9. MANAGEMENT OF ACCEPTABLE RISKS: for each of the risks that have been determined as acceptable, measures will be established aimed at mitigating the possible impact on the privacy of individuals who could be re-identified.  

10. FINAL REPORT: The possible measures that are established will be known by all individuals involved in the anonymization processes and in the processing of anonymized data. All personnel involved will have knowledge and training regarding the actions they should take to mitigate the impact resulting from the materialization of any of the re-identification risks of individuals. In order to minimize its impact, the final report will reflect the existing risks by category and the measures or
Recommendations to be implemented in the processes of anonymization and in the exploitation of anonymized information.  
11. RISK REVIEW: The risk analysis must be conducted periodically throughout the entire lifecycle of the information and whenever changes occur in the anonymization processes or in the handling of anonymized information. Periodic reviews aim to verify that the actual state of risks aligns with the acceptable risk of reidentification, assessing the effectiveness of the measures in place to mitigate the potential impact that reidentification of individuals may have. For its part, the data controller of the anonymized data must take into account the initial risk catalog prepared by the data controller to continue developing its own analysis throughout the lifecycle of the anonymized information.  
In addition to ensuring the privacy of the data subjects, the data controller will determine the objectives that the anonymized information must meet based on the legitimate interests of its recipient. The design of the anonymization process will be conditioned by the final objective of the anonymized information, leading to either restricted use information or open data.  
When the anonymized information is intended to become restricted use information, the privacy of personal data will be reinforced through confidentiality agreements that will be part of the set of legal guarantees of the anonymization process. In the case of restricted use anonymized information, the data controller may consider the development of possible contractual clauses, codes of conduct, and certification mechanisms that include the recipient's commitment not to attempt any reidentification of individuals and that guarantee the privacy of the information even in the event of reidentification breaches.  
When it is intended to anonymize data belonging to the special categories referred to in Article 9 of the GDPR, the existence of a team to study the feasibility of the anonymization process could be taken into account. The work of this team will be particularly relevant, and its main task will be to prepare a feasibility report that will detail the specific reasons and conditions for the anonymization of specially protected data. This report may include, among other things, the ethical foundations or linkages of the anonymization process.

The pre-anonymization of microdata is the initial part of the anonymization process, in which the possible identification variables (direct and indirect) to be considered in the design of the anonymization tools will be determined. During the pre-anonymization process, the following will be taken into account:  
–  
The determination of variables: personal data, direct and indirect identifiers, specially protected data, and other confidential data.  
–  
The classification and sensitivity of the variables by categories: direct identification, geographical identification, specially protected, numerical, temporal, metadata, etc.  
–  
Identification variables that cannot be anonymized and that must be eliminated from the anonymization process.  
–  
Anonymized variables that are essential for the purpose to which the anonymized data will be allocated.  
Once a categorization of the variables has been made, the necessary protection criteria are established to ensure the privacy of individuals, aiming to minimize the amount of personal information that will be used during the anonymization process. The anonymization process of variables cannot be addressed without first defining the possible identification variables that will be truly necessary for the purpose to which the anonymized information will be allocated. In this process, there are variables or microdata that are tangible identification elements, but there are also other indirect identification variables that allow for the identification of individuals in a less tangible way. This fact contributes to the difficulty of the process of identifying variables.  
Starting from a dataset that has been collected from the data subjects following the principles established in Article 5 of the GDPR, the data is suitable for a specific purpose. In this dataset, we have:  
–  
Microdata or direct identifiers of individuals: all those characteristics that by themselves allow for the identification of a person.  
–  
Indirect identifiers: while they do not identify a person, the combination of several indirect identifiers could allow for the identification of a person.  
4 Indirect identifiers or quasi-identifiers: variables that in combination with other information allow for the identification of individuals, for example: gender, marital status, postal code or other location data, significant dates (birth, date of hospital admission, etc.), professions, race, membership in minority social groups, economic income, etc.

The process of data anonymization will be carried out in a structured manner, taking into account the purpose intended for the data once anonymized, ensuring the privacy of individuals and avoiding distortion of the results of the anonymized information in relation to the non-anonymized data.  
In this phase of the process, attention must be paid to the specific difficulties of anonymization for certain variables, such as, for example, in cases where it is necessary to anonymize voice recordings, image recordings, or biometric and/or genetic information.  
The objective of this phase is to minimize the number of variables that allow for the identification of individuals, restricting access to confidential information to the work team involved in the process and optimizing the computational cost of operations with anonymized data.  
The act of reducing the existing information to the minimum necessary to meet the objectives that the anonymized information must fulfill directly implies a reduction in the risk of reidentification, as a smaller amount of personal data will result in a lower inherent risk arising from the processing carried out during the anonymization process: breach of the duty of confidentiality, loss of information, security breaches, theft of keys, etc.  
Some aspects that may be considered by the data controller to address the elimination or masking of identification variables may include the following:  
–  
Determine the purpose of the anonymized data: retention periods, statistical use, scientific use, or any subsequent use.  
–  
Establish the confidential variables necessary for the processing of anonymized data and identify the confidentiality variables that will not be necessary in the processing of anonymized data. A confidentiality variable will be understood as any existing information about a person, whether it allows for their identification or whether their identification is not possible a priori.  
–  
Elimination of unnecessary direct or indirect identifying data: names, date of birth, phone number, ID number, email, postal address, bank account numbers, vehicle registration numbers, mobile device identifiers, serial numbers, IP address, biometric identifiers, photographs or images, etc.  
–  
Segregated control of users with access to personal data and users with access to anonymized data: the information, whether anonymized or not, may be structured, and each user has access to the data necessary for them to perform their work, so that the rest of the information or variables that could allow for the reidentification of subjects and are not necessary for the performance of a worker's functions are not accessible to them.

**Use of Ranges to Mask Individuals When Specific Microdata Exists That Allows for Direct Identification of Individuals or Specific Groups.** For example, in the case of extremely small groups of individuals, the information of this small group must be diluted within a larger numerical group by adding, if necessary, a reference to a percentage that highlights the existence of the smaller group as part of a larger set.

**3.8 SELECTION OF ANONYMIZATION TECHNIQUES: KEYS**

1. **HASH ALGORITHMS:** The utility of encryption algorithms is unquestionable when we need to anonymize microdata, with "hash" algorithms being particularly useful. A hash algorithm is a mechanism that, when applied to a specific piece of data, generates a unique or nearly unique key that can be used to represent that data. For example, we have a piece of data that we want to hide or anonymize, and for this, we use a hash algorithm, such as SHA1 or MD5. By applying the algorithm to a specific piece of data, we obtain a key or digital fingerprint that can be used to replace the actual data. The hash algorithm generates a digital fingerprint and makes it impossible to reconstruct the original data from the fingerprint, and on the other hand, any variation in the original data will result in a different digital fingerprint. In computational terms, it could be said that modifying a single bit in the original information stored on a computer would result in a different key or a different digital fingerprint.

The hash algorithm allows us to generate the same digital fingerprint from the same piece of data or microdata, but starting from a specific digital fingerprint, we will never be able to obtain the original data, thus guaranteeing confidentiality as it is a one-way mathematical operation. The keys resulting from the application of a hash algorithm are commonly known as "digital fingerprints" because they are understood to uniquely represent a specific piece of data or microdata.

However, a hash algorithm alone is not sufficient to make the anonymization irreversible, as small text strings such as, for example, the microdata corresponding to a person's postal code, a phone number, etc., can be easily re-identified with a computer program that generates consecutive numbers and their corresponding digital fingerprints. If what we want is to guarantee the anonymization of a microdata, it is necessary to use a cryptographic mechanism that ensures the confidentiality of the anonymized data.

HMAC can be used in combination with various hash algorithms such as, for example, MD5, and on the resulting fingerprint or key from the hash algorithm, a cryptographic algorithm is applied that generates a new fingerprint or key based on a secret key.**  
The use of HMAC in combination with non-trivial secret keys and a diligent key destruction policy can help ensure the irreversibility of the anonymization process. When the keys used with HMAC are retained, they can be used to generate pseudonymized data that require subsequent re-identification of the data subjects. Secret key hash mechanisms can be useful for masking data. However, there must be a procedure that allows for the secure deletion of the keys and the ability to verify that the procedure has been followed to ensure the irreversibility of the process.  
**2. ENCRYPTION ALGORITHMS:** Encryption algorithms with homomorphic properties open new possibilities for the processing of anonymized data. A homomorphic encryption algorithm allows operations to be performed on encrypted data in such a way that the result of the operations is the same as if the operations had been performed on the unencrypted data. The results of operations with encrypted data yield equally encrypted values that can later be decrypted by the user who possesses the decryption key. The homomorphic encryption scheme opens the possibility for the processing of anonymized personal data, ensuring the privacy of the processing and that the results of the processing will be accessible only to the holder of the decryption key.  
The implementation of homomorphic encryption schemes can provide a high degree of confidentiality to data processing in the cloud, data processing obtained from wearables, telemedicine systems, etc.  
**3. TIME STAMP:** It is also important to consider the possibility of using timestamp algorithms in the anonymization process in order to guarantee the date and time when the anonymization was performed, or even electronic signature algorithms that allow for the electronic identity of the person who performed the anonymization to be guaranteed.  
**4. LAYERS OF ANONYMIZATION:** Along with these masking and anonymization processes, we can use what could be termed layer anonymization. For example, the data controller has anonymized all data that could serve to re-identify individuals and sends the information to its legitimate recipient who, in order to prevent re-identification from occurring, decides to perform a second anonymization of the already anonymized data. In this way, the recipient of the anonymized information ensures that their processes use their own anonymization resources, preventing the identity of individuals from being affected in the event of fragility in the anonymization processes of the data controller.

In short, the recipient of the information ensures the privacy of individuals in the processing of anonymized data with their own quality guarantees. This reanonymization process can be used in an interdepartmental manner so that, as the data moves from one department to another, different anonymization processes are carried out, making it necessary for all actors involved in the various layers of anonymization to be present for a real reidentification. 

It can therefore be said that the anonymization process can be single-layer or multi-layer. We speak of a single-layer anonymization process when the anonymization of the variables is performed only once and the process is considered complete; however, sometimes the reanonymization of variables or multi-layer anonymization can provide additional guarantees to prevent the reidentification of individuals. 

The criteria for layer-based anonymization can be established in response to the requirements of the information, the processing of the anonymized data, or the anonymization policy of the data controller of the anonymized data: 

– Considering the classification of identification variables: it is possible to develop a procedure such that there are several levels of anonymization. The identifying data that has the lowest level of criticality can be anonymized in the initial layer with a certain set of secret keys. In this phase, all data that allows for the identification of individuals is coded. Subsequently, it is possible to proceed to a second anonymization of the personal data with a second set of secret keys with information that has been classified with a higher degree of criticality, and so on, the data can be reanonymized, thereby strengthening the anonymization chain as many times as necessary. This method will establish a difficulty proportional to the criticality or sensitivity of the anonymized information, multiplying the effort required for the reidentification of individuals.

– Considering internal organization: sometimes the processing of data is structured by departments within an organization, such that each department needs to access the information for a different purpose. In this case, it is possible to use sets of secret keys to proceed with the anonymization and reanonymization specifically for each department. This multi-layer anonymization procedure guarantees the privacy of the anonymized data in an interdepartmental manner, making the participation of several departments necessary to reach the original data, which will only be available to the department that performed the initial anonymization.

Example: The key AAA replaces the name of a person; it would be possible to apply a hash algorithm adding random text such as AAA1k2j3j so that over the pseudonym AAA we would have a secret key (1k2j3j) that would strengthen the confidentiality chain; in this case, the possibility of having a secret key policy to carry out the anonymization should be considered. If we apply an MD5 hash algorithm to the chain AAA, reidentification would be relatively simple through brute force mechanisms, whereas adding text or an additional key to the pseudonym would imply greater effort to reidentify a person.
–
Considering the specific guarantees of the anonymization policy of the data controller of the anonymized data: the recipient of the anonymized information decides to apply its own anonymization policy to process the information with its own guarantees so that in the event of a reidentification breach, it is possible to identify the origin and responsibility for the breach in the anonymization.

In relation to anonymization techniques, the Article 29 Working Party issued its Opinion 05/2014, which provides a technical analysis regarding the robustness, weaknesses, and guarantees of anonymization techniques. This Opinion outlines some of the limits, risks, and errors that may occur as a result of the anonymization techniques used.

It is important to note that the anonymization of information will always generate, regardless of the good practices employed, a certain degree of distortion between the anonymized information and the non-anonymized information. This distortion is known as "privacy differential," which will be taken into account in the anonymization processes, and whose indices must be quantifiable and acceptable within the purpose for which the anonymized data will be used, which may imply a certain degree of distrust towards the results of the information analysis.

The rapid technological advancement could render the chosen anonymization techniques obsolete in a short period due to the emergence of more secure techniques or techniques that allow for their breach. The selection of technical anonymization measures should also be subjected to the risk analysis mentioned for each anonymization process, assisting in the establishment of guidelines or principles that enable and assist the person responsible for the anonymization process in choosing or deciding on the most appropriate techniques at each specific moment.

In line with the aforementioned principles and in order to guarantee the confidentiality of the anonymized information, it is necessary to develop a map of information systems that ensures separate environments for each processing of personal data or anonymized personal information.

It is advisable that the anonymization process is carried out starting from personal data but in a segregated environment. At the same time, the exploitation of the anonymized information should take place in an environment separate from the environments where personal data is exploited or where the anonymization of the information is carried out.

The segregation of environments for information processing will also imply the segregation of personnel who access the information and personal data. An additional guarantee to prevent re-identification is that those involved in the processing of anonymized personal information do not have access to non-anonymized personal data or cannot access the knowledge of the mechanisms and keys of anonymization used in the anonymization processes.
