EXECUTIVE SUMMARY  
Technology enables both private companies and public administrations to use personal data on an unprecedented scale in carrying out their activities. Digital transformation and the increasing use of digital services also entail new risks and challenges for individual recipients of the corresponding services, businesses, and society as a whole.  
In this context, the purpose of the GDPR is to provide a solid and coherent framework for the protection of fundamental rights concerning data protection in the European Union and, thus, to ensure a uniform, homogeneous, and high level of protection throughout the Union. By ensuring individuals' control over their own personal data, trust is generated, and legal and practical security is reinforced for individuals, economic operators, and public administrations. Effective guarantees of the fundamental right to personal data protection and a set of homogeneous principles, rights, and compliance tools among the Member States of the European Union will result in the free movement of personal data within the Union and the development of the European digital market. Therefore, the EU legal framework in the field of personal data protection is a facilitating element, not an obstacle, to the development of a data economy that aligns with the values and principles of the Union, and it is the foundation upon which to build a European data governance model.  
A Data Space can be defined as a federated and open infrastructure to enable sovereign access to data, based on governance, policies, rules, and standards that define a framework of trust for all stakeholders. European and national Data Space initiatives present models of great organizational and technological complexity, as well as a large scale in the number of affected subjects, in the diversity of categories of data processed, in the social strata involved, in geographical breadth, in retention periods, in the number of stakeholders, and others. These initiatives are not intended as a reduction or compromise of the rights and freedoms of individuals concerning the protection of their personal data, but rather open up a horizon of possibilities that, to ensure sustainability concerning the European model of rights and freedoms, require an objective and critical analysis of their implementation from a design perspective that aligns with the impact of the processing.  
This document constitutes a first approach to GDPR compliance for Data Spaces through the application of the principles of proactive responsibility and data protection by design. Without seeking to transfer the text of the GDPR to this document, nor to be exhaustive, the document addresses the set of definitions derived from the GDPR, various European regulations, specific standards, and vocabulary in the field of Data Spaces. Following this, a brief enumeration of the basic regulatory framework, which is under development at the time of drafting this document, that affects Data Spaces when they involve the processing of
Page: 3 of 104  
personal data. Next, an approach to the processing of personal data within the framework of Data Spaces is made.  
The document concludes with two major chapters. The first discusses the applicability of data protection by design in Data Spaces. To this end, it must be taken into account that access to the data provided by a Data Space is defined as any use of data in accordance with specific requirements, but, and it is important to clarify this, without necessarily implying the transmission or download of the data. In this regard, there are various technological resources that allow the reuse of personal data with data protection guarantees, offering more options than anonymization. The document concludes with a chapter on issues related to data protection in Data Spaces concerning important aspects such as the involvement of Data Protection Officers, or the management of risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals, both from the perspective of their individuality and from their social perspective, among others.  
The "Approach to Data Spaces from the Perspective of Data Protection Regulation" is aimed at data controllers and processors involved in Data Spaces, as well as Data Protection Officers, data protection advisors, and all stakeholders who, within the framework of a data sharing model, carry out personal data processing, or who authorize, supervise, or facilitate such processing.  

Keywords: Data Space, Data protection by design, risk management, European data strategy, GDPR, Data Governance Regulation, DGA.

I. INTRODUCTION  
Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (hereinafter General Data Protection Regulation, or GDPR) is the norm that protects the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, their right to the protection of personal data.  
The protection of natural persons in relation to the processing of their personal data is a fundamental right. Article 8, paragraph 1, of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union ("the Charter") and Article 16, paragraph 1, of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU) establish that everyone has the right to the protection of personal data concerning them.  
Technology enables both private companies and Public Administrations to use personal data on an unprecedented scale in carrying out their activities. The digital transformation and the increasing use of digital services also entail new risks and challenges for individual recipients of the corresponding services, businesses, and society as a whole. Therefore, the GDPR arises from the demand to strengthen and specify the rights of Data Subjects and the obligations of those who process and determine the processing of personal data.  
The purpose of the GDPR is to provide a solid and coherent framework for the protection of fundamental rights concerning data protection in the European Union and, thus, to ensure a uniform, homogeneous, and high level of protection throughout the Union. The aim of the regulation is to guarantee control by individuals over their own personal data, generate trust, and reinforce legal and practical security for individuals, economic operators, and public authorities.  
To achieve this objective, in addition to the existence of the regulation, strict enforcement and equivalent supervision among Member States will be necessary.  
Effective guarantees of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and a set of homogeneous principles, rights, and compliance tools among the States of the European Union will result in the free movement of personal data.

circulation of personal data in the Union. If there are divergences among Member States regarding the level of guarantee of the right to data protection, or in their form of supervision, it would hinder the free movement and proper functioning of the internal market. Therefore, the requirement for a uniform level of compliance with the GDPR is fundamental for the free movement of persons, goods, and services, and for the development of the European digital market. The EU legal framework in the field of personal data protection is a facilitating element, not an obstacle, to the development of a data economy that aligns with the values and principles of the Union, and it is the foundation upon which to build a European model of data governance.  
This document analyzes from the perspective of data protection a technological model for the efficient implementation of the "data internal market" known as Data Spaces, in terms of its implications for the processing of personal data. A Data Space does not have a single definition, although it can be defined as a federated and open infrastructure to enable sovereign access to data, based on governance, policies, rules, and standards that define a framework of trust for all participants. Normatively, legal differences arise regarding the definition of the participants, the limits of the processing, and the necessary guarantees, depending on whether it involves the reuse of data held by public sector bodies, services to establish commercial relationships among participants, or, for example, Data Spaces in specific sectors. A Data Space is distinct from centralized information storage, data lakes, data warehouses, bilateral data exchanges, or neutral points, although the underlying technologies for creating a Data Space may, in many cases, coincide with the technologies used to implement the previous solutions.

This document constitutes a first approach for GDPR compliance of Data Spaces. It could not be otherwise when, in addition to the intrinsic complexity of the organizational and technical solutions proposed, one must add the complexity of a regulatory package that is also under full development. Although the GDPR clearly establishes the obligation to apply the principles of proactive accountability and data protection by design, it would not be the first time that innovative processing is deployed without considering these principles. Therefore, although the GDPR is applicable to all personal data processing regardless of the technical means used to implement it, and that data controllers must seek advice from Data Protection Officers (DPOs) and data protection experts from the conception of the processing, early action by the Supervisory Authority is essential to ensure that certain aspects are defined correctly. Without seeking to transfer the text of the GDPR to this document, nor to be exhaustive, an association of the concepts and terms used will be made, guidance will be provided to apply data protection measures and guarantees by design in the processing within the framework of a Data Space, both in defining its architecture and in its governance mechanisms, and specific aspects regarding principles, rights, and obligations concerning Data Spaces will be addressed.  
This document does not constitute a mandatory compliance guide, and its interpretation should be made without prejudice to the applicable sectoral regulations. It is directed at data controllers and processors involved in Data Spaces, as well as DPOs, data protection advisors, and all participants who, within the framework of a data sharing model, carry out personal data processing, or who authorize, supervise, or facilitate, technically or organizationally, their processing.  
In its preparation, special consideration has been given to the opinions on this topic issued by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS), and the guidelines of the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) on privacy engineering.  
The document is structured into the following chapters:  
• First, a chapter of definitions, in which definitions from the GDPR, various regulations from the European digital package, specific regulations, and vocabulary from the field of Data Spaces are grouped and related.  
• A chapter on the basic regulatory framework that affects Data Spaces when they involve the processing of personal data.  
• A first approach to personal data processing within the framework of Data Spaces.  
• The applicability of data protection by design in Data Spaces.  
• And finally, a chapter with issues related to data protection in Data Spaces, which does not aim to be exhaustive, but rather a first approach.

DEFINITIONS  
This chapter contains the definitions of the most relevant terms used both in this document and in the regulations and technical references related to Data Spaces.  
• Access: any use of data in accordance with specific technical, legal, or organizational requirements, without necessarily implying the transmission or download of the data.27  
• Data aggregator: a service that allows the consolidation of existing data from different sources in one place.  
• Anonymization: Processing of a set of personal data that generates a new set of data that disables the ability to relate these data to any identified or identifiable person.28  
• Federated learning: a machine learning technique that trains an algorithm through a decentralized architecture of devices that contain their own local and private data. Created by Google in 2017, this approach contrasts with techniques where all data is uploaded to a centralized server. As a result, the integrity of the information being used for learning is preserved without compromising privacy and security.  
• Data quality: from the perspective of a Data Space29, data quality is a subjective attribute30 associated with a dataset regarding its usefulness for a specific processing31. This concept is distinct from the data quality of the accuracy principle32 of the GDPR.  
• Data catalog: a collection of descriptions of datasets, systematically organized and containing a public user-oriented part, where information related to the individual parameters of the datasets can be accessed electronically through an online portal33.  
• Data cataloging: processing performed on data or a dataset that allows the association of the necessary metadata for its subsequent exploitation. It generally involves the generation of resource (data) catalogs that can be made available to multiple stakeholders.34

• Hyperscaler: a basic platform service that specifically provides massive storage and processing service in the cloud that can scale a distributed computing environment to thousands of servers.

• Data subject, Data subject, Affected: identified or identifiable natural person. THE REGULATORY FRAMEWORK FOR DATA SPACES  
To the extent that personal data is processed in a Data Space, the regulatory framework begins to be defined by the General Data Protection Regulation and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD).  
In relation to the use of data in the digital world, a package of European and national regulations is being developed that does not modify the regime for the processing of personal data for any of the regulated activities or the information requirements set out in the GDPR, and in case of conflict with Union Law regarding the protection of personal data or national law adopted in the matter of personal data protection, the latter must prevail.

IV.  
DATA SPACE TREATMENTS  
The material scope of the GDPR is the processing of personal data. In mixed data sets,  
when the processing of non-personal data is inextricably linked to personal data, the  
processing will also be subject to the GDPR.  
The GDPR does not cover technological technologies or infrastructures, as these are  
means to implement data processing. A Data Space is an infrastructure that allows for  
the implementation of multiple treatments. To the extent that a Data Space involves the  
processing of personal data, it will be subject to data protection regulations, without  
prejudice to the applicable sectoral regulations.  
Any reuse of personal data must always respect the principles of lawfulness, fairness,  
and transparency, as well as the limitation of purpose, data minimization, accuracy,  
storage limitation, integrity, and confidentiality, in accordance with Article 5 of the  
GDPR. For full compliance with the GDPR, the treatments must be well defined, and to  
achieve this, it will begin by precisely determining their purposes, their controllers, and  
their legitimacy.  
A.  
CATEGORIES OF PARTICIPANTS IN A DATA SPACE FROM THE GDPR PERSPECTIVE  
From the perspective of data protection, the following participants or roles in the Data  
Space could be identified:  
1. Data Subjects  
2. Data Controller  
3. Data User  
4. Data Space Mediators  
5. Technical and Legal Enablers  
6. Supervisor of Access Requests  
7. Others, such as supervisory authorities.  
This division of participants has an educational character. In practical application, it will  
be possible to find entities that perform multiple roles to a greater or lesser extent. In  
the DGA, a type of Data Space is defined, the Data Cooperatives, in which a natural  
person could act as a Data Subject, Data Controller, Mediator, and Data User. On the  
other hand, the DGA also limits the roles that can be adopted in the case of Data  
Intermediation Services.

Data Subject  
In general frameworks of data spaces, the Data Subject, that is, the identified or identifiable natural person whose personal data is intended to be processed, could be associated with the definition of "data producer" used in some Data Space frameworks. When the figure of "data producer" is associated with systems or services that collect or generate personal data of natural persons (e.g., IoT systems), to the extent that such data is linked to identified or identifiable persons, we will continue to speak of data of a data subject.  
However, when we talk about personal data, a data subject is not merely a "data producer" but a natural person whose data can only be processed in accordance with the compliance of the principles, legitimacy, respect for rights, and other obligations established in the GDPR.  
Data Controller  
In the "Definitions" chapter of this document, the definition of Data Controller has been transcribed, reflecting that established in the DGA, as the person who has the right to grant access to certain personal or non-personal data. Data Controllers, within the framework of a Data Space, could perform operations for data communication, apply mechanisms to allow on-premises processing, carry out pseudonymization and anonymization treatments, other treatments such as extracting synthetic data, providing access with the implementation of differential privacy, and making data communications to other controllers or others.

In general frameworks of Data Spaces, this figure may be labeled as the "data owner" or "data custodian." This designation is misleading when referring to personal data because a data controller does not own the data of the Data Subjects but has a legal basis that legitimizes them for processing in accordance with the obligations established in data protection regulations.  
The definition of Data Controller may change for specific Data Spaces established by regulation, defining entities from a specific sector or refining its definition.  
Data User  
The Data User is any natural or legal person who has legitimate access to certain personal or non-personal data and the right, including that granted by the GDPR in the case of personal data, to use them for commercial or non-commercial purposes. In other fields, it may receive other names, such as "data consumer." In Directive 2019/1024, the term "end user" is used for those data reusers from public sector bodies who act as Data Users.

Enabler  
The Enablers in the context of a Data Space would be those who provide support to all the previously described participants to ensure that the implementation is carried out in an efficient and coherent process, implementing governance and management mechanisms among multiple participants, avoiding duplications and repetitions of tasks, and facilitating procedures and requests.  
The functions of the Enablers could include94 providing components for access to the Data Space, for mediation, for identity management and secure data communication, or for managing the data space. They could also provide applications that allow working with the data, such as machine learning models, visualizers, data cleaning tools, or quality analysis tools, etc., provide vocabularies and ontologies, and orchestration services that enable the automation of various activities, among others.  
The Single Point of Information95 defined in the DGA, in its role of making available a consultable list of assets that contains a summary of all available data resources, would act as an Enabler. Also, as an example, a cloud storage service provider could be a technological Enabler that contributes to the creation of that infrastructure.  
An Enabler would not have an exclusively technical character but would also implement organizational measures (of governance or control, for example, of traceability or monetization), and also legal assistance. It is considered appropriate to emphasize the position of this participant in the Data Space model from the perspective of the GDPR.

Control Authorities  
In matters of data protection, the competent authorities will be those indicated in the GDPR, which in the case of Spain will be the AEPD, or the Autonomous Authorities according to their competence. In principle, and as in any other regulated sector subject to the supervision of sectoral authorities, this does not imply that, in the case of personal data processing, there is a lack of competence of the Data Protection Supervisory Authority. In any case, the AEPD emphasizes the advisability of defining the most effective cooperation mechanisms possible between these sectoral authorities and the Data Protection Supervisory Authorities.  
B.  
PROCESSING AND PURPOSES WITHIN A DATA SPACE  
Processing is any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not. The most important aspect that defines processing is its purpose.  
The principle of purpose requires defining the reason for processing certain personal data. This means that one must be as specific as possible about the purposes for which processing justifies the collection and processing of personal data. To define the purpose, it is not enough to express a will regarding the necessity of the processing, especially when based on generic social benefit purposes; it must be objectively grounded in the fact that these purposes will be achieved with the proposed processing, that is, the suitability analysis of the specific implementation of the processing.

The status of data controller or processor will be attributed to an entity in relation to a processing activity, based on the decision-making regarding the purposes and means, such that the same entity may be a controller for some processing activities and a processor for others. An entity will be considered a processor in a processing activity when it is processing the data on behalf of a controller. If it is carrying out the processing without a controller, or has not been appointed by the controller in accordance with Article 28.1 of the GDPR, or does not have a contract or legal act binding it to the controller, it infringes the GDPR by determining purposes and means, or if it acts in its own name and without evidence of acting on behalf of another, establishes relationships with data subjects, or if it, while appearing as a processor, uses the data for its own purposes, then it will be responsible for the processing.  
For a processing activity, there may be more than one controller, and one should not confuse the communication of data between two controllers with the existence of a controller-processor relationship or co-responsibility.  
When one has the status of controller, they will have the obligation to ensure and be able to demonstrate compliance with data protection regulations. When one has the status of processor/sub-processor, they must comply with the obligations established for processors in data protection regulations, particularly in Article 28.3 of the GDPR. For example, the controller will be the one obligated to ensure that the data subject can exercise their rights, but not the processor, although the processor has the obligation to assist the controller in accordance with the nature of the processing.  
When roles are assigned by regulation, this assignment should also be made respecting what is established in the GDPR, particularly that the controller can exercise over the processor the obligations established in Article 28 of the GDPR, such as the duty of diligence in Article 28.1, control over sub-processors in Article 28.2, and the stipulations in Article 28.3. In case this is not possible, it is recommended to structure the relationship as data communications between controllers.

Data User  
To the extent that the Data User defines the purposes of the processing and the means (which in this case will be to use the infrastructure of the Data Space) for the processing of personal data, the Data User will be responsible for such processing and therefore must comply with all obligations arising from data protection regulations.  
As soon as the Data User determines to implement all or part of the processing through the Data Space, the measures adopted to ensure compliance with the GDPR must be coordinated, where appropriate, with other parties involved such as the Data Space Mediators, the Data Subjects, or the Enablers.  
Enablers  
In the event that they do not process personal data, they may not have a role within the framework of the GDPR.  
When they process personal data for their own purposes, they will be responsible for processing. They will be processors (or sub-processors) when they process personal data on behalf of controllers or other processors in compliance with the requirements of Article 28 of the GDPR.

V.  
PRIVACY BY DESIGN IN A DATA SPACE  
The implementation of the principles, rights, and obligations of the GDPR requires data controllers to adopt appropriate measures to achieve this. These measures could be legal, organizational, and also technical tools. Regarding the latter, and concerning the free movement of personal data, the GDPR states in its initial recitals that technology must facilitate solutions that implement a high level of protection for personal data. In any case, the adequacy of the measures will be established according to the context, nature, scope, purposes, and risks to the rights of the Data Subjects involved in the processing.  
The principles of data minimization and protection by design and by default are essential when processing involves significant risks to the fundamental rights of individuals. Considering the latest technical advances, all parties involved in data exchange must apply technical and organizational measures to protect these rights. Such measures include not only anonymization, pseudonymization, and encryption. These techniques are neither the only ones nor, in many cases, the most appropriate. There are other increasingly used technologies that allow the introduction of algorithms into the data and obtain valuable information without the need for transmission between parties or the superfluous copying of raw or structured data. Examples of these techniques include differential privacy, generalization, suppression, and randomization, the use of synthetic data, federated learning, Secure Processing Environments, and other tools and technologies for enhancing privacy protection (PET). Member States must support public sector bodies to make optimal use of these techniques and thus facilitate the maximum possible data for exchange.  
In the introduction, we highlighted that Data Spaces, as a technical and governance infrastructure, must be characterized by allowing the adoption of the aforementioned measures, among others, to implement the necessary data protection guarantees that enable the free movement of personal data within the Union.

In the DGA, “access” is defined as any use of data in accordance with specific technical, legal, or organizational requirements, without necessarily implying the transmission or download of the data.140 That is to say, within the framework of Data Spaces, a distinction is made between two concepts that may seem similar but are very different:
• Access to data through transmission or download.

The purpose of this section is not to provide a complete and exhaustive list, nor to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the obligations and implications they entail, but rather to gather a series of examples. Non-personal data processing  
When processing in the Data Space does not involve any processing of personal data, whether by the Data Subject, by the Data Mediators, by the Data User, or in any other way, these guidelines on architecture would not be applicable.  
There will be two sources of non-personal data: datasets resulting from anonymization processes and those datasets that are non-personal by origin, such as, for example, the geolocation database of mobile phone antennas. However, due diligence would be required from the Data Mediator, or in their case from the Data User and Data Subjects, to assess whether re-identification of individuals is possible due to the massive accumulation of data from various sources and the use of novel technologies.  
Regarding anonymized data, it should be noted that, as the volume of data received increases, the possibilities of re-identification also increase despite being non-personal data, especially when data from various sources are received148. These sources include anonymized data as originally non-personal data. Therefore, among the functions of the Data Mediators in the Data Space, conducting an initial review of the strength of the anonymization treatment of a dataset prior to making it available to the Data User is of particular relevance.

Anonymization: Secure Multiparty Computation  
Secure Multiparty Computation151 or SMPC (for its acronym in English, Secure Multiparty Computation). This is a cryptographic protocol that, through Secret Sharing, allows a secret data point to be segmented into different parts, so that when the information is shared, the original data cannot be revealed by any of the sources. In the protocol, the desired result is obtained without the need to reveal any sensitive data, and the result obtained does not suffer any deviation.  
This strategy is useful in certain scenarios and requires technological assistance to implement it.  
Anonymization: Differential Privacy  
Differential Privacy152 allows for the guarantee, through the incorporation of random noise into the original information, that in the result of the data analysis process to which this technique has been applied, there is no loss in the utility of the obtained results. It is based on the Law of Large Numbers, a statistical principle that states that as the sample size grows, the average values derived from it approach the true mean value of the information. In this way, the addition of random noise to all data allows compensating for these effects and producing an "essentially equivalent" value.  
An example of use can be found in the United States Census Bureau153, which applies differential privacy to ensure the accuracy of its statistics and prevent personal information from being revealed even through them, thereby increasing citizens' trust in the security of the data they provide.  
Anonymization: Documents Oriented to Anonymization  
Recital 9 of the DGA, for the case of data reuse, expresses the need to develop data processing operations in which anonymization is considered from the concept of the data itself and in which data formats allow for efficient anonymization "by design": "In order to facilitate the protection of personal and confidential data, and to expedite the process of providing such data for reuse in accordance with this Regulation, Member States should encourage public sector bodies to create and make available data in accordance with the principle of 'open documents by design and by default' mentioned in Article 5, paragraph 2, of Directive (EU) 2019/1024 and promote the creation and acquisition of data in formats and with structures that facilitate rapid anonymization in this regard."

Homomorphic encryption154 is a default privacy technique that is suitable for cases where a data controller outsources part of an activity to a processor and wants to technically ensure that the processor will not access the data. In a traditional scheme, the controller transmits the information to the processor in an encrypted form to protect confidentiality during transit. Once the processor has received it, they decrypt and process it. However, this scheme presents both legal and technical risks, so the ideal solution to minimize risks would be for the processor not to have the ability to decrypt the information, and for all processing to be carried out on the data encrypted by the controller. This way, it would prevent a disloyal processor or a third party from accessing the data and using it for different purposes. One way to achieve this protection is through the so-called homomorphic encryption.  
Therefore, homomorphic encryption allows operations to be performed on encrypted data and to obtain results, also encrypted, equivalent to the operations performed directly on the original information.  
On the other hand, private information retrieval (PIR, for its acronym in English)155 is a cryptographic technique that allows the user to retrieve an entry from a database without revealing to the data custodian the element that has been retrieved and to unlink the information that could be inferred regarding who is accessing it156.  
An example can be taken of a company that wants its customers to access a database. In a predetermined environment, each time a customer accesses the database, the data custodian knows which entry has been accessed. Over time, the data controller will be able to know which entries in the database are of interest to the customers. By implementing the private information retrieval technique, the data controller minimizes the amount of information revealed about the data that has been accessed, as the PIR technique prevents the data controller from knowing which entries have been queried.  
Finally, federated learning157 techniques, both horizontal and vertical, can also be highlighted for artificial intelligence applications based on machine learning. Federated learning techniques are a category of PET (Privacy-Enhancing Technology) that allow the development of machine learning systems without the need to communicate personal data among the participants. These techniques can be either horizontal or vertical and are key in the new scenarios that arise for the improvement and development of society, such as Data Spaces.

Processes in which it is not possible to anonymize the data  
There may be processes that, by their very nature, do not allow for an adequate level of quality with anonymized data. This circumstance must be properly evaluated in the DPIA and must have passed the analysis of suitability, necessity, and strict proportionality by the data controllers.  
In the context of risk assessment, particularly regarding the assessment of the proportionality of the processing, there are three options, ordered from lowest to highest risk, for implementing the processing:  
1. Transfer the desired processing by the Data User to the Data Controllers (e.g., using federated learning techniques).  
2. Transfer or communicate personal data to the Data Space, to a Mediator, and transfer the processing to a Secure Processing Environment provided by
Page: 62 of 104  
the same (applying pseudonymization or anonymization in the Data Space or other strategies, as applicable).  
3. Transfer or communicate personal data to the Data Space and from there to the Data User.  
The latter case will be the last to consider, after discarding all previous ones based on a strict necessity analysis. Furthermore, it will require a more restrictive evaluation regarding the necessity, as mentioned, and proportionality of the processing, that is, contemplating stricter guarantees for data protection.  
Any processing that involves the extraction of personal data from the scope of the Data Subject must apply a prior data minimization analysis. In particular, one or more methods such as the following should be applied:  
• Elimination of unnecessary fields and metadata (e.g., in the case of images).  
• Decrease the granularity of the information in the transmitted fields.  
• Decrease the frequency of the events collected.  
• Noise with statistical characteristics that does not degrade the necessary quality.  
• Obfuscation.  
• Grouping (e.g., between 40 and 45 years old).  
• Scrambling.  
• Tokenization.  
• Application of encryption techniques.  
Among the encryption techniques to be employed, the use of modern strategies such as the aforementioned homomorphic encryption, as well as attribute-based encryption, proxy re-encryption, polymorphic encryption160, and others should be considered.  
Additionally, within the framework of a Data Space, it must be taken into account that the personal data collected by a Data Subject may be subject to the following additional treatments that must be recorded and included within the DPIA:  
1. Treatments within the Data Subject itself to fulfill the purposes of the Data Space or the Data Users.  
2. Transfer of personal data to the Data Space.  
3. Transfer of personal data by the Data Space to the Data User.

VI.  
ISSUES REGARDING THE PROTECTION OF PERSONAL DATA IN A DATA SPACE  
To reinforce and implement the control of individuals over their own personal data, the GDPR establishes a set of principles and rights, as well as obligations for those who process personal data, which extend throughout the text of the regulation.  
The principles of lawfulness, fairness, transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, security, and proactive accountability are all mandatory, autonomous, and complementary to each other, and cannot be reduced to one another, such as considering that security for the protection of personal data subsumes any of the previous ones. Therefore, processing in a Data Space must comply with all of them.  
Failure to comply would hinder the free movement of personal data within the European Union and would therefore constitute an obstacle to the exercise of economic activities. The proper functioning of the internal market requires that the free movement of personal data in the EU is not restricted or prohibited for reasons related to the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data, and to prevent this from happening, processing must be implemented with appropriate measures that ensure and allow for demonstration of compliance with the GDPR, in addition to ensuring that such measures are reviewed and updated as necessary.  
Some of the issues arising from data protection regulations for the specific framework of Data Spaces have already been developed in the previous sections. This chapter will address some other relevant issues, all without intending to be exhaustive and without prejudice to applicable sectoral regulations.  
A.

The participants in the Data Space must determine whether they have the obligation to appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO), in relation to the scale of the processing.

In this regard, and to the extent that these supervisory actions are carried out by research ethics committees, the legislator itself requires these committees, in the fields of health, biomedicine, or pharmaceuticals, to include among their members a data protection delegate or, failing that, an expert with sufficient knowledge of the GDPR when dealing with research activities that involve the processing of personal data or pseudonymized or anonymized data171.  
The DPD must participate in the definition of governance models and policies of the Data Space, analysis and evaluation of use cases, selection of data protection measures by design, management of personal data breaches, and in the advising and supervision of impact assessments.  
B.  
RISK MANAGEMENT AND IMPACT ASSESSMENT FOR DATA PROTECTION  
The data controller has the obligation, according to articles 24 and 35 of the GDPR, to manage risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of individuals and, where applicable, to conduct an impact assessment for data protection (EIPD). This obligation must be exercised by the data controllers that are proposed regarding the infrastructure of the Data Space, and also by the legislator when it is the one that normatively establishes a Data Space172. Therefore, it is of vital importance to identify the different data controllers that may exist behind the authorization of access to the data, and the role of the Access Supervisor, supported, where applicable, by a Data Protection Enabler, is fundamental.  
Proper risk management for rights and freedoms includes risk assessment and the selection, implementation, review, and updating of appropriate measures to ensure compliance. A data processing model in the Data Space that does not manage risk in accordance with the principles of data protection by design and by default could mean pushing all data protection principles to the maximum, particularly those of minimization, retention, and limitation of processing.  
Risk management is not a formal requirement, but rather a tool for decision-making on how to implement use cases of processing from the design stage to ensure compliance with data protection regulations and minimize the impact on Data Subjects. Risks to Fundamental Rights  
It is evident that there is a need to manage from the design the risks to Data Subjects that may arise from the occurrence of personal data breaches in Data Spaces. However, the risks to fundamental rights go beyond personal data breaches, as the very processing of data may pose a risk or limitation to those fundamental rights. The guidelines WP248173 establish that protection must extend to other fundamental rights, such as freedom of expression, freedom of thought, freedom of movement, prohibition of discrimination, freedom of conscience and religion, inviolability of the home or communications, or effective judicial protection.  
It should be noted that many of the processing activities in Data Spaces will involve participants from both public authorities and private entities, and whose legal basis can be found in the regulation.  
Both the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)174 and the opinions of the EDPS175 state that the impact assessment of a regulation concerning data protection must be carried out in cases where the proposed legislative measure involves the processing of personal data. Any data processing operation provided for by legislation constitutes a limitation of the right to the protection of personal data, regardless of whether such limitation may be justified. In turn, the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) has held that the storage by a public authority of data relating to a person's private life amounts to a limitation of the right to respect for private life176.  
The repeated jurisprudence of the CJEU establishes that “to determine the existence of an interference with the fundamental right to respect for private life, it is irrelevant whether the information is sensitive or whether the affected individuals have suffered any type of inconvenience”177. The assessment of respect for the essence of the right may, in some cases, require a thorough legal analysis, hence the importance of assessments of the suitability, necessity, and strict proportionality of the processing.

High Risk  
Processing within a Data Space involves, at a minimum, access, which could include communication and storage with third parties, of massive amounts of personal data from various sources with a novel technical/organizational solution, on a large scale, in a comprehensive, systematic manner, which includes data association and combination, automated, and oriented towards the application of innovative technologies178. It must be taken into account, particularly for European Data Spaces, that the possibility of processing affecting all Data Subjects or Interested Parties is opened, with massive collection of information regarding categories of data, granularity and frequency, correlation of different sources, access by multiple parties, etc., and for multiple purposes.  
Although risk management for the rights and freedoms of the interested parties is required for all processing according to Article 24.1, high-risk processing179 has the obligation to conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA). • The cases in Article 28.2 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD).

Social Risk  
Data protection measures, particularly those aimed at data minimization or those related to security for managing access control, are generally aimed at minimizing the individual impact on the right to data protection due to the excessive processing of personal data by a controller or the compromise of such personal data. However, when we find ourselves within the framework of Data Spaces where the extent of processing, both in terms of data categories, categories of Interested Parties, or even retention periods, is very extensive, it is necessary to establish specific data protection measures from the design stage to reduce potential impacts both at the individual level and on society as a whole.  
The perspective of the risk that the impact of a massive number of Interested Parties or Data Subjects suffering a compromise of their personal data poses to society must also be taken into account. The intrusion into the right to data protection arises from the mere accumulation of personal information in certain organizations. In these cases, not only must each of the individual impacts on fundamental rights be considered as a sum of these, but it also has a multiplicative effect that affects the foundations of our society: the lack of trust in institutions, the manipulation of large sectors of the population or particularly vulnerable groups, the jeopardizing of massive sectors of the population that make mitigation measures unfeasible, etc.

Cooperation among participants  
The data controller may receive assistance from third parties to fulfill its obligations. Article 28.3.f of the GDPR establishes the obligation for processors to provide such assistance when appropriate. Specifically, the GDPR states that the processor “shall assist the controller in ensuring compliance with the obligations laid down in Articles 32 to 36, taking into account the nature of the processing and the information available to the processor.” Furthermore, Recital 78 of the GDPR states that “When developing, designing, selecting, and using applications, services, and products that are based on the processing of personal data or that process personal data to fulfill their function, producers of products, services, and applications should be encouraged to take into account the right to data protection when developing and designing these products, services, and applications, and to ensure, with due regard to the state of the art, that controllers and processors are in a position to comply with their obligations regarding data protection.”  
The complexity of the Data Spaces environment, in which there may be various areas of responsibility, makes collaboration among all participants essential when managing the risks associated with personal data processing, whether they are controllers, joint controllers, processors, sub-processors, or technology providers. Effective and efficient data protection will require a coordinated effort to propose and select implementation scenarios for use cases oriented towards data protection by design and a combined approach to the solution that complies with the GDPR. The DPIA and the solutions that manage the limitations and risks to rights and freedoms must arise from a common effort, and the result must be unique.  
Such cooperation must occur in the implementation and management of governance measures, policies, data protection strategies by design and by default, security measures, breach management, etc., aimed at managing risks to rights and freedoms.

Scenarios related to the execution of the DPIA  
The set of scenarios in which a DPIA can be carried out with collaboration among participants can be as broad as the scenarios of data processing proposed within the Data Space and must specifically take into account the roles of controller, joint controller, processor, sub-processor, or technology provider without access to data that each of the participants adopts in the processing.

Example 1  
A Data Mediator proposes to build a repository with anonymized information collected from multiple Data Subjects. In that case, each Data Subject must conduct a risk analysis of the anonymization processing and, if applicable, a DPIA. The Mediator, in turn, must conduct a risk analysis of the consolidation of several anonymized sources based on the characteristics of the anonymous data, its diversity, its volume, and the possible processing of...

re-identification, and, where applicable, a DPIA. In the execution of said DPIA, from which recommendations on how to carry out anonymization should be concluded, and to conclude that there is a strategy that allows it to be carried out with sufficient guarantees and quality, the Mediator and the Data Subjects must work in coordination to extract the conclusions.

Example 2  
A Data User wishes to carry out processing on data in the possession of a Data Mediator acting as the data controller, which can be carried out through anonymization. This Mediator will perform a prior anonymization process before transferring the data to the Data User. In this case, the Data Mediator must manage the risk and, where applicable, the DPIA of the anonymization processing. This evaluation must be carried out in coordination with the Data User, both to determine their data quality requirements and to assess the possibility of re-identification that could exist from other data sources accessed by the User.

Example 3  
The Data User requests to carry out processing on non-anonymized data that a Mediator already has available, for which the Mediator acts as the data controller and on which it will enable execution in a Secure Processing Environment so that the Data User extracts only information that is not personal in nature.  
The Data Mediator must manage the risk and, where applicable, the DPIA of the processing within the scope of its obligations in enabling a set of non-anonymized data. The Data User must also manage the risk and the DPIA of the processing in its area of responsibility since, although the process is carried out in a Secure Processing Environment of the Mediator, the User has its share of responsibility in the same. Both must be coordinated regarding the guarantees established in the Secure Processing Environment, the possibility of remote access to said secure processing environment, the processing carried out therein, and the procedure for extracting results.

Example 4  
The Data User requests to carry out processing on non-anonymized data that the Mediator does not physically have in its systems but is cataloged as being in the hands of various Data Subjects. The Mediator can manage access to Secure Processing Environments for the Data Subjects to implement the requested processing using data protection strategies from the design phase that allow for the extraction of anonymized information.

The Data Subjects, as controllers, must manage the risk and, where applicable, the DPIA of the processing within the scope of their obligations in the processing. The Data Mediator would take on the role of Enabler, at least in the task of serving as a bridge between Data Subjects and Data Users, and the Data User must manage the risk and the DPIA of the processing in its area of responsibility. The risk assessment and the measures adopted cannot be carried out efficiently without collaboration between Subjects, Mediator, and User; therefore, in the execution of risk management, they must act in a coordinated manner, possibly assisted by a third party or with the leadership of the Mediator in its role as Enabler.  
Review and update of measures  
Article 24 of the GDPR establishes the need for the review and update of measures when these conditions change, as the measures adopted must be adequate to ensure and demonstrate compliance. In a Data Space, the elements that make up the executed processing (nature, scope, context, and purposes) will change very dynamically, just as the risks to the rights and freedoms that arise from them will. Therefore, in the governance of the Data Space, mechanisms must be established to execute the review and update of measures, particularly concerning the risks of re-identification and the need for re-anonymization of data sets.  
Article 32.1.d of the GDPR goes beyond that obligation to review security measures, requiring regular reviews. Due to the risk of these environments, it is recommended to conduct reviews annually, with the frequency of reviews possibly increased after the DPIA is conducted for the various use cases in the Data Space if it reduces the risk.

Resources and transparency  
The AEPD has published numerous resources for risk management for rights and freedoms, as well as for the execution of the data protection impact assessment process that can be found on the AEPD website.  
Making the results of the DPIA public is a good measure of transparency to increase trust in the processing carried out within the framework of Data Spaces.

Risk management in the selection of processors/sub-processors  
One of the main virtues of the GDPR is its flexibility in adapting to new technological contexts, as the principle of proactive responsibility requires controllers to go beyond mere compliance with the minimums directly required in the text of the regulation and to condition processing on adequate management of risks to the rights and freedoms of Data Subjects.  
Regarding the selection of processors/sub-processors, and particularly Access Guardians, in addition to the possible loss of effectiveness of certain guarantees, we could face new risks. Such risks may arise from a greater concentration of data, from the massive impact of potential personal data breaches, subjection to regulations of third countries, and a reduced ability to enforce and control by controllers and the Supervisory Authority (e.g., when accessing traceability information or the impossibility of accepting or rejecting data processing sub-processors). Additionally, there are risks to availability and resilience that may arise from unilateral changes in service conditions, the best-effort offering in service quality conditions, the modification and discontinuity of services following policies that do not meet the needs of the controllers, the executive actions of authorities from third countries, the withdrawal of services due to geopolitical events, etc.  
All these risks must be evaluated, and as a general recommendation to reduce risk, it is advised to limit as much as possible the reliance on data processing processors.

Consent Management  
The Data Subject or Interested Party must also have the possibility to give, modify, or withdraw their consent for the processing of their personal data for purposes other than those that legitimized its processing. When consent is the chosen legitimizing basis for processing in a Data Space, this consent must be a manifestation of free, specific, informed, and unequivocal will. Specifically, it must comply with the requirements set forth in Article 7 of the GDPR, which is developed in Recitals 32, 42, and 43 of the GDPR and more detailed in Guidelines 5/2020 of the EDPS. Consent should not be considered freely given when the interested party does not have genuine or free choice or cannot deny or withdraw their consent without suffering any detriment. Within the detriment to the Data Subject is the social or emotional pressure that may be exerted in certain situations and contexts.  
In particular and among others, the DGA establishes the framework for the altruistic transfer of data, both personal and corporate. In the case of personal data, the altruistic transfer of data would be based on the GDPR consent of the Data Subjects. Data Mediators offering services to Data Subjects must inform and, where appropriate, advise in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible manner about the intended uses of the data by data users and the general conditions applicable to such uses before the interested parties give their consent. It would also be necessary to specify the territory of the third country in which the data is intended to be used.  
The Data Space must provide tools to obtain consent from Data Subjects as well as to withdraw their consent. The withdrawal of consent shall not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent prior to its withdrawal.  
In providing consent, it will be necessary to determine mechanisms to establish the granularity of such consent, regarding categories of data, categories of processing, and categories of recipients. Granularity must allow for the expression and respect of the will and rights of the data subjects. In this regard, the adoption of "white" lists as well as "black" lists could be considered to precisely define preferences that reflect the moral or ethical values of the Data Subjects.  
Granular consent requires the establishment of resources for its management in the Data Space, and these resources must be defined from the design stage. Poorly defined granularity criteria can lead to future problems in their application, with uncertainties in their application for specific cases and lack of effectiveness, hence the importance of their proper definition from the design of the Data Space. GOVERNANCE, DATA PROTECTION POLICIES, PROCEDURES, AND CODES OF CONDUCT  
One of the objectives of the Data Spaces is to establish a data governance framework. In fact, it has been mentioned throughout the document and is considered vital for implementing data protection by design. This section is included at the end of the text because the said governance framework must encompass a large part of the obligations and recommendations developed herein to ensure compliance with the GDPR.  
In particular, Article 24.2 of the GDPR regarding the responsibility of the data controller states that “When provided in relation to processing activities, among the measures mentioned in paragraph 1, the application, by the data controller, of appropriate data protection policies shall be included.” The Data Space is proportionately suitable for the definition and application of data protection policies within the data governance framework.  
The EDPB and the EDPS have already stated, within the framework of the EHDS, that “success will also depend on the establishment of robust data governance and effective safeguards for the rights and interests of natural persons that fully comply with the GDPR”265.  
In this data protection policy, applicable to all participants in the Data Space, it must be conveyed how the principles and rights established in the data protection regulations and the guidelines of this document will be concretely, practically, and effectively materialized. The data protection policy does not establish the “what,” which is already developed in the regulation, but must describe the “how” compliance with the GDPR will be ensured and demonstrated. Therefore, a data protection policy for processing within the framework of a Data Space should include references regarding, among others:  
1. The involvement of DPOs and data protection advisors in the design of the Data Spaces and the processing within them.  
2. The procedures for authorizing the processing of personal data in the Data Space.  
3. The precise definition of the purposes of the processing.  
4. The establishment of the legal bases for the processing.
