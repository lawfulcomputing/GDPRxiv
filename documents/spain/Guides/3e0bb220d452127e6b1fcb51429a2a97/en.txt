EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The widespread use of information and communication services and technologies has made encryption one of the most important measures for protecting data security. Properly implemented cryptographic mechanisms provide robust measures for the protection of personal data in automated processing, ensuring confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity. 
The strength and robustness of an encryption system, that is, its ability to withstand attacks aimed at breaking the protection it provides, depends on its behavior as a system, not exclusively on each component independently. To offer an adequate level of protection, encryption systems must be effective and efficient within the framework of each specific processing, in addition to being operational. 
The GDPR explicitly mentions encryption as a measure for mitigating security risks in the protection of personal data to: 
• ensure an appropriate level of security for the risk to the rights and freedoms of data subjects, 
• guarantee that is part of the conditions for compliance with the GDPR, 
• serve as a safeguard that reduces the likelihood of an impact on the data subjects in the event of a personal data breach.  
Therefore, as a protective measure, encryption will not have the same impact on all processing, and it will necessarily be complemented by other privacy guarantees and security measures. 
For encryption to be an effective measure in a processing, the controller or processor must verify, evaluate, and assess all elements involved in the encryption process, beyond merely selecting an algorithm or a specific implementation of it. On one hand, it is necessary to determine the requirements that the encryption system must meet in the context of the processing, and on the other hand, a validation must be carried out to ensure that these requirements are satisfied, as well as to monitor that they are maintained over time. In any case, it must be taken into account that the protection of personal data involves considering the lifespan of such data, which can be as long as the life of the data subject, and this in the context of technological changes that occur over long periods of time. It is important to highlight that encryption does not imply anonymization, although it could be used as a tool for pseudonymization. 
These guidelines outline the elements that it is advisable to evaluate in the design and validation of a cryptographic system used in the processing of personal data, taking into account its significance in that processing, and especially focused on those cases where encryption is used to preserve confidentiality. Additionally, a non-exhaustive and non-mandatory list of controls is proposed to assist the controller or processor under the GDPR, the functional manager within these entities, the DPO, data protection advisors, and internal and external auditors in the selection, validation, and monitoring of encryption systems within the framework of a specific processing, as part of privacy by design and proactive responsibility efforts.

1 INTRODUCTION  
Encryption is a procedure by which information (referred to as plaintext) is transformed into a seemingly unintelligible set of data (or encrypted information). To achieve this objective, current cryptography combines transformations based on mathematical algorithms, "secure" implementations of these algorithms, and the use of keys. The characteristic aspect of encryption is that, without access to the appropriate key, it should be unfeasible (at least within a certain timeframe and resource framework) to access the content of the encrypted information or alter it without the changes being detected.  
Encryption as a measure is explicitly mentioned in Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation or GDPR), and in Organic Law 7/2021, of May 26, on the protection of personal data processed for the purposes of prevention, detection, investigation, and prosecution of criminal offenses and the execution of criminal sanctions (LO 7/2021).  
In the GDPR, it appears among the measures that can be applied to mitigate security risks in the protection of personal data (Recital 83 and Article 32), as a safeguard that helps establish the compatibility of processing (Article 6(4)) or as a safeguard that reduces the likelihood of an impact on the data subjects in the context of a personal data breach and makes communication to the data subjects optional (Article 34).  
Encryption is an appropriate security measure as long as its implementation, as well as its operation, aligns with the characteristics and impact of the processing. Otherwise, it becomes a measure that generates a false sense of security that relaxes the application of other complementary measures in the processing, particularly privacy guarantees by design.  
Limiting the issue of encryption to the mere selection of an algorithm is a simplistic view that overlooks essential aspects that can render such a measure ineffective. An encryption system or cryptosystem is much more complex and can be understood as all the elements that make up the processes of encryption and decryption framed within the processing of personal data: the algorithm, its implementation, the creation and management of the key lifecycle, the tools that comprise its suite, communication, the devices used, the governance model, etc. The application of the principle of proactive responsibility (Art. 5.2 and Art. 24.1 of the GDPR) necessitates a validation of the cryptographic system in personal data processing within the framework of the nature, scope, context, and specific purposes of each processing.  
At the level of detail in the design, validation, and supervision of the cryptographic system must be commensurate with the importance and relevance of encryption in the processing of data, as well as the impact that such processing has on the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  
The role of encryption in a processing could be that of an additional safeguard among the many that are implemented in a specific processing, or even a measure.

The strength of a cryptographic system is an objective estimate of the likelihood that it will be compromised within a given period of time. This likelihood should be very low, in the case of data protection, throughout the entire lifespan of that personal data. This strength does not depend solely on the algorithm used or the length of the key, and not all systems have the same strength. Using a very strong encryption system can incur high costs, both in implementation and in the operation of the processing, and may also conflict with compliance with other requirements (unrelated to data protection) that the same processing requires concerning latency, setup time, consumption, resources, performance, portability, usability, cost, return on investment in security, etc.  
However, from the GDPR perspective, lowering security levels below adequate levels, putting the rights and freedoms of data subjects at risk, based on criteria other than protection for rights and freedoms is not acceptable.  
In particular, in the case where the main guarantee on which a processing operation rests is the encryption of personal data, it is necessary to carry out a thorough validation of the strength of the cryptosystem. This validation must be performed "by design," and the use of the encryption system must be integrated into the processing as established in the Privacy by Design Guide published by the AEPD.  
In any case, the personal data protection policies, which would form part of the governance framework or information policy of the entity, referred to in Article 24 of the GDPR, must reflect the strategies of the controller concerning cryptographic systems from a data protection perspective and be integrated into the information systems security management procedures.

2 INITIAL CONSIDERATIONS  
2.1 RECIPIENTS  
These guidelines are directed at those responsible and those in charge/sub-in-charge of processing to whom the GDPR or LO 7/2021 applies, who implement cryptography in their personal data processing. Therefore, it is also aimed at data protection officers, advisors on personal data protection, data protection auditors, security specialists, and functional managers of the responsible or in-charge entities. This guide is also recommended for developers of encryption solutions intended for the processing of personal data and, in general, for developers of products and services in ICT systems.  
2.2 OBLIGATIONS  
The data controller and any potential processors/sub-processors (art. 32.1 of the GDPR) have the obligation to ensure that the security measures implemented to manage risks to rights and freedoms are effective and are subjected to a process of regular verification, evaluation, and assessment (art. 32.1.d).  
It should be emphasized that the measures established under Article 32 must be evaluated regularly, unlike other appropriate technical and organizational measures, in order to ensure and demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with Article 24.1 of the GDPR, which states that they must be reviewed and updated when necessary.  
The practical implementation of a processing operation (which defines the nature of the processing) will involve the acquisition of components, the use of services, possibly the hiring of processors, sub-processors, and the communication of data, which will also affect the cryptosystem as part of the security measures.  
The data controller must define the distribution of obligations in the management of security with the processors, particularly concerning the encryption system, taking into account that, normally, part of the direct management will remain in their hands. In the case of components and the use of third-party services for the implementation of the cryptosystem, the controllers and processors/sub-processors must be diligent in requiring effectiveness certifications that meet the requirements of the specific processing and the applicable regulations.  
2.3 THE ROLE OF THE DPO  
In cases where a DPO is designated, they have, among other functions, the responsibility to inform and advise the controller or processor, and their employees, about the obligations arising from the GDPR (art. 39.1.a), to supervise compliance with data protection regulations, staff awareness and training, audits (art. 39.1.b), and, in the case of a Data Protection Impact Assessment, to advise and supervise its implementation (art. 39.1.e).  
This advisory and supervisory mission could materialize in general data protection audits and regular audits concerning the proper application of security measures. In particular, the DPO must be informed of

It is also good practice to regularly supervise how the processors/sub-processors and the suppliers of the organizations maintain their encryption system, starting, for example, with a ‘vendor assessment’.  
2.4 BEYOND CONFIDENTIALITY  
The encryption system can be used both to protect the confidentiality of personal data and to determine the integrity of the information, as well as in processes of authentication, non-repudiation, and pseudonymization.  
Although this document focuses on encryption as a measure to preserve confidentiality, it may be helpful when processing employs encryption systems for the previously mentioned purposes, and when the failure of such measures could affect fundamental rights concerning data protection.  
2.5 SECURITY BY DESIGN AND VALIDATION  
The data controller, before including an encryption system in the processing, must carry out two tasks:  
• Determine from the design the strength requirements of the encryption system that are necessary in the processing and  
• Validate that such requirements are being effectively met in the implementation of the information systems and in their operation.

Article 24.1 of the GDPR establishes that the data controller shall apply appropriate technical and organizational measures in the processing. The measures, to be appropriate, must be established based on, on one hand, the nature, context, scope, and purposes of the processing, and on the other hand, the risk that the processing poses to the fundamental rights and freedoms of individuals. In relation to the risk to fundamental rights and security, Article 32 establishes that the measures taken must be appropriate to ensure an adequate level of security. As the Supreme Court has already established, security measures are an obligation of means, not of results. The measures must have three objectives: first, to ensure the protection of rights and freedoms; second, compliance with regulations; and third, to be able to demonstrate it. Furthermore, protection and compliance are not one-off actions but require regular review and updating, in the case of security measures.  
The requirements established in the previous paragraph (guaranteeing, demonstrating, and appropriateness of the measures) require establishing from the design the requirements of strength and robustness of the cryptosystem that are necessary in the processing. The requirements of strength and robustness must be established objectively and must be proportional to the potential impact of the processing and, in this case, to the importance of the encryption system within the set of security measures in that processing. Therefore, four scenarios are proposed in these guidelines when auditing a cryptographic system based on the role that cryptography plays in the processing. Scenarios that, ordered from least to greatest demand for protection, would be:  
• The encryption system is a data protection measure by default.  
• The encryption system is a measure that manages medium and low risks in the processing.  
• The encryption system is a measure that, complementarily, manages high risk to rights and freedoms in processing.  
• The encryption system is the main or most significant measure to manage a high risk to rights and freedoms in the processing, or to legitimize the processing.  
On the other hand, both Article 24 and Article 32 of the GDPR require the controller to conduct objective periodic evaluations and reviews. An instrument for the review and updating of the measures is the audit, where the effectiveness of the policies, procedures, and technical and organizational measures should be validated. An audit of the encryption system, its configuration, implementation, and use, conducted from the perspective of data protection, may be part of a more general data protection audit of the processing.  
An encryption system that is not regularly evaluated, verified, and validated in the context of the processing is a system that does not provide objective guarantees. Note that a validation, or an audit, is not a process of "reverse engineering." **2.6 REQUIREMENTS AND LIFETIME OF PERSONAL DATA**

Each processing operation will have specific requirements related to the encryption system (latency, memory limitations, performance, HW/SW resources, consumption, cost, etc.), in addition to strength and robustness requirements. All of these are interrelated; for example, lower latency will likely require higher consumption and may increase costs. Similarly, greater strength will depend on and impact the other requirements.

The strength requirements (algorithmic security and key size) and robustness (implementation and operational security) of the encryption system differ from one application to another: some applications will require merely ensuring confidentiality for a few days against unsophisticated adversaries, while others will require the need to protect confidentiality against sophisticated unauthorized entities for years, as in the case of trade secrets and industrial information or information with significant individual or social impact. The type of data lifetime, understood as the period during which it is relevant to maintain the message confidential (or intact), is the relevant criterion for determining the strength and robustness requirements of the cryptosystem.

In cases where encryption techniques are used to add additional security measures to the processing of personal data, it is essential to consider both the impact that the disclosure of the data may have and what the data lifetime is from the perspective of the GDPR, that is, the data lifetime. As defined by the GDPR in Article 4.1, personal data has that nature as long as it is information about an identified or identifiable natural person.

If encryption is to be the determining protection measure for processing, storing, or transmitting information whose disclosure may pose a high risk to fundamental rights and freedoms (geolocation of minors, information about victims of gender-based violence, medical histories, habits of vulnerable or intimate individuals, profiles or financial information of a significant portion of the subjects of a country, etc.), we would be talking about strength and robustness requirements that offer reasonable protection for many years.

2.8 SNLD ATTACKS  
In relation to the two previous sections, the increase in storage and digital processing capabilities has brought to light the need to consider future SNLD attacks, an acronym for "Store Now, Decrypt Later," also known as HNLD.  
Currently, it is possible to have vast data storage capabilities for long periods. This makes it possible to collect encrypted data and keep it until the information can be revealed (or the right moment to allocate resources to do so). Furthermore, technological advancements, especially concerning quantum computing, highlight the weakness of commonly used encryption systems in the near future.  
The impact that the realization of these attacks could have in the future, both for the privacy of individuals and society as a whole, must be evaluated. This evaluation must be particularly careful in cases where vast amounts of personal data under the responsibility of Public Administrations are stored in external services that are protected, in transit, or in storage, by means of encryption.  
2.9 THE KEY AS PERSONAL DATA  
A key belonging to a natural person is a unique identifier, and its use allows for the identification of the person using it; under these conditions, it is therefore considered personal data within the framework of specific treatments.  
2.10 ENCRYPTED INFORMATION: AT REST, IN TRANSIT, AND IN COMPUTATION  
In simplified terms, there are two approaches that could be considered as typical cases of cryptosystems:  
•  
Encryption of data at rest: data is encrypted for protected storage against loss of confidentiality.  
•  
In transit: data is encrypted prior to being sent to a recipient, either over networks or using other types of media. The recipient may or may not have the means to decrypt it, as can occur with encrypted storage in the cloud. Data can be encrypted either to protect it during transmission or for its remote encrypted storage.  
•  
In computation: data is generated encrypted or is encrypted while being processed, subsequently treated always without decryption, decrypting only the result when necessary.

2.11 PRIVACY ENHANCING CRYPTOGRAPHY  
PET or Privacy Enhancing Technologies are those products and services developed specifically to facilitate the implementation of privacy measures in treatments. A subset of them is PEC or Privacy Enhancing Cryptography. Regarding PEC, the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) points out a series of advanced cryptographic tools particularly suitable for implementing privacy-by-design strategies:  
• Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPoK)  
• Secure Multi-Party Computation  
• Homomorphic Encryption  
• Group and Ring Signatures  
• Private Set Intersection Protocols (PSI)  
• Private Information Retrieval Protocols (PIR)  
• Structured Encryption (StE)  
• Searchable Symmetric Encryption (SSE)  
Through these tools, it is possible to implement advanced solutions suitable for different scenarios and use cases, such as, for example:  
• Credential management for access to services with guarantees of anonymity.  
• Management of encrypted and shared databases, with different levels of access.  
• Design of data analysis methods and machine learning on encrypted data.  
• Verification and auditing with privacy guarantees in different environments.

In turn, the processes of verification, evaluation, and assessment must be documented in their planning, execution, and results. Furthermore, there must be a follow-up on the results and recommendations resulting from these processes.

3 VALIDATION OF THE CRYPTOGRAPHIC SYSTEM FOR DATA PROTECTION  
The data controller and the processor must carry out “a process of regular verification, evaluation, and assessment of the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of the processing” (Art. 32). For brevity, this process of verification, evaluation, and assessment has been referred to as validation23. If the data controller has decided to use encryption to protect personal data, the implemented encryption system must be validated based on the nature, context, scope, purposes, and risks of the processing.  
The relevance of encryption as a measure for protecting personal data in a processing may vary, and similarly, the validation requirements for the cryptosystem(s) must be adapted. The level of analysis and management of the encryption process must be proportional to the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects and to the role of encryption as a measure for compliance with the GDPR in the processing. Risk management will determine how the same can be mitigated or avoided using technical and organizational measures from the initial phases of the design of personal data processing. This management and implementation in the processing must comply with the principle of proactive accountability, which will imply, among other things, being documented and verifiable.  
As in any security system, it must be taken into account that the overall strength and robustness of the encryption system will be equal to that of the weakest element.  
3.1 EVALUATION OF ENCRYPTION ELEMENTS  
When validating the encryption system, it may be necessary to include, among others, the selected algorithm; the implementation of the algorithm; key management; verification of procedures; the encryption suite; data preprocessing; communication protocols; the interaction of encrypted data with applications and storage; and, finally, the review of the organizational aspects of the management of the system and the encryption material.  
The components of an encryption system can be decomposed for a more detailed analysis. The decomposition criterion presented here has been made with the aim of identifying elements that are known to have been sources of vulnerabilities.

Limits on encryption security:  
The uniqueness distance, also called the uniqueness point, is the minimum number of characters of the encrypted text needed to reduce the number of possible keys to one. Beyond that amount, the cipher is theoretically breakable given sufficient resources. It does not depend on the algorithm but on the entropy of the message space and the actual length of the key. For example, a text in Spanish encrypted with AES 256 exceeds the uniqueness distance with more than 95 characters.

4 CONCLUSION  
As stated in Article 32, section 1.b, the encryption system, like all security elements as a whole, must be regularly verified, evaluated, and assessed regarding its effectiveness in protecting the rights and freedoms of individuals, among others. This is an obligation of those responsible and those in charge/subordinate, and the DPO or, failing that, data protection advisors must be involved in advising and supervising the regular process of verification, evaluation, and assessment of the encryption system.  
An encryption system is a complex operation that is included in many treatments and cannot be addressed superficially. When the encryption system is compromised in a personal data treatment, risks to rights and freedoms materialize that will be specific to each treatment. To determine these risks, it is necessary, in addition to identifying which data has been compromised, to assess the impact that the compromise of that data may have on the affected individuals and on society. The strength and robustness of the encryption system must be proportionate to that impact.  
Considering that no security system incorporates a guarantee of infallibility, the responsible party should not limit measures to manage risks to rights and freedoms solely to security measures, particularly by relying entirely on the encryption of information for risk management. The responsible party must incorporate, from the very concept of the treatment, privacy measures to minimize the impacts arising from a possible realization of a personal data breach, such as policies for data protection, privacy by default and design (minimization, early anonymization, pseudonymization, data deletion, aggregation, low granularity, transparency, etc.), governance mechanisms, and management of personal data breaches through the development and execution of contingency plans, among others.

6.1 ANNEX: SYMMETRIC, ASYMMETRIC, AND MIXED KEY SYSTEMS  
6.1.1 Symmetric Encryption  
The schemes for symmetric encryption are based on the hypothesis that the users involved in the communication share a secret key (completely unpredictable to an adversary). Fundamentally, two types of schemes are used:  
6.1.1.1  
Block Cipher. Modes of Operation  
A block cipher processes fixed-size information blocks (typically 128 or 256 bits) that are encrypted or decrypted with similarly sized keys. Currently, the most recommended block cipher is AES, standardized by NIST in 2001. The minimum recommended block (and key) size is 128 bits.  
As relevant as choosing an appropriate block cipher is implementing a mode of operation that dictates how to manipulate sets of data larger than one block. The most recommended modes are CCM and GCM (see https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/legacy/sp/nistspecialpublication800-38c.pdf and https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/legacy/sp/nistspecialpublication800-38d.pdf).  
6.1.1.2  
Stream Cipher.  
A stream cipher is usually suitable for the dynamic encryption of data (for example, transmitted via streaming). When the AES block cipher is used in CTR mode, the result is a cipher of this type.  
6.1.2 Asymmetric Encryption  
Asymmetric encryption schemes are characterized by the existence of a division of the key material, such that only the receiver has a secret key, making their so-called public key available to the public for access by any sender. The most commonly used symmetric schemes to date are RSA encryption (specifically, the so-called RSA-OAEP variant) and those based on the discrete logarithm problem, often in groups associated with elliptic curves (El Gamal). For long-term security, it is necessary to seek other alternatives, as these schemes are vulnerable to quantum attacks (see 2.1.6).  
6.1.3 Hash Functions  
Hash functions or message digests are fundamental components of many cryptographic schemes, in addition to being used in isolation to provide integrity proofs. Thus, it is common for the transmission of a document X (encrypted or not) to be completed with the sending of a digest H(X), where H is a cryptographic function that allows, to some extent, the detection of modifications (intentional or not) to the text X that may have occurred in transit. The currently most recommended hash functions belong to the so-called SHA family; for example, SHA-256, SHA-512/256, SHA3-256, SHA3-512. The numerical suffix indicates the number of output bits, that is, the size of the document digest (which is fixed, not dependent on the size of the input document).  
6.1.4 Key Establishment Mechanisms  
The establishment of cryptographic keys (for later use in symmetric scenarios) can be done through both symmetric and asymmetric mechanisms. In fact, mixed cryptographic mechanisms are usually considered, in which after
Page: 49 of 53  
to exchange a cryptographic key using an asymmetric mechanism (such as Diffie-Hellman or EC Diffie-Hellman) this key is used to encrypt with a block cipher (such as AES). If the key establishment is to be done by symmetric means, it is also possible (see ISO/IEC 11770-2:2018).  
6.1.5 Authentication  
Authentication involves verifying the identity of those who communicate with us, or the origin of a message or block of data, which is crucial in complementing the guarantees provided by encryption. There are different mechanisms for this purpose,  
6.1.5.1  
Passwords  
These are a relatively weak mechanism of authentication, which should be implemented exclusively under mechanisms that limit the number of access requests (to avoid dictionary attacks). It is important not to store passwords in plain text or summarized through a hash, to prevent access data leakage.  
6.1.5.2  
Signatures  
The use of digital signature schemes is always the best way to authenticate communication, although it is important to have solid key and certificate management. The most recommended signature mechanisms are RSA-based signatures, discrete logarithm-based signatures (DSA and its variants on elliptic curves ECDSA, ECKDSA), as well as Merkle signatures (XMSS+ or LMS) – with the latter being preferable for the long term. RSA and DSA signatures are vulnerable to quantum attacks, so for long-term applications, it is recommended to use post-quantum tools.  
6.1.5.3  
MACs  
Message Authentication Codes (MACs) are used to generate tags that allow for data authentication in the symmetric scenario (both for the generation and verification of the tag, the same symmetric key will be needed). MACs are usually constructed from block ciphers or hash functions. Prominent examples include CMAC, HMAC, or GMAC, always with the recommendation that the generated tags be at least 96 bits and the associated keys be at least 128 bits.  
6.1.6 Post-Quantum Scenario  
Since 2017, the North American technology and standards office (NIST) has established an international process for selecting cryptographic algorithms that are resistant to quantum attacks. This process focuses on mechanisms for key encapsulation (called KEM), and digital signature schemes. KEMs have the particularity of adapting to give rise to asymmetric encryption schemes and key exchange schemes, so having secure KEMs provides a complete set of tools for basic uses in cryptography. In July 2022, the first schemes to be standardized were identified; the KEM CRYSTALS-Kyber (based on lattices) and the signatures CRYSTALS-Dilithium and FALCON (also based on lattices), as well as the signature SPHINCS+, which is built from hash functions.  
Many international organizations are developing guidelines to facilitate the transition to the post-quantum world, that is, the replacement of cryptography based on discrete logarithm and factorization with tools grounded in other mathematical problems, such as decoding problems or those related to lattices. See, for example, the recently published guide in Europe by ETSI https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_tr/103600_103699/103619/01.01.01_60/tr_103619v010101p.pdf.

6.2 ANNEX: KEY LENGTH  
When defining the strength of a key by its length in bits, it is important to consider that in asymmetric encryption the sizes are one way, and in symmetric encryption they are very different (They operate in different ranges. AES is the standard for symmetric encryption from NIST and uses keys of 128 bits, with key lengths of 128, 192, or 256 bits (this would actually be the minimum recommended size to withstand quantum attacks in the medium to long term). Asymmetric algorithms use longer keys of 1024 bits, 2048, or 3072 bits.  
In 2003, RSA stated that its 1024-bit key is equivalent to an 80-bit symmetric key. Its 2048-bit key is equivalent to a 112-bit symmetric key, and the 3072-bit key is equivalent to a 128-bit key. RSA recommends using at least 1024-bit keys if you want to keep your documents secure until 2010, and using a 2048-bit key if you want the documents to be secure until 2030. The 3072-bit key is indicated for documents that must remain secure after 2030. A NIST document defines that a 15360-bit asymmetric key is equivalent to a 256-bit symmetric key in post-quantum environments, although in the medium to long term it is advisable to use other encryption methods that are more resistant to known quantum attacks.  
The recommended key sizes for post-quantum tools can, for example, be consulted in the recent NIST report https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2022/NIST.IR.8413-upd1.pdf. For example, the recommended key sizes for encrypting with CRYSTALS-Kyber range from 800 to 1568 bits for the public key, approximately doubling these sizes for the corresponding secret keys.  
6.3 ANNEX: ADVICE REGARDING AN ENCRYPTION SYSTEM  
Encryption is a very powerful tool for protecting data. Encryption as a guarantee measure to reduce or mitigate the risk in the processing of personal data will only be effective if its use is appropriate and correct. Otherwise, it becomes useless if the device, procedures, protocols, or people involved in its operation are compromised.  
Cryptography tends to fail in its practical application more than in errors in the theoretical algorithm. Real-world encryption systems are implemented across multiple hardware and software products, combining primitives and tools, which can present risks. But even with good algorithms and protocols for most cases, errors in configuring and validating the use of the encryption system are the source of most vulnerabilities.  
Some common mistakes that should be avoided are87:  
• Not employing specialists in cryptography and data protection to define a cryptosystem to protect personal data.  
• Relying on tools, services, third-party support, or your own personnel without appropriate regular validations.  
• Not training employees.  
• Not validating the cryptosystem that depends on managers, suppliers, and third parties.  
• Not adjusting the strength of the cryptosystem to the actual impact that a breach could produce.  
• Encrypting information that does not need to be encrypted.  
• Creating your own algorithm or other elements of the encryption suite.  
• Using default configurations.  
• Making an unvalidated deployment of updates.  
• Incorrectly protecting and/or managing cryptographic keys.  
• Reusing elements to implement part of the encryption system.  
• Using channels that avoid using cryptography for "convenience."  
• Not employing overlapping protection methods: encryption-encryption, steganography-encryption, encryption-dedicated lines, etc.  
• Not using authenticated encryption systems in communications.  
• Employing randomness that does not meet the requirements for a cryptographic system.  
• Failing to adhere to the "need to know" principle regarding the cryptosystem.  
• Not implementing a key management lifecycle.  
• Distributing information or keys at some point in plaintext.  
• Prioritizing functionality over security.  
The final advice is to conduct periodic audits of procedures and the use of cryptographic material, which includes periodically preparing "traps" to determine if monitoring is taking place, if our own users are not following procedures, and if the system is sufficiently strong (ethical hacking).
