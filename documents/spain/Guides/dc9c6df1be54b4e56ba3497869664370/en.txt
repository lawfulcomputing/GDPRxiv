EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
 
This document is a guide for risk management concerning the rights and freedoms of data subjects applicable to any processing, regardless of its level of risk. Additionally, for cases of high-risk processing, it incorporates the necessary guidelines for conducting a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) and, where applicable, the prior consultation referred to in Article 36 of the GDPR. 
This guide updates and unifies those presented more than three years ago by the AEPD: the “Practical Guide to Risk Analysis for Personal Data Processing” and the “Practical Guide for Data Protection Impact Assessment.” The objective of the guide is to incorporate the lessons learned from the application of risk management in the field of data protection, as well as the new criteria and interpretations from both the AEPD and the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) and the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS). In addition to gathering accumulated experience, it aims to improve materials designed to assist compliance by data controllers, providing a unified view of risk management and DPIA. 
Finally, this document will facilitate the necessary integration of risk management for rights and freedoms, and in general, compliance with the GDPR, into the management and governance processes of entities. 
The guide consists of three main sections divided into chapters: a first section with a description of the foundations of risk management for rights and freedoms, a second section that includes a basic methodological development for the application of risk management for rights and freedoms, and a final section focused on cases where it is necessary to conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment, with specific methodological guidelines in this regard. 
This document is primarily directed at data controllers, data processors, and data protection officers (DPOs). 
 
 
Keywords: data protection, proactive responsibility, risk, impact assessment, DPIA, management, governance, policies, impact, measures, safeguards, data protection by design, data protection by default, prior consultation, DPO, rights, security.

I.  
INTRODUCTION  
In any new activity, reflecting beforehand to identify potential problems and anticipate future difficulties allows for rational decision-making and actions with a guarantee of success. The effort dedicated to weighing the possible consequences of future actions must be proportional to the potential harm or impact that could arise from them. When this approach is applied to the governance of an organization, it is referred to as "risk management." The degree of effectiveness and efficiency of this management determines the maturity level of the entity.  
Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR) aims to align the protection of personal data with the evolution of increasingly complex processing activities. This entails implementing a compliance model known as "accountability" or "proactive responsibility." This model is oriented towards processing, and its management is based on a risk-based approach. Therefore, the GDPR is a compatible and integrable model with the modern management of any organization.  
Prior to the full application of the GDPR, three years ago, the AEPD presented the guides titled "Practical Guide to Risk Analysis for the Processing of Personal Data" and "Practical Guide for the Data Protection Impact Assessment." Since then, and within the framework of lessons learned from the application of risk management, new criteria and interpretations have been developed, both by the AEPD and by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) and the European Data Protection Supervisor (EDPS). This guide unifies the two previous guides for several purposes: to gather accumulated experience, improve materials that assist in compliance for data controllers, provide a unified view of risk management and the DPIA, and facilitate the integration of risk management into the management and governance processes of entities.  
This document is a guide for risk management concerning the rights and freedoms of data subjects applicable to any processing, regardless of its risk level. Furthermore, for cases of high-risk processing, it incorporates the necessary guidance to conduct the Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA).

This document is primarily aimed at controllers, processors, and data protection officers (DPOs). As established in Article 24 of the GDPR, the controller is the one who has the obligation to ensure the adoption of measures to manage the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. In the case of high-risk processing, the GDPR establishes that it is the responsibility of the controller to carry out the DPIA with the advice, where appropriate, of the DPO (Articles 35 and 39 GDPR). 
On the other hand, Article 28, in its paragraphs 3.c, f, and h, establishes the obligation of the processor to assist the controller and provide them with the tools and information to demonstrate compliance. Consequently, this document is also aimed at processors in the context of the development of these obligations. 
Finally, in order to fulfill the functions established in Article 39 of the GDPR, this guide is practically mandatory reading for data protection officers. 
As a closing to this introduction, and beyond the obligation to comply with the principle of proactive responsibility in the GDPR, it is important to emphasize a crucial issue: evading the activities of identifying and managing risks in a processing operation, ignoring them, or procrastinating on the duties and obligations of the controller, is a significant source of future problems for data subjects, citizens, users, employees, and for the controller itself. The greatest harms will not arise from high-risk processing when it is well managed. The negative impacts, in the medium and long term, will occur in those poorly managed processing operations, where threats and the severity of their consequences are ignored.

II. 
CONCEPTS ASSOCIATED WITH RISK MANAGEMENT 
Risk management is a key element in the processes of any organization and is an inherent part of the management of any entity, project, or human activity.  
Risk management consists of a set of ordered and systematized actions aimed at controlling the possible (probability) consequences (impacts) that an activity may have on a set of goods or elements (assets) that need to be protected. Risk management requires an analysis, that is, a critical and objective reflection on a treatment, and necessitates making decisions that must be translated into concrete actions (controls) that minimize the impact on the assets to tolerable levels. 
The GDPR demands the identification, evaluation, and mitigation, carried out in an objective manner, of the risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals in the processing of personal data. Mitigation must be carried out through the adoption of technical and organizational measures that ensure and also allow for the demonstration of the protection of such rights. These must be determined with reference to the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing. Furthermore, these measures will be reviewed and updated when necessary. In short, the GDPR requires a risk management process for the rights and freedoms of data subjects. 
In compliance with the principle of proactive responsibility or “accountability,” risk management must be documented. However, it is important to distinguish between reports that document risk management actions and risk management itself. Risk management is not a document but a process that translates into actions and is evidenced documentarily. 
A. 
RISK MANAGEMENT  
Risk management is one of the pillars of the management of any organization. 
Every entity, when it intends to reliably launch a new product or service, must manage the elements of uncertainty that arise from its nature, scope, context, and purposes. ISO standards15 define the concept of “risk” as the “effect of uncertainty on the achievement of objectives,” understanding such effect as any positive or negative deviation from what was initially planned, considering that objectives can vary according to the activity area of an organization.  
When an organization faces the development of a new activity, which will be realized in the form of a treatment, elements of uncertainty arise. Uncertainties manifest from different perspectives. For example, every entity must be able to ensure that it will have the necessary capital to carry out the new initiative, that is, it must manage financial risk. To launch a project, not only is capital necessary, but also the availability of the necessary human and material resources, in time, place, and manner, which are subject to issues of availability, adequacy, etc., which implies managing the execution risk of the same project. In its implementation, novel techniques and technologies will be used that generate uncertainties in their performance, which implies managing the risk of technical reliability.  
Other risks that need to be managed include: the risk to the safety of individuals; the risk to business continuity; fraud risks; determining whether the activity will generate sufficient benefits, material or immaterial, to offset the investment, that is, conducting a cost/benefit analysis, etc.  
Moreover, the new activity is not isolated from the rest of the entity. The treatment unfolds within the internal context of the organization, so it is necessary to analyze the impact it will have on other activities of the organization (e.g., in the use of personnel, spaces, etc.), as well as the opportunity cost risk that entails relegating other possible initiatives due to resource limitations.  
Furthermore, the activity and the organization operate within a changing social and economic context with which it is necessary to interact. For example, the activity must comply with regulations (compliance risk) and will need to adapt to future regulatory changes (legal risk). Risks must be managed.

Practically everyone is managing risks from different perspectives and in an integrated manner. For example, when acquiring a vehicle, they have analyzed the ability and means to pay for it (financial risk), they have assessed the risk of purchasing the vehicle from third parties instead of from the dealership (fraud risk), the mechanical reliability of one brand compared to another (technical risk), which performs better in an accident (safety risk), whether the investment is worthwhile in relation to the intended use (cost/benefit analysis), if it is environmentally friendly (environmental risk), the legal and economic protection against potential damages to third parties (civil liability risk), whether it would be better to purchase a house before a vehicle (opportunity cost analysis), the possibility of certain types of vehicles being banned in the future (legal risk), etc. Every prudent person will conduct this analysis, weighing pros and cons, in a comprehensive manner and making decisions and taking measures to minimize risk. The difference between individuals when conducting this analysis will lie in the depth of it and the formality they have employed for their study. Both will depend on the impact that this, or any other, activity may have on their lives.
**B.  
RISK MANAGEMENT IN THE GDPR**  
The GDPR refers to the term "risk" seventy-three times throughout the text, and specifically in Articles 4.24, 23.2.g, 24.1, 25.1, 27.2.a, 30.5, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 39.2, 49.1, among others. In particular, Article 24.1 establishes:  
Taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with this Regulation. Such measures shall be reviewed and updated as necessary.  
The "risk-based approach" is developed in the "Statement on the role of a risk-based approach in data protection legal frameworks WP218" of the Article 29 Working Party (hereinafter the WP218 Statement) and is not a novel concept within the framework of data protection.  
The GDPR establishes the obligation to manage the risk that processing poses to the rights and freedoms of individuals. This risk arises both from the very existence of the processing and from its technical and organizational dimensions. The risk arises from both automated data processing and manual processing, from human elements, and from the resources involved. The risk arises from the purposes of the processing and its nature, as well as from its scope and the context in which it takes place.  
The GDPR does not establish a practical-methodological criterion for risk management. In this respect, the GDPR allows for the integration of risk management for rights and freedoms with the rest of the organization's risk management resources, policies, and governance.  
In general, the GDPR does not require any explicit formalities when executing risk management, without prejudice to the aforementioned "accountability" obligations. However, for processing that involves a high risk, the GDPR does establish minimum requirements that must be met in its management. These derive, in particular, from the obligations set forth in Articles 35 "Data Protection Impact Assessment" (DPIA), and Article 36 of the GDPR. In this regard, the European Data Protection Board has developed the document "Guidelines on Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) and determining whether processing ‘is likely to result in a high risk’ for the purposes of Regulation (EU) 2016/679" (hereinafter referred to as WP248 Guidelines throughout this text).  
The WP248 Guidelines define the concepts of "risk" and "risk management":  
A "risk" is a scenario that describes an event and its consequences estimated in terms of severity and probability.

C. THE RISK TO RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS

The risk to rights and freedoms primarily concerns, as stated in the WP248 Guidelines, the rights to data protection and privacy. D.  
RISK MANAGEMENT OF COMPLIANCE VS RISK TO RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS  
The risk of regulatory compliance can be defined as the management of the risk that the entity faces of incurring legal or administrative penalties, significant financial losses, or reputational damage due to non-compliance with legal regulations, internal rules, and codes of conduct applicable to the activities, in this case, of a controller or processor.  
Risk management for rights and freedoms is not aimed at managing the risk to the organization arising from regulatory non-compliance. The former is oriented towards the data subject, as noted in the previous section, while the latter is a risk management that focuses on protecting the interests of the entity. Therefore, non-compliance or possible non-compliance with the principles and rights established in the GDPR and its implementing regulations is not subject to risk management that could affect the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  
The WP218 Declaration interprets that the rights and fundamental principles established in the GDPR that controllers must comply with must be guaranteed, regardless of the characteristics of the processing and the risk management process for rights and freedoms.

E.  
RISK MANAGEMENT IN ALL TREATMENTS  
All activities involving the processing of personal data entail a risk for the individuals whose data is being processed and, in particular, for their rights and freedoms. Even in cases where the data controller, whether due to the type of data or the type of activity of the organization, might assume that there is a low risk for the data subjects or even the absence of risk.  
The WP248 Guidelines clarify the importance of conducting risk management in processing activities even when the processing is not high risk:  
…the mere fact that the conditions giving rise to the obligation to carry out a DPIA have not been met does not diminish the general obligation of data controllers to implement measures to adequately manage risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects.  
In the same vein, regarding the general obligations of the data controller and the data processor, Article 28 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD) raises the need to consider the risks that could arise as a result of personal data processing.  
Therefore, the concept of "zero risk" does not exist when we talk about risk management, particularly when discussing the risks that personal data processing may pose. There will always be an inherent or initial risk implicit in any processing, and once measures and safeguards have been applied to minimize it, there will still be a residual risk.  
The WP218 Declaration interprets that the risk-based approach must be a scalable and adaptable process to the specific situation of each processing activity. The risk-based approach must be proportionate, and the risk management process for rights and freedoms must be guided by principles of effectiveness and efficiency. The complexity of the risk management process must be adjusted not to the size of the entity, the availability of resources, the specialty or sector of the entity, but to the possible impact of the processing activity on the data subjects and the inherent difficulty of the processing. If an entity intends to undertake a processing activity and does not have the capacity to carry out the necessary risk management, it will be obliged to seek some form of assistance, such as resorting to external consultancy, to carry it out appropriately.  
When the processing is of low risk, the AEPD provides tools to guide and facilitate its management. The AEPD also provides tools to assist in conducting an initial analysis in high-risk processing activities.  

F.  
PROACTIVE MANAGEMENT IN RISK MANAGEMENT  
The WP218 Declaration interprets that the risk-based approach must go beyond a limited focus on reacting to harm caused to the data subject. Risk management should not be reduced to merely managing the consequences that have occurred for the data subject, such as in the case of a personal data breach. Risk management must include a preventive approach.

G.  
RISK MANAGEMENT AS A PROCESS  
Risk management involves activities such as contextualizing, identifying, analyzing, evaluating, acting, and reviewing. Risk management should be approached as a process that is transversal to the entire organization and connected with other existing processes in order to achieve a global, comprehensive, effective, and efficient risk management framework that encompasses the organization as a whole and in all its dimensions. The commitment adopted by the entity's leaders in this management is a key factor for success.  
The WP218 Declaration interprets that risk management for rights and freedoms should not be reduced to a “checkbox exercise.” On the contrary, risk management, as understood by the ISO 31000 standard, is a process formed by a set of activities and tasks that allow for controlling the uncertainty related to a threat, through a sequence of initiatives or actions.  
Applications, guides, or checklists aimed at risk management can be very valuable support tools, providing useful information for carrying out such management. However, it should be kept in mind that the final analysis, decision-making, and execution of the action plan are the exclusive responsibility of management and the potential executors of such measures, with the tools being mere instruments of assistance and support for management tasks.

RISK MANAGEMENT FOR RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS AND THE DPIA 
The GDPR establishes the obligations related to the data protection impact assessment (DPIA), fundamentally, in Articles 35 and 36. As stated in Article 35: 
“When it is likely that a type of processing, …, poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller shall carry out, …, an impact assessment.”  
The GDPR does not require that a DPIA be conducted for every processing of personal data, but it does establish that it is mandatory when there is a likelihood that it poses a high risk. The existence of a reasonable degree of presumption that the processing may pose a high risk makes the conduct of a DPIA essential. 
1. 
Definition of DPIA as a process that requires action 
The text of the Regulation does not provide a definition for the term “data protection impact assessment” or DPIA. The EDPB does develop the definition of DPIA in Guidelines WP248 as: 
“… a process designed to describe the processing, assess its necessity and proportionality, and help manage the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons arising from the processing of personal data by evaluating them and determining the measures to address them.” 
According to this, the EDPB considers that the DPIA is a “process” and, therefore: 
• 
Reducing the DPIA to a one-off and isolated activity in time is incompatible with the concept of process as interpreted in Guidelines WP248. 
• 
The DPIA must be documented, but the DPIA is more than the report that reflects its results.  
• 
The DPIA must assess the risks “by determining the measures to address them.” 
The DPIA requires the controller to act and has a greater dimension than mere formalism reflected in a document that can undergo minimal changes to adapt it to any processing. 
The DPIA is a process of analyzing a processing that extends over time, throughout the entire lifecycle of a personal data processing, and must be

C. 
THE EVALUATION OF THE LEVEL OF RISK OF PROCESSING FOR THE RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS OF NATURAL PERSONS 
The evaluation of the level of risk has two objectives. The first is to optimize efforts to mitigate and manage risks proportionally. The second is to determine whether the level of risk requires compliance with the provisions of Articles 35 and 36 of the GDPR (high-risk scenarios). 

1. 
Risk assessment process 
The risk assessment process is a discipline with a consolidated methodology common to any risk management process. To assess the level of risk associated with a processing activity, the following tasks must be carried out: 
• Identify risk factors or threats for rights and freedoms. 
• Analyze them, in terms of their impact and probability, to carry out the evaluation of the inherent level of risk arising from each of the risk factors. 
• Evaluate the overall level of risk of the processing for the rights and freedoms of the processing. 
We can define a risk factor or threat as a potential cause from which harm to the rights and freedoms of natural persons may arise. Risk factors may originate from the processing itself, such as its purpose, the processing operations, or the technology used, among others. They may also originate from collateral and undesirable effects arising from the internal or external context of the organization. Every risk factor has a potential level of impact on the data subjects. Additionally, there will be a certain probability that such a risk factor will materialize effectively. The probability will depend on both internal factors related to the processing and external (contextual) factors. 

2. 
Inherent risk and residual risk 
Inherent risk is the result of assessing the level of risk prior to the implementation of measures and safeguards to reduce the risk arising from each of the risk factors. This involves considering the combined effect of all possible risk factors in the possible scenarios in which they could materialize with a certain probability, producing a certain impact. All of them must be evaluated collectively, taking into account their cumulative action and combined effect. 
Residual risk is the result of assessing the level of risk after implementing measures and safeguards aimed at reducing the risk arising from each of the sources of risk. The objective is for the residual risk to be reduced to an acceptable level of risk.

3. 
Identification of risk factors 
Identifying a risk factor involves detecting the source that may give rise to an event capable of impacting the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. The regulations prior to the GDPR, particularly Title VIII of the regulation approved by R.D. 1720/2007, which has since been replaced, conducted an assessment of the level of risk of the
Page: 34 of 160

files of personal data concerning data security and not concerning the rights and freedoms of individuals. This analysis had the following limitations:
• It was focused on files, not on processing.
• It was fundamentally based on the categories of data used.
• It was solely aimed at determining security measures.
• It had been carried out by the legislator.
• The security measures were a list of minimums, which were understood by many data controllers as a closed list.

However, the current context is much more complex, and such a simplified model does not address current situations. Therefore, the GDPR requires that:
• The risk level assessment be a task of the controller that should focus on the processing operations carried out and not on the possible content of a data file.
• The risk level assessment take into account all aspects of the processing, which derive from the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing.
• The measures and guarantees to be adopted are not limited to security measures but also involve measures regarding the design of the processing, governance and data protection policies, data protection measures by design (unlinkability, transparency, and control), management of personal data breaches, and, where applicable, the conduct of a DPIA.

D.  
RISK TREATMENT  
Risk treatment involves taking measures and implementing guarantees that specifically reduce the level of risk to rights and freedoms. This task goes beyond mere statements or apparent commitments. It requires executing actions to reduce, eliminate, or control the identified risks. Treating risks translates into reducing potential harm by either decreasing the likelihood of their occurrence or the impact they represent. This task, as part of risk management, must be carried out regardless of whether it is a high-risk treatment or not.  
In risk management terminology, "risk treatment" refers to the selection of measures and guarantees to reduce, eliminate, or control the identified risks. Within the framework of data protection, the correct expression would be "treatment of the risks of processing."52 As this could be confusing, the terms "address," "reduce," "manage," or "take care of" will be used interchangeably throughout the text.  
The measures and guarantees aimed at addressing or reducing risk in processing are commonly referred to as "controls" in risk management terminology. The GDPR already indicates some guarantees that must be implemented in cases where there is a high risk to rights and freedoms. These include, for example, the opportunity to apply data protection policies (art. 24.2) or the obligation to notify data subjects of a personal data breach (art. 34), among others.  
Addressing the risk factors of processing is an iterative process that fundamentally takes place during the conception and design stages of its lifecycle. In each iteration, controls will be applied to reduce the probability or impact of the identified risk factors, and the residual risk will be reassessed as many times as necessary until an acceptable level of risk is achieved. Control measures should be considered independently for each identified risk, although their combined effect will be evaluated later, establishing as many control measures as necessary to achieve an acceptable level of risk.  
The controls derived from risk management can be applied to multiple processing operations that are similar. Some of the controls may also be part of the controls applied from other data protection strategies of an organization, such as: data protection policies of the controller, data protection by design and by default policies, or security by design and by default policies.

5. Resilience
Article 32.1 of the GDPR requires both availability and resilience, concepts that may be related but are distinct. Availability is the property of an asset to be accessible and usable on demand by an authorized entity. 
The term resilience refers to the capacity of an organism, organization, system, or society to adapt to changes without altering its basic structural and functional characteristics. These must adapt without reaching the breaking point, paralysis, or crisis. After this adaptation, the entity can return to its original state or even improve its suitability to the environment. This means the ability to flexibly handle extreme situations and overcome them. 
Organizational resilience studies resilience in organizations. To achieve it, the organization must anticipate, prepare, respond, and adapt to changes or events that may occur. These can be changes in the economic, political, legal, social, environmental context, etc.; as well as damage to infrastructure, acts of terrorism, cybercrime, etc.

**6. Integration of Risk Management Requirements for Rights and Freedoms in the ISMS**  
Information security management is a very mature discipline, with well-known and implemented management models (ISMS or Information Security Management System) and guidelines (ISO 27000 standards or ENS) that can be considered security standards.  
Information systems are a projection of the entity's treatments, as they are the means that support it; therefore, the policies of information systems should be a projection of the entity's information policies. In turn, security policies are a subset of the entity's information policies.  
The design, maintenance, and operation of information systems, both manual and automated, are nourished by a set of functional and non-functional requirements. One of those non-functional requirements is security requirements, aligned with the entity's objectives and which, in turn, translate into specific security requirements of the information system.

However, in organizations where management levels were poor, information system policies have been the driving force behind the organization of the entity's processes. Information system administrators must implement the security requirements provided by the security officer with a comprehensive view of the organization, but they should not make decisions regarding security beyond the technical and operational issues related to the administration of the systems themselves.

Non-functional security requirements may arise from various objectives of the entity in general and from the data treatments carried out, in particular: security of individuals, theft, fraud, etc. Additionally, it will be necessary to consider other functional and non-functional requirements, besides those derived from regulations, contractual obligations, certifications, etc.

7. 
Measures for managing personal data breaches. 
Incident management is one of the specific security control objectives. The GDPR dedicates two articles to this objective, Articles 33 and 34, and three recitals, Recitals 85, 86, and 88, where it establishes obligations for controllers regarding the notification of personal data breaches to the Supervisory Authority and the communication of their occurrence to the data subjects based on the risk to the rights and freedoms they represent. 

Breach management is an obligation of the controller, which must include the establishment of preventive and reactive measures based on risk and that impacts the risk management cycle of processing. That is, the controller must, preventively, implement mechanisms for detecting and managing breaches. The detection of a breach not only involves the use of technical means but also requires that complete information about the dimensions and impact of the breach reaches the decision-making bodies that must act upon it in a timely manner. 

Furthermore, before a personal data breach materializes, the controller must be prepared to comply, at a minimum, with the obligations arising from the regulation. The degree of preparedness that the controller must have should be determined based on the risk assessment that the occurrence of a personal data breach represents. 

On the other hand, once the immediate reaction actions to the breach have been completed, the controller will need to address, within the risk management cycle, all necessary measures to prevent the incident or breach from occurring again. This task will involve reviewing the controls that were related to the occurrence of the breach, as well as possibly implementing new controls that may be necessary. 

In another vein, the regulation establishes a series of obligations in the event of a security breach that do not depend on conducting a risk analysis, such as the obligation of the processor to notify, without undue delay, the controller of personal data breaches affecting the commissioned processing (Art. 33.2) or the obligation of the controller to document any personal data breach, including the facts related to it, its effects, and the corrective measures taken (Art. 33.5). 

Another obligation that does not depend on risk management is that the controller must be able to detect the materialization of personal data breaches.

IMPLEMENTATION OF CONTROLS, VERIFICATION, AND REEVALUATION: RISK MANAGEMENT AS A CONTINUOUS PROCESS 
Once the set of controls has been decided, it is necessary to deploy them throughout the process of conception, design, and implementation of the processing, as well as in its evolution or when the need for review is detected. 
In risk management, it is essential to continue observing the processing and auditing its results periodically to ensure that the effectiveness of the implemented measures is maintained, the results obtained are as expected63, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes have not been altered.  
The verification of the correct application of the measures and guarantees, as well as the review of the level of risk and its management, is a process that must be carried out throughout the entire lifecycle of the processing. It is recommended to conduct a verification process during the implementation phase to ensure and validate that the control measures defined in the Action Plan have been correctly implemented.  
The ideal scenario is that these tasks, in general, are integrated into the entity's policies and procedures regarding the management of the processing lifecycle. Ultimately, they should be reflected in a treatment management action plan. 
There are several references in the GDPR to this need to introduce risk management throughout the different stages of the processing lifecycle. Article 24

A.
DATA PROTECTION POLICIES
Data governance is the process by which policies and procedures are implemented to ensure effective and efficient management of information within the entity. These policies are projected in the management of each specific treatment. Among the policies that may be necessary in an organization, at least data protection policies must be considered as a means of risk reduction.
When it comes to personal data, the data governance established in the organization must ensure compliance with rights and freedoms in accordance with the GDPR. To this end, personal data treatments must be supported by the effective implementation of the principles related to treatment (Art. 5 GDPR), taking appropriate measures and providing sufficient guarantees. Under the principle of proactive accountability, the policies must be commitments established at the management level of the organization.
Recital 78 states, “… the data controller must adopt internal policies…” and Article 24.2 establishes, “When provided… the application… data protection policies.”66 If we understand the term “policies”67 as the set of guidelines that govern the actions of an organization in a specific matter or field, data protection policies define a way of acting for the organization regarding personal data treatments throughout their entire lifecycle.
Therefore, what the GDPR requires concerning data protection policies is the effective, practical, and executive aspect of a set of guidelines, going beyond the reference to the formal aspect of the existence of a document titled “data protection policy” where there is merely a formal reproduction of the provisions of the GDPR and it is reduced to a mere declaration of the controller's willingness to comply with regulations. Regarding policies, as in risk management, it is necessary to avoid confusing substance with form, as it is the substance that the GDPR demands.

SECTION 2: BASIC METHODOLOGY FOR THE APPLICATION OF RISK MANAGEMENT FOR RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS

V. DESCRIPTION AND CONTEXTUALIZATION OF THE PROCESSING

Proper risk management for rights and freedoms requires knowledge of the details of the processing. 

The best way to describe a processing activity is one that aligns with the process descriptions already used in the management and quality systems of the entity. Two requirements must be met for such a description to be adequate. The first is that the description must extend to include the information established in Article 30 of the GDPR, regarding the minimum requirements for records of processing activities. The second is that the information it contains must be sufficient to enable effective risk management for the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

There are different methodologies to formalize the study of a processing activity. This chapter offers a methodology for the description of processing that has a minimum character and aims to assist those who need to implement a methodology from scratch. Any other methodology could be suitable if it meets the criteria outlined in the previous paragraph. When choosing one methodology over another, it is recommended that it be integrated into the management systems of the organization.

The granularity that must be achieved in the description of the processing should be sufficient to enable such management. In this regard, the processing could be studied at three levels of detail:
• High-level study of the processing.
• Analysis of the structure of the processing, or decomposition of the processing into phases for individual study.
• Analysis of the data lifecycle.

The depth of the study of the processing, that is, the decision to conduct only a high-level study or to reach a complete analysis of the data lifecycle, will depend on the potential level of risk and the complexity of the processing.

D.  
ASSET INVENTORY  
An asset is defined as any good or resource that may be necessary to implement and maintain a processing operation at any stage of its life cycle, from its conception and design to the withdrawal of processing.  
All assets should be included, organized, and kept up to date in what is referred to as the processing asset inventory. This asset inventory is not only relevant from a data protection perspective but also applies to other basic functions for the management of the entity: accounting, depreciation, maintenance, resource allocation, security, etc., so its existence is expected in organizations that have implemented quality models. In that case, risk management for rights and freedoms must be integrated with this inventory.  
In the case at hand, the level of detail to be included in the asset inventory should be sufficient to identify and manage risk efficiently and, at the same time, to demonstrate such management.  
The asset inventory should be determined based on a structured analysis of the processing or through any other procedure determined by the entity, of equal or greater effectiveness.  
A correct asset inventory at the organizational level, not only confined to processing activities, will allow for the identification of relationships or collateral effects between different processing operations. Additionally, it may lead to an improvement in the economy of resources necessary to carry out such an inventory and the subsequent task of identifying and managing the risks that may arise from each asset.  
In an entity, it is common for several processing operations to access the same sets of common data and make use of common assets in data collection, processing, communication, etc. These assets that implement common operations and are shared among different processing operations are, in many cases, legacy systems. In other cases, such as the implementation of apps on mobile systems, processing operations are developed using standard third-party components shared among several applications that commonly access data processing services.

VI.  
IDENTIFICATION AND ANALYSIS OF RISK FACTORS  
The identification and analysis of risk factors for the rights and freedoms of individuals is the preliminary step to assessing the level of risk of the processing.  
This chapter will develop a methodology for such identification and analysis. This methodology is indicative and is not the only possible one, allowing a wide margin of freedom for the entity to choose the most suitable one for its characteristics and needs. In particular, it is recommended to use (and expand in relation to data protection) the methodology used for risk management in the organization.  
Similarly, for SMEs and Startups where the processing is, in principle, of low risk, the AEPD provides the tools FACILITA-RGPD and FACILITA-EMPRENDE, which, in addition to helping with regulatory compliance, allow for an initial approach to risk management. Likewise, the AEPD has published the tool GESTIONA_EIPD, which guides users through the basic elements that must be taken into account in risk analyses of processing and in impact assessments. This tool provides the minimum basis to initiate risk management activities in the context of the GDPR, including compliance requirements to enable small and medium-sized enterprises, which do not have a framework for risk management in their organization, to begin the tasks of identifying, evaluating, managing the risks of personal data processing, and executing DPIAs. Finally, there is the prototype tool EVALÚA_RIESGO RGPD, which aims to assist controllers and processors in identifying the risk factors for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects present in the processing, making an initial assessment of the intrinsic risk, including the need to carry out a DPIA, and estimating the residual risk if measures and guarantees are used to mitigate specific risk factors.  
All the aforementioned tools aim to serve as assistance and support for decision-making. These tools generate the basic documentation to initiate risk management, which, as previously noted, is a process that guides the effective implementation of measures and guarantees to protect the rights and freedoms of data subjects. However, this result should not be understood as definitive and must be reviewed and adapted to the specific circumstances of the controller in compliance with the proactive responsibility obligation imposed by the regulations.  
The only requirement that any methodology used by a controller or processor must meet is that it has at least the same effectiveness as the one presented here for the identification and analysis of risk factors for the rights and freedoms of data subjects.  
In the context of active responsibility, the identification and analysis of risk factors will always be documented and justified so that the controller can demonstrate that the decisions made at each moment regarding risk management have been the most appropriate based on the information available (“accountability”).
Page: 74 of 160

A.  
IDENTIFICATION OF RISK FACTORS  
In the GDPR, and in its development through the LOPDGDD, special regulations, and lists, guides, and guidelines approved by data protection authorities, a set of risk factors is identified. These identified factors constitute a minimum list that must be managed.  
However, the data controller cannot and should not limit itself to addressing only the risk factors explicitly identified in the regulations. In risk management, one must go further, and during the analysis phase, also identify and evaluate those risk factors that arise from the specific processing based on its nature, scope, or extent, or the purposes it pursues, without forgetting, either, those that arise from the current (internal and external to the organization) and future context of the processing.  

B.  
THE ANALYSIS OF RISK FACTORS  
For each of the identified risk factors, the data controller must determine the inherent impact, that is, the impact that results from not considering the measures and guarantees for rights and freedoms. The impact will depend on the harm that may be caused to the data subjects in particular and to society as a whole, in the realm of their rights and freedoms, in the short, medium, and long term.  
In turn, it will also be necessary to determine the probability that the identified risk materializes.  
A priori, in the GDPR we can see defined two bands or levels of risk: high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals and its absence, which we could call “not high risk.” Adjusting the analysis to only the two levels of risk referred to in the GDPR could pose a difficulty when establishing effective risk management, as it would limit the granularity for the evaluation of residual risk and, in general, for risk management throughout the lifecycle of the processing.  
Under these premises, and as a general approach to achieve a balance between the ease and effectiveness of the risk management process, it is proposed to establish four levels of risk impact (very significant, significant, limited, and very limited) as well as four levels of probability of occurrence (very high, high, low, and unlikely), so that their combined values allow for the establishment of the following risk levels: very high, high, medium, and low.

D.  
RISK DERIVED FROM PERSONAL DATA BREACHES  
In practice, the process of assessing the level of risk cannot be carried out without considering the possible consequences of personal data breaches on the data subjects in order to establish criteria for consistency in the risk assessment, preventing the initial risk assessment from differing in relation to the consequences for the data subjects resulting from the loss of confidentiality, integrity, availability of data, reversal of anonymization/pseudonymization, use of data for incompatible purposes, breach of guarantees, etc.  
In identifying and analyzing risk factors, it is necessary to determine the harms that may arise from the occurrence of personal data breaches in their various dimensions. In this case, both breaches and the notion of security must be understood in an extensive manner, including the problems that may arise concerning the technical measures for risk reduction (e.g., pseudonymization measures), as well as technical problems in data processing systems (e.g., in transactional systems).  

1.

VII.  
RISK LEVEL ASSESSMENT OF THE TREATMENT  
The assessment of the total risk level of the treatment is obtained from the result of the risk level assessment for each of the identified risk factors in the treatment. The interdependence of the different risk factors could raise the risk level of the treatment above the worst-case scenario of each risk factor considered individually.  
In the event that the treatment is not within the specified cases that require conducting a DPIA (see chapter "Analysis of the obligation to conduct a DPIA"), it is necessary to assess its risk level. The objective is both to determine the obligation/necessity of conducting a DPIA and to determine the specific risk level of the treatment in order to implement the appropriate measures and guarantees.  
When there are different risk factors, it is necessary to interpret how these factors, considered independently, could interact with each other to increase the risk level of the treatment (accumulated risk factor), through the analysis of their dependencies and combined effects or the mutual interactions that exist among them.  
Given a set of identified risk factors for the treatment, as well as their associated risk level, various methodologies can be used to assess the total risk level resulting from the treatment. Regarding the specific methodology for determining the risk level of the treatment concerning the rights and freedoms of individuals, it is recommended that it be integrated within the general methodology of the organization.  
By definition, in any methodology, the risk level of the treatment will not be lower than the highest risk level that any identified risk factor has reached in that treatment individually.  
A.  
SIMPLIFIED APPROACH  
A simplified approach to determine the risk level generically is to establish a simple formula to evaluate the accumulation of risk factors as follows:  
•  
The value of the level for each identified risk is quantified as follows:  
o Low: 0.2  
o Medium: 0.5  
o High: 0.7  
o Very High: 0.9  
•  
If for a set of identified risk factors {RF1, RF2, RF3… RFn} the following risk levels have been evaluated {RL1, RL2, RL3… RLn}, the risk level of the treatment (RLT) could be calculated as:  
o RLT_a = (RL1 + RL2) – (RL1 * RL2)  
o RLT_b = (RLT_a + RL3) – (RLT_a * RL3)  
o …  
o RLT = (RLT_n + RLn) – (RLT_n * RLn)

B.  
APPROACH THROUGH DEPENDENCY ANALYSIS  
Risk factors are not isolated and watertight compartments. Risk factors can have relationships with each other, both with other risk factors within the treatment and with other treatments carried out in the same entity, which may have additive, multiplicative, or even exponential effects.  
The analysis of such dependencies is used in security risk analysis concerning assets and is studied in a restricted manner in this field, although it can be extended to all risk factors in data protection.  
In this case, the assets are often resources shared among several treatments or processes of the organization, so we could speak of the repercussive risk factor as the risk inherited by some assets from those other assets they depend on, particularly the result of accumulating the inherited risk among assets in terms of probability and impact. In this sense, it will be necessary, when appropriate, to carry out the analysis of dependencies of the assets and assign the value of the repercussive risk to the higher-level asset based on the risks of the lower-level assets it depends on.

VIII. CONTROLS TO REDUCE RISK  
Once the risk factors have been identified and the level of risk of the processing has been determined, steps will be taken to reduce this level of risk to an acceptable value. Below is a list of measures and guarantees, generically referred to as controls, that could be adopted to manage the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects in the following dimensions:  
• The concept of processing  
• Governance and data protection policies  
• Data protection by design  
• Security in processing  
This list is not intended to be exhaustive, but rather illustrative of possible measures that exist to address risks. It is also not a mandatory list of minimum measures that must be adopted in any processing. The controller or processor must manage the risk by addressing the specific peculiarities of their processing. In this sense, the controller or processor must select or define the most appropriate measures and guarantees to address the specific risks that have been identified.  

A. MEASURES ON THE CONCEPT AND DESIGN OF PROCESSING  
These measures will act on the very definition of the nature, scope, context, or purposes of the processing, that is, the essence of the processing as conceived and designed. Among the possible measures or guarantees that could be adopted are:  

Regarding the nature:  
- Change, reorder, or reorganize the phases of the processing.  
- Eliminate any phase of the processing.  
- Isolate and segregate phases of the processing from each other so that they process data in a more limited way. For example, ensuring that some phases do not process personal data (e.g., anonymized data) or resorting to pseudonymization.  
- Review the data processing procedures.  
- Change the technical choices for implementing processing operations to less invasive and/or more mature technologies.  
- Change, in the previous sense, to technologies with greater reliability from the data protection perspective by resorting, for example, to the use of PETs (Privacy Enhanced Technologies).

DATA PROTECTION MEASURES BY DESIGN  
Data protection controls by design are one of the measures to be considered in the risk management for rights and freedoms, as established in section 1 of Article 25 of the GDPR.  
The AEPD, in its "Privacy by Design Guide," translates the principle of data protection by design into practice, understanding it as the need to include the protection of personal data as one of the factors to be taken into account in the specification phase of product and service requirements, alongside others such as security requirements, data protection by default, accessibility, or performance.  
The objectives of personal data protection by design that must be considered for products, applications, and services being developed are six. On one hand, there are three aimed at protecting information security, confidentiality, integrity, and availability traditionally, which are developed in the following section "Security Measures for the Protection of Rights and Freedoms." On the other hand, there are those specific to unlinking, transparency, and control:

Table 38 Objectives of privacy protection by design.
Page: 112 of 160

The Privacy by Design Guide contains a development of the six objectives. Below is a brief summary that condenses the information related to unlinking, transparency, and control.

1. 
Minimize  
The objective of this strategy is to avoid the processing of data that is not necessary for the purposes pursued in the processing. It involves the implementation of measures aimed at:  
•  
Selecting: choosing only the relevant sample of individuals and the necessary attributes.  
•  
Excluding: preemptively excluding subjects and attributes that are irrelevant to the processing carried out.  
•  
Pruning: partially eliminating personal data as soon as it is no longer necessary.  
•  
Deleting: completely removing personal data as soon as it is no longer relevant.  

2. 
Hide  
This strategy focuses on limiting the exposure of data, establishing the necessary measures to ensure the protection of confidentiality and unlinking objectives. Hiding requires the application of the following strategies:  
•  
Restricting: managing access to personal data restrictively.  
•  
Obfuscating: making personal data unintelligible to those who are not authorized to access it.  
•  
Dissociating: removing the linkage between datasets that must be kept independent, as well as the identifying attributes of data records to avoid correlations between them.  
•  
Aggregating: Grouping information related to several subjects using generalization and suppression techniques.  

3. 
Separate  
The objective of this strategy is to avoid, or at least minimize, the risk of processing, within the same entity, different personal data belonging to the same individual and used in independent treatments. The measures may include:  
•  
Isolating: collecting and storing personal data in different databases or applications that are independent from a logical point of view or even run on different physical systems, adopting additional measures to ensure that unlinking.  
Another way to temporally isolate personal data is through the periodic renewal of unique identifiers that point to an individual.  

•  
Distributing: disseminating the collection and processing of different subsets of personal data corresponding to different types of processing across processing and management units that, within the organization, are physically independent and use different systems and applications, attempting to implement decentralized and distributed architectures with local processing of information whenever possible instead of centralized solutions with unified access that depend on the same control unit.  

4. 
Abstract  
The objective is to limit the detail of the personal data being processed to the maximum extent. Unlike the ‘minimize’ strategy, which performs a prior selection of the collected data, this strategy focuses on the degree of detail with which the data is processed and on its aggregation process through:  
•  
Summarizing: generalizing the values of the attributes using intervals or ranges of values, instead of using the specific value of the field.  
•  
Grouping: aggregating the information of a group of records into categories instead of using the detailed information of each of the subjects belonging to the group, working with average or general values.  
•  
Perturbing: using approximate values or modifying the actual data by employing some type of random noise instead of working with the exact value of the personal data.  

5. 
Inform  
This strategy aims to implement the objective and principle of transparency beyond the minimums established by the Regulation, when the additional mechanisms implemented allow for a reduction of risks for the data subjects, in the following ways:  
•  
Facilitating: providing data subjects with additional details regarding the processing.  
•  
Explaining: providing information related to the processing in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible manner using clear and simple language.  
•  
Notifying: communicating to data subjects particulars, incidents, or changes in the nature, scope, context, purposes of the processing, or in their risks, beyond the obligations established in the GDPR.  

6. 
Control  
It aims to provide data subjects with control over the processing of their data beyond what is established in the GDPR, allowing them to manage the risk, in the following ways:  
•  
Consenting: more protective mechanisms for the collection and withdrawal of consent.  
•  
Alerting: allowing the user to set alerts related to the processing of their personal data.
Page: 114 of 160

• Choose: provide the user with control over the granular functionality [109] of applications and services.  
• Update: implement more agile mechanisms that facilitate users' review, update, and rectification of data.  
• Withdraw: provide mechanisms for users to delete or request the deletion of personal data more efficiently.  

7. Comply  
This strategy refers to the effective implementation, from the design stage, of procedural guarantees, policies, and governance measures related to data protection as part of the specific processing, seeking:  
• Define: specify data protection policies within the entity prior to the design of the processing and determine which are applicable to them.  
• Maintain: Review the effectiveness of the implemented policies.  
• Defend: Implement mechanisms in the processing that ensure the application of the policies.  

8. Demonstrate  
The objective is the implementation of accountability policies, from the perspective of demonstrating compliance, in the processing in question. To achieve this, it is necessary to:  
• Record: document each and every decision made over time regarding the concept, design, and implementation of the processing, even if they have been contradictory, identifying who made them, when, and the justification for doing so. The record must be supported by authenticity mechanisms such as electronic signatures or time stamps.  
• Audit: systematically, independently, and documentarily review the degree of compliance with data protection policies in the processing.  
• Inform: make this information available to the Control Authority, the interested parties, or potential third parties, such as the entity supervising a code of conduct, as appropriate and aimed at potentially reducing risks.

D. SECURITY MEASURES FOR THE PROTECTION OF RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS  
Security measures for the protection of rights and freedoms must be understood in a broad sense. That is, not only covering aspects related, for example, to unauthorized access, but also other possible threats. Examples of such threats include natural causes, accidents, human errors, and potential errors in the functioning of automated processing, particularly those arising from systems that infer new personal data or make automated decisions about individuals.
Page: 117 of 160

As noted in the previous sections, the security requirements for the protection of rights and freedoms are part of the comprehensive security management of an organization (security of the organization, of individuals, of information, continuity of processes, fraud prevention, corporate image, etc.). The selection of controls, in this case, must be guided by an appropriate risk management approach for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. In the following sections, one possible way to achieve this integration will be described. However, it is recommended to integrate the management of security for rights and freedoms into the policies already established within the entity.

A. 
ASSESSING RESIDUAL RISK  
The assessment of the level of risk of the processing must be carried out before implementing the measures, in order to determine the level of intrinsic risk. However, it is also necessary to recalculate it after applying the measures and guarantees to reduce the risk. In this way, it will be possible to determine whether the risk has been reduced to the desired threshold. This is how the measures should be implemented and the residual risk calculated until it reaches acceptable levels. Likewise, if the nature, scope, context, or purposes of the processing change, it will be necessary to reassess. 

Figure 28: Risk assessment cycle. 
Each risk factor may have several associated controls (measures and/or guarantees) assigned to reduce its level of risk, and for each of these controls, the effectiveness must first be assessed individually and then aggregated.  
These can influence the probability and/or impact of a potential threat, and their effectiveness can be evaluated based on personal or external experience regarding those controls. On the other hand, the same control may reduce the risk level of more than one risk factor. 
The value of effectiveness (aggregated effectiveness) must be determined for each identified risk factor, classified into the following levels: 
• 
Negligible or limited effectiveness: The probability and impact of the threat will not be affected by the implementation of the controls; they remain practically at the same level or undergo slight variations. The risk level of that risk factor will not be reduced. 
• 
Significant effectiveness: The probability and impact of the threat are significantly reduced. In that case, the new risk level will be estimated. 
• 
Maximum effectiveness: The probability and/or impact of the threat are drastically reduced to negligible values or close to them. The risk level of that risk factor will be reduced to low.

B. 
ACCEPTABLE RISKS 
As previously noted, the level of "zero risk" does not exist. A compromise must be found between the achieved level of residual risk and the viability of the processing, which means making a decision about when a level of risk is acceptable. 
Without prejudice to the obligations of the controller, residual risk levels that are low and medium may be considered acceptable, which will require proportional management efforts throughout the lifecycle of the processing. 
This means that if the controller determines during the analysis that their processing has a residual risk level above medium, they will need to adopt new necessary measures and guarantees to manage the identified risks. Once designed to be incorporated into the processing, in an iterative process, the risk level will be reassessed and the process repeated until the residual risk level is deemed acceptable. 
As expressed in the WP248 Guidelines: “An example of unacceptable high residual risk includes cases where data subjects may face significant, or even irreversible, consequences from which they cannot recover (e.g.: unauthorized access to data posing a threat to the life of data subjects, dismissal, financial danger) or when it seems obvious that there will be a risk (e.g.: due to an inability to reduce the number of people accessing the data due to their modes of exchange, use, or distribution, or when a known vulnerability is not corrected).” 
If the entity merely "adjusts" its assessment so that the obtained result aligns exclusively with its interests, then a proper treatment of the risk factors will not have been carried out, and the controller will incur the responsibilities that correspond to them.

SECTION 3: DATA PROTECTION IMPACT ASSESSMENT  
X.  
DATA PROTECTION IMPACT ASSESSMENT  
The DPIA and risk management are integrated activities. The DPIA is an indivisible part of risk management for rights and freedoms and must be executed within its framework.  
The data protection impact assessment has been introduced in the section "Risk management for rights and freedoms and the DPIA" of the chapter "Concepts associated with risk management."  
In this section, the following concepts have been described in more detail:  
• The DPIA is mandatory when there is a high probability of risk in the treatment.  
• The DPIA is a process.  
• The DPIA is integrated into risk management for rights and freedoms.  
• The DPIA expands the requirements of risk management.  
• The DPIA must translate into positive actions for the implementation of measures and guarantees for risk management.  
• The DPIA is also a tool to demonstrate compliance.  

Figure 29: Basic characteristics of the DPIA  
All the chapters preceding this section are necessary and are part of the DPIA. The following chapters will develop the specificities of the DPIA.  
A.  
WHO CONDUCTS THE DPIA  
The DPIA is a specific obligation of the data controller, as established in Article 35.1. This means that it is the controller who assumes the responsibilities arising from its execution and the results it yields.  
As interpreted in Guidelines WP248:  
"Any other person, inside or outside the organization, can carry out a DPIA, but the data controller remains ultimately responsible for the task."
Page: 130 of 160

The data processor shall seek the advice of the data protection officer, if appointed (Article 35, paragraph 2), and such advice, along with the decisions made by the controller, must be documented in the DPIA. The data protection officer must also oversee the completion of the DPIA [Article 39, paragraph 1, letter c)]. Further guidance is provided in the Guidelines of the Article 29 Working Party on the Data Protection Officer, 16/EN WP 243.  
If a processor carries out the processing wholly or partially, that processor must assist the controller in conducting the DPIA and must provide the necessary information.  
Therefore, data processors are obligated to assist the controller in ensuring compliance with the obligations established in Articles 32 to 36, that is, in risk management and in conducting the DPIA, taking into account the nature of the processing and the information available to the processor (Article 28.3.f). Additionally, it may be advisable for the controller to seek support from third parties.  
B.  
WHEN THE DPIA SHOULD BE CONDUCTED  
Regarding the timing of when it should be conducted, Article 35.1 of the GDPR establishes that:  
..., the controller shall carry out, prior to the processing, an assessment of the impact of the processing operations on the protection of personal data, ...  
The DPIA is a process that is integrated into the risk management process for rights and freedoms. Within that management process, an important aspect of the DPIA is its a priori nature, that is, the obligation to carry it out before the processing activities commence.  
The GDPR emphasizes this prior nature concerning the effective execution of the processing. Explicitly, it does not require that it be carried out prior to other stages of the processing life cycle, such as its design or implementation. In this way, the GDPR limits itself to exercising its competencies, that is, it does not assess other considerations that go beyond the protection of personal data. The rights and freedoms of citizens will be affected when the processing is executed effectively; therefore, it is before these rights and freedoms are compromised that the DPIA must be conducted.  
However, and although the GDPR does not assess this, it would be highly advisable for the entity to carry out the DPIA before initiating the design and effective implementation process. Every phase of the development life cycle of a processing operation prior to its deployment involves making investments to carry out developments, acquisitions, organizational changes, hiring, etc. In this regard, the Guidelines WP248 state literally in the following terms:  
“The DPIA should be seen as a tool to assist in decision-making regarding processing.”  
From which it can be inferred that the DPIA is advisable to be conducted in the conception and design phases of the processing. There are two reasons that support this approach. The first is to protect the investment made by the controller in
Page: 131 of 160

the processing, but this reason does not fall within the competencies of data protection.  
The second is to comply with the principles of data protection by design. These principles require that the selected safeguards be guided by risk management and implemented during the conception and design phase of the processing, being integrated into it and extending to all stages of its life cycle. Data protection by design is not an additional layer or an element that can be added later. Therefore, a DPIA, which may imply that changes need to be made to the processing to introduce modifications, safeguards, or measures to reduce risks, must be carried out before and during the design phase.  
C.  
EXCEPTIONS FOR CONDUCTING THE DPIA BEFORE THE START OF PROCESSING ACTIVITIES  
The risk-based approach of the GDPR implies that the DPIA should be understood as a process and not as a state. Therefore, while the DPIA must be conducted before the implementation of the processing, its review and adaptation extend to all stages of its life cycle.  
If changes occur during the life of the processing that are external to the controller, such as contextual changes or an unforeseen expansion of the scope, it will be necessary to update the DPIA and, if applicable, generate a new report and action plan with the additional control measures that need to be implemented within the framework of risk management before continuing with the processing. If the DPIA was not conducted because the initial circumstances did not require or recommend it, then it will be necessary to conduct the DPIA from scratch. In the previous cases, the DPIA must be executed immediately.  
If the controller intends to change the nature, scope, or purposes of the processing, and the new circumstances require or recommend a DPIA, this must be carried out before starting the processing activities with the new updates.  
Among the previous cases are those processing activities that were already underway before the full entry into force of the GDPR. Regarding this last case, it should be noted that the WP248 Guidelines establish:  
…even if a DPIA is not required on May 25, 2018, it will be necessary, at the appropriate time, for the data controller to carry out such an assessment as part of its general obligations of proactive accountability.  
Therefore, it is the controller's obligation to review the level of risk in ongoing processing activities (see the chapter on reviewing the level of risk) to determine the appropriate time to conduct the DPIA.  

The GDPR does not address data protection AFTER design.

XII.  
ANALYSIS OF THE NEED TO CONDUCT a DPIA  
The set of mandatory treatments is not a closed list nor a limitation for the controller. Occasionally, it may be necessary or advisable to conduct a DPIA for a treatment that is not included in this list of mandatory treatments, either due to the potential intrinsic risk that the controller has identified in the treatment, because the controller implements the DPIA as a tool to demonstrate compliance, or, as stated in the WP248 Guidelines, it is unclear whether a DPIA is required or not:  
In cases where it is unclear whether a DPIA is required, the Article 29 Working Party (GT29) recommends conducting one, as this assessment represents a practical tool to help data controllers comply with data protection legislation.  
And as stated in the Guidelines, when, although not all criteria for a DPIA to be mandatory are met, some of them are present to such an extent that the controller considers it necessary to carry it out:  
However, in some cases, a data controller may consider that a treatment that meets only one of these criteria requires a DPIA.  
Therefore, it is important to keep in mind that the fact that a personal data treatment is not included in the mandatory cases does not always imply that it is unnecessary to carry out the DPIA. The GDPR does not limit the controller's decision-making capacity when deciding whether to conduct one or not. The GDPR establishes a mechanism of proactivity that allows the controller to decide on the need to carry out the DPIA in line with the inherent risks associated with the treatment based on its nature, scope, context, and purposes (Recital 76 and Article 35.1 of the GDPR).  
In particular, it is necessary to consider that the lists of mandatory and excluded treatments established by the supervisory authorities and referred to in Article 35 of the GDPR (35.4 and 35.5) are indicative and do not limit the decision-making power of the controller regarding their personal data treatments and the exercise of proactive responsibility.  
In particular, the WP248 Guidelines interpret:  
the chief information security officer (CISO), if appointed, as well as the data protection officer, could suggest that the controller conduct a DPIA on a specific processing operation, and should assist stakeholders in the methodology, help assess the quality of the risk assessment and whether the residual risk is acceptable, and develop specific knowledge for the context of the data controller;  
the chief information security officer (CISO), if appointed, or the IT service, should provide assistance to the controller and could propose conducting a DPIA on a specific processing operation, depending on security and operational needs.

Thus, both the DPO, or if not appointed the data protection advisor, and the CISO may suggest conducting a DPIA. These suggestions must be documented, as well as the decisions made based on them.  
Therefore, regardless of whether it is a mandatory treatment or not for conducting a DPIA, the controller may decide to carry it out in order to conduct a more detailed analysis of the personal data treatment in the interest of greater diligence in implementing proactive responsibility. Valid reasons also include improving the quality of their products and services, fostering a culture of data protection within their organization, or simply as a mechanism to ensure the trust of their clients.
**XIII. ASSESSMENT OF THE NECESSITY AND PROPORTIONALITY OF THE PROCESSING**

One of the requirements of Article 35 of the GDPR concerning the DPIA is the one appearing in section 35.7.b of the GDPR, the obligation to carry out "an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations in relation to their purpose."

The European Data Protection Supervisor has published two guidelines for conducting this analysis concerning European regulatory development. Although they are not directly applicable to the analysis of any possible type of processing, they are very useful as they outline the foundations for the assessment of necessity and proportionality. They specify the principle of proportionality followed by judges and courts when conducting a balancing act when different legally protected rights or goods come into conflict.

This principle of proportionality, applied to the assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing, translates into conducting a balancing act based on three criteria:

1. **Suitability Assessment**  
It must be determined whether the processing is appropriate for the intended purpose. The processing addresses certain deficiencies, demands, requirements, obligations, or objective opportunities and can achieve the proposed objectives with sufficient effectiveness.

2. **Necessity Assessment**  
It must be determined whether the intended purpose cannot be achieved by a less harmful or invasive means, that is, whether there is no alternative processing that is equally effective for achieving the intended purpose.

This evaluation must conclude with a decision to carry out or not carry out the processing, or, if applicable, to modify it to comply with the three required assessments. 
During the evaluation process, elements may be identified that, when included in the processing, modify it and make it compliant with the principle of proportionality, so this evaluation and adjustment process should be understood as a process of improvement that can and should be carried out in several iterations until an appropriate design of the processing is achieved. 
Before addressing this evaluation from scratch, it is essential to consult any previous analyses of necessity and proportionality that may have been conducted on similar processing, consult the guidelines of the EDPB, the resolutions or legal reports of the AEPD, or the case law, where evaluations of processing that bear similarity based on the nature, scope, context, or purposes of the processing may exist, allowing for the identification of the limits to which the processing in question must adhere. The conclusions of these evaluations must be taken into account throughout the assessment. 
In no case is it recommended to continue with the DPIA when the processing does not pass the necessity and/or proportionality assessment. It should be noted that these are compliance requirements mandated by the GDPR; requirements that cannot be addressed with alternative measures to compliance itself, such as technical and organizational measures. 
For its practical application, this guide proposes a sequential analysis process, starting with the suitability assessment. This process must be adjusted by the controller to their working methodology, the characteristics, and the particular circumstances of the specific processing. The only requirement is that a complete analysis of the three indicated dimensions is carried out.

A. 
COMMON ERRORS REGARDING THE ASSESSMENT OF THE NECESSITY AND PROPORTIONALITY OF THE PROCESSING 
During the practical application of the GDPR, the following errors have been observed regarding the assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing. 
First, the DPIA is an analysis within the framework of risk management for rights and freedoms, and its purpose is not to determine the legitimacy of the processing or its legal bases. 
Second, this assessment should not be confused with the necessity analysis for conducting a DPIA (see chapter “Analysis of the Necessity to Conduct a DPIA”), which will depend on the level of risk of the processing that has been identified. 
Finally, it should be noted that the assessment of the proportionality of the processing operations concerning their purposes (Article 35.7.b GDPR) should not be confused with the obligation of the controller to use only data that is adequate, relevant, and limited to the purpose of the processing (Article 5.1.b and Article 25.2 GDPR). Article 5.1.b and Article 25.2 of the GDPR require the controller to conduct an analysis aimed at applying the principle of minimization, which involves determining, in each of the operations that constitute the processing, the...
Page: 141 of 160

data and treatment operations that are minimal and necessary to address the purposes of the treatment.  
B.  
SUITABILITY ASSESSMENT  
In the suitability assessment, it must be evaluated whether the proposed treatment, as it is presented, achieves the necessary effectiveness to fulfill the purposes it aims for. This effectiveness must necessarily be demonstrated objectively by the data controller, for which the following must be carried out:  
1. Definition of the effectiveness threshold of the treatment: Objectively, qualitatively, and based on evidence, establish what the effectiveness threshold should be to meet the purposes of the treatment (some examples could be a margin of error of 5% in a resulting value, a detection of 95% of cases, or a fraud possibility below 1%).  
2. Evaluation of the effectiveness of the proposed treatment: Objectively, qualitatively, and based on evidence, evaluate the effectiveness of the treatment as it has been proposed, verifying whether it addresses the stated needs and to what extent (determine if it genuinely resolves those shortcomings).  
The suitability assessment must be rational, analytical, and based on facts and objective data.  
Based on the information obtained in this analysis, a decision will be made whether to proceed with the treatment as it is proposed or not.  
C.  
NECESSITY ASSESSMENT  
To carry out the necessity assessment, the following steps must be followed:  
1. Determination of the relevance of the purposes of the treatment: Evaluate that the purposes of the treatment have sufficient importance to be addressed with a high-risk treatment (prior to a proportionality assessment described later).  
2. Verification of the adequacy of the treatment operations: Verification that each of the specific operations of the treatment is aimed at fulfilling the purposes of the treatment in an objectively demonstrable manner.  
3. Justification of the current configuration of the treatment: Evaluate that there are no other treatments, which are already underway or could be proposed, that resolve the stated purposes without incurring a high risk, even if it is necessary to introduce some modification to meet the pursued purposes.  
In practice, there is usually not a single way to achieve the purposes for which a data treatment is oriented. Depending on how it is implemented, different risk scenarios can be proposed. When considering those other possible alternative treatments, it is necessary to identify those that, using less intrusive means, achieve at least equal effectiveness.  
That is to say, it must be evaluated whether the pursued purpose can be achieved by other means, such as, for example, using other data (of different nature, extent, or anonymized), reducing the universe of affected individuals (quantitatively or...
Page: 142 of 160

qualitatively speaking), making use of other less invasive technologies, applying other procedures or means of treatment (modifying those initially planned), etc., and even determining whether minor modifications to the existing treatments meet the needs identified in the first point. 

Once that evaluation is carried out, the option that achieves the necessary effectiveness while being the least harmful and intrusive to the rights of individuals must be chosen. Ultimately, it will be decided whether to proceed with the treatment as proposed or not. 

1. 
Expiration Clauses 
A very important aspect that arises from the analysis of necessity is that it is necessary to determine whether the justification for the treatment is based on the requirement to respond to a specific urgent situation (for example, in the case of an essential public interest such as security or health issues, in which case it must be included in a legal norm). In that case, it is essential to identify whether there is a high risk for the responsible party, the State, or the citizens (a risk distinct from that concerning the rights and freedoms of the interested parties) and, in particular, for those groups of citizens who may be considered in a situation of special vulnerability. 

The weighing of the circumstances must be carried out on a case-by-case basis and according to the circumstances of the moment, as there are no absolute rights nor absolute limits to them. Precisely because these limits can vary over time as a result of the evolution and change of the conditions that imposed them, it is necessary to establish, in the case presented and in other similar cases that may be identified, the so-called expiration clauses of the treatment, understood as those circumstances that may occur and make the treatment unnecessary based on its nature, scope, context, and purposes. 

In these cases, the responsible party must incorporate measures to monitor the actual validity of the circumstances that justified the treatment. If these circumstances disappear, the suitability and legality of the treatment must be reevaluated (Article 6 GDPR). In this way, it will be avoided that treatments originated based on provisional measures aimed at responding to an extraordinary and urgent situation become permanent, definitive, and lacking justification.

F.  
FINAL DECISION AND DOCUMENTATION OF THE EVALUATION OF NECESSITY AND PROPORTIONALITY  
The evaluation of necessity and proportionality must lead to a decision on whether the processing is viable or, on the contrary, it is not viable according to how it is proposed.

XIV. DOCUMENTATION OBLIGATION
The DPIA is a process that needs to be documented, both in its conclusions and in its development process.
In the WP248 Guidelines, it is stated that:
...even if a processing operation corresponds to the previously mentioned cases, a controller may not consider that such processing "likely entails a high risk." In these cases, the controller must justify and document the reasons for not conducting a DPIA and include/register the opinions of the data protection officer. The justification and decision not to conduct the DPIA, made within the framework of risk management, must also be adequately documented. Failing to carry out documentation activities concerning the active responsibility activities developed, or not adequately and justifiably documenting the decisions of the controller, will prevent them from demonstrating compliance with their obligations and could lead to the initiation of an infringement procedure by the Supervisory Authority.

A. ACCESS OF THE SUPERVISORY AUTHORITY TO THE DPIA
All documentation related to the DPIA must be available to the Supervisory Authority in two cases:
• Submission of a prior consultation (Article 36 GDPR).
• Request by the Supervisory Authority within the powers granted to it by Article 58 of the GDPR.
Therefore, there is no obligation to systematically submit the entire DPIA conducted by the controller to the Supervisory Authority. However, when the conditions of obligation are met, failing to submit a DPIA to the Supervisory Authority or doing so incompletely could be considered inaccurate information and, therefore, constitute an infringement.
Regarding the submission of the prior consultation to the Supervisory Authority, the GDPR, in its Article 39 letter e), establishes that the DPO must be the point of contact for such management:
“e) act as a contact point for the Supervisory Authority for matters related to processing, including the prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and consult, where appropriate, on any other matter.”

XV. 
COLLECTING THE OPINION OF STAKEHOLDERS OR THEIR REPRESENTATIVES

One of the requirements imposed by the DPIA when managing risks (Art. 35.9) is that, "Where appropriate, the controller shall seek the opinion of the stakeholders or their representatives regarding the intended processing, without prejudice to the protection of public or commercial interests or the security of processing operations." 

This consultation aims to make stakeholders, or their representatives, aware that the processing is of high risk, detailing the risk factors, the potential impacts of data breaches, and the intrusion on their rights and freedoms that this entails. Furthermore, the consultation must make stakeholders aware that such risks are being taken for a greater good that compensates for them, identifying it precisely. In any case, the consultation must offer and gather from stakeholders alternatives to achieve the same objectives in a less intrusive manner for their collective interests. 

Therefore, the consultation with stakeholders is intended to be an instrument of transparency and assessment of whether the suitability and necessity of the processing is justified and whether the proportionality is adequate. The consultation cannot be reduced to a satisfaction survey that only presents an image of the benefits, much less if those benefits are not directly related to the purpose of the processing and do not contribute to a general good.

XVI. PRIOR CONSULTATION WITH THE SUPERVISORY AUTHORITY  
Article 36 of the GDPR establishes the obligations that the data controller must fulfill directly, and the processor indirectly, in the event that a DPIA has been conducted and, as a result, it has been concluded that the residual risk of such processing could endanger the rights and freedoms of citizens.  
The prior consultation is not an isolated action; rather, it must be integrated into the risk management strategy for rights and freedoms mandated by the GDPR. In this way, the prior consultation is more than just the simple submission of the DPIA to the Supervisory Authority. The prior consultation involves active collaboration, monitoring of the process, and providing the Supervisory Authority with any additional information it may require during the consultation evaluation process, from which actions could be derived by the controller.  
However, it should be noted that, regardless of whether or not the Supervisory Authority needs to be consulted due to the level of residual risk, the obligations to maintain a documentary record of the execution of the DPIA and any updates that may be made to the assessment remain in effect.  
A.  
PURPOSE OF THE PRIOR CONSULTATION  
The purpose of the prior consultation process is to present to the Supervisory Authority a processing activity that is compliant with the GDPR, for which there is an obligation or an assessment of the opportunity or appropriateness of conducting a DPIA, but which entails a level of residual risk to the rights and freedoms of citizens that could be deemed unacceptable.  
The prior consultation does not aim to:  
• Request the Supervisory Authority to assess the lawfulness of a processing activity.  
• Request the Supervisory Authority to establish the legal bases for carrying out a processing activity.  
• Transfer to the Supervisory Authority the obligation of the controller to carry out the assessment of the proportionality and necessity of the processing.  
• The systematic validation of any processing by the Supervisory Authority.  
• The validation of the DPIA by the Supervisory Authority.  
• Request the Supervisory Authority to carry out the identification and assessment of the risks or the level of inherent risk of a particular processing activity.  
• Request the advice of the Supervisory Authority for the conduct of the DPIA.  
• Require the Supervisory Authority to indicate to the controller alternatives to compliance requirements such as proportionality, necessity, duty to inform, legal bases, conditions of consent, etc., understanding that  

The residual risk is not the risk that could originally arise from the processing, but rather the risk that persists even after implementing the set of measures that the controller has deemed appropriate to address the identified risks.

B. 
THE OBLIGATION TO CARRY OUT A PRIOR CONSULTATION
The Supervisory Authority must be consulted regarding the appropriateness of a processing activity, and always before initiating it, when a data protection impact assessment shows that, in the absence of guarantees, security measures, and mechanisms intended to mitigate risks, such processing would entail a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, and the data controller considers that the risk cannot be mitigated by reasonable means in terms of available technology and implementation costs.
Furthermore, data controllers must consult the Supervisory Authority whenever the law of the Member States requires them to consult such authority or to obtain its prior authorization regarding the processing initiated by a controller in the exercise of a mission carried out in the public interest, particularly processing related to social protection and public health (Article 36, paragraph 5).
Therefore, the prior consultation with the Supervisory Authority is not an obligation for any processing, not even for those processes in which the controller has carried out a DPIA. The existence of a risk in the processing, understood as a risk to the rights and freedoms of citizens, is not a condition that obliges the prior consultation with the Supervisory Authority, just as the obligation or necessity to carry out a DPIA is not equivalent to the obligation to carry out a prior consultation.

The main objective of the GDPR is to protect the rights and freedoms of data subjects whose personal data is being processed. Additionally, and secondly, the GDPR also provides a framework of legal certainty to the controller based on a risk-based approach and self-regulation. In this framework of active responsibility, legal certainty is guaranteed to the extent that the controller has effectively adopted and executed the management of the processing it carries out and has the capacity to demonstrate such management process through the control measures it deems appropriate based on the specificities existing in each of its processing activities at any given time and taking into account the variables already mentioned and defined in the GDPR itself: nature, scope or extent, context, and purposes.

Finally, this advice should not be understood in absolute terms regarding the processing but in relation to those aspects that motivate the prior consultation and the information provided by the controller.

H. 
TRANSPARENCY AND CONFIDENTIALITY OF PRIOR CONSULTATIONS
The prior consultations, as we have seen previously, must include detailed information on the implementation of the processing and, even, technical and organizational aspects of the responsible entity. These aspects can be of great importance as they reflect issues that are highly relevant to the entity, such as its policies, values, procedures, strengths, weaknesses, corporate security measures, and business objectives that are crucial for understanding the scope of the processing and its context.

1. 
Transparency
Regarding the determination of whether the information submitted to the Supervisory Authority within the framework of a prior consultation, as provided in Article 36 of the GDPR, is subject to Law 19/2013 on Transparency, Access to Public Information, and Good Governance (LTAIBG), it should be noted that, on the one hand, such information would be excluded from the scope of active publicity, as it would not fall within any of the cases contemplated in Articles 7 and 8 of the LTAIBG. 
On the other hand, such information would also not, in principle, be subject to the right of access to public information. Although it is public information, it would be affected by the limits on the right of access established in Article 14, to the extent that its dissemination would cause harm to (i) the prevention, investigation, and sanctioning of criminal, administrative, or disciplinary offenses, (ii) the administrative functions of surveillance, inspection, and control, (iii) economic and commercial interests; (iv) economic and monetary policy, or (v) professional secrecy and intellectual and industrial property.

XVII. CONCLUSIONS  
As noted in the introduction, risk management involves a reflective exercise that must be carried out before engaging in any processing of personal data. Its objective is to identify and anticipate potential adverse or unforeseen effects that the processing may have on the data subjects. Risk management should enable the data controller to make the necessary decisions and actions to ensure that the processing complies with the requirements of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, guaranteeing and being able to demonstrate the protection of the rights of the data subjects.  
Risk management is a task that confronts the expectations and aspirations of the data controller with the reality that arises from the consequences of the effective implementation of the processing for the data subjects. It also confronts the short-term objectives of the controller with a long-term vision of the consequences that processing operations may have on the data subjects. Furthermore, it requires a critical analysis of the relationships of a processing operation with its environment.  
The risk management required by the GDPR is specifically aimed at protecting the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, and it should not be confused with other risk orientations, such as compliance risk management. Moreover, risk management for rights and freedoms extends beyond merely managing security risks.  
The proper performance of this task requires the controller to do more than just prepare documents or use decision-making support tools. Risk management requires studying, analyzing, acting, and assuming responsibilities, as well as conducting continuous and ongoing monitoring. Therefore, risk management is a process and, as such, must be effective (achieving its objectives) and efficient (carried out with minimal cost and resource use).  
As a basic tool for executing proactive responsibility, risk management for rights and freedoms must be included and form an indivisible part of the organization’s management policies. Therefore, risk management for rights and freedoms is a task that must be performed "by default" when processing personal data.  
Risk management and Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) are intimately linked processes. The DPIA is a specificity within risk management. Therefore, the DPIA cannot exist without being part of the risk management for rights and freedoms, extending it with a series of additional requirements. While risk management is mandatory for all processing, the specific obligations established for the DPIA are mandatory exclusively for high-risk processing.  
The obligation to conduct a DPIA, established in the GDPR and its development, is a statement of minimums. That is to say, the DPIA may be advisable or necessary in other cases, regardless of whether it is mandatory for specific cases. At times, its use may be advisable as a tool to "ensure and be able to demonstrate" compliance with the GDPR. In any case, regardless of guidelines or lists of obligations, risk management including the guarantees of a DPIA must be carried out "when it is likely that a type of processing, particularly if it uses new technologies, by its nature, scope, context, or purposes, poses a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons."
