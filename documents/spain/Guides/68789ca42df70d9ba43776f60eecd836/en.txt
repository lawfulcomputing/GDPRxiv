ORGANIC LAW 3/2018, OF DECEMBER 5, ON PERSONAL DATA PROTECTION AND 
GUARANTEE OF DIGITAL RIGHTS  
NEWS FOR CITIZENS  
 
1. Right to know the register of personal data processing activities of public organizations  
Citizens have the right to know the list of personal data processing activities carried out by the public organizations with which they interact.  
These organizations must clearly and simply specify who processes personal data, for what purpose the data is processed, and what legal basis legitimizes that processing. 
 
2. Right of citizens to be informed about the exercise of their rights  
Citizens have the right to be informed about the processing of their personal data and the ways to exercise their rights of access, rectification, deletion, the right to limit processing, as well as portability and opposition.  
The means to exercise these rights must be easily accessible to the citizen.  
No public or private organization can deny the exercise of these rights if the citizen wishes to exercise them in a manner different from that offered. 
 
3. Verification of citizens' personal data by public sector bodies and organizations  
Public sector bodies and organizations may verify, without needing to request the consent of the interested party, the accuracy of the personal data provided by citizens and held by the public sector. 
 
4. Communication of citizens' personal data to private entities by public sector bodies and organizations  
Public sector bodies and organizations may communicate citizens' personal data to private entities that request it:  
a) when they have the consent of the citizens,  
b) or when they find that there is a legitimate interest in the requesting private entity that outweighs the rights and interests of the concerned citizens. 
 
5. Submission of documentation to administrative procedures by citizens: modification of Article 28 of Law 39/2015  
Law 30/1992 already recognized the right of citizens not to provide to administrative procedures the documents held by the Administration or that had been prepared by it. The legal basis for the processing of personal data by the Administration was the consent of the citizen, which was understood to be tacitly granted if the interested party did not expressly oppose.  

Both the General Data Protection Regulation and the new Organic Law eliminate the need to obtain the citizen's consent, not even tacit, by establishing compliance with a mission in the public interest or, in particular, the exercise of public powers as the main legitimizing legal basis for the processing of personal data by public sector bodies and organizations.  
Furthermore, the new wording provided by the Organic Law to Article 28 of Law 39/2015 recognizes the citizen's possibility to oppose public sector bodies and organizations consulting or requesting the aforementioned documents, but in that case, the citizen must necessarily provide them so that the Administration can ascertain that the established requirements are met. Otherwise, they will not be able to consider their request, precisely because the required conditions would not have been demonstrated.  
In any case, this right of opposition does not apply in cases of verification or inspection powers. 
 
6. Identification of citizens in administrative acts  
The new Law prohibits the joint use of surnames, first name, and full number of the official identification document of citizens in those administrative acts that are to be published or notified through announcements.  
From the entry into force of the Organic Law:  
• When an administrative act must be published, the person will be identified by their first name and surnames, adding four random numerical digits from their official identification document.  
• When an administrative act is notified through announcements, the person will be identified exclusively by the number of their identification document.  
In both cases, when the person lacks an identification document, they will be identified only by their first name and surnames. 
 
7. Consent as the legal basis that legitimizes the processing of citizens' personal data  
The General Data Protection Regulation and the Organic Law include several legitimizing legal bases for the processing of personal data by private organizations: prior contractual relationship that contemplates the processing, consent of the citizen, or legitimate interest that prevails over the rights of individuals, among others.  
Therefore, currently, it is not necessary for the individual to consent to the processing of their personal data if there is another legal basis that legitimizes the processing.  
In cases where the citizen's consent is necessary due to the absence of another legitimizing basis, the Law establishes that it must be a manifestation of free, specific, informed, and unequivocal will by which a person accepts the processing of their personal data, whether through a declaration or a clear affirmative action. Tacit consent or consent by omission is excluded.

Furthermore, when it is intended that the citizen's consent legitimizes processing for a variety of purposes, it must be specifically and unequivocally stated that consent has been granted for all of them. A contract or the provision of a service cannot be denied on the grounds that the person does not consent to the processing of their personal data for purposes unrelated to that contract or the provision of that service.  

8. Personal data of deceased persons  
Individuals related to a deceased person by family ties, or de facto, or their heirs may request access, rectification, or deletion of the personal data of the deceased person, unless the deceased expressly prohibited it during their lifetime or a law establishes otherwise.  
This section was not included in the 1999 law, which left it to the entities and companies providing services to address or not address the requests for deletion of profiles of deceased persons at the request of their heirs. This omission previously generated conflicting situations, especially on the internet, as the Agency could not oversee the requests made.  

9. Minors  
- Consent and rights: The minimum age at which a minor can give consent for their personal data to be processed remains at 14 years. Holders of parental authority may continue to exercise, on behalf of minors under 14 years, the rights of access, rectification, deletion, opposition, or others that may correspond to them in the context of the protection of their personal data.  
In the case of the right to deletion, if a person exercises it regarding data that has been provided, by themselves or by third parties, to social networks or other information society services during their minority, the provider must delete that data upon simple request.  
- Digital education: The AEPD has promoted the establishment in the new Law of teaching in schools about the responsible use of new technologies. The Law particularly emphasizes the obligations of the educational system to ensure the full integration of students into the digital society and in learning to use digital media in a safe and appropriate manner to guarantee their privacy, including specific training in academic curricula and requiring that teachers receive adequate training in this matter.  
The Government must submit a bill specifically aimed at guaranteeing these rights within one year from the entry into force of the Law, and the educational administrations will have the same period to include such training in the curricula.  
- Data dissemination: Measures are contemplated for the protection of minors' data on the Internet, indicating that parents, guardians, or legal representatives should ensure that minors make balanced and responsible use of digital devices. It also provides for the intervention of the Public Prosecutor's Office when the use or dissemination of images or personal data of minors on social networks or information society services may imply an unlawful intrusion into their rights.  

10. Inclusion in creditworthiness information systems (“delinquent files”)  
Citizens may be included in creditworthiness information systems when they maintain a debt of more than 50 euros with any service provider (the previous law did not establish any minimum amount).  
Citizens may not remain registered in these systems for more than 5 years, counted from the due date of the payment obligation (the previous law established a period of 6 years).  
The Law establishes that these information systems may be consulted:  
- when the person consulting has a contractual relationship with the individual and this relationship involves the payment of a specific amount,  
- when the person has requested financing, deferred payment, or periodic billing.  
If, as a result of the consultation made, the request to enter into the contract is denied or the contract is not concluded, the person who consulted must inform the affected individual of the result of the consultation.  

11. Limitation of advertising activity of companies: the “Robinson lists”  
Citizens can register in advertising exclusion systems (known as “Robinson Lists” www.listarobinson.es) to avoid unwanted advertising through postal, telephone, or electronic channels.  
Citizens registered in the Robinson Lists will only receive advertising from the companies they have authorized.  

12. Rights of employees  
The Law guarantees the right to privacy of employees in the workplace against the use of video surveillance devices and sound recording, as well as against the use of digital devices and geolocation systems, of which they must be informed in an express, clear, and unequivocal manner.  

13. Right to be forgotten on social networks and other equivalent services  
Any citizen may request the deletion of the data they have published on social networks and other equivalent information society services.  
Any citizen may request the deletion of data concerning them that has been provided by third parties for publication on these same services:  
- because they are inadequate, inaccurate, irrelevant, outdated, or excessive, or have become such over time, taking into account the purposes for which they were collected or processed, the time elapsed, and the nature and public interest of the information, or  
- because the citizen may invoke the precedence of their rights over the maintenance of that data.

Citizens shall not have the right to the deletion of such data when it has been provided by third parties in the exercise of personal or domestic activities.

14. Submission of complaints to organizations with a Data Protection Officer (DPO)  
When organizations have designated a Data Protection Officer (DPO), citizens may, before filing a complaint with the Agency, address the DPO for assistance. The DPO shall communicate the decision made to the citizen within a maximum period of two months.  
Furthermore, when the citizen submits a complaint to the Agency, it may forward the complaint to the data controller or to the DPO, if one has been appointed, for a response within one month.  
This aims to ensure that the citizen can obtain a swift resolution to the raised conflict.
