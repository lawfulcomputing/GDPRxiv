EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Technology is changing the way Public Administrations (hereinafter, PAs) work and their relationship with citizens, aiming to improve response times, facilitate accessibility, simplify procedures, and save costs. On the other hand, there is a specific risk associated with the processing of personal data by PAs using emerging technologies.  
In this regard, this document aims to analyze some of the technologies being applied in PAs, highlighting some of their most characteristic aspects from the perspective of data protection and revealing some of the inherent risks associated with their use in any processing.   
This document is not intended to be a guide for compliance with specific processing activities. To this end, the AEPD has been publishing specific guides on some of the topics that will be addressed below, as well as guidelines on the GDPR or specific national regulations developed, documents that will be referenced throughout the text. The objective of this document is to highlight some aspects that could be more characteristic of the use of these technologies in processing carried out by PAs.  
Therefore, the described list of compliance guarantees and risks to be managed is not exhaustive, but rather a relationship of those basic aspects to consider in any processing that uses such technologies. The list of emerging technologies is also not complete. The content of this document is open and is intended to be completed in successive versions based on the contributions received and extending it to other specific technologies with the aim of establishing risk control objectives associated with each of them. 
The intended recipients of this document are primarily the Data Protection Officers of PAs and those public employees responsible for promoting, managing, and using these technologies in the Administration, although the content, and especially the included references, may be useful to a broader audience, such as managers from the private sector who may act as processors or developers for PAs, as well as citizens themselves, to understand how these technologies affect them within the framework of the services provided by PAs.  
This document has sought a simple style for the text, as far removed from formality as possible in both technical and legal aspects, to serve as an introduction for those in the public sector who have to face the design of processing activities that include emerging technologies. 
 
Keywords: GDPR, public administrations, eAdministration, Digital Administration, risks, data protection, privacy, Big data, blockchain, cookies, artificial intelligence, social networks, smart cities, technology.

I. INTRODUCTION  
A.  
TECHNOLOGY AND PUBLIC SERVICE  
Technology invades us, envelops us, and should make our lives easier while preserving, even enriching, our rights and freedoms. A significant part of our personal and professional lives consists of interacting through computers and mobile phones using technology that compels us to continuously renew ourselves and develop new skills.  
Technology has made available to us possibilities that were previously unimaginable. We can shop from home, talk while seeing people who are kilometers away, and manage much more complex tasks in a much faster manner than before. However, this same technology, which serves as an ally to facilitate our daily lives, can, when misunderstood, pose a threat to our rights and freedoms. Our dependence on machines and their proper functioning is practically total; never in human history have so many people known so much about us, our tastes, customs, or secrets, and never has an individual in front of a computer had so much power in their hands to help or harm others.  
The use of information and communication technologies (ICT) by the public sector, the Digital Administration, allows for the provision of more advanced mechanisms to implement both the services they provide and those required for their own internal functioning. These services are, in many cases, treatments of personal data that are implemented using one or more technologies, providing effectiveness, efficiency, availability, interoperability, and the rationalization of resources, among other benefits.  
Public managers, who carry out the processing of personal data, have the obligation to comply with the regulations on data protection within their area of competence, and must apply the necessary technical and organizational measures in each case to guarantee, among other things, the rights of citizens, transparency, and the application of the principles of proactive responsibility, as well as the other processing principles required in the General Data Protection Regulation1 (hereinafter GDPR).  
The adequacy of a processing activity to the GDPR requires that any step taken in the design, implementation, and management of the processing has all the guarantees from the outset. To put it in a very informal way, it is necessary to respond to the following questions: “who?” (Articles 24 and 26 on responsibility), “for what purpose?” (Article 5.1.b GDPR. Principle of purpose limitation), “why?” (Article 5.1.a Principle of lawfulness and fairness and the rest of Chapter II), “how much?” (Article 5.1.c Principle of data minimization), “when?” (Article 5.1.e Principle of storage limitation). Then come the “how?” (Article 5.2 Principle of proactive responsibility, which also encompasses 6.1.d and e, and Chapters IV and V), the “where?” (Articles 28 on the processor, 29, and Chapter V), and the “in what manner?” regarding the rights of the data subjects that must be preserved (Article 5.1.a Principle of transparency and Chapter III).

B.  
THE RISK TO RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS IN PUBLIC ADMINISTRATIONS  
The General Data Protection Regulation and the Organic Law on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights2 (hereinafter, LOPDGDD) contemplate the possibility that the introduction of disruptive, innovative, or not sufficiently mature technologies in processing activities may be a factor that increases the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects; a risk that must be assessed.  
The assessment, management, and minimization of the risk to rights and freedoms is an obligation of the data controller (Articles 23.2.g, 24.1, 25, 32, 33, 34, 35, and 36 among others) and is part of the compliance checklist. The GDPR, although it provides some indications, is not specific in identifying and outlining how to manage the risk of each processing activity in a specific manner.  
Any processing implemented within public administrations entails a series of risks that must be managed just like any other process developed within an organization. This management does not differ from that which any other entity must carry out in the scope of its activities involving the processing of personal data. For example, any entity when launching a product or service must manage, among others, the financial risk of undertaking and implementing a new initiative, the cost risk in relation to the benefit it provides, the risk that the new service can be deployed according to a schedule, the opportunity cost risk, the risk of reliability of the technical options or technologies to be used, the legal risk of future regulatory changes, the compliance risk that exposes it to administrative, civil, or criminal sanctions, the environmental risk, the security risk concerning the continuity of the system, the risk of reputational attacks, the risk of fraud, etc. All these risks are not analyzed separately but must be analyzed integrally3 to reach a decision within the framework of a holistic approach that considers the overall context of the processing.  
Part of this integral analysis includes the management of risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals. This assessment is not related to the risk of compliance with the principles of processing, rights, and obligations established in the GDPR, that is, with the assessment of the possibility of incurring sanctions, significant financial losses, or reputational losses due to non-compliance with laws, regulations, internal standards, and codes of conduct.  
The management of risk to rights and freedoms in the context of personal data processing must consider the possibility that, even when formally complying with the provisions of data protection regulations, the context and scope in which

It should be noted that the processing of personal data in public authorities (AA.PP.) involves different risks compared to the risks of processing that may be carried out by any other data controller, which arise, at least, from the volume of affected subjects, the extent of the data collected, the impossibility, in many cases, of opposing the processing, and the inherent power or asymmetry that exists between public authorities and the citizens or interested parties whose data is being processed. On the other hand, regardless of the fact that all processing in public authorities is guided by a spirit of public service, the same spirit that motivates the work of their employees, these potential risks could materialize for citizens in certain situations, such as, for example, situations of breakdowns in the rule of law, situations of abuse by public officials, in circumstances of mass or selective filtering of personal data as a result of security breaches, in cases of possible legislative changes even in third countries to which the data may have been transferred, in cases of corruption, in situations of uncontrolled emergencies, etc.  
Consequently, public authorities, as they are responsible for processing citizens' data, must identify those risks to which the processing may be exposed before launching new processing activities or modifying existing services that make use of new technologies. They must also adopt the necessary technical and organizational measures that, from the design and by default, allow for the elimination or at least mitigation to an acceptable level of the harm that may arise from the processing for the rights and freedoms of individuals.  
It should be noted that the citizens who may find themselves in a risky situation are not only the administered but also the public employees themselves in the exercise of their functions.  
It is evident that there is no zero risk; both ignoring it and even attempting to address it to reduce its impact or likelihood of occurrence will not make it disappear. Risk is not static and evolves continuously as a result of the very evolution of the environment. This is why, once identified, it requires the controller to make a continuous supervisory effort and ongoing management of that risk. The correct attitude is to know the risk, assess its consequences, take measures to minimize it, and control its effectiveness in a changing context. This continuous supervision scheme is what is defined as risk management.

II.  
COOKIES AND OTHER TRACKING TECHNOLOGIES  
A.  
WHAT ARE COOKIES  
Once the use of the Internet became widespread, the opportunity soon arose to know, for example, whether a visitor to a website was doing so for the first time, where they had come from, which other pages of the portal users had visited, whether they had registered, or to give them the possibility to save settings or session data. Cookies allow for the implementation of these functionalities and many more. For example, cookies enable advertisers to know which websites users visit in order to offer them products that match their preferences; portal managers may also be interested in knowing the pages visited to obtain statistics and make decisions, and so on.  
User activity tracking on the network is implemented through devices that are generically referred to as cookies. This is a strategy used by servers to store and retrieve information from a user's device, allowing for the processing of personal data when they browse and interact with the various applications and content displayed on the Internet. In reality, many techniques that allow for the active or passive tracking of users fall under the term cookies, often in a non-transparent manner. Among these tracking technologies, there are those that utilize device characteristics, unique identifiers, and user browsing habits. Each time we request a page, an image, or content from a web server, we are communicating, at the very least, our IP address, which reveals our geographical location; but also the model of the browser we are using, and consequently, our operating system, the device we are connecting with, and how up-to-date it is. A web server can know if the browsing user has a pop-up blocker, how much memory their device has, what graphics card they are using, or how they move the mouse across the screen. All this information is grouped under the generic name of fingerprint or device fingerprint and can be used on Internet servers to link all user activity and thus create a profile of them.  
The GDPR addresses cookies in its recital 308, recognizing their ability to create profiles of individuals and identify them. The special regulation that governs the use of cookies for information society services is included in Article 22.2 of Law 34/2002, of July 11, on information society services and electronic commerce (LSSI). The criteria of the AEPD for compliance with cookie regulations for information society service providers are outlined in the Spanish regulation as a translation of the English word "device" used in Directive 2002/58/EC or the ePrivacy Directive. The semantic extension of the word "device" includes: artifact, strategy, gadget, or resource.

B.  
COOKIES AND PUBLIC ADMINISTRATIONS  
The Applicability of the LSSI  
The subjects obligated by the special regulations governing cookies are the service providers of the information society, understood as “any natural or legal person who provides services normally for a fee, at a distance, by electronic means and at the individual request of the recipient, as well as those services not remunerated by their recipients, insofar as they constitute an economic activity for the service provider” 12.  
As explained on the page about the Law on Information Society Services13 of the Ministry of Economic Affairs and Digital Agenda:  
In general, the LSSI does not apply to Public Administrations, since they do not have the character of a service provider of the information society defined in its annex. Thus, certain typical activities of the Administrations, such as the electronic management of tax collection or information about third-party services (such as mere information on a Town Hall's website about existing rural houses in the municipal area) are considered public or general interest activities distinct from the "economic activity" referred to in the LSSI.  
However, when the activity of an Administration does have an economic character (for example, the sale of tourist books by a public entity dependent on a Town Hall), the LSSI will be applicable.  
That is to say, under this approach and generally speaking, a Public Administration that has a website, by virtue of having it, would not necessarily be considered a provider of services of the information society and, consequently, as a subject obligated by the LSSI. It can only be considered as such if it offers a “service normally provided for a fee, at a distance, by electronic means and at the individual request of the recipient,” for example, if a service involving an economic activity, such as the sale of books, tickets, etc., is provided on a domain belonging to a Public Administration. In that case, the LSSI will apply to those specific domains where such economic activity is carried out.

Use of tracking technologies in third-party components  
As mentioned in the previous section, portals and mobile applications often serve as large containers or structures from which elements provided by third-party suppliers hang. When browsing web pages, users are actually accessing multiple components such as images, embedded videos, fonts, styles, advertisements, statistical counters, social media widgets, or various plugins that are distributed across multiple third-party servers, and each of them can install its cookie or record our visit. When public administrations build their services using such components, they must audit whether these use tracking technologies, not only when designing the system but also during the operational period (system and processing lifecycle) and remain vigilant about the emergence of these potential tracking elements in updates of their official portals, as well as in HTML emails or images, RSS news feeds, or any other product.  
C.  
RISKS TO RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS ASSOCIATED WITH THE USE OF COOKIES  
One of the main risks of using cookies or other tracking technologies is the collection of personal information beyond what is necessary for the purpose of processing, especially concerning special categories of data, whether directly or indirectly, or providing means for third parties to do so. Therefore, it is necessary to assess, even for technical cookies, to what extent their use may lead to the collection or inference of additional information about the subject and to take appropriate measures to minimize such risk.  
This circumstance will be more serious when tracking technologies are used by third parties, as is the case with tracking technologies employed by components embedded in the website or in a mobile application of the Administration. This can even occur indirectly when websites or apps are built using elements such as fonts served by third parties, images, maps, tools, or other applications. Also, when, to facilitate the availability of the page, the content is distributed among multiple servers that do not belong to the responsible party.  
It is important to consider the risks in the use of tools for implementing websites, such as the aforementioned CMS, as they tend to update dynamically. Configurations may vary between versions, resulting from automatic updates, and in some cases, these are not subject to prior quality control.

A.  
WHAT ARE SOCIAL NETWORKS  
Social networks are one of the most used information channels on the Internet. In fact, along with television (72%) and newspaper websites and applications (44%), social networks (53%) are the media that Internet users rely on the most for information.  
The information contained in social networks can be of various types:  
• Publications of documents, texts, photographs, videos, links, physical activity data, etc.  
• Comments and responses to publications, forming a dialogue thread between publishers and readers.  
• User profiles, which can be both individuals and organizations. They contain personal and contact information, as well as a history of their publications and their connections with other elements of the network (friends, followers, etc.). Social networks offer different mechanisms for protecting their users' content.  
Users find in social networks a simple, accessible, and immediate channel through which they can share content, socialize, or even provide a showcase to promote their products or services, generally at no cost (or even receiving money for their publications, as in the case of influencers). In social networks, there is a processing that goes beyond mere content exchange between users and allows for the monetization (earning money) of social network activity. On one hand, the social network profiles users to publish personalized ads that, being tailored to their tastes and characteristics, have a very high impact. On the other hand, it could sell users' data to companies or institutions interested in profiling their customers and potential customers to offer them products or services through other channels. These processes are usually legitimized either by the user's consent or within the framework of the execution of a contract with or without economic consideration.  
To publish, and sometimes to access content on a social network, it is necessary to register as a user. Depending on the social network, it may be mandatory for the user to identify themselves with a real identity, thus preventing the user from registering anonymously, with pseudonyms, or from having multiple accounts with various identities. Other networks, however, preserve the anonymity of their users at least at the time of registration.  
Within social networks, the rules for acceptable publications or etiquette are established by the service provider, which generally relies on a community or participatory structure. Social networks, for European users, are subject to the legislation of the European Union (EU), such as the GDPR, and to the applicable laws where their users are located. CLOUD COMPUTING  
A.  
WHAT IS CLOUD COMPUTING  
Cloud computing is a way of using servers in remote locations flexibly and transparently. Instead of having their own equipment, purchased and hosted on the organization's premises, a provider 'rents' virtual equipment or specific services, which are managed over the network. This model eliminates the issues of initial investments and obsolescence, allowing the customer to pay at any given moment for the computing capacity, bandwidth, or services they need. The service provider can contractually guarantee service quality parameters, privacy guarantees, security aimed at other purposes, maintenance, software package updates, etc.  
Although in the early days of cloud computing the tools offered by the provider were the same as those a customer could purchase and host on their machines, there are now databases, libraries, programming languages, and specific software components for the cloud, as well as certifications, specialists, and an entire academic discipline.  
Depending on the type of services offered to the customer, we can distinguish between IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service) solutions if the provider only provides raw storage and processing capacity; PaaS (Platform as a Service) if the basic utilities to build applications are provided on which to develop solutions, and SaaS (Software as a Service) when the customer finds all the final tools in the cloud to implement their organization's processes. Another possible classification of clouds is the separation between public and private. We speak of a public cloud when providers offer 'loose' services to different clients, such as web space, storage, or processing capacity. At the other end, we speak of a private cloud when the provider offers a series of grouped services in a closed manner to third parties. We could compare a private cloud to a private network or an intranet of an organization, with its elements isolated from the outside, except for localized access, and with centralized management.  
With the popularization of cloud services, there were occasions when services were offered that were not transparent about the location where the data is processed. This virtualization and delocalization is not without risks for both security and the protection of personal data.

BIG DATA OR MASS DATA PROCESSING  
A.  
WHAT IS BIG DATA  
According to the definition provided by ISO, when we talk about Big Data87, or mass data processing, we refer to large sets of data characterized by their volume, variety, velocity, and/or variability, which require scalable technology for efficient storage, manipulation, management, and analysis.  
Mass data processing technologies have developed significantly in recent years, opening a wide range of treatments. The Internet has made a vast amount of data available to all of us that can be utilized. Public Administrations themselves, through the Aporta Initiative88 and the portal datos.gob.es, promoted by the Ministry of Economic Affairs and Digital Transformation and the Public Business Entity Red.es, promote the opening and reuse of public information and the development of advanced services based on data.  
The massive analysis of data has allowed us to obtain information in real time, or almost real time, from information sources and datasets distributed around the world. There are specific software platforms to extract, load, and transform (the acronyms ELT or Extract, Load and Transform are used as opposed to the acronym ETL for dashboards) data from different sources and exploit that information. This exploitation can take very varied forms, ranging from presenting it graphically, for example, as dashboards, to constructing new information or profiles by regrouping the data in a way different from how it was obtained.  
From the perspective of personal data protection, it is crucial to ensure that there is a legitimacy89 for such mass data processing and, in the case that special categories of data are included, it is necessary to lift the prohibition for their processing90 beforehand. These conditions must be met not only for the data included in the collection of collected data but also for those that may be inferred from the crossing and connection of the original data.  
In the design phase of Big Data treatments, it is necessary to objectively analyze what amount of data is necessary and sufficient in relation to the purpose of the processing91, to adhere to the principle of data minimization92, and not to adopt "just in case" strategies where, without having established prior selection criteria, the maximum possible amount of data is collected. This problem can be exacerbated in the case of mass data collection supported by sensors in processing contexts such as those carried out in Smart Cities.

The aggregation process to obtain knowledge involves combining data, often from different sources of information, with the privacy risks this represents for the data subjects:  
1. Re-identification of individuals or singularization, increasing the likelihood of this occurring the larger the volume of data processed, even in those datasets that apparently may not contain primary or explicit identifiers114.  
2. Linkability of different records of the same data subject or group of data subjects, whether in the same dataset or through the connection of heterogeneous and independent data sources, via correlation analysis.  
3. Inference, from personal data classified as quasi-identifiers115, of much more critical personal information that was not intended to be processed.  
These privacy risks must be evaluated from the very conception of the processing that makes use of Big Data techniques and their analytical tools for information exploitation, incorporating, from the design stage, the necessary strategies to mitigate them, which will have been identified as a result of the data protection impact assessment carried out.  
When the information of the same person is enriched with data from different sources of information, we may likely discover new connections or nuances of their personality that would not have manifested separately. That is, we deduce and derive new information about the individual, so that, regardless of the specific purpose of the processing, it is necessary to analyze the risk of unwanted inferences occurring, especially regarding special categories of data.  
Moreover, it is even possible that, by cross-referencing several data sources that were supposedly anonymous, through data aggregation, the identity of specific individuals may be revealed: from general traits, the number of individuals in the intersection of all of them decreases, until specific individuals are identified116. The risks of re-identification must be measured, evaluated, and managed by taking the necessary measures to reduce the likelihood of such re-identification occurring, even anticipating possible reactive measures to mitigate any potential harm that could arise to a natural person in cases where re-identification does occur. If it concerns data of special categories, such as medical data, we find ourselves in a situation of greater risk due to the greater impact it represents on the rights and freedoms of individuals, so the guarantees to be adopted must be superior, a matter that must also be considered when processing data of minors or individuals in conditions of special vulnerability.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE  
A.  
WHAT IS ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE  
The term artificial intelligence or AI is associated with machines or information systems that, in the performance of their functions, are capable of learning from their own experiences and solving problems, more or less complex, in different situations, such that they give the impression of 'thinking' or showing some intelligence128.  
This intelligence, in a process like adjusting the focus of a camera129, may seem of little importance, but it can also be very sophisticated, as in the case of an expert medical diagnosis system. In recent years, artificial intelligence has emerged from laboratories and has been incorporated into multiple everyday systems: internet search engines, machine translation, smartwatches, smart refrigerators, smart mobile apps, and of course, intelligent systems in public administrations.  
Under the umbrella of artificial intelligence, very diverse techniques130 are included.  
We could talk about natural language recognition, machine learning, pattern recognition, etc. Many times, the intelligent system is capable of performing very complex tasks, such as playing chess or planning logistics routes, but at other times it is used in the context of small decisions that would be easy and repetitive for a human, thus allowing machines to replace people. This is the case with text or telephone chatbots131, for scheduling appointments or maintaining a closed dialogue with a user, or also with spam filters or facial recognition in photographs, or even the recognition of expressions or moods.  
Another element that has favored the introduction of artificial intelligence in many processes is the way applications are developed. While traditional program development is based on precisely defining a process by specifying a series of elementary instructions, systems based on machine learning132 employ a different technique. This development starts from less precise instructions on how to do the work133, but sufficient to begin operating, and the system is 'trained' with test data, learns the relationships between inputs and outputs, and corrects its results when it fails, ultimately obtaining a system that knows how to work thanks to its 'previous experience'. This way of developing systems, which closely resembles how humans learn certain skills, works well with some tasks that are difficult to define and detail.

BLOCKCHAIN AND DISTRIBUTED LEDGER TECHNOLOGIES  
A.  
WHAT IS BLOCKCHAIN  
Blockchain or chain of blocks156, DLT or Bitcoin are terms that have emerged forcefully in recent times and have generated a lot of interest in recent years. Technologists and many people have been surprised by the novelty of virtual currencies like Bitcoin157, and by their apparent transparency and integrity of the stored information.  
In simple terms, we can define blockchain as a network of participants (peers or nodes) that share a distributed ledger in which it is recorded who owns what (assets) and who transacts with whom (transactions). Unlike traditional centralized systems where databases are controlled by a single central authority, in blockchain all nodes maintain a copy of this ledger, making it extremely difficult to manipulate the recorded information without the network, understood as the set of participants, being aware of the attempted change. Blockchain gets its name from the way this ledger is organized: a set of blocks in which transactions are grouped and that are linked to each other, in chronological order, through a cryptographic mechanism called hash that guarantees the integrity and immutability of the information recorded in the chain.  
Blockchain is a particular case of a broader concept known as distributed ledger technologies or DLT, which refer to distributed and decentralized databases managed by several participants, typically equivalent from an authority standpoint. In the absence of a central entity that performs the functions of verification and validation of information, mechanisms of consensus among participants are established to decide how decisions are made, how data is updated, and how the stored information is maintained in a consistent state.  
Proponents of blockchain envision a future where intermediaries or notaries are not needed to safeguard an account, a contract, or a record. All transactions would be recorded in seemingly immutable and transparent chains that could even be automatically updated through smart contracts158 hosted on the chain itself (the so-called smart contracts).  
There are several types of blockchains, but they all share, to a greater or lesser extent, some common characteristics: transparency, immutability, decentralization, integrity, and trust. These characteristics enable one of their main attractions, which is the elimination of intermediaries in the processing of transactions between parties, which is also one of their main weaknesses when it comes to establishing legal guarantees for their operation.  
To move towards this scenario, several blockchain infrastructures have been promoted, which differ from each other in the visibility or scope of their operations (public or private) and in the management of the blockchain (permissioned or non-permissioned).

As with any other technology, a blockchain implementation may be exclusively processing information that does not contain personal data, such as, for example, a record of the movement of goods. However, once a blockchain network is used for the custody of personal data, it is evident that the processing involved must comply with the requirements established in the GDPR, including, among others, a clear identification of the controllers (and other roles), a legal basis for the processing, guarantees of confidentiality and availability of the data, and the possibility for the data subject to exercise rights.  
Another issue to consider in the deployment of such networks is how they will be affected by the location of the data. The GDPR is very clear regarding data transfers between countries with different levels of protection, and the idea of a decentralized storage network does not facilitate the application of those guarantees. On the other hand, information encryption algorithms are becoming increasingly robust, but we also discover every day how the presumed robustness of a few years ago is now compromised using increasingly accessible equipment.  
Before launching a blockchain project, the compatibility of the solution with the regulatory requirements imposed by the General Data Protection Regulation must be analyzed. Specifically, the following aspects must be carefully examined:  
•  
Responsibility for processing. The blockchain, although it depends on the type of blockchain network implemented, is, by definition, a decentralized system where it is difficult to identify the controller(s) of the processing.  
•  
Right to erasure and rectification. One of the principles on which blockchain is based is the impossibility of altering the content without producing an inconsistency, so the existence of solutions for the deletion or modification of recorded information must be carefully analyzed.  
•  
Limited data retention. One of the principles of processing personal data is that “they should only be kept in a way that allows the identification of the data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of processing the personal data,” so it is necessary to implement alternative mechanisms that address the immutability inherent to the network.  
•  
Security. In some blockchain implementations, there are two important security aspects that may be at risk. The first is the confidentiality of the data when information is exposed on the network. The second, and less obvious, is availability, since, although in principle the information is distributed across many nodes, there is generally no guarantee of the availability of those nodes (service level agreements), not even of a single node.

RISKS TO RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS ASSOCIATED WITH BLOCKCHAIN  
Prior to the implementation of a blockchain-based solution that requires the processing of personal data, it is necessary to assess, within the framework of a data protection impact assessment, the necessity and proportionality of using this technology compared to other possible alternatives and the additional risks it introduces by its very definition and design.  
Another important decision, once blockchain has been chosen as the technological solution, relates to the choice of the type of network that will be implemented to support the processing. If there is already a processing project that complies with the provisions of the GDPR, the risk of opting for other types of blockchain instead of choosing a private and governed blockchain network that is under the total control of a Public Administration must be evaluated.  
The implementation of blockchain over the services offered requires Public Administrations to conduct a proportionality assessment, evaluating the main benefits of the services offered against the main challenges in terms of data protection mentioned above. It is the responsibility of the Public Administration to assess whether the adopted technological solution is the most appropriate or, on the contrary, introduces risks that cannot be managed. In particular, it is important to evaluate which type of blockchain network architecture (public, private, permissioned, and non-permissioned) best fits the solution, as not all of them represent the same level of risk, in addition to defining the most appropriate governance model. It should not be overlooked that the failure to...

The possibility of technically complying with any of the obligations required by the GDPR for the data controller should not be considered a risk susceptible to treatment but rather a regulatory non-compliance.  
In the event that the blockchain network used relies on a governance mechanism that is not under the control of the public authorities, it must be anticipated that changes may be introduced that jeopardize the principles and rights of data protection. Furthermore, it is necessary to determine whether there is a risk that the quality of service levels, in terms of node availability or response times, may not be met. In an extreme case, we could find ourselves in a situation where the nodes of the blockchain network cease their activity. Therefore, in addition to legal guarantees, contingency plans must be in place to ensure the continuity of processing, which could include, for example, portability options to ensure that at least the rights of the data subjects can be protected.  
The security of blockchain fundamentally relies on the robustness of its cryptographic mechanisms. Every cryptographic system has a limited and indeterminate lifespan. Therefore, it is necessary to manage the obsolescence timeline and the possibility that at some point the entire system may be compromised.  
The nature of the distributed system makes it more complex to control access to data, as well as to maintain a record of accesses. This circumstance can be exacerbated when the chosen blockchain model allows for the dynamic entry and exit of participants.  
In relation to this point and the dynamic configuration of participants in blockchain, it is necessary to manage the chosen consensus mechanism model when generating the blockchain and continuously assess the risk of manipulation.  
By virtue of data protection by design173, a pseudonymization strategy is to limit the information uploaded to the blockchain to commitments174 or, if this is not possible, in the form of hashes175 using a hash function with a key or, at least, in the form of encryption to ensure the confidentiality of the information recorded on the chain. The publication of personal data in clear text on the blockchain should be avoided; such data should be stored off-chain, in traditional databases or other information systems of the data controller.  
It should not be forgotten that blockchain is the solution, or one of the technological solutions, that supports data processing, within which there will be other tools for access, management, and even, intermediary mechanisms for users. All these elements have their own vulnerabilities, specifically regarding security, so risks must be analyzed and necessary measures implemented to prevent identity theft in the execution of transactions176 177 178 179 and ensure comprehensive security.

VIII. SMARTCITIES OR INTELLIGENT CITIES  
A.  
WHAT ARE INTELLIGENT CITIES  
The concept of intelligent city or Smart City has been used by the European Commission to group a set of very diverse technological projects aimed at optimizing resource management in cities through the use of technology applied to manage, in a more efficient manner, the typical services of a city, such as transportation, infrastructure, electricity, water and gas supply, waste management, or social assistance.  
The most characteristic projects of intelligent cities are based, first of all, on the automated collection of data through various types of sensors distributed throughout the city: traffic sensors, human movement sensors, air quality sensors, energy consumption sensors, etc. Next, there is an automated analysis of this data, which can be carried out in an integrated manner and even enriching the data from other sources. If these data are properly combined, they can serve to size and predict what services and in what quantity need to be offered. Finally, there is a phase of drawing conclusions or making decisions that could, in some cases, be applied automatically through triggers, for example, to regulate vehicular traffic in a large city, and that may involve other technologies reviewed in this document such as artificial intelligence.  
In other words, although one can speak of Smart City technology, it could be considered as the integration with a purpose of urban management of different technologies such as artificial intelligence techniques and Big Data, which also appear in this document, and, in particular, the development of IoT (Internet of Things) projects.  
B.  
SMART CITY AND PUBLIC ADMINISTRATIONS  
Within the framework of the competencies of Public Administrations to provide quality services to citizens with the greatest possible efficiency and sustainability, Smart City technology offers municipal leaders the ability to obtain information, in real-time through sensors or data sources of certain services, about the behavior of cities and their inhabitants. Some data sources could be smart counters for pedestrians, the use of mobile phone data, transport card data, among many others.

The study evaluates aspects of 176 of the main cities in the world with the aim of scoring and comparing, among other aspects, the use of technologies that improve the quality of life and facilities for their citizens. In the last example, we can see that not all data processed in a smart city is personal. Furthermore, the achievement of efficiency objectives in Smart Cities does not justify mass and indiscriminate collection of personal data.  
Following the principle of minimization185, the data collected must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.  
The purpose of the information collected may be to execute both public functions and scientific research or statistical purposes. In such cases, the compatibility of these latter treatments with the previous purposes must be considered, as established in Article 5.1.b and Article 89 of the GDPR, provided they are subject to appropriate safeguards. For example, when it comes to the automatic, real-time collection of information for statistical purposes, among other legal, technical, and organizational measures, the size of the sample must be analyzed, determined by the number of subjects or elements of a population necessary for the obtained data to be representative and effective. The amount of personal data collected, understood both as the extent of the number of subjects and the set of data collected from the same subject, must undergo a proportionality and necessity analysis.  
In this regard, for many treatments related to the Smart City, it does not seem necessary to uniquely identify citizens; rather, the purpose could be fulfilled by using anonymous and aggregated data. To predict when and where traffic peaks occur, where it is necessary to optimize energy supply, or where waste collection needs improvement, it is not necessary to process the identifiers of the individuals receiving these services.  
Public Administrations, adhering to the principle of minimization and considering the necessity and proportionality of the treatment, must analyze whether it is required to identify citizens, assessing whether the use of anonymous and aggregated data is sufficient for the objectives of the treatment. To predict when and where traffic peaks occur, where it is necessary to optimize energy supply, or where waste collection needs improvement, the use of identifiers of the individuals receiving these services should be avoided.  
Another aspect to consider is that the principle of fairness186 of the treatment must be adhered to, meaning that the data collected will be used for the intended purpose and the original objective. For example, when a treatment aims to ensure that a certain area of the city has access restrictions, it is necessary to differentiate the purpose of controlling that access from the sanctioning activity against those who do not comply with such restrictions. Therefore, the treatment must seek maximum effectiveness in the original purpose while being proportional to the degree of intrusion into privacy.

To manage a collection of projects such as those associated with smart cities, collaboration between public administrations and private companies, as well as other groups, may be required to exchange data among themselves. This exchange requires interoperable formats and accessible data.

Providing them with the appropriate information regarding the collection and use of data, beyond strict compliance with Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR and the application of privacy by design principles as stated in Recital 39, allows citizens to be aware of the risks, rules, safeguards, and rights related to the processing of personal data as well as how to exercise their rights in relation to the processing, thus increasing their level of trust and commitment to the project. The public administration's website is also a good place to publicize specific data-sharing projects with other public or private entities, thereby clarifying and extending aspects of the purpose and highlighting the added value intended to be achieved with these projects. The demands for the generation of open data and data sharing with third parties must be reconciled with the principles of transparency that allow for the legitimization of the processing to be preserved.  
C.  
RISKS TO RIGHTS AND FREEDOMS ASSOCIATED WITH SMART CITIES  
When drafting contracts between controllers, joint controllers, and processors, it is important to consider that Smart City projects are long-term approaches, with consequences that may evolve significantly due to changes in the social or technological context. The maintenance of devices and systems, ownership and responsibility for updating them, as well as support for the controller to manage all risks to rights and freedoms (including the likely DPIA) are aspects that must be reflected in these contracts.  
Public administrations must be aware that the greater the amount of information collected and the frequency of its acquisition, the greater the inherent risk to the protection of citizens' data. These processes are inherently high-risk.

We all have unique behavioral habits that reveal our work, whom we live with, our health, or even our political and religious beliefs. The risk of re-identification must be assessed, and measures must be taken to mitigate it, such as applying differential privacy techniques, employing information aggregation strategies to avoid correlations, resorting to local and distributed processing to reduce the amount of data stored centrally by a single controller, etc.193  
The mass installation of sensors and actuators increases the likelihood of security failures in the three domains: confidentiality, availability, and integrity, so a very delicate aspect to consider is security against possible failures and intentional attacks194. This situation materialized massively in 2007 in the city of Tallinn195. The security risk analysis from the perspective of data protection must provide maximum guarantees to prevent unauthorized access that allows monitoring of individuals or massive filtering of personal data, one of the major risks faced by Smart Cities.  
Security cannot be absolute, but preventive plans for continuous auditing can be implemented, such as ethical hacking, analysis of how risks evolve based on context (for example, with the deployment of new services and technologies), and measures that minimize the impact that a breach may have, no matter how unlikely it may be. These measures include applying data minimization criteria, separating data across different systems, in time and categories, obscuring data through encryption techniques or early pseudonymization, or blocking data. In some cases, it might be interesting to allow the data subject to have control over the automatic collection of their data, implementing autonomous and transparent mechanisms that involve the participation of the citizens themselves, so that they can verify the effective compliance with the established guarantees.  
There are two critical moments that may pose a greater risk to rights and freedoms: the moment of deployment and the moment of withdrawal of the system. The first implies that the system will not be adjusted, particularly concerning data protection risks, so it is advisable for deployments to be limited and to seek the advice of the supervisory authorities. The second is at the moment of withdrawal or replacement of the entire system, as an abandoned or semi-abandoned system can result in an added vulnerability.

IX.  
CONCLUSIONS  
The digital transformation of public administrations (AA.PP.) is a reality resulting from the intensive use of new technologies, some of which are cutting-edge, aimed at improving both their internal operations and the quality of service to citizens. The possibilities they offer in various sectors such as health, education, or research are infinite, and it is undeniable that they are a key element for improving the effectiveness and efficiency in the provision of public services.  
Regardless of the fact that all services implemented in public administrations are guided by a spirit of public service, the processing of personal data within these organizations carries a characteristic risk that arises from the volume of affected subjects, the extent of the data collected, the impossibility, in many cases, of opposing the processing, and the existing imbalance between the administration and citizens.  
The inherent risks can materialize for the interested parties in situations of breakdowns of the rule of law, abuse by public officials, mass or selective leaks of personal data, security breaches, legislative changes even in third countries, corruption, uncontrolled emergencies, etc.  
The fundamental right to data protection originated from the need to prevent and avoid the tragic excesses that occurred in the 20th century, which were based on extensive, intrusive, and, in some cases, somewhat automated processing of personal data, even despite the efforts of public employees who fought against a system that had already surpassed them.  
In this regard, it is important to remember that data protection regulations are an instrument that, in addition to protecting the rights and freedoms of citizens, allows emerging technologies in society to be employed in public administrations in a sustainable manner and in line with the social responsibility policies of the institutions.  
Data protection regulations in Europe and in Spain are modern and well-aligned with good practices in current engineering and project management. This is essentially a proactive approach that anticipates problems and highlights the responsibility of the actors; and a continuous risk management process, avoiding it, detecting it early, and planning the response. In this sense, it can be said that complying with the law and doing things sensibly go hand in hand.  
Technology is changing the world, and it is important to design treatments that ensure that this change is for the better, at least in terms of respecting the rights and freedoms of individuals. Introducing technology into the traditional processes of public administrations, or implementing new processes based on it, is becoming increasingly easier, but this does not mean it should be more risky, intrusive, or uncontrolled. On the contrary, the creation of new services in a constantly developing society, both socially and technologically, introduces a greater exposure to risk that requires individual analysis and management in each case and conditions that those responsible for the processing must...

B.  
CONTACT WITH DATA PROTECTION AUTHORITIES  
The Spanish Agency for Data Protection is the independent administrative authority at the state level responsible for supervising the application of the LOPDGDD and the GDPR. In addition, the Autonomous Communities of Andalusia, Catalonia, and the Basque Country also have autonomous data protection authorities that can exercise the functions and powers attributed by the Regulation to the supervisory authorities, in the cases provided for in Article 57.1 of the LOPDGDD, namely:  
a) Processing for which the entities comprising the public sector of the corresponding Autonomous Community or the Local Entities included in its territorial scope are responsible, or those providing services through any form of direct or indirect management.  
b) Processing carried out by natural or legal persons for the exercise of public functions in matters that fall under the competence of the corresponding Autonomous or Local Administration.  
c) Processing that is expressly provided for, where applicable, in the respective Statutes of Autonomy.  
In accordance with all of the above, in general, Public Authorities must notify security breaches to the AEPD through the channel enabled on the electronic headquarters. However, in the case of Andalusia, Catalonia, and the Basque Country, when security breaches occur in the aforementioned cases, the competent supervisory authority will be that of the corresponding Autonomous Community, namely:  
•  
In the case of Andalusia: the Council of Transparency and Data Protection of Andalusia through its electronic window  
•  
In the case of Catalonia: the Catalan Data Protection Authority (https://apdcat.gencat.cat/es/inici/) through its electronic headquarters.  
•  
In the case of the Basque Country: the Basque Data Protection Agency via email avpd@avpd.eus
