I.  
PRIVACY BY DESIGN  
CONCEPT OF PRIVACY BY DESIGN  
The idea of ‘data protection by design’ has existed for over 20 years and has been intensely worked on under the terminology of ‘privacy by design’ (Privacy by Design, PbD). This concept was developed by Ontario’s Information and Privacy Commissioner, Ann Cavoukian, in the 1990s; presented at the 31st International Conference of Data Protection and Privacy Commissioners in 2009 under the title “Privacy by Design: The Definitive Workshop” [1][2] and internationally accepted at the 32nd International Conference of Data Protection and Privacy Commissioners held in Jerusalem in 2010, with the approval of the “Resolution on Privacy by Design” [3].  
This resolution recognized the importance of incorporating privacy principles within the design, operation, and management processes of organizational systems to achieve a comprehensive protection framework regarding data protection. Furthermore, it encouraged the adoption of the Foundational Principles of Privacy by Design defined by Ann Cavoukian and invited Data Protection Authorities to actively work and promote the incorporation of privacy by design in the policies and legislation regarding data protection of their respective States.

FOUNDATIONAL PRINCIPLES OF PRIVACY BY DESIGN

PbD is based on building privacy as the default mode of operation within the business model of organizations, extending to the information technology systems that support data processing, the related business processes and practices, and the physical and logical design of the communication channels used. This assurance of privacy can be achieved through the implementation of the seven Foundational Principles defined by Ann Cavoukian [10] [11]:

1. Proactive, not reactive; preventive, not corrective.
PbD involves anticipating events that affect privacy before they occur. 

Any system, process, or infrastructure that will use personal data must be conceived and designed from the ground up, identifying, a priori, the possible risks to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects and minimizing them so that they do not materialize into harm. A PbD policy is characterized by the adoption of proactive measures that anticipate threats, identifying the weaknesses of the systems to neutralize or minimize risks rather than applying corrective measures to resolve security incidents once they have occurred. In other words, PbD avoids the "fix-it policy" and anticipates the materialization of the risk event. 

This implies:
• A clear commitment from the organization, which must be driven from the highest levels of management.
• The development of a culture of commitment and continuous improvement by all employees, as a policy is of no use if it does not translate into concrete actions that are informed by their results.
• Definition and assignment of specific responsibilities, so that each member of the organization clearly knows their functions regarding privacy.
• Development of systematic methods based on indicators for the early detection of processes and practices that are yielding deficient results in ensuring privacy.

2. Privacy by Default  
Privacy by Design (PbD) aims to provide the user with the highest level of privacy given the state of the art and, in particular, that personal data is automatically protected in any system, application, product, or service.  
The default settings must be established from the design at a level that is as respectful as possible in terms of privacy. In the event that the subject does not take any configuration action, their privacy must be guaranteed and remain intact, as it is integrated into the system and configured by default.  
This principle, put into practical terms, is based on data minimization throughout all stages of processing: collection, use, retention, and dissemination.  
To achieve this, it is necessary to:  
• Set collection criteria limited to the purpose pursued by the processing.  
• Limit the use of personal data to the purposes for which they were collected and ensure that there is a legitimate basis for the processing.  
• Restrict access to personal data to the parties involved in the processing according to the principle of "need to know" and based on the function they perform by creating differentiated access profiles.  
• Define strict retention periods and establish operational mechanisms that ensure compliance.  
• Create technological and procedural barriers that prevent unauthorized linking of independent data sources.  

3. Privacy Embedded in the Design Phase  
Privacy must be an integral and inseparable part of systems, applications, products, and services, as well as of the business practices and processes of the organization.  
It is not an additional layer or module added to something pre-existing, but must be integrated into the set of non-functional requirements from the very moment it is conceived and designed.  
To ensure that privacy is taken into account from the earliest stages of design, it is necessary to:  
• Consider it a necessary requirement in the life cycle of systems and services, as well as in the design of the organization’s processes.  
• Conduct a risk analysis for the rights and freedoms of individuals and, where applicable, impact assessments related to data protection, as an integral part of the design of any new processing initiative.  
• Document all decisions made within the organization with a "privacy design thinking" approach.  

4. Full Functionality: "Win-Win" Thinking  
Traditionally, it has been understood that gaining privacy comes at the cost of losing other functionalities, presenting dichotomies such as privacy vs usability, privacy vs functionality, privacy vs business benefit, and even privacy vs security.
Page | 9 of 58

This approach is artificial [12] and the objective must be to seek the optimal balance in a "win-win" type of search, with an open mindset towards new solutions to achieve fully functional, effective, and efficient systems, also at the level of privacy. 

To this end, from the early stages of product and service conception, the organization must: 
• Assume that different and legitimate interests can coexist: those of the entity and those of the users it serves; and that it is necessary to identify, evaluate, and appropriately balance them.  
• Establish communication channels to collaborate and consult with stakeholders in order to understand and converge multiple interests, which may initially seem divergent. 
• If the proposed solution poses threats to privacy, seek new solutions and alternatives to achieve the various functionalities and interests pursued, but always keeping in mind that the risks to user privacy must be adequately managed. 

5. Ensuring Privacy Throughout the Lifecycle 
Privacy is born in the design, before the system is operational, and must be guaranteed throughout the entire lifecycle of the data. 
Information security imposes confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of the systems that house it. Privacy also guarantees unlinkability, transparency, and the ability for the data subject to intervene and control the processing (intervenability). 

To integrate privacy throughout all stages of data processing, the various operations involved (collection, recording, classification, retention, consultation, dissemination, limitation, deletion, etc.) must be carefully analyzed, and the most appropriate measures to protect the information must be implemented in each of them, including: 
• Early pseudonymization or anonymization techniques such as k-anonymity [13]. 
• Classification and organization of data and processing operations based on access profiles. 
• Default encryption so that the "natural" state of the data in case of loss or theft is "illegible." 
• Secure and guaranteed destruction of information at the end of its lifecycle.

6. Visibility and Transparency  
One of the keys to ensuring privacy is the ability to demonstrate it, verifying that the processing is in accordance with the information provided.  
Transparency in data processing is established as a pillar to demonstrate diligence and proactive responsibility before the Control Authority and as a measure of trust before the subjects whose data is processed. As stated in Recital 39 of the GDPR [14], it must be completely clear to individuals that personal data concerning them is being collected, used, consulted, or otherwise processed, as well as the extent to which such data is or will be processed.  
Promoting transparency and visibility involves adopting a series of measures such as:  
• Making public the privacy and data protection policies that govern the operation of the organization.  
• Developing and publishing concise, clear, and intelligible information clauses that are easily accessible and allow data subjects to understand the scope of the processing of their data, the risks they may be exposed to, as well as how to exercise their rights regarding data protection.  
• Although not mandatory for all controllers, making public, or at least easily accessible to data subjects, the list of processing activities carried out in the organization.  
• Disclosing the identity and contact information of the person responsible within the organization for privacy matters.  
• Establishing accessible, simple, and effective communication, compensation, and complaint mechanisms directed at data subjects.  

7. Respect for User Privacy: Maintaining a User-Centered Approach  
Without disregarding the legitimate interests pursued by the organization through its data processing, the ultimate goal must be to guarantee the rights and freedoms of users whose data is subject to processing, so any measures adopted must aim to ensure their privacy. This means designing processes, applications, products, and services “with the user in mind,” anticipating their needs.  
The user must have an active role in managing their own data and in controlling how others manage it. Their inaction should not undermine privacy, reiterating one of the previously mentioned principles advocating for a privacy-by-default configuration that offers the highest level of protection.  
A design of processes, applications, products, and services focused on ensuring the privacy of data subjects involves:  
• Implementing “robust” privacy-by-default settings and informing users of the consequences to their privacy that result from modifying preset parameters.

OBLIGATED PARTIES TO DATA PROTECTION BY DESIGN  
The GDPR establishes ‘data protection by design’ as a legal compliance requirement. Article 83 [15] considers it punishable [16] not to comply with this obligation, just as its correct application constitutes one of the criteria for assessing the severity of an infringement.  
As stated in Article 25 of the GDPR [17], the obligation to implement data protection by design is applicable to all data controllers regardless of their size, the type of data processed, or the nature of the processing. Specifically, they are required to apply appropriate technical and organizational measures “both at the time of determining the means of processing and in the processing itself.”  
While compliance with this obligation specifically applies to the data controller, in light of Recital 78 [18] and what is established in Article 28 of the GDPR [19], data protection by design extends to other actors involved in the processing of personal data such as suppliers and service providers, product and application developers, or device manufacturers.

II. 
SYSTEM PRIVACY REQUIREMENTS

Understanding how the processing of personal data can affect individuals' privacy is key to designing and developing reliable systems from a data protection perspective. The GDPR enshrines in its Article 5 [20] the basic principles that must be taken into account when carrying out processing, so that these six principles (lawfulness, fairness and transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality) combined with the principle of proactive accountability become the core of the regulation and the objective that every system, application, service, or process must guarantee in its design, in addition to the functional requirements specific to the system.

PRIVACY AND SECURITY OBJECTIVES

Traditionally, the design of secure and reliable systems has focused on analyzing risks and responding to threats that affect security objectives that are more oriented towards privacy:  
• confidentiality, preventing unauthorized access to systems,  
• integrity, protecting them from unauthorized modifications of information, and  
• availability, ensuring that data and systems are available when needed.  

However, although unauthorized access and modification of personal data can be a critical aspect threatening individuals' privacy, there are other risk factors that may arise during authorized data processing [21] and that must be identified during the risk assessment for the rights and freedoms of data subjects. The loss of autonomy in decision-making, excessive data collection, re-identification, discrimination and/or stigmatization of individuals, bias in automated decisions, and the lack of user understanding of the scope and risks of unauthorized, invasive, or incorrect processing or profiling are examples of privacy risks that clearly affect the rights and freedoms of individuals and cannot be managed using a traditional risk model focused exclusively on the protection of security objectives.

Considering the described scenario and the potential privacy risks associated with the planned and authorized operation of systems that collect, use, and disclose personal data, it is necessary to expand the analytical framework to cover both the risks arising from unauthorized processing and those that may arise from planned and permitted processing of information. To address these potential risks, three new privacy-specific protection objectives [22][23] must be included in the analytical framework, and their guarantee becomes a safeguard for the processing principles established by the GDPR:  
• Unlinkability: aims to ensure that the processing of information is carried out in such a way that personal data from one processing domain cannot be linked to personal data from another different domain or that establishing such a link would require disproportionate effort. This privacy objective minimizes the risk of unauthorized use of personal data and the creation of profiles through the interconnection of information belonging to different data sets,

III.  
PRIVACY ENGINEERING: THE ENGINEERING OF PRIVACY  
Privacy engineering is a systematic process driven by a risk-based approach whose objective is to translate the principles of privacy by design (PbD) into practical and operational terms within the lifecycle of information systems responsible for processing personal data:  
- specifying the privacy properties and functionalities that the system must meet in a way that allows for its design and implementation (definition of privacy requirements)  
- designing the architecture and implementing the system elements that cover the defined privacy requirements (design and implementation of privacy)  
- confirming that the defined privacy requirements have been correctly implemented and meet the expectations and needs of the stakeholders (verification and validation of privacy)

IV. 
PRIVACY DESIGN STRATEGIES 
The state of the art identifies eight privacy design strategies [31][32] known as ‘minimize’, ‘hide’, ‘separate’, ‘abstract’, ‘inform’, ‘control’, ‘comply’, and ‘demonstrate’. 

These eight strategies can be classified into two categories [31]: data-oriented strategies and process-oriented strategies. The former, which include the strategies of ‘minimize’, ‘hide’, ‘separate’, and ‘abstract’, are more technical in nature and focus on a privacy-friendly processing of the collected data. The latter, which include the strategies of ‘inform’, ‘control’, ‘comply’, and ‘demonstrate’, have a more organizational character and are oriented towards defining processes that implement responsible management of personal data.

Minimize  
The objective of this strategy is to collect and process the minimum amount of data possible, so that by avoiding the processing of data that is not necessary for the purposes pursued in the processing, potential impacts on privacy are limited. This can be achieved by collecting data from fewer subjects (reducing the size of the study population) or collecting less data from subjects (reducing the volume of information gathered), for which the following tactics can be used:  
• Select: choose only the relevant sample of individuals and the necessary attributes, adopting a conservative approach when establishing the selection criteria and processing only the data that meets that criterion (whitelist).  
• Exclude: this is the reverse approach to the previous one, and it involves preemptively excluding subjects and attributes that are irrelevant to the processing being carried out (blacklist). In this case, an open attitude should be adopted, attempting to exclude as many records as possible unless it can be justified that they are absolutely necessary for the intended purpose.  
• Trim: partially eliminate personal data as soon as it is no longer necessary, which involves determining in advance the retention period for each of the collected data and establishing automatic deletion mechanisms when that period is met. In cases where the data is part of a record containing more information that needs to be retained, the value of unnecessary fields can be changed to a predetermined default value.  
• Delete: completely remove personal data as soon as it is no longer relevant, ensuring that recovery is not possible, even from backups.  
It is also necessary to consider that only strictly necessary data should be communicated and shared, and in cases of processing that infer new personal information, those generated data that are not necessary for the intended purpose should also be selected for exclusion.  
Hide  
This strategy focuses on limiting the exposure of data, establishing the necessary measures to ensure the protection of confidentiality and unlinking objectives. The following tactics are useful in response to this strategy:  
• Restrict: manage access to personal data restrictively by limiting it through an access control policy that implements the "need to know" principle both in space (detail and type of data accessed) and in time (stages of processing).  
• Obfuscate: make personal data unintelligible to those who are not authorized to access it by using encryption and hashing techniques, both in storage and transmission operations of the information.  
• Disassociate: eliminate the linkage between datasets that must remain independent, as well as the identifying attributes of data records to avoid correlations between them, with special attention to metadata.  
• Aggregate: Group information relating to several subjects using generalization and suppression techniques[33] to avoid correlations.  
Separate  
The objective of this strategy is to avoid, or at least minimize, the risk that during processing, in the same entity, different personal data belonging to the same individual and used in independent treatments could lead to a complete profiling of the subject. To achieve this, it is necessary to maintain independent processing contexts that hinder the correlation of groups of data that should be disconnected. The following tactics contribute to implementing the separation strategy:  
• Isolate: collect and store personal data in different databases or applications that are independent from a logical perspective or even run on different physical systems, adopting additional measures to ensure that unlinking, such as the scheduled deletion of indexing tables between databases.

• Distribute: disseminate the collection and processing of different subsets of personal data corresponding to different types of processing across independent processing and management units that, within the organization, are physically separate and use different systems and applications, attempting to implement decentralized and distributed architectures with local information processing whenever possible instead of centralized solutions with unified access that depend on a single control unit.  
Abstract  
The underlying idea of using this strategy is to limit the detail of the personal data being processed as much as possible. Unlike the ‘minimize’ strategy, which performs a prior selection of the collected data, this strategy focuses on the level of detail with which the data is processed and its aggregation through the use of three tactics:  
• Summarize: generalizes the values of the attributes using intervals or ranges of values, instead of using the specific value of the field.  
• Group: aggregates the information of a group of records into categories instead of using the detailed information of each individual belonging to the group, working with average or general values.  
• Disturb: use approximate values or modify the actual data by employing some type of random noise instead of working with the exact value of the personal data.  
In each processing, it is necessary to study how the level of detail of the input data affects the outcome and what precision is required for the processing to be effective. In particular, the time elapsed since the data was collected can affect its relevance, so it is advisable to periodically review the stored information and apply these types of strategies [34].  
Inform  
This strategy is the implementation of the objective and principle of transparency established by the Regulation and aims to ensure that data subjects are fully informed about the processing of their data in a timely manner. Whenever processing is carried out, the subjects whose data is processed should know what information is being processed, for what purpose, and to which third parties it is communicated, in addition to the other information established in Articles 13 [35] and 14 [36] of the GDPR. Transparency regarding this information becomes a basic privacy requirement as it allows data subjects to make informed decisions about the processing carried out and to provide, where appropriate, free, specific consent.

Any modification that occurs in the processing regarding the information previously provided should be communicated, including possible security breaches that may significantly affect the rights and freedoms of data subjects. This strategy is supported by the existence of privacy clauses that facilitate the overall provision of this information to the interested parties along with the use of the following tactics:
• Provide: supply the interested parties with all the information required by the GDPR regarding which personal data is processed, how it is processed, and why by identifying the reason and purpose. Details should be provided regarding the data retention periods, as well as the communications of this data to third parties. Along with all this information, which must be easily accessible and provided continuously over time to promote genuine transparency, it should also be indicated how and with whom data subjects can contact to raise questions regarding their privacy, as well as the rights that assist them in matters of personal data protection.
• Explain: provide information regarding the processing in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible manner using clear and simple language. To avoid dense, complex, and cumbersome information policies, it is advisable to adopt a layered or tiered approach in which basic information is presented at a first level, summarized, at the same time and medium in which the data is collected, and refer to additional and detailed information available at a second level [37].
• Notify: communicate the processing to the interested parties when the data is not collected directly from them, at the moment when it has been obtained and no later than within one month, or if it is to be used to communicate with them, in the first communication. They should also be informed if there is an intention to transfer the data to third parties. Mechanisms should also be implemented to notify the interested parties of security breaches that have occurred and that may pose a high risk to their rights and freedoms, using clear and simple language to describe the nature of the breach.
Given that the procedures for collecting information can vary widely, the modes of informing must be adapted to the circumstances of each of the means employed, including, additionally, the possibility of using standardized icons that provide a joint view of the intended processing.
Control
This strategy is closely linked to the informing strategy and aims to provide the interested parties with control over the collection, processing, uses, and communications made regarding their personal data by implementing mechanisms that allow the exercise of the rights of access, rectification, deletion, opposition, portability, and limitation of processing, as well as the granting and withdrawal of consent or the modification of privacy options.

in applications and services. The implementation of these mechanisms is supported by the use of the following tactics:
• Consent: collect the consent of data subjects in cases where there is no other legal basis, which must be given unequivocally, through a statement or a clear affirmative action, and must be explicit in certain situations such as the processing of sensitive data, the adoption of certain automated decisions, or international transfers. Furthermore, the data subject must be able to withdraw their consent at any time, through mechanisms and procedures that ensure it is as easy to withdraw as it is to give.
• Alert: make the user aware at the moment when personal data is being collected, even when they have already been generically informed of the legal basis justifying the processing or even if they have given their consent.
• Choose: provide granular functionality [38] of applications and services, particularly basic functionality, without it being contingent on consent for the processing of personal data that is not necessary for its execution.
• Update: implement mechanisms that facilitate users or even allow them to directly review, update, and rectify the data provided for a specific processing in order to ensure that it is accurate and reflects reality.
• Withdraw: provide mechanisms for users to delete or request the deletion of the personal data they have provided to a controller within the framework of a processing activity. 

Technological advancement, while allowing for continuous information collection, enables data to be easily managed by the data subjects themselves through the implementation of privacy platforms that allow them to access, update, cancel, and modify the configured privacy options. These functionalities must be considered from the design of the application.

Compliance
This strategy ensures that personal data processing is compatible and respects the legal requirements and obligations imposed by the regulations. To achieve this, it is necessary to define a privacy framework and a governance structure that includes a data protection policy supported by senior management, as well as the roles and responsibilities that ensure compliance. The culture of privacy must be an essential part of the organization and involve all its members, for which the following tactics can serve as a catalyst:
• Define: specify a data protection policy that reflects the internal clauses of privacy communicated to the data subjects. Structures must be created and resources allocated to support this policy and ensure that the processing activities carried out by the organization comply with and are in accordance with data protection regulations. A plan must also be developed and implemented.

training and awareness for all members of this entity aimed at ensuring a committed and responsible attitude as part of proactive responsibility.  
• Maintain: support the defined policy by establishing procedures and implementing the necessary technical and organizational measures. The existence of effective mechanisms and procedures must be reviewed to ensure the exercise of rights, the management and notification of security incidents, the adequacy of potential data processing assignments to legal requirements, and the accreditation of compliance with the obligations imposed by the regulations.  
• Defend: ensure compliance, effectiveness, and efficiency of the privacy policy and the procedures, measures, and controls implemented to verify that they respond at all times to the reality of the processing activities and the day-to-day operations of the organization.  
The role of the Data Protection Officer is fundamental in the implementation of this strategy, advising the controller and supervising compliance with data protection regulations within the organization. The implementation of privacy management models, such as that proposed by the recent ISO/IEC 27701:2019 standard [39], which specifies the requirements and provides guidance for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continuously improving a Privacy Information Management System (PIMS), is also effective.  
Demonstrate  
The objective of this strategy goes a step further than the previous one and aims for the data controller to demonstrate, in accordance with Article 24 of the GDPR [40], compliance with the data protection policy being applied, as well as with the other legal requirements and obligations imposed by the Regulation, both to the data subjects and to the supervisory authorities. From a practical standpoint, it is the implementation of accountability or proactive responsibility required by the Regulation, based on a critical, continuous, and traceable self-analysis of all decisions made within the framework of processing and ensuring genuine governance of personal data within the organization. The following tactics allow for the execution of this strategy to ensure and demonstrate that the processing is compliant with the Regulation:  
• Record: document each and every decision made over time even if they have been contradictory, identifying who made them, when, and the justification for doing so.  
• Audit: systematically, independently, and documentedly review the degree of compliance with the data protection policy.  
• Inform: document the results of the audits conducted and any incidents that occur in the operations of personal data processing and make it available to the supervisory authority when necessary. In the case of new processing, if the result of the data protection impact assessment indicates that the processing would entail a high risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects if the controller does not take measures to mitigate them, carry out the prior consultation referred to in Article 36 [41] of the GDPR.  
Conducting a risk analysis and, where appropriate, a data protection impact assessment along with the documentation of the decisions made based on the results obtained are a good starting point to not only establish the privacy requirements that must be implemented in applications and systems as part of privacy by design but also to fully document how personal data processing is carried out and ensure compliance with the principle of proactive responsibility. Other resources to demonstrate compliance with obligations by the controller include adherence to codes of conduct and certification mechanisms as optional instruments to implement the demonstration strategy.

**V. PRIVACY DESIGN PATTERNS**  
Once the privacy objectives and strategies that the product, system, application, or service must incorporate as part of its definition are established, it is necessary to integrate them into its design. To do this, privacy design patterns are used as reusable solutions employed to address common and repeatable privacy issues that arise repeatedly in a specific context during the development of products and systems.  

Typically, the description of a design pattern contains, at a minimum, its name, purpose, description of the application context, objectives, structure, implementation (relationship with other patterns), consequences of its application, and known uses.

**VII. CONCLUSIONS**  
In a context where organizations and companies develop services based on intensive use of personal data, whose impact on privacy is amplified by the use of disruptive technologies, it is necessary to adopt effective and efficient technical and organizational measures that contribute to ensuring respect for the rights and freedoms of individuals regarding their personal data processing.  

Ensuring privacy and establishing a governance framework that guarantees the protection of personal data does not represent an obstacle to innovation. On the contrary, it offers advantages and opportunities for various participants:  
• For organizations, it means improving efficiency, optimizing processes, establishing a cost-reduction strategy, and gaining a competitive advantage.  
• For the market, it means developing sustainable economic models in the long term.  
• For society as a whole, it means being able to access the benefits of technological advances without compromising the freedom and independence of individuals.  

In short, ensuring privacy is innovation in itself and introduces a new technological discipline: privacy engineering.  

The effective and efficient implementation of privacy principles requires that they be an integral part of the nature of products and services, and for this, they must be considered from the initial phase of conception, design, and development as part of the overall specifications, both functional and non-functional. This approach is known as Privacy by Design.  

Privacy by Design involves using a methodological approach oriented towards risk management and proactive responsibility that allows for setting privacy requirements through practices, procedures, and tools. To this end:  
• Based on risk analysis, both specific data protection objectives (unlinkability, transparency, and control) and security objectives from the perspective of privacy (confidentiality,
Page | 33 of 58

availability and integrity), which guarantee the basic principles established in Article 5 [61] of the GDPR.
• Next, the privacy strategies will be studied in which the requirements of each privacy objective are specified, both data-oriented and process-oriented. These strategies are: ‘minimize’, ‘hide’, ‘separate’, ‘abstract’, ‘inform’, ‘control’, ‘comply’, and ‘demonstrate’; and for each of them, the protection tactics that effectively implement them will be identified.
• Once in the design phase, the selected tactics will be integrated through already known solutions, that is, the privacy design patterns, which address common and repeatable problems, accessing the available catalogs, from which a selection is presented in this document.
• Finally, in the development phase, the concrete implementation of these patterns will be carried out. This implementation will be performed by the development team either by programming the necessary functionality in code or, if possible, by using existing ICT solutions, that is, utilizing Privacy Enhancing Technologies.
In any case, data protection by design is an obligation of the data controller, and it is the controller who must ensure it regardless of the form of development, acquisition, or outsourcing of the system, product, or service, and cannot fully delegate to third parties (manufacturers and processors) the responsibility for applying this principle.
In compliance with their duty of diligence, they must actively participate in privacy engineering tasks by defining the requirements that need to be considered, continuously monitoring their correct implementation, and verifying their full operability before the system goes into production, so that the privacy of individuals whose data is being processed is guaranteed.

ARTICLE 5 PRINCIPLES RELATING TO PROCESSING  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (‘lawfulness, fairness, and transparency’);  
b) collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (‘purpose limitation’);  
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’);  
d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable steps must be taken to ensure that personal data that are inaccurate with respect to the purposes for which they are processed are erased or rectified without delay (‘accuracy’);  
e) kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of the processing of personal data; personal data may be stored for longer periods provided that they are processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, without prejudice to the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures as required by this Regulation to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject (‘storage limitation’);  
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, using appropriate technical or organizational measures (‘integrity and confidentiality’).  
2. The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and be able to demonstrate it (‘accountability’).”

ARTICLE 24 RESPONSIBILITY OF THE DATA CONTROLLER  
“1. Taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with this Regulation. Such measures shall be reviewed and updated as necessary.  
2. When provided in relation to processing activities, among the measures mentioned in paragraph 1, the implementation by the data controller of appropriate data protection policies shall be included.  
3. Adherence to approved codes of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may be used as elements to demonstrate compliance with the obligations of the data controller.”  
ARTICLE 25 DATA PROTECTION BY DESIGN AND BY DEFAULT  
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the cost of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity that the processing entails for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall implement, both at the time of determining the means of processing and at the time of the processing itself, appropriate technical and organizational measures, such as pseudonymization, designed to apply in a way
effective the principles of data protection, such as data minimization, and integrate the necessary safeguards in the processing, in order to comply with the requirements of this Regulation and protect the rights of the data subjects.  
2. The data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure that, by default, only personal data that are necessary for each specific purpose of the processing are processed. This obligation shall apply to the amount of personal data collected, the extent of its processing, its retention period, and its accessibility. Such measures shall ensure, in particular, that, by default, personal data are not accessible, without the intervention of the individual, to an indeterminate number of natural persons.  
3. An approved certification mechanism under Article 42 may be used as an element to demonstrate compliance with the obligations set out in paragraphs 1 and 2 of this Article.”  
ARTICLE 28 PROCESSOR  
“1. When processing is to be carried out on behalf of a data controller, the controller shall choose only a processor that provides sufficient guarantees to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures, so that the processing is in compliance with the requirements of this Regulation and ensures the protection of the rights of the data subject.  
2. The processor shall not engage another processor without the prior written authorization, specific or general, of the controller. In the latter case, the processor shall inform the controller of any intended changes concerning the addition or replacement of other processors, thereby giving the controller the opportunity to object to such changes.  
3. The processing by the processor shall be governed by a contract or other legal act under Union or Member State law, which binds the processor to the controller and establishes the subject matter, duration, nature, and purpose of the processing, the type of personal data and categories of data subjects, and the obligations and rights of the controller. Such contract or legal act shall stipulate, in particular, that the processor:  
a) shall process the personal data only on documented instructions from the controller, including with regard to the transfers of personal data to a third country or an international organization, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law applicable to the processor; in such a case, the processor shall inform the controller of that legal requirement before the processing, unless that law prohibits such information on important grounds of public interest;  
b) shall ensure that persons authorized to process personal data have committed themselves to confidentiality or are under an appropriate statutory obligation of confidentiality;  
c) shall take all necessary measures in accordance with Article 32;  
d) shall respect the conditions referred to in paragraphs 2 and 4 for engaging another processor;  
e) shall assist the controller, taking into account the nature of the processing, by means of appropriate technical and organizational measures, insofar as this is possible, so that the controller…
Page | 54 of 58

may comply with its obligation to respond to requests aimed at exercising the rights of the data subjects established in Chapter III;  
f) will assist the controller in ensuring compliance with the obligations established in Articles 32 to 36, taking into account the nature of the processing and the information available to the processor;  
g) at the controller's choice, will delete or return all personal data once the provision of processing services has ended, and will delete existing copies unless the retention of personal data is required under Union or Member State law;  
h) will make available to the controller all necessary information to demonstrate compliance with the obligations established in this article, as well as to enable and contribute to the performance of audits, including inspections, by the controller or another auditor authorized by the controller.  
In relation to the provisions of item h) of the first paragraph, the processor will immediately inform the controller if, in its opinion, an instruction infringes this Regulation or other provisions regarding data protection of the Union or Member States.  

4. When a processor engages another processor to carry out specific processing activities on behalf of the controller, the same data protection obligations as those stipulated in the contract or other legal act between the controller and the processor referred to in paragraph 3 will be imposed on this other processor, through a contract or other legal act established in accordance with Union or Member State law, particularly the provision of sufficient guarantees for the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures in such a way that the processing is compliant with the provisions of this Regulation. If that other processor fails to comply with its data protection obligations, the initial processor will remain fully responsible to the controller for the compliance of the other processor's obligations.  
5. The adherence of the processor to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may be used as an element to demonstrate the existence of sufficient guarantees referred to in paragraphs 1 and 4 of this article.  
6. Without prejudice to the controller and the processor entering into an individual contract, the contract or other legal act referred to in paragraphs 3 and 4 of this article may be based, in whole or in part, on the standard contractual clauses referred to in paragraphs 7 and 8 of this article, including when they form part of a certification granted to the controller or processor in accordance with Articles 42 and 43.  
7. The Commission may establish standard contractual clauses for the matters referred to in paragraphs 3 and 4 of this article, in accordance with the examination procedure referred to in Article 93, paragraph 2.  
8. A supervisory authority may adopt standard contractual clauses for the matters referred to in paragraphs 3 and 4 of this article, in accordance with the consistency mechanism referred to in Article 63.  
9. The contract or other legal act referred to in paragraphs 3 and 4 shall be in writing, including in electronic format.

10. Without prejudice to the provisions of Articles 82, 83, and 84, if a data processor infringes this Regulation by determining the purposes and means of processing, they shall be considered responsible for the processing in relation to such processing.”  
ARTICLE 32 SECURITY OF PROCESSING  
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d) a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
2. In assessing the appropriateness of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the processing of data, in particular as a result of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data.  
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this Article.  
4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data may only process such data on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law.”  
ARTICLE 36 PRIOR CONSULTATION  
“1. The controller shall consult the supervisory authority before proceeding with the processing when a data protection impact assessment pursuant to Article 35 indicates that the processing would result in a high risk if the controller does not take measures to mitigate it.  
2. When the supervisory authority considers that the intended processing referred to in paragraph 1 could infringe this Regulation, in particular when the controller has not sufficiently identified or mitigated the risk, the supervisory authority shall, within eight weeks of the request for consultation, advise the controller in writing, and where applicable, the processor, and may use any of its powers mentioned in Article 58. This period may be extended by six weeks, depending on the complexity of the intended processing. The supervisory authority shall inform the
Page | 56 of 58

responsible party and, where applicable, to the person in charge of such extension within one month from the receipt of the consultation request, indicating the reasons for the delay. These deadlines may be suspended until the supervisory authority has obtained the information requested for the purposes of the consultation.  
3. When consulting the supervisory authority in accordance with paragraph 1, the data controller shall provide the following information:  
a) where applicable, the respective responsibilities of the controller, joint controllers, and processors involved in the processing, particularly in the case of processing within a corporate group;  
b) the purposes and means of the intended processing;  
c) the measures and safeguards established to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subjects in accordance with this Regulation;  
d) where applicable, the contact details of the data protection officer;  
e) the data protection impact assessment established in Article 35; and  
f) any other information requested by the supervisory authority.  
4. Member States shall ensure that the supervisory authority is consulted during the preparation of any legislative measure to be adopted by a national Parliament, or of a regulatory measure based on such legislative measure, that relates to processing.  
5. Notwithstanding the provisions of paragraph 1, the law of the Member States may require data controllers to consult the supervisory authority and obtain its prior authorization in relation to processing by a controller in the exercise of a task carried out in the public interest, particularly processing related to social protection and public health.”  
ARTICLE 83 GENERAL CONDITIONS FOR THE IMPOSITION OF ADMINISTRATIVE FINES  
“1. Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines under this article for the infringements of this Regulation indicated in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 are, in each individual case, effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.  
2. Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure to those provided for in Article 58, paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j). In deciding on the imposition of an administrative fine and its amount in each individual case, the following shall be duly taken into account:  
a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing operation in question as well as the number of data subjects affected and the level of damage suffered;  
b) the intentionality or negligence in the infringement;  
c) any measures taken by the controller or processor to mitigate the damage suffered by the data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the controller or processor, taking into account the technical or organizational measures they have implemented pursuant to Articles 25 and 32;
e) any previous infringement committed by the controller or the processor;  
f) the degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority in order to remedy the infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects of the infringement;  
g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement;  
h) the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, in particular whether the controller or processor notified the infringement and, if so, to what extent;  
i) when the measures referred to in Article 58(2) have been previously ordered against the controller or processor in relation to the same matter, compliance with those measures;  
j) adherence to codes of conduct under Article 40 or to certification mechanisms approved under Article 42; and  
k) any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement.  
3. If a controller or processor intentionally or negligently violates various provisions of this Regulation for the same processing operations or related operations, the total amount of the administrative fine shall not exceed the amount provided for the most serious infringements.  
4. Infringements of the following provisions shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 10,000,000 EUR or, in the case of an enterprise, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 2% of the total annual global turnover of the preceding financial year, opting for the higher amount:  
a) the obligations of the controller and the processor under Articles 8, 11, 25 to 39, 42, and 43;  
b) the obligations of certification bodies under Articles 42 and 43;  
c) the obligations of the supervisory authority under Article 41(4).  
5. Infringements of the following provisions shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 20,000,000 EUR or, in the case of an enterprise, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 4% of the total annual global turnover of the preceding financial year, opting for the higher amount:  
a) the basic principles for processing, including the conditions for consent under Articles 5, 6, 7, and 9;  
b) the rights of data subjects under Articles 12 to 22;  
c) the transfers of personal data to a recipient in a third country or an international organization under Articles 44 to 49;  
d) any obligation under the law of the Member States adopted pursuant to Chapter IX;  
e) the failure to comply with a decision or a temporary or definitive limitation on processing or the suspension of data flows by the supervisory authority under Article 58(2), or the failure to provide access in violation of Article 58(1).  
6. The failure to comply with the decisions of the supervisory authority under Article 58(2) shall be sanctioned in accordance with paragraph 2 of this article with administrative fines of up to 20,000,000 EUR or, in the case of an enterprise, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 4% of the total annual global turnover of the preceding financial year, opting for the higher amount.  
7. Without prejudice to the corrective powers of the supervisory authorities under Article 58(2), each Member State may establish rules on whether and to what extent administrative fines may be imposed on authorities and public bodies established in that Member State.  
8. The exercise by a supervisory authority of its powers under this article shall be subject to appropriate procedural safeguards in accordance with the law of the Union and of the Member States, including effective judicial protection and respect for procedural guarantees.  
9. When the legal system of a Member State does not provide for administrative fines, this article may be applied in such a way that the initiation of the fine corresponds to the competent supervisory authority and its imposition to the competent national courts, while ensuring that these legal remedies are effective and have an effect equivalent to the administrative fines imposed by the supervisory authorities. In any case, the fines imposed shall be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. The relevant Member States shall notify the Commission of the legislative provisions they adopt under this paragraph by 25 May 2018 at the latest and, without delay, any subsequent law of amendment or modification that is applicable to them.
