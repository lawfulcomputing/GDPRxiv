The received consultation includes the request from the President of the Working Group for the Protection of Personal Data of the Community of Madrid for the AEPD to issue an “opinion directed to the competent legislative body regarding the need to amend the national regulations governing the use of electronic signatures, in the sense of removing the DNI/NIE from the electronic certificate of public employees, to protect the personal data of public employees and to comply with the data minimization principle of the GDPR.”  

In support of this request, the submitted document includes the following arguments:  

“1. The DNI/NIE of a public employee is not a data that must legally appear in administrative acts nor, therefore, in electronic certificates or in the electronic signature of public employees.  
2. Article 1.c) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), establishes as a principle regarding the processing of personal data the principle of data minimization, according to which personal data must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.  
3. The national regulations governing the use of electronic signatures, Law 59/2003, of December 19, on electronic signatures, establishes in its article 11 that a recognized electronic certificate must mandatorily include, in the case of natural persons, their identification composed of their name and surname and the number of the national identity document or a pseudonym that is unequivocally identified as such.  
4. The AEPD is the public authority for data protection control in the national territory and has the power, under Article 58.3.b) of the GDPR, to issue, on its own initiative or upon request, opinions intended for the national Parliament, the Government of the Member State, or, in accordance with the law of the Member States, to other institutions and bodies, as well as to the public, on any matter related to the protection of personal data.  
5. Likewise, it is among the functions of the AEPD, as established in Article 57.1.c) of the GDPR, to advise, in accordance with the law of the Member States, the national Parliament, the Government, and other institutions and bodies on legislative and administrative measures relating to the protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons with respect to processing.  
6. By Resolution of February 18, 2019, of the Technical General Secretariat of the Vice-Presidency, Ministry of the Presidency, and Government Spokesperson, the Working Group for the Protection of Personal Data of the Community of Madrid was created, among whose functions is to request advice and information from the bodies and entities it deems necessary, including the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, for the development of its own purposes.  
7. This Working Group, in its meeting on October 21, 2020, agreed to request the AEPD to issue an opinion directed to the competent bodies for the amendment of the national regulations governing the use of electronic signatures, removing the DNI from the electronic certificate of public employees.”  

I  
In relation to the received request, it should be emphasized that there are numerous inquiries being made to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection regarding the processing of the data corresponding to the DNI/NIE in electronic signature systems, particularly when it comes to the signing of documents by public employees in the exercise of the functions assigned to them, which is why it was deemed appropriate to analyze this issue by the Legal Department.  
In this regard, in report 150/2019, the inquiry made by the University of Seville regarding the compliance with data protection regulations, its principles, and guarantees, of the system for verifying electronically signed documents was studied, which at the time of verification offers the requester the DNI number along with the full name of the signer. The inquiry raised how in the electronic signature a code appears, the CSV, secure verification code, through which the authenticity and integrity of the document can be verified. The purpose is, therefore, to verify that the electronic document in question is authentic, the signature is valid, and that it has not been altered since the signature (integrity). These are the validation data referred to in Article 3.40) of Regulation (EU) No 910/2014 of the European Parliament and of the Council of July 23, 2014.

The person or Public Administration in possession of the signed document can verify its authenticity and integrity; for this purpose, the CSV must be entered into electronic signature verification devices, resulting in information that indicates, first of all, that the digital signature is correct and that the CSV is valid. Additionally, it provides the following information:
- Original document without signature.
- Original document with signature.
- Signature resolution, file with the extension .csg or .xsig that contains the signature data and shows the name and ID number of the signer, among other data.

In the aforementioned report 150/2019, after analyzing the regulations in force at that time, the following conclusion was reached:
Therefore, regarding the use of electronic signature systems by public employees, since the ID number is not a data that must legally appear in administrative acts, it should not appear either in electronic certificates or in the electronic signature, ensuring in any case that it is not known through secure verification code systems, and opting, among other possible additional identification systems, for other identification numbers, for the reflection of the position/department in which the public employee is framed, or even for the expansion of the cases of use of pseudonyms, taking into account the public activity carried out.

In this way, compliance with the principle of data minimization would be achieved, establishing the appropriate guarantees by the legislator in accordance with the aforementioned Article 87 of the GDPR, and the national regulations governing the use of electronic signatures should be modified in this regard.

Hence, this Agency reiterates the criterion set forth in report 150/2019, although a new report is issued to incorporate the regulatory modifications that occurred after it, especially the approval of Law 6/2020, of November 11, regulating certain aspects of electronic trust services and Royal Decree 203/2021, of March 30, which approves the Regulation on the action and operation of the public sector by electronic means.

II
For the purposes of this report, the first point to highlight is that the personal data corresponding to the number of the National Identity Document is not a data that, according to current regulations, should appear among the data that allow identifying the acting public employee. 

In this regard, Royal Decree 1465/1999, of September 17, which establishes criteria for institutional image and regulates documentary production and printed material of the General State Administration, after generally establishing in its Article 3 the obligation to formalize administrative documents through handwritten signature or other electronic means that allow verifying their authenticity, regulates in its Article 4 the content of such acts in order to identify the author thereof, without requiring, at any time, the record of the ID/NIE:

Article 3. Formalization of documents.
1. Every document containing administrative acts, including those of mere procedure, must be formalized.
Formalization is understood as the verification of the authenticity of the will of the issuing body, expressed through handwritten signature or by symbols or codes that guarantee such authenticity through the use of electronic, computer, or telematic techniques or means in accordance with the provisions of Royal Decree 263/1996, of February 16, which regulates the use of electronic, computer, and telematic techniques by the General State Administration.
2. In other documents, especially those of informative content, formalization will not be required, being sufficient with the record of the authorizing body of the corresponding document.

Article 4. Preparation of documents.
1. In all documents containing administrative acts, including those of mere procedure, whose recipients are citizens, a heading must appear that includes at least the following data:
a) The title of the document, which will clearly and precisely express the type of document, its essential content, and, where applicable, the procedure in which it is included.
b) The number or key assigned for the identification of the file in which the document is integrated, in order to facilitate its mention by the citizen in communications directed to the Administration.

2. In the documents that, in accordance with section 1 of the previous article, must be formalized, the following must be included:
a) The full title of the position or job of the holder of the competent administrative body for the issuance of the document; as well as the name and surname of the person formalizing the document.
b) In cases where, in application of articles 13 and 16 of Law 30/1992, of November 26, on the Legal Regime of Public Administrations and the Common Administrative Procedure, this is done by delegation of powers or delegation of signature, such circumstance must be stated, expressing the delegation provision and the title of the position or job of the person formalizing.
c) The place and date on which the document was formalized.
d) The identification of the recipient of the document, stating the name and surname if it concerns a natural person, the corporate name in the cases of private legal entities, or the full name of the body or entity to which it is addressed.
Therefore, the aforementioned regulation requires certain data that allows the recipient to correctly identify the author of the administrative act being notified, among which is not included the one related to the National Identity Document, which is logical insofar as it concerns acts issued in the exercise of the public powers that officials have legally attributed and not acts issued in a private capacity as citizens. It should also be added that the act already includes other data that allow for the correct identification of the act and its author, such as the file number, the full title of the position or job of the holder of the competent administrative body for the issuance of the document, the name and surname of the person formalizing the document, and finally the date on which it was formalized—this will allow verification of whether the position of the signer was in force on that date.
Therefore, these are the data that have been considered necessary in order to fulfill the purpose of identifying the author of the act, without the need to include additional data that, being personal data, would be contrary to the principle of data minimization established in article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, according to which personal data shall be “adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.”

“In this way, regarding the issues raised by the consultant, this Agency considers that the implementation of an electronic signature system does not have to modify the content of the documents that public employees sign in the exercise of their powers if such modification does not originate from a regulation. The content of the electronic certificate, which must meet the requirements established by Law 7/2011 and its implementing regulations, should not be confused with the content of the document resulting from the electronic signature, which must include the data required by the applicable regulations, and that in the cases to which the consultation seems to refer, that is, the documents containing administrative acts including mere procedural acts, will be those indicated in article 4 of Royal Decree 1465/1999, of September 17, except in those cases where a special regulation determines other contents.

Therefore, the incorporation, both in the signature of electronic or paper documents and in the watermark, of the data relating to the ID card of the signing official could constitute excessive processing and, consequently, contrary to the principle of proportionality established in article 4 of Organic Law 15/1999.”
III
Therefore, the issue at hand is whether, since it is not necessary according to the regulations governing the content of administrative acts to include the ID/NIE of the public official signing the act, the inclusion of the same as a consequence of the use of the electronic signature instead of the handwritten signature is contrary to the regulations on personal data protection, which requires analyzing whether such inclusion is mandated by the regulations governing electronic signatures.

The electronic signature has been regulated in Spain by Law 59/2003, of December 19, on electronic signatures, which represented the transposition into the Spanish legal system of the repealed Directive 1999/93/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of December 13, 1999, which establishes a community framework for electronic signatures. However, subsequently, two regulations have been approved at the European level that have a significant impact on the subject matter of this report: (i) Regulation (EU) 910/2014 of the European Parliament and of the Council of July 23, 2014, on electronic identification and trust services for electronic transactions in the internal market and repealing Directive 1999/93/EC (eIDAS Regulation) and (ii) Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation).

Regarding Regulation (EU) 910/2014, it provides for a new regulation of the electronic signature. This Regulation has introduced the concept of qualified electronic signature, which replaces that of recognized electronic signature, and defines it as “an advanced electronic signature created by a qualified electronic signature creation device and based on a qualified electronic signature certificate,” with the “qualified electronic signature certificate” being an electronic signature certificate issued by a qualified trust service provider that meets the requirements set out in Annex I.

As a consequence of the above, Law 6/2020, of November 11, regulating certain aspects of electronic trust services, has been published, which repeals Law 53/2003, of December 19, on electronic signatures, along with those provisions incompatible with Regulation (EU) 910/2014, and complements it in those aspects that have not been harmonized and are left to the discretion of the Member States. The new law therefore refrains from reproducing the provisions of the Regulation, which is directly applicable, limiting itself to the essential regulation to cover the aspects that the European norm refers to the decision of the Member States.

According to Article 3 of the eIDAS Regulation:
10) “electronic signature” means data in electronic form attached to or logically associated with other electronic data which is used by the signatory to sign;
11) “advanced electronic signature” means an electronic signature that meets the requirements laid down in Article 26; these are:
a) uniquely linked to the signatory;
b) created using electronic signature creation data that the signatory can use, with a high level of confidence, under their sole control, and
c) linked to the data signed therewith in such a way that any subsequent modification of the data is detectable.
12) “qualified electronic signature” means an advanced electronic signature created by a qualified electronic signature creation device and based on a qualified electronic signature certificate;

Regarding the legal effects of electronic signatures, Article 25 of the eIDAS Regulation establishes that:
1. Legal effects or admissibility as evidence in judicial proceedings shall not be denied to an electronic signature solely on the grounds that it is an electronic signature or because it does not meet the requirements of a qualified electronic signature.
2. A qualified electronic signature shall have a legal effect equivalent to that of a handwritten signature.
3. A qualified electronic signature based on a qualified certificate issued in a Member State shall be recognized as a qualified electronic signature in all other Member States.

In the use of the electronic signature, the electronic certificates that allow for the verification of the signatory's identity are of paramount importance. Thus, as established in Article 3 of the eIDAS Regulation, an electronic certificate is “an electronic attestation that links the validation data of a signature to a natural person and confirms at least the name or pseudonym of that person.” For its part, the qualified electronic signature certificate “is an electronic signature certificate issued by a qualified trust service provider that meets the requirements set out in Annex I of the eIDAS Regulation.”

Therefore, in accordance with the eIDAS Regulation, the qualified electronic signature, which has the same value as a handwritten signature, is essential, according to Article 24 of the Regulation, that “when issuing a qualified certificate for a trust service, a qualified trust service provider shall verify, by appropriate means and in accordance with national law, the identity and, where applicable, any specific attribute of the natural or legal person to whom a qualified certificate is issued.”
Legal Office
Law 6/2020 states in its Article 7, section 1 that “the identification of the natural person requesting a qualified certificate will require their personal appearance before the officials responsible for verifying it and will be accredited by means of the National Identity Document, passport, or other legally accepted means. The personal appearance of the natural person requesting a qualified certificate may be waived if their signature on the application for the issuance of a qualified certificate has been legitimized in the presence of a notary.”  
Consequently, the accreditation of identity when requesting a certificate must be carried out with the DNI, which Article 8.1 of Organic Law 4/2015, of March 30, on the protection of citizen security, as amended by Royal Decree-Law 14/2019, of October 31, which adopts urgent measures for public security reasons in the field of digital administration, public sector contracting, and telecommunications, defines as “The National Identity Document is a public and official document and will have the protection granted to these by law. It is the only document with sufficient value on its own for the accreditation, for all purposes, of the identity and personal data of its holder.” 
To complete this regulation, Annex I of the eIDAS Regulation governs the requirements that qualified electronic signature certificates must meet, which must contain the following data: 
a) an indication, at least in a format suitable for automatic processing, that the certificate has been issued as a qualified electronic signature certificate; 
b) a set of data that unequivocally represents the qualified trust service provider issuing the qualified certificates, including at least the Member State in which such provider is established, and
- for legal entities: the name and, where appropriate, the registration number as recorded in the official registers,
- for natural persons, the name of the person; 
c) at least the name of the signer or a pseudonym; if a pseudonym is used, it must be clearly indicated; 
d) validation data of the electronic signature corresponding to the electronic signature creation data; 
e) data relating to the start and end of the validity period of the certificate; 
f) the identity code of the certificate, which must be unique for the qualified trust service provider; 
g) the advanced electronic signature or the advanced electronic seal of the issuing trust service provider; 
h) the location where the certificate supporting the advanced electronic signature or the advanced electronic seal referred to in point g) is available free of charge; 
i) the location of the services that can be used to check the validity status of the qualified certificate; 
j) when the electronic signature creation data related to the electronic signature validation data are located in a qualified electronic signature creation device, an appropriate indication of this, at least in a form suitable for automatic processing. 
For its part, Law 6/2020, regarding the identity and attributes of the holders of qualified certificates, states in its Article 6: 
1. The identity of the holder in qualified certificates shall be recorded as follows: 
a) In the case of electronic signature and website authentication certificates issued to natural persons, by their name and surname and their National Identity Document number, foreign identity number, or tax identification number, or through a pseudonym that is unequivocally recorded as such. The above numbers may be replaced by another code or identifying number only if the holder lacks all of them for a lawful reason, provided that it uniquely and permanently identifies them over time. 
2. If the certificates allow for a representation relationship, they shall include the identity of the represented natural or legal person in the forms provided in the previous section, as well as an indication of the document, public if required, that reliably certifies the signer's powers to act on behalf of the person or entity they represent and, if registration is mandatory, the registration data.

In the opinion of this Agency, the identification that must appear in the certificate (“at least the name of the signer or a pseudonym,” as indicated in the aforementioned Annex I of Regulation (EU) 910/2014) must be distinguished from the obligation to adequately identify the holder by the trust service provider, and therefore does not necessarily have to express all the information considered by the trust service provider to identify the holder of the electronic signature certificate. The information of the applicant that the trust service provider has verified is included in Article 24.1 of Regulation (EU) 910/2014, which establishes that “When issuing a qualified certificate for a trust service, a qualified trust service provider shall verify, by appropriate means and in accordance with national law, the identity and, where appropriate, any specific attribute of the natural or legal person to whom a qualified certificate is issued.” In this regard - concerning the lawfulness of processing the identifying data of the applicant to grant them the electronic signature certificate - this Agency expressed its opinion in its report 283/2017, regarding the Draft Law 6/2020 regulating certain aspects of electronic trust services:

“In this way, the aforementioned provision imposes on providers the legal obligation to process the necessary data for identification.
As indicated, such data will be the name, surname, national identity document or equivalent document or, where applicable, a pseudonym, if it is unequivocally recorded.
Article 4.1 of Organic Law 15/1999 states that “Personal data may only be collected for processing, as well as subjected to such processing, when they are adequate, relevant, and not excessive in relation to the scope and the determined, explicit, and legitimate purposes for which they have been obtained.” In this same sense, the second paragraph of Article 17.2 of Law 59/2003, already reproduced earlier, expressed itself.

V
On the other hand, the inclusion of the DNI/NIE of the signer in the electronic signature must be analyzed in light of the new data protection regulations, consisting of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and guarantee of digital rights.
The GDPR addresses concerns regarding the processing of the national identification number, empowering Member States to regulate the conditions under which such processing may occur and requiring the adoption of adequate safeguards to protect the application of the regulation, as stated in its Article 87:
Article 87 Processing of the national identification number.
Member States may additionally determine the specific conditions for the processing of a national identification number or any other means of general identification. In that case, the national identification number or any other means of general identification shall be used only with adequate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the data subject in accordance with this Regulation.
Precisely, in order to introduce the necessary safeguards in the processing of the DNI/NIE by Public Administrations, the LOPDGDD has introduced specific regulation in its seventh additional provision:

VI
Therefore, regarding the use of electronic signature systems by public employees, since the DNI/NIE/NIF is not a data that must legally appear in administrative acts, it should not appear either in electronic certificates or in the electronic signature, ensuring in any case that it is not known through secure verification code systems, with the option of choosing, among other possible additional identification systems, other identification numbers, reflecting the position/department in which the public employee is situated or, even, by expanding the cases of use of pseudonyms, depending on the public activity carried out, a possibility now opened by the recent Law 6/2020, of November 11, regulating certain aspects of electronic trust services in paragraph 1 of its Article 6.
In this way, compliance with the principle of data minimization would be achieved, establishing the appropriate guarantees by the legislator in accordance with the aforementioned Article 87 of the GDPR, and the national regulations governing the use of electronic signatures in the field of certificates issued for personnel in the service of the General Administration of the State and its linked or dependent public bodies should be modified in this regard. In this context, it would also be desirable to adopt a common pseudonym certificate profile that, meeting the requirements for qualified certificates, establishes as an identifier a professional identification number constructed based on the same coding rule applicable to the entire General Administration of the State and its dependent public bodies.
