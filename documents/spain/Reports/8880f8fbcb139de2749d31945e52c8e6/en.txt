The consultation raises the question of whether it is compliant with the provisions of data protection regulations for a company managing and operating private parking facilities to request from the General Directorate of Traffic —DGT— the address of the owner of the vehicle that has used the parking facility, based on the legitimate interest of the consulting party, derived from the contract that the driver of said vehicle enters into upon accessing a parking facility.  

Thus, the opinion of this Agency is requested regarding the legality of providing companies that operate and manage private parking facilities with the information contained in the Vehicle Registry about the addresses of the owners of the vehicles that use the parking facility, in order to be able to claim, if applicable, the unpaid amount, based on their legitimate interest derived from the vehicle deposit contract.

Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation -GDPR-), and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights -LOPDGDD- constitute the legal framework of reference in Spain that affects the protection of personal data.  

These regulations govern the principles and foundations to which the collection and processing of personal data by any public or private entity that carries out personal data processing in the exercise of its activity must adhere, without prejudice to the existing special rules for certain activities or processing, such as the processing of personal data subject to Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offenses or the execution of criminal sanctions, and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Framework Decision 2008/977/JHA of the Council.

First of all, it should be noted that Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, particularly by means of an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that person.” 
In turn, according to the content of the “Definition” in point 2 of Article 4 of the GDPR, for there to be processing of personal data, it is required to carry out “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of making available, comparison or interconnection, restriction, erasure, or destruction.” For its part, the definition of “file” is contained in section 6 of Article 4, which refers to “any structured set of personal data, accessible according to specific criteria, whether centralized, decentralized, or distributed in a functional or geographical manner.”

This purpose - consolidated in the General Data Protection Regulation as the principle of “purpose limitation” - is also enforceable in light of the provisions of Article 5.1 b), which states that “Personal data shall be: (…) b) collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and shall not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific and historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (‘purpose limitation’).”
The legitimate purpose for which data is requested by interested parties, through the mechanism implemented by the General Directorate of Traffic, will be determined, case by case, by the need to know one or more of the data contained in their Vehicle Registry, with access not being legitimate for mere convenience, but - as outlined in the aforementioned reports from the Agency - when the requester holds the corresponding legitimate and direct interest.

At this point, it should be noted that the data protection regulations provide for different legal bases for legitimacy that may allow for the processing of personal data. According to Article 6 – “Lawfulness of processing” – of the General Data Protection Regulation - GDPR -, such processing is lawful, and therefore legitimate, when:
“1.   Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:
a)  the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes; 
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the implementation at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures; 
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject; 
d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person; 
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller; 
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that such interests are not overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child. (the bold text is ours)
The provisions of letter f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions.”
This Agency has had the opportunity to analyze the legal basis for data processing by public administrations in its legal reports 108/2018, 155/2018, and 175/2018. In these reports, it is stated that:
“It should be noted that the Administration is bound by the principle of legality, so that, unlike individuals, it can only carry out what the legal system expressly allows it to do. This is the meaning of the provisions in Articles 9.1 and 103 of the Constitution, so that when the law and the legal system have not attributed to the Administration the corresponding powers to act in a given situation, such action cannot be carried out unless the legal system has previously attributed such powers to it. Therefore, there is no empty space where, in the absence of law, the Administration can act. This is what has been referred to as the doctrine of the positive binding of the Administration to legality.”

(García de Enterría). Consequently, for the Administration to act, it requires prior legal authorization (understood here as regulatory authorization). This is true whether the Administration acts in the realm of public law or in the realm of private law. Thus, we can see that even in the latter realm, where it would operate on the same level as private individuals, the Administration needs a norm that authorizes it to act.

For its part, the tenth additional provision of the LOPDGDD — Communications of data by the subjects listed in Article 77.1 — states that:
“The controllers listed in Article 77.1 of this organic law may communicate the personal data requested by private law subjects when they have the consent of the affected parties or deem that there is a legitimate interest on the part of the requesters that prevails over the rights and interests of the affected parties in accordance with the provisions of Article 6.1 f) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.” (the bold text is ours)
Note that the REPD itself establishes in its Recitals examples and guidelines to specify the processing based on legitimate interest. Thus, Recital 47 states that “The legitimate interest of a data controller, including that of a controller to whom personal data may be communicated, or of a third party, may constitute a legal basis for processing, provided that the interests or the rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail, taking into account the reasonable expectations of the data subjects based on their relationship with the controller. Such legitimate interest could arise, for example, when there is a relevant and appropriate relationship between the data subject and the controller, as in situations where the data subject is a client or is in the service of the controller. In any case, the existence of a legitimate interest would require a meticulous assessment, even if a data subject can reasonably foresee, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that processing for such a purpose may occur. In particular, the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject could prevail over the interests of the data controller when the processing of personal data occurs in circumstances in which the data subject does not reasonably expect further processing to take place. In interpreting this provision, the Article 29 Working Party of Directive 95/46 issued Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC, dated April 9, 2014 (WP 217). This Group, in its Opinion, already pointed out that “Article 7, letter f), should not be considered a legal basis that can only be used sparingly to cover gaps in rare or unforeseen situations as ‘a last resort’, or as a final possibility if other grounds for legitimacy cannot be applied. It should also not be perceived as a preferred option nor should its use be unduly extended because it is considered less restrictive than other legal bases. On the contrary, it is a means as valid as any other to legitimize the processing of personal data.”

Finally, regarding the “balancing test,” it states the following:
“A proper assessment of Article 7, letter f), is not a direct weighing examination that consists solely of weighing two easily quantifiable and comparable ‘weights’ on a scale. On the contrary, this examination requires a comprehensive consideration of a series of factors, in order to ensure that the interests and fundamental rights of the affected parties are duly taken into account. At the same time, it is a modifiable test, which can vary from simple to complex, and it is not necessary for it to be unduly burdensome.
The factors that must be considered when conducting this balancing test will include:
- the nature and source of the legitimate interest, and whether the processing of data is necessary for the exercise of a fundamental right, arises otherwise from public interest, or benefits from the recognition of the affected community;

- the impact on the data subject and their reasonable expectations regarding what will happen to their data, as well as the nature of the data and the manner in which it is processed;

- the additional safeguards that could limit an undue impact on the data subject, such as data minimization, privacy protection technologies, increased transparency, the general and unconditional right to voluntary exclusion, and data portability.”

“To verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions: whether such a measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, in addition, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and, finally, whether it is balanced or proportionate, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test).” (emphasis added)
Thus, in the case submitted for report, the suitability test must necessarily be negative, as the proposed measure does not serve the pursued purpose, that is, access to certain personal data —such as the domicile— of the holder of a vehicle does not necessarily lead to obtaining personal information about the driver whose contractual non-compliance is being pursued. 
Furthermore, the aforementioned access to information regarding the domicile of the registered holders of a vehicle should be deemed necessary considering the facts and the circumstances at hand, motivating that the choice of this type of processing of personal data is the most appropriate, relevant, and proportional measure that can be adopted.

Furthermore, it should be noted that Article 6 of the GDPR, in its paragraph 4, prohibits the processing of personal data for different purposes, which may only be established - exceptionally - by Union or Member State law, provided that it "constitutes a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society to safeguard the objectives indicated in Article 23.1" or, failing that, assessing the compatibility of the purposes according to the criteria specified, which must be subject to restrictive interpretation, requiring the existence of adequate safeguards.
In accordance with these provisions, the purpose and legitimate activity of the data processor will delimit the possibility of collecting and processing such data, and this processing must also be limited to the data provided or suitable for that purpose. Thus, the processing of data that exceeds what is necessary to achieve the intended purpose is not lawful, as it violates the principle of "data minimization," as set forth in Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR. In this regard, see our reports 0044/2020 and 0133/2018.
At the same time, the data may only be used for the purposes that justify that collection, and the affected party must be informed of these purposes - pursuant to Article 13 of the GDPR - and it is not lawful to use it for other purposes. Consequently, the access sought by companies that operate and manage private parking lots to the data regarding the address of the vehicle owners held in the Vehicle Registry of the JCT is neither lawful nor legitimate, as the requirements set forth in the tenth additional provision of the LOPDGDD are not met.
