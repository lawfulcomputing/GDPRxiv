N/REF: 0054/2023  

The inquiry raises the compliance of this Agency with the interpretation it makes of Article 5.1 of Law 2/2023, of February 20, regulating the protection of individuals who report regulatory infringements and the fight against corruption, which attributes the status of data controller to the governing body or management body of each entity or organization obliged by this law.  
In this inquiry, after analyzing the new legal regime of the internal information system regulated by Law 2/2023, of February 20, the patrimonial liability of the administrators regulated in Royal Legislative Decree 1/2010, of July 2, which approves the consolidated text of the Capital Companies Act, and the concept of data controller according to Article 4.7 of the GDPR, the following conclusions are drawn:  
Considering Article 5 of the Whistleblower Protection Act in conjunction with the Legal System, we understand that the legislator's intention is not to exempt the Company itself from liability but to ensure the active participation of the Board of Directors in the management of the whistleblower channel and, especially, in its implementation, making it responsible for the same, in order to guarantee the maximum protection of individuals who report actions or omissions that may constitute infringements according to the provisions of Article 2 of Law 2/2023.  
Thus, at the level of data protection, this inquirer considers the following, unless a better opinion is provided by this Agency:  
1. The Board of Directors is responsible—not for the processing of personal data incorporated into the whistleblower channel—but regarding the development of the functions assigned to it to ensure the implementation of a whistleblower channel that complies with the requirements established by the Law.  
2. The Company obliged to have the internal information system as provided in the Law is the data controller of the data processing derived from the daily and habitual management of the whistleblower channel, in accordance with the definition of data controller in Article 4.7 of the GDPR.

I  
The controversial issue arises from the wording of Article 5.1 of Law 2/2023, of February 20, regulating the protection of individuals who report regulatory infringements and the fight against corruption, which establishes the following:  
Article 5. Internal information system.  
1. The governing body or management body of each entity or organization obliged by this law shall be responsible for the implementation of the internal information system, after consulting with the legal representation of the workers, and shall have the status of data controller in accordance with the provisions of the regulations on personal data protection.  
The consideration of the governing body or management body of each entity or organization as the data controller derives from what this Agency informed in the draft law in our Report 20/2022, in which we stated the following:  

II  
Without prejudice to the above, it is appropriate to analyze the main issues that the submitted text raises regarding personal data protection, albeit with the conciseness appropriate to the urgency with which it is requested.  
In this regard, the following issues can be identified: the legal position of the participants in the processing of personal data; subjects whose personal data may be subject to processing; legal basis; application of data protection principles and limitations on the rights of the affected parties.  
Starting with the legal position of the participants in the processing of personal data, it must begin with the definition of "data controller" contained in Article 4.7 of the GDPR: the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if Union law or the law of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the data controller or the criteria.

In the internal information system, the purposes and means of processing would be determined by law, establishing in Article 5.1 of the draft law that “The governing body or management body of each entity or body obliged by this law shall be responsible for the implementation of the internal information system, after consulting with the legal representation of the workers,” and defining, in its subsection 2, the requirements that such systems must meet in any of their management forms:

2. The internal information systems, in any of their management forms, must:
a) Allow communication of information regarding the infringements provided for in Article 2 to all persons referred to in Article 3.
b) Be designed, established, and managed securely, ensuring the confidentiality of the identity of the informant and any third parties mentioned in the communication, as well as the actions developed in the management and processing of the same, protecting data and preventing access by unauthorized personnel.
c) Allow the submission of communications in writing or verbally, or both.
d) Integrate the different internal communication channels that may be established within the entity.
e) Ensure that the communications submitted can be effectively processed within the corresponding entity or body so that the first to know of the possible irregularity is the employer itself.
f) Be independent and appear distinct from the internal information systems of other entities or bodies, without prejudice to what is established in Articles 12 and 13 below.
g) Have a responsible person for the System in the terms provided in Article 10 of this law.
h) Have a policy or strategy that states the general principles regarding internal information systems and protection of the informant and that is duly publicized within the entity or body.
i) Have a procedure for managing the communications received.
j) Establish guarantees for the protection of informants within the entity or body itself, respecting, in any case, the provisions of Article 9.

Therefore, by virtue of the functions legally attributed to it, it is the responsibility of the governing body or management body of each entity or body obliged to hold the status of "data controller" of personal data, in accordance with the provisions of the regulations on personal data protection, which should be included in the text of Article 5 itself.

At this point, the draft law allows for shared means both in Article 12, regarding the private sector, and in Article 14 regarding the public sector. In these cases, depending on how that collaboration is articulated, we could find ourselves facing the figure of joint responsibility referred to in Article 26 of the GDPR, provided for cases where “two or more controllers jointly determine the purposes and means of processing shall be considered joint controllers of the processing” or, conversely, in a situation of respective controllers regarding the processing of personal data they carry out. Therefore, in the opinion of this Agency, it cannot be determined a priori the legal position that will correspond to each of the data controllers. However, it is reminded that, in the event that joint responsibility is articulated from the perspective of personal data protection, the agreement referred to in the aforementioned Article 26 of the GDPR must be signed.

On the other hand, Article 6 of the draft law regulates the “management of the draft by an external third party,” which would be considered a data processor, as appropriately established in the text submitted, which establishes the specific obligation for it to provide adequate guarantees of respect for data protection (an essential requirement under Article 28.1 of the GDPR):

Recital 54 of Directive (EU) 2019/1937 expressly refers to the possibility of outsourcing in the following terms: “Third parties may also be authorized to receive reports of infringements on behalf of legal entities in the private and public sectors, provided they offer adequate guarantees of respect for independence, confidentiality, data protection, and secrecy. Such third parties may include external whistleblowing platform providers, external advisors, auditors, trade union representatives, or employee representatives.” Although the aforementioned recital refers to the receipt of reports, Article 8 of the Directive provides in its paragraph 5 that “Whistleblowing channels may be managed internally by a designated person or department for that purpose or may be provided externally by a third party,” and in paragraph 6 that “Private sector legal entities with between 50 and 249 employees may share resources for the receipt of reports and any investigation that must be carried out.” Furthermore, Article 9.1.c) states that “The internal reporting procedures and follow-up referred to in Article 8 shall include the following: c) the designation of an impartial person or department competent to follow up on reports, which may be the same person or department that receives the reports and will maintain communication with the whistleblower and, if necessary, will request additional information from them and provide a response.”

In the opinion of this Agency, there is no clear regulation in the draft law regarding the actions that may be subject to outsourcing, which may generate legal uncertainty and, from the perspective of personal data protection, limit the processing of personal data that may be carried out by a data processor. The regulation in this regard, as set forth in Article 32, by including among the subjects that may access personal data the data processors, is not clarifying in this respect, as it is limited to the scope of “their competencies and functions,” which, at least on this point, raises the aforementioned doubts.

Therefore, it must be clarified in Article 6 the actions that may be subject to outsourcing, specifying whether it is limited to the receipt of information or also includes other actions, including or not the processing of the same, and adapting, if necessary, Article 12, in order to eliminate any doubts regarding the processing of personal data that may be entrusted to a data processor.

Furthermore, the aforementioned Opinion highlighted “the difficulties in implementing the definitions of the Directive in a complex environment where many hypothetical situations may arise involving the actions of data controllers and processors, either alone or jointly, and with varying degrees of autonomy and responsibility” and that “The Group acknowledges that the concrete application of the concepts of data controller and data processor is becoming increasingly complex. This is primarily due to the growing complexity of the environment in which these concepts are used and, in particular, to an increasing trend, both in the private and public sectors, towards organizational differentiation, combined with the development of ICT and globalization, which may lead to new and difficult questions being raised and, at times, a diminished level of protection for the data subjects.”

However, at the present time, it must be taken into account that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to the protection of personal data, which is now based on the principle of "accountability" or "proactive responsibility" as repeatedly pointed out by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and is reflected in the Preamble of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD): “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or processor of the risks that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.” Within this new system, it is the data controller who, through the instruments regulated in the GDPR itself such as the record of processing activities, risk analysis, or data protection impact assessment, must ensure the protection of this right by complying with all the principles set out in Article 5.1 of the GDPR, adequately documenting all decisions made in order to be able to demonstrate compliance.

Additionally, based on this principle of proactive responsibility, directed primarily at the data controller, and in order to strengthen the protection of the affected individuals, the GDPR has introduced new obligations that are enforceable not only against the controller but, in certain cases, also against the processor, who may be sanctioned in the event of non-compliance with these obligations.

However, it is more common for legislation, rather than directly naming the controller or setting the criteria for their appointment, to establish a task or impose on someone the duty to collect and process certain data. In such cases, the purpose of the processing is usually determined by law. The controller will normally be the one designated by law to fulfill this purpose, this public task. This would be the case, for example, of an entity assigned certain public tasks (for example, social security) that could not be fulfilled without collecting at least some personal data, and that, therefore, creates a database or a registry to carry out such tasks. In this case, although indirectly, the legislation establishes who the controller is. More frequently, the law may impose on public or private entities the obligation to retain or provide certain data. These entities would generally be considered the controllers necessary to fulfill this obligation.

Therefore, based on these criteria, and considering that Article 5 applies to both the public and private sectors, the purpose pursued with our Report 20/2022 was to contribute to the correct identification of the controllers and possible processors, but without the intention of attributing to the Board of Directors of a commercial company any responsibility regarding the processing of personal data in the internal information system differentiated from that which corresponds to the company itself concerning other personal data processing in accordance with Article 4.7 of the GDPR, nor to alter the liability regime established in the regulations on personal data protection to adapt it to the regime of joint liability of the directors set forth in Article 236 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2010, of July 2, which approves the consolidated text of the Capital Companies Act.

Similarly, in the public sector, the status of controller will correspond to the entity or body obliged by law and not to its governing body, without prejudice to the fact that in this area it is a common practice in the preparation of records of processing activities to identify the governing or executive body that holds the corresponding competencies as the controller, thereby facilitating the identification of the administrative body that makes the corresponding decisions regarding the processing of personal data and the exercise of the rights of the affected parties, a practice accepted and followed by this Agency, but without excluding the status of controller of the corresponding entity or body.

For all these reasons, the correct interpretation of Article 5 of Law 2/2023, of February 20, from the perspective of personal data protection, requires identifying as the controller the entity or body obliged by law to have an internal information system, without prejudice to the fact that the necessary decisions for its correct implementation must be made by the corresponding administrative body or governing body.
