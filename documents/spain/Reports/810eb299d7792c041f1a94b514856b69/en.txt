The inquiry raises whether it is compliant with the regulations on personal data protection, Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR), and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD), for the Occupational Risk Prevention Service of public employees of the Public Administration of the Region of Murcia to access their medical records in order to carry out various activities.  
Specifically, it expressly identifies the following as such:  
- To study diseases that may be related to work, solely for the purpose of identifying any relationship between the causes of illness and the health risks that may arise in the workplace.  
- To communicate diseases that could be classified as occupational, as established by Article 5 of Royal Decree 1299/2006, of November 10, which approves the list of occupational diseases in the Social Security system.  
- To provide first aid assistance and emergency care to workers in need.  
- To promote health promotion programs in the workplace, in coordination with the National Health System.  
- To collaborate with the National Health System, as established by Article 38 of the Regulation of Prevention Services, with primary health care services and specialized health care for the diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation of work-related diseases, as well as in health and epidemiological campaigns organized by the competent public health authorities.  
- To collaborate with health authorities in epidemiological surveillance tasks, provision, and maintenance of the Health Information System in Occupational Health, as established in Article 39 of the Regulation of Prevention Services.  

I  
Before addressing the compliance with data protection regulations that the processing to be analyzed entails, it is necessary to differentiate between two essential concepts to resolve the inquiry posed, namely the difference between "occupational medical history" and ordinary or conventional medical history.  
While the former is linked to the competencies and functions of Occupational Risk Prevention Services, as it pertains to Occupational Health, without an official definition, the latter can be considered as that which is linked to any medical-assistance action, whether carried out by primary care services or specialized care, through the National Health System or through private healthcare. The various basic and regional regulations define its purpose and content. Thus, Article 14 of Law 41/2002, of November 14, which regulates the autonomy of the patient and rights and obligations regarding information and clinical documentation, hereinafter LAP, establishes:  
1. The medical history comprises the set of documents related to the care processes of each patient, with the identification of the doctors and other professionals who have intervened in them, in order to achieve the maximum possible integration of the clinical documentation of each patient, at least, within each center.  

For its part, Law 3/2009, of May 11, on the rights and duties of users of the healthcare system of the Region of Murcia, applicable to the territorial scope of the inquiry, provides in its Article 50:  
1. The medical history is the set of health documents in which all clinical information of a patient is recorded, which is generated in the different care processes of the user.

II
Having laid out the terms of the inquiry, it should be indicated that for the processing of personal data to be compliant with the GDPR, several elements of the data protection system must be fulfilled, including the principles of data protection contained in Article 5.1 of the GDPR, which states that personal data shall be:
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (“lawfulness, fairness, and transparency”);
b) collected for specified, legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; according to Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (“purpose limitation”);
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”);
d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable steps must be taken to ensure that personal data that are inaccurate with respect to the purposes for which they are processed are erased or rectified without delay (“accuracy”);
e) kept in a form which permits identification of the data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of the processing of personal data; personal data may be stored for longer periods as long as they are processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, without prejudice to the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures imposed by this Regulation to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject (“storage limitation”);
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, through the application of appropriate technical or organizational measures (“integrity and confidentiality”).
In this case, and considering the terms of the inquiry, the analysis of this report will focus on the principles of lawfulness (paragraph a) and purpose limitation (paragraph c). 
All of this is without prejudice to addressing other aspects related to the right to data protection that may be directly or indirectly affected.
III
The principle of lawfulness is the one that determines under what circumstances or conditions the processing of personal data must be considered lawful.

To respond to this inquiry, one must start from the regulation contained in Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), which displaces the application of Organic Law 15/1999, and therefore the issue raised must be examined in light of what is provided therein.  
Said Regulation defines in its Article 4.15 health data as “personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, revealing information about their health status.” 
Recital 35 of the Regulation clarifies this definition by stating that “Among the personal data related to health, all data concerning the health status of the data subject that provide information about their past, present, or future physical or mental health status should be included. This includes information about the natural person collected in connection with their registration for health care purposes, or in connection with the provision of such care, in accordance with Directive 2011/24/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council; any number, symbol, or data assigned to a natural person that uniquely identifies them for health purposes; information obtained from tests or examinations of a part of the body or of a bodily substance, including that derived from genetic data and biological samples, and any information relating, by way of example, to a disease, a disability, the risk of suffering from diseases, medical history, clinical treatment, or the physiological or biomedical status of the data subject, regardless of its source, for example, a doctor or other health professional, a hospital, a medical device, or an in vitro diagnostic test.”
The Court of Justice (CJEU) interpreted this concept broadly, considering as health-related data the information concerning all aspects, both physical and mental, of a person's health, whether related to their health status (that they have injured a foot) or that they are in a situation of partial sick leave (CJEU ruling of November 6, 2003, Lindqvist, C-101/01).

II
Consequently, health data processing must be carried out, in any case, in accordance with the provisions established by European law and by national regulations with the rank of law that govern them. 
Regarding legal limitations, it should be noted that the right to the protection of personal data is a fundamental right, the content of which consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that enables the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which data this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who holds that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose such possession or use” (STC 76/2019, of May 22, and STC 292/2000, of November 30). Furthermore, these rulings also indicated the necessity for the interference to be provided for in a law or regulation of the European Union, respecting, in any case, the principle of proportionality. 
Specifically, the Constitutional Court, in the aforementioned STC 76/2019, of May 22, after citing, among others, its previous STC 292/2000, of November 30, states:

In STC 49/1999, FJ 4, we defined the constitutional function of this legal reservation in the following terms:

For the purposes that are relevant here, it is sufficient to recall that, to determine whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions: whether the measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, in addition, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and, finally, whether it is balanced or proportionate, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test; SSTC 66/1995, of May 8 [RTC 1995, 66], F. 5; 55/1996, of March 28, FF. 7, 8, and 9; 270/1996, of December 16, F. 4.e; 37/1998, of February 17, F. 8; 186/2000, of July 10, F. 6).”

circumstances have a limited scope of application (labor, social, associative, health, judicial, etc.) or respond to a specific purpose, thus, in themselves, they delimit the specific treatments that they authorize as exceptions to the general rule. The processing of special categories of personal data is one of the areas in which the General Data Protection Regulation has expressly recognized that Member States have "room for maneuver" when it comes to "specifying their rules," as stated in its Recital 10. This legislative configuration margin extends both to the determination of the enabling causes for the processing of specially protected personal data—i.e., to the identification of essential public interest purposes and the assessment of the proportionality of the processing to the pursued end, essentially respecting the right to data protection—and to the establishment of "appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject" [art. 9.2 g) GDPR]. The Regulation therefore contains a specific obligation for Member States to establish such guarantees, in the event that they enable the processing of specially protected personal data.

On the other hand, regarding the guarantees that the legislator must adopt, the aforementioned judgment No. 76/2019 of May 22 reminds us that 
"In light of the potential intrusive effects on the fundamental right affected resulting from the processing of personal data, the jurisprudence of this Court requires the legislator to establish adequate technical, organizational, and procedural guarantees, in addition to fulfilling the aforementioned requirements, to prevent risks of varying probability and severity and to mitigate their effects, as only in this way can respect for the essential content of the fundamental right itself be ensured."

This judgment reiterates, therefore, the need to have adequate guarantees for the treatments intended to be carried out, with special care when those treatments involve special category data, as is the case here, because, as the Constitutional Court adds:
Finally, we must remember that the General Data Protection Regulation establishes the minimum, common, or general guarantees for the processing of personal data that are not special.
