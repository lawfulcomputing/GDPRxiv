Legal Advisory Office  
N/REF: 0101/2019  

The inquiry raises whether the content of Additional Provision Seventeenth of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD) in relation to the laws cited therein, should be considered a closed list or can be interpreted to include other non-indicated norms, for the purposes of lifting the prohibition on the processing of special categories of data provided for in Article 9.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (GDPR).  

This inquiry originates from the possibility that the Medical Inspection Services of the Autonomous Community of the Canary Islands may access medical records from both primary care and specialized care for the purpose of carrying out their functions of verification, control, confirmation, and termination of temporary incapacity.  

Access is intended to be carried out under the Additional Provision Third of Law 11/1994, of July 26, on Health Organization of the Canary Islands and in relation to Decree 113/2013, of November 15, on the medical evaluation of public sector personnel of the Autonomous Community of the Canary Islands. Considering that these norms are not included in the Additional Provision Seventeenth of the LOPDGDD, the inquiry is raised regarding the interpretation and scope of the same.  

I  
Taking the above into account, the inquiry must be resolved from two perspectives: a general one regarding the interpretation, content, and scope of Additional Provision 17 of the LOPDGDD in relation to the laws that legitimize the processing of health data, and another from a more specific perspective, focused on the case that originates the inquiry, that is, whether access to the medical record under the terms proposed fits within the current legal framework represented by the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, without prejudice to what is indicated in the aforementioned additional provision.

Therefore, what the additional provision under analysis does is identify in certain sections of Article 9.2 of the GDPR those data processing activities that arise from the application of the laws cited, which does not imply that the processing of personal data carried out as a result of the application of other regulations does not find accommodation in the exceptions of the aforementioned provision, since the reference to the legal reserve contained in Article 9.2 of the LOPDGDD will allow for the existence of regulations that are not included, even those that have not yet been approved, which may provide coverage for the exception to the prohibition of processing special categories of data.

If this were not the case, it would imply the impossibility of carrying out the processing of special categories of data, specifically health data, that do not derive from the same, and it would render sections g), h), i), and j) of Article 9.2 of the GDPR devoid of material content due to the impossibility of effective application. 

Consider, for example, the processing of health data derived from the application of Organic Law 3/2013, of June 20, on the protection of the health of athletes and the fight against doping in sports activities; this is a law that is not cited in the additional provision under analysis and that supports the processing of health data and allows for the lifting of the prohibition in Article 9.1 of the GDPR.

The legislator of the LOPDGDD has specified that certain processing activities may be carried out under the protection of Article 9.2 of the GDPR, but the current regulatory framework allows for certain data processing activities to find support in other regulations and in other cases despite not being deduced from the analyzed additional provision.

In this regard, the European Data Protection Board (formerly the Article 29 Working Party) in its opinion on Guidelines on Automated Individual Decision-Making and Profiling for the purposes of Regulation 2016/679 indicates that to process special categories of data, coverage must be found in Article 9.2 of the GDPR, and once the general prohibition is excepted, one must refer to the cases in Article 6 of the GDPR to legitimize the processing in question. The processing of special categories of personal data is one of the areas in which the General Data Protection Regulation has expressly recognized that Member States have "leeway" when it comes to "specifying their rules," as stated in its recital 10. This legislative configuration margin extends both to the determination of the enabling causes for the processing of specially protected personal data—namely, the identification of essential public interest purposes and the assessment of the proportionality of the processing to the pursued end, while essentially respecting the right to data protection—as well as to the establishment of "appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject" [art. 9.2 g) GDPR]. The Regulation therefore contains a specific obligation for Member States to establish such guarantees, in the event that they enable the processing of specially protected personal data.

On the other hand, regarding the guarantees that the legislator must adopt, the aforementioned judgment No. 76/2019 of May 22, after recalling that “In view of the potential intrusive effects on the fundamental right affected resulting from the processing of personal data, the jurisprudence of this Court requires the legislator to establish, in addition to complying with the aforementioned requirements, adequate guarantees of a technical, organizational, and procedural nature, which prevent risks of varying probability and severity and mitigate their effects, as only in this way can respect for the essential content of the fundamental right itself be ensured,” analyzes what the norm must contain regarding the aforementioned guarantees: “Therefore, the resolution of the present challenge requires us to clarify a doubt raised regarding the scope of our doctrine on adequate guarantees, which consists of determining whether the adequate guarantees against the use of information technology must be contained in the law itself.”

The issue can only have a constitutional answer. The provision of adequate guarantees cannot be deferred to a moment subsequent to the legal regulation of the processing of personal data in question. The adequate guarantees must be incorporated into the legal regulation of the processing itself, either directly or by express and perfectly delineated reference to external sources that possess the appropriate normative rank. Only this understanding is compatible with the dual requirement that arises from Article 53.1 of the Spanish Constitution (RCL 1978, 2836) for the legislator of fundamental rights: the legal reserve for the regulation of the exercise of the fundamental rights recognized in Chapter Two of Title One of the Constitution and the respect for the essential content of such fundamental rights.
According to reiterated constitutional doctrine, the legal reserve is not limited to requiring that a law enables the restrictive measure of fundamental rights, but it is also necessary, in accordance with both what are referred to as - at times - normative predetermination requirements and - at other times - quality of law requirements, as well as respect for the essential content of the right, that in that regulation the legislator, who is primarily obliged to weigh the conflicting rights or interests, predetermines the assumptions, conditions, and guarantees under which the adoption of restrictive measures of fundamental rights is appropriate. This mandate of predetermination regarding essential elements, which are also ultimately linked to the proportionality assessment of the limitation of the fundamental right, cannot be deferred to a subsequent legal or regulatory development, nor can it be left in the hands of the individuals themselves” (FJ 8).
