I  
The draft bill submitted aims to guarantee the fundamental rights of children and adolescents to their physical, mental, psychological, and moral integrity against any form of violence, ensuring the free development of their personality and establishing comprehensive protection measures that include awareness-raising, prevention, early detection, protection, and redress for harm in all areas of their lives.  

Regarding the matter of personal data protection, the regulation to which the draft bill submitted for consultation must comply is Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), fully applicable since May 25, 2018, and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights.  

The aforementioned Regulation extends its protection, as established in its Article 1.2, to the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, their right to the protection of personal data, defined in its Article 4.1 as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  

Furthermore, Article 4.1 defines “processing” as “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination, or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”  

The commission of acts of violence against children and adolescents may involve, especially when carried out through information and communication technologies, unlawful processing of personal data, which is why this Agency has launched a priority channel to report the unlawful dissemination of sensitive content, a system aimed at providing a rapid response in exceptionally delicate situations, such as those involving the dissemination of sexual or violent content.  

Despite the advantages provided by new technologies and the services they offer, they are sometimes used as a means to extend forms of violence that promote the public humiliation of victims, severely damaging their privacy. The characteristics of ICT have given rise to new threats for minors and women victims of violence, stemming, among other things, from the speed with which information spreads in this environment, the ability to access information through search engines, and the difficulties in its removal. The ease of viral dissemination and the persistence and lack of forgetfulness in the online environment entail new risk situations, such as unauthorized access to and disclosure of sensitive information, intimate photographs or videos; surveillance and monitoring of online activities; damage to women's reputations; behaviors known as “sextortion” or online sexual harassment.  

The widespread and intensive use of mobile devices and the Internet, social networks, and services such as instant messaging or geolocation have served as a conduit for the proliferation of violent behaviors, with evidence showing that, in many cases, the Internet and its services and applications have been used to control, intimidate, harass, humiliate, and blackmail women and minors. The recording and dissemination of personal images is one of the most commonly used tools in cases of school bullying and its online version, cyberbullying, as well as in sexual harassment of minors, such as grooming or sexting, sometimes with tragic consequences.  

Therefore, this Agency considers it necessary that the draft bill subject to this report reflects this circumstance in its Exposition of Motives.

Constitution is an independent right from the right to privacy. In this sense, ruling 292/2000 stated:

“…the Court has already declared that Article 18.4 of the Spanish Constitution contains, in the terms of STC 254/1993, an institute guaranteeing the rights to privacy and honor and the full enjoyment of the remaining rights of citizens, which is, moreover, in itself 'a fundamental right or freedom, the right to freedom against potential assaults on the dignity and freedom of the person arising from the illegitimate use of the automated processing of data, which the Constitution calls 'informatics', what has been referred to as 'informatic freedom' (F.J. 6, reiterated later in SSTC 143/1994, F.J. 7, 11/1998, F.J. 4, 94/1998, F.J. 6, 202/1999, F.J. 2). The guarantee of the private life of the person and of their reputation today has a positive dimension that exceeds the scope of the fundamental right to privacy (Article 18.1 of the Spanish Constitution), and translates into a right of control over data relating to the person themselves. The so-called 'informatic freedom' is thus the right to control the use of the same data inserted in a computer program (habeas data) and includes, among other aspects, the citizen's opposition to certain personal data being used for purposes other than the legitimate one that justified its acquisition (SSTC 11/1998, F.J. 5, 94/1998, F.J. 4).

This fundamental right to data protection, unlike the right to privacy in Article 18.1 of the Spanish Constitution, which shares the objective of providing effective constitutional protection of personal and family private life, attributes to its holder a bundle of powers that consists mainly of the legal power to impose on third parties the performance or omission of certain behaviors whose specific regulation must be established by Law, which in accordance with Article 18.4 of the Spanish Constitution must limit the use of informatics, either by developing the fundamental right to data protection (Article 81.1 of the Spanish Constitution), or by regulating its exercise (Article 53.1 of the Spanish Constitution). The peculiarity of this fundamental right to data protection in relation to the closely related fundamental right of privacy lies, therefore, in its different function, which consequently implies that its object and content also differ.

The function of the fundamental right to privacy in Article 18.1 of the Spanish Constitution is to protect against any invasion that may occur in that area of personal and family life that the person wishes to exclude from the knowledge of others and from the intrusions of third parties against their will (see STC 144/1999, of July 22, F.J. 8). In contrast, the fundamental right to data protection seeks to guarantee that person a power of control over their personal data, over its use and destination, with the purpose of preventing its illicit and harmful trafficking to the dignity and rights of the affected individual. Ultimately, the right to privacy allows for the exclusion of certain data about a person from the knowledge of others, for this reason, and this has been stated by this Court (SSTC 134/1999, of July 15, F.J. 5; 144/1999, F.J. 8; 98/2000, of April 10, F.J. 5; 115/2000, of 10).

This guarantee imposes on public authorities the prohibition of becoming sources of that information without the necessary safeguards; and also the duty to prevent risks that may arise from the improper access or disclosure of such information. However, this power of disposition over one's personal data is of no value if the affected individual is unaware of what data is held by third parties, who holds it, and for what purpose. Hence the uniqueness of the right to data protection, since, on one hand, its object is broader than that of the right to privacy, as the fundamental right to data protection extends its guarantee not only to privacy in its constitutionally protected dimension under Article 18.1 of the Spanish Constitution (C.E.), but also to what this Court has sometimes defined in broader terms as the sphere of personality rights that belong to the realm of private life, inextricably linked to the respect for personal dignity (STC 170/1987, of October 30, F.J. 4), such as the right to honor, expressly cited in Article 18.4 C.E., and likewise, in the broad expression of Article 18.4 C.E., to the full exercise of personal rights. The fundamental right to data protection expands the constitutional guarantee to those data that are relevant to or have an impact on the exercise of any personal rights, whether or not they are constitutional rights and whether or not they relate to honor, ideology, personal and family privacy, or any other constitutionally protected good. Thus, the object of protection of the fundamental right to data protection is not limited only to the intimate data of the person, but to any type of personal data, whether intimate or not, whose knowledge or use by third parties may affect their rights, whether fundamental or not, because its object is not only individual privacy, which is covered by the protection granted by Article 18.1 C.E., but personal data. Consequently, it also encompasses those public personal data, which by virtue of being so, accessible to anyone's knowledge, do not escape the power of disposition of the affected individual because their right to data protection guarantees it. For this reason, the fact that the data is of a personal nature does not mean that only those related to the private or intimate life of the person are protected, but that the protected data includes all those that identify or allow the identification of the person, which may serve for the creation of their ideological, racial, sexual, economic profile, or of any other nature, or that serve for any other utility that in certain circumstances constitutes a threat to the individual.

To ensure adequate protection of the fundamental right to data protection and the rapid removal of content that seriously infringes upon it, the AEPD has created a specific reporting channel to prevent the continuation of the unlawful processing of personal data in particularly serious cases such as victims of gender-based violence, abuse or sexual assault, or harassment, or any other especially vulnerable group such as minors, individuals discriminated against due to their sexual orientation or race, persons with disabilities or serious illness, or those at risk of social exclusion. The AEPD's priority channel for reporting the unlawful dissemination of sensitive content and requesting its removal aims to provide a rapid response in exceptionally delicate situations, such as those involving the dissemination of sexual or violent content. The objective is to establish a communication channel so that the Agency, as an independent authority, can take urgent measures, if necessary, to limit the dissemination and access to personal data.  
In order to ensure the effectiveness of this channel and its use by minors, it is necessary to enable the possibility of reporting by themselves for minors with sufficient maturity, without the need to be accompanied by an adult.  
Furthermore, considering that, in many cases, the unlawful processing of personal data is carried out by other minors, it is necessary to specify the responsibility of those over 14 years of age, to whom the LOPDGDD recognizes the capacity to consent to the processing of their personal data and to exercise the corresponding rights, as well as to establish the responsibility of parents, guardians, foster parents, and legal or de facto custodians, in that order, regarding the failure to comply with the obligation imposed on them that entails a duty to prevent the administrative infringement attributed to minors.

Therefore, the first requirement for the processing to proceed for purposes other than those indicated in the aforementioned provision is that it must be established in a norm with the rank of law. However, such a norm must comply with the requirements of proportionality that justify the limitation of the fundamental right to data protection, as the Constitutional Court has repeatedly pointed out. All of this leads to the need to assess whether the necessary conditions for appreciating the existence of proportionality in the processing would be met in the case presented. To this end, it is essential to remember that the aforementioned proportionality requires, according to the doctrine of the Constitutional Court, following the principles established by the European Court of Human Rights, the overcoming of a triple test, in the sense of determining whether the measure adopted is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test), whether it is also necessary, in the sense that there is no other, less intrusive measure to achieve such a purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test), and finally, whether it is balanced or proportionate, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test), that is, whether the interference produced in the holder of the right subject to restriction by the measure is the minimum necessary to achieve the legitimate aim pursued with it.

Furthermore, the enabling legal norm that overcomes the aforementioned proportionality test should establish each and every one of the conditions and requirements of the intervention, as well as the appropriate guarantees of a technical, organizational, and procedural nature, as the Constitutional Court ruling 76/2019 of May 22 reminds us:
