The inquiry raises the question of what the status should be, as responsible or in charge of processing, in accordance with Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR) and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD), concerning the parties involved in the management of the business related to Collective Investment Institutions (CII hereinafter).  

I  
The inquiry arises following the issuance of Report 12/2021 from this Legal Advisory Office, in which, based on the information provided at that time, the following conclusion was reached:  
“VI  
Therefore, of the possibilities raised by the inquiry, the appropriate option from the perspective of data protection is to consider, on one hand, the SGIIC as responsible for the data of the investors, from the moment the subscription order is executed. From the perspective of the data subject, there will be an investment contract and therefore the SGIIC is responsible for the processing of that data.  
And, on the other hand, the ESI or sub-distributor as responsible for the processing of the investors' data derived from the investment service. From the perspective of the data subject, there will be an investment services contract and therefore the ESI is responsible for the processing of that data.  
The distribution platform should be considered as in charge of the processing of the SGIIC and also in charge of the processing of the sub-distributor or the ESI. It is a separate entity from those contractually linked by a contract (distribution contract or sub-distribution contract) whose object derives from the provision of a service that involves data processing (automated procedures (...) such as collection, registration, (...), communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection), regarding which it makes no decisions but merely follows instructions from both, and which also does not establish direct relationships with the data subjects, who only have a legal relationship with the SGIIC through the investment contract, and with the ESI through the investment services contract.”  
Therefore, between the SGIIC and the distribution platform, and between the ESI and the distribution platform, there must be a processing agreement in the terms provided in Article 28.3 of the GDPR.”

II
Recital 79 of the GDPR highlights the importance of determining the role of the parties involved in any data processing, stating that (…) The protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, as well as the responsibility of the controllers and processors, also regarding the supervision by the control authorities and the measures adopted by them, require a clear attribution of responsibilities under this Regulation, including cases where a controller determines the purposes and means of processing jointly with other controllers, or where processing is carried out on behalf of a controller.(…)
Article 4.7 of the GDPR defines the data controller as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of processing, the controller or the specific criteria for their appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States.”  
It also refers -in paragraph 8 of its Article 4-, to the processor as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller.” 
For its part, the LOPDGDD refers in its Article 33, paragraphs 1 and 2, to the processor, stating that:
“Article 33. Processor.
1. Access by a processor to personal data that is necessary for the provision of a service to the controller shall not be considered a communication of data as long as the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and this one are complied with.”

The entity that, in its own name and without evidence of acting on behalf of another, establishes relationships with the affected parties shall be considered the data controller and not the processor, even if there is a contract or legal act with the content specified in Article 28.3 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679. This provision shall not apply to data processing assignments made under public sector procurement legislation. 
The entity that, while acting as a processor, uses the data for its own purposes shall also be considered the data controller.
In general, the role of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, so that in those cases where the data controller entrusts a third party with the provision of a service that requires access to personal data, such access cannot be considered a transfer of data from the controller to the service provider, but rather that the processing of the data is carried out by the processor on behalf of and for the controller as if the controller were carrying it out itself.
However, in practice, more complex situations may arise depending on the different roles of the actors and the processing itself, as occurs in this consultation, and it is necessary to refer to the interpretative criteria established by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB hereinafter) in Guidelines 7/2020 of September 2, 2020 “On the concepts of controller and processor in the GDPR,” from which the following sections are noteworthy:
12. The concepts of controller and processor are functional concepts: their purpose is to assign responsibilities according to the actual functions of the parties. This implies that the legal status of an actor as a "controller" or "processor" should primarily be determined by their actual activities in a specific situation, and not by the formal designation of an actor as a "controller" or "processor" (for example, in a contract).
21 (…) In most situations, the "determining body" can be easily and clearly identified by reference to certain legal or factual circumstances from which "influence" can normally be inferred, unless other elements indicate otherwise. Two categories of situations can be distinguished: 1) control derived from legal provisions; and (2) control derived from factual influence. (…)
22 (…) There are cases where control can be deduced from an explicit legal competence, for example, when the controller or the specific criteria for its designation are established by national or Union law (…) the legislator has designated as the controller the entity that has a genuine capacity to exercise control.
23 The law will establish a task or impose an obligation on someone to collect and process certain data. In those cases, the purpose of the processing is usually determined by law. The controller will normally be the one designated by law to carry out this purpose, this public task (…) More generally, the law may also impose on public or private entities the obligation to retain or provide certain data. These entities would normally be considered responsible for the processing necessary to fulfill this obligation.
25. The need for a factual assessment also means that the role of a data controller does not derive from the nature of an entity that is processing data, but from its specific activities in a specific context. In other words, the same entity may act simultaneously as the controller for certain processing operations and as the processor for others, and the qualification as controller or processor must be assessed with respect to each specific data processing activity.
26 (…) When an entity engages in the processing of personal data as part of its interactions with its own employees, customers, or members, it will generally be the one that can actually determine the purpose and means of the processing and, therefore, acts as the controller in the sense of the GDPR. (…)
27 (…) The conditions of a contract are not decisive in all circumstances, as this would simply allow the parties to assign responsibility as they see fit. It is not possible to become a controller or evade the obligations of a controller simply by configuring the contract in a certain way when the factual circumstances suggest otherwise.

28. If one of the parties decides in fact why and how personal data is processed, that party will be a controller, even if a contract states that it is a processor. Similarly, it is not because a commercial contract uses the term "subcontractor" that an entity is considered a processor from the perspective of data protection legislation. (…)
75. Two basic conditions for qualification as a processor are: Being a separate entity in relation to the controller and Processing personal data on behalf of the controller. (…)
78. Processing personal data on behalf of the controller requires, first of all, that the independent entity processes personal data for the benefit of the controller. In Article 4, paragraph 2, processing is defined as a concept that includes a wide range of operations ranging from collection, storage, and consultation to use, dissemination, or any other form of making available and destruction. In practice, this means that any imaginable processing of personal data constitutes processing. (…)
79. Secondly, processing must be carried out on behalf of a controller, but not under its authority or direct control. Acting "on behalf of" means serving the interests of another person and recalls the legal concept of "delegation." In the case of data protection legislation, the processor is required to follow the instructions given by the controller at least regarding the purpose of the processing and the essential elements of the means. (…)
80. Acting "on behalf of" also means that the processor cannot carry out the processing for its own purpose(s).
81. The EDPB reminds us that not all service providers that process personal data during the provision of a service are "processors" in the sense of the GDPR. The role of a processor does not derive from the nature of an entity that is processing data, but from its specific activities in a specific context. The nature of the service will determine whether the processing activity amounts to processing personal data on behalf of the controller in the sense of the GDPR.

IV
Next, it is necessary to address the relationship between the SGIIC and the consulting party or distribution platform, which in the previous report was qualified as that of controller and processor, and which, with the information now provided regarding the contract to which they are linked, deserves a new analysis. 

As a starting point, it should be noted that the consulting party claims that it does not provide any service to the SGIIC, in the sense that it does not outsource any of the elements that make up its business, which is the sale of shares of the IIC. Its material relationship begins when the platform has channeled a purchase order that it receives from the sub-distributor because a client of the latter wants to acquire a share. 

That is to say, there is a contract that will regulate this relationship, but it does not imply an outsourcing of any service, simply because the distribution platform does not “sell” financial products, which is the object of the business of the SGIICs. 

The distribution platform cannot be considered as a “provider of financial products” as the SGIIC is considered under MIFID II regulations (Directive 2014/65/EU of May 15, 2014, Regulation (EU) 600/2014 of May 15, 2014). 

This consideration that the distribution platform is not a processor of the SGIIC can also be deduced from the analysis of the contractual relationship. To this end, the consulting party also provided the contract it signs with the SGIIC, from whose content the following considerations deserve to be made. 

There are elements in the contract from which it can be inferred that we are dealing with two independent data controllers, or two differentiated parties in a business, and that neither party receives instructions from the other, but rather they are on an equal footing mutually bound by the contract; that is to say, there is no hierarchical relationship of dependence, but rather bidirectional obligations. 

For example, in clause 1.4 it states that “None of the parties involved in this Contract may assign, in whole or in part, its obligations and rights arising from it, without the prior written consent of the other.” This means that in the event of subcontracting by either party with a third party, it must be subject to the agreement of the other party. 

In the relationships between controller and processor, the figure of subcontracting occurs, which must be authorized by the former (Article 28.4 GDPR); however, here it is observed that it is each party, on an equal footing, that must consent to the other regarding that possibility. 

Examples of these bidirectional obligations can also be found in the regulation of mutual indemnification duties (clauses 3.8, 6.8, and 7.5), or in the duties of information (Clause Four). 

That is to say, it is not that in the contracts of controller-processor there are no duties of indemnification or information, but rather that in practice what usually happens is that the processor provides a service to the controller and, on the other side, the controller pays the price for the provision of the service, and therefore, it is the processor that usually bears the duties of indemnification and transmission of information, given the obligations that each assumes. However, in this case, it is clearly observed that both are in a similar position; it is a relationship in that sense of equal to equal.

Special mention should be made of the clause regarding the use of advertising, as there are also reciprocal duties for both parties to request authorization to use images, texts, or logos from each other. (Fifth Clause). 
In practice, in a relationship between the data controller and the data processor, it is the controller who must authorize the processor to use the corporate image of the entity, which aligns with the logic that the processor does not establish relationships with third parties; rather, as they act on behalf of the controller, their actions are conceived as a whole, as part of the controller. 
In this case, it is observed that both parties could indistinctly use each other's corporate image or logo, provided that authorization is granted by both parties. Again, it is evident that we are in a position of equality and not hierarchical dependence. 
The clause 7.8 is also relevant, from which it is inferred that we are not dealing with a mandate, due to the deduction made - and which is intended to be avoided - regarding SGIIC "taking advantage" of what has been done by the distribution platform, to bypass it and acquire its own clients. 
That is to say, it is not the usual scheme of controller-processor in which it is intended to prevent the processor, given the information it has received, from replacing the controller of the processing and establishing direct relationships with clients, ultimately becoming a new controller and bypassing the original one. So much so that the LOPDGDD itself makes a provision in this regard by stating, in its article 33.2, that: The entity that, in its own name and without it being recorded that it acts on behalf of another, establishes relationships with the affected parties will be considered the controller of the processing and not the processor, even when there is a contract or legal act with the content established in article 28.3 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (...). The entity that, being designated as a processor, uses the data for its own purposes will also be considered a controller of the processing. 
Thus, in the aforementioned clause, what is intended to be avoided is precisely the opposite scenario, where the controller does not take advantage of the "supposed processor," which does not make sense from the perspective of the relationship between controller and processor. 
Therefore, we also find here another element to assert that we are not dealing with a controller and processor relationship. 
Clause 7.9 also establishes the obligation for SGIIC to protect the data to which it has had access as a result of the execution of the contract. That is to say, it is the "supposed controller" who commits to this obligation, whereas it is usually the processor who commits to protect the data to which it has had access as a result of the provision of the service (of the mandate). Therefore, this is another example that we are not facing a relationship in terms of controller-processor. 
Finally, it should be noted that in the Eighth Clause regarding sub-distribution contracts, it is not inferred that SGIIC authorizes the distribution platform to contract sub-distributors; rather, from the literal interpretation of the clause, it is deduced that both parties agree to this possibility, and therefore, it is part of the contractual obligations assumed by the platform. 
Regarding the Annex on Data Protection included in the contract, it is important to pause on the section regarding the Retention of personal data processed, as it indicates that the parties will (...) proceed to block the personal data of the other party that they process as the controller of the processing (...), meaning that both parties are observed to be independent controllers of the processing and that a reciprocal duty of blocking is established. If we were dealing with the controller-processor figure, it would be the processor who has duties regarding what to do with the data it has processed on behalf of the controller once the service provision has concluded. So much so that article 33.3 of the LOPDGDD establishes that: 3. The controller of the processing will determine whether, when the provision of services by the processor concludes, the personal data must be destroyed, returned to the controller, or delivered, if applicable, to a new processor. 
In this case, it is an obligation of both parties, which also leads us to consider that we are not dealing with a relationship between controller and processor. 
And finally, another consideration that must be taken into account in the data protection annex is that the distribution platform is even expressly referred to as the controller of the processing when regulating the communication of processed personal data. Obviously, if we were dealing with the figure of the processor, it would not have such designation.
