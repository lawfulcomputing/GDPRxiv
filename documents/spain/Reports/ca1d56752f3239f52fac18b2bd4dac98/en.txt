In the consultation letter, various issues related to the figure of the Data Protection Officer (DPO) are raised, incorporated into our legal system by virtue of the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC.  
The consulting association is interested in the opinion of this Legal Office on certain issues related to the appointment of Data Protection Officers (DPOs) and the performance of their functions within the entities to which their designation refers, referring to the need to comply with the formal and material requirements established by the applicable regulations, especially regarding their degree of professionalization and independence.  

I  
Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation - GDPR), and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights - LOPDGDD - constitute the legal framework of reference in Spain that affects the protection of personal data.  
These regulations govern the principles and foundations to which the collection and processing of personal data by any public or private entity that carries out personal data processing in the exercise of its activity must adhere, without prejudice to the existing special regulations for certain activities or processing, such as, for example, the processing of personal data subject to Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offenses or the execution of criminal sanctions, and the free movement of such data, which repeals Framework Decision 2008/977/JHA of the Council.  

As this Agency has repeatedly pointed out and is reflected in the Explanatory Memorandum of the LOPDGDD, “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model based fundamentally on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the data controller or processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.”  
A fundamental role within the new model of active responsibility will be played by the Data Protection Officer. Following the Explanatory Memorandum of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, “the figure of the Data Protection Officer acquires significant importance in Regulation (EU) 2016/679, and this is reflected in the organic law, which is based on the principle that it can be either mandatory or voluntary, integrated or not into the organization of the controller or processor, and can be either a natural person or a legal entity. The appointment of the Data Protection Officer must be communicated to the competent data protection authority. The Spanish Data Protection Agency will maintain a public and updated list of Data Protection Officers, accessible to anyone. Knowledge in the field can be accredited through certification schemes. Furthermore, they cannot be removed except in cases of willful misconduct or gross negligence. It is noteworthy that the Data Protection Officer allows for the establishment of a means for the amicable resolution of complaints, as the interested party may present before them a complaint that has not been addressed by the controller or processor.”

II  
Firstly, the consulting association seeks the opinion of this Legal Office regarding the criteria for the designation of the DPO, with reference to the necessary theoretical and practical preparation for their proper designation, and with special mention of the need for DPO professionals to exist following the designation of legal entities as DPOs. According to the consultation, at times, (i) specialized entities that offer services in the field of data protection have been performing DPO functions without formally proceeding to their appointment, while in other cases, (ii) certain entities are designated as DPOs without having professionals with the necessary preparation within their organization to fulfill their duties, (iii) the appointment of the DPO refers to an excessive number of clients such that it is very likely they cannot attend to their functions with the required professional solvency, or (iv) the appointment of the DPO would lack validity due to non-compliance with the criteria required for their designation.

Section 4 of CHAPTER IV of the GDPR - articles 37 to 39 - regulates in detail the figure of the Data Protection Officer. This regulation is complemented by the provisions in CHAPTER III of TITLE V of the LOPDGDD, in which articles 34 to 37 contain some specificities directly applicable to our domestic law.  
Specifically, the designation of the Data Protection Officer is included in article 37 of the GDPR, with the scope of subjects obliged to their appointment being expanded in article 34 of the LOPDGDD.  
In particular, article 37.1 a) imposes the mandatory designation of a DPO in cases where the processing is carried out by a public authority or body, except for courts acting in the exercise of their judicial function, also considering their appointment based on the type of activities carried out by the controller (or processor), when - article 37.1 b) - the main activities of the controller or processor consist of processing operations that, by their nature, scope, and/or purposes, require regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects on a large scale, or when - article 37.1 c) - the main activities of the controller or processor consist of large-scale processing of special categories of personal data under article 9 and data related to criminal convictions and offenses referred to in article 10.  
This provision is complemented by the provisions in article 34.1 of the LOPDGDD, which specifies certain cases in which the designation of a DPO is mandatory. From the regulation contained in this article, the obligation to designate a DPO in relation to the multiplicity of data processing referred to is derived, thus responding to the demands arising from the nature and characteristics of such processing, which impose the need for the appointment of the DPO in order to better guarantee the proactive compliance with data protection regulations.  
As stated, in all cases of articles 37 of the GDPR and 34 of the LOPDGDD, the designation of a DPO must proceed.  

III  
From the above, it can be inferred that the requirement for the appointment of a DPO should not be interpreted merely as a formality, and the requirements established in the applicable legal norms must be met.  
Consequently, it is necessary to conduct a brief analysis of the functions and resources that the DPO must have.
Legal Office
Thus, for the proper resolution of the issues raised, it must be based on the important functions that Article 39.1 of the GDPR assigns to the DPO:
“1. The data protection officer shall have at least the following functions: 
a) to inform and advise the controller or the processor and the employees involved in the processing of the obligations arising from this Regulation and from other data protection provisions of the Union or of the Member States; 
b) to oversee compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, other data protection provisions of the Union or of the Member States, and the policies of the controller or the processor regarding the processing of personal data, including the allocation of responsibilities, awareness-raising and training of staff involved in processing operations, and the corresponding audits; 
c) to provide advice as requested regarding the data protection impact assessment and to monitor its application in accordance with Article 35; 
d) to cooperate with the supervisory authority; 
e) to act as a contact point for the supervisory authority on issues related to processing, including the prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and to consult, where appropriate, on any other matter.”
Consequently, these are advisory and supervisory functions aimed at ensuring adequate compliance with the regulations on personal data protection, noting that Article 39.2 states that “The data protection officer shall perform their duties with due regard to the risks associated with processing operations, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing.” Likewise, Article 38.1 clearly establishes that “The controller and the processor shall ensure that the data protection officer is involved appropriately and in a timely manner in all issues related to the protection of personal data.”
In addition to the important advisory functions assigned to the DPO, including cases where it is necessary to carry out a data protection impact assessment due to high-risk processing, and specifying the supervisory functions, Article 36 of the LOPDGDD provides that “The officer may inspect the procedures related to the subject matter of this organic law and issue recommendations within the scope of their competencies,” that “In the exercise of their functions, the data protection officer shall have access to personal data and processing operations, and the controller or the processor may not oppose this access on the grounds of any duty of confidentiality or secrecy, including that provided for in Article 5 of this organic law,” and that “When the data protection officer identifies a relevant breach of data protection, they shall document it and immediately communicate it to the governing and management bodies of the controller or the processor.”
Furthermore, regarding relations with this Agency, it should be noted that it is the responsibility of the DPO, in accordance with Article 39.1.d) of the GDPR, to cooperate with the supervisory authority, with particular emphasis on the regulation that Article 37 of the LOPDGDD establishes regarding the intervention of the data protection officer in the event of a complaint to the data protection authorities:
“1. When the controller or the processor has designated a data protection officer, the affected party may, prior to submitting a complaint against them to the Spanish Data Protection Agency or, where appropriate, to the regional data protection authorities, address the data protection officer of the entity against which the complaint is made.
In this case, the data protection officer shall communicate to the affected party the decision that has been made within a maximum period of two months from the receipt of the complaint.
2. When the affected party submits a complaint to the Spanish Data Protection Agency or, where appropriate, to the regional data protection authorities, they may refer the complaint to the data protection officer so that the latter responds within one month.
If, after this period, the data protection officer has not communicated to the competent data protection authority the response given to the complaint, that authority shall continue the procedure in accordance with the provisions of Title VIII of this organic law and its implementing regulations.
3. The procedure before the Spanish Data Protection Agency shall be that established in Title VIII of this organic law and its implementing regulations. Likewise, the autonomous communities shall regulate the corresponding procedure before their regional data protection authorities.”
On the other hand, Article 39.1.e) of the GDPR also establishes as functions of the DPO “to act as a contact point for the supervisory authority on issues related to processing, including the prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and to consult, where appropriate, on any other matter.” Precisely, in light of the new model established in the GDPR and the functions assigned to the DPO regarding advising the controller and making inquiries to the AEPD, it is a reiterated criterion of this Legal Office that “if the controller has doubts about
Legal Office
The legal basis that may determine the lawfulness of a specific processing must consult its data protection officer in cases where, as in the present case, their appointment is mandatory, who must provide the precise advice. Only in the event that the data protection officer has legal doubts about the matter submitted for their consideration that cannot be resolved with the criteria already informed by the AEPD or due to new issues arising from the application of the new legal framework for the protection of personal data, which have a general scope where a report that contributes to legal certainty is advisable, may the data protection officer elevate such a consultation to this Legal Office, accompanying the consultation with their own report in which the issues subject to consultation are analyzed in detail and with justification.” This has been stated by this Agency in various legal reports, including report 148/2019, dated November 24, 2020.

For the proper fulfillment of these duties, the GDPR requires certain training requirements for the DPO, and that they be provided with the necessary resources. 

The training requirements are referred to in Article 37.5 of the GDPR, which states that “The data protection officer shall be designated based on their professional qualities and, in particular, their specialized knowledge of law and practice in the field of data protection and their ability to perform the tasks specified in Article 39.”

For its part, Article 35 of the LOPDGDD adds that “Compliance with the requirements established in Article 37.5 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 for the designation of the data protection officer, whether a natural or legal person, may be demonstrated, among other means, through voluntary certification mechanisms that particularly take into account the obtaining of a university degree that certifies specialized knowledge in law and practice in the field of data protection.”

In order to better interpret and apply these provisions, one may refer to the guidelines contained in the Article 29 Working Party document “Guidelines on Data Protection Officers” -WP243-, last revised and adopted on April 5, 2017, which, in relation to the knowledge and skills of the DPO, indicate the following points:

· Level of knowledge
The required level of knowledge is not strictly defined, but it must be commensurate with the sensitivity, complexity, and volume of the data that an organization processes. For example, when the data processing activity is particularly complex or involves a large amount of sensitive data, the DPO may need a higher level of knowledge and support. There is also a difference depending on whether the organization systematically transfers personal data outside the European Union or if such transfers are occasional. Thus, the DPO must be chosen carefully, duly considering the data protection issues that arise in the organization.

· Professional qualities
Although Article 37, paragraph 5, does not specify the professional qualities that should be taken into account when designating the DPO, an important factor is that they have knowledge of national and European legislation and practices in the field of data protection and a deep understanding of the GDPR. It is also useful for supervisory authorities to promote adequate and periodic training for DPOs. Knowledge of the business sector and the organization of the data controller is also beneficial. Additionally, the DPO must have a good understanding of the processing operations carried out, as well as the information systems and the security and data protection needs of the data controller. In the case of a public authority or body, the DPO must also possess a solid understanding of the rules and administrative procedures of the organization.

· Ability to perform their functions
The ability of the DPO to perform their functions must be interpreted both in reference to their personal qualities and knowledge as well as their position within the organization. Personal qualities should include, for example, integrity and a high level of professional ethics; the primary concern of the DPO should be to enable compliance with the GDPR. The DPO plays a fundamental role in promoting a culture of data protection within the organization and contributes to the implementation of essential elements of the GDPR, such as principles related to data processing, the rights of data subjects, data protection by design and by default, the record of processing activities, the security of processing, and the notification and communication of data security breaches.

On the other hand, the need to provide the DPO with the necessary resources to perform their functions is established as an obligation of the controller in Article 38.2 of the GDPR: “The controller and the processor shall support the data protection officer in the performance of the tasks mentioned in Article 39, providing the necessary resources for the performance of those tasks and access to personal data and processing operations, and for the maintenance of their specialized knowledge.”

In this regard, the Guidelines of the Article 29 Working Party indicate the following:
Legal Office
“Article 38, paragraph 2, of the GDPR provides that the organization shall support its DPO by 'providing the necessary resources for the performance of [their] tasks and access to personal data and processing operations, and for the maintenance of their specialized knowledge.' The following aspects must be particularly taken into account: 
· Active support for the DPO's work by senior management (at the board level). 
· Sufficient time for the DPO to fulfill their functions, which is particularly important when a part-time internal DPO is appointed or when the external DPO carries out data protection in addition to other obligations. Otherwise, the conflict between priorities could lead to neglect of the DPO's obligations. It is essential to have enough time dedicated to the DPO's tasks. It is advisable to establish a percentage of time for the DPO's own work when it is not carried out full-time. It is also advisable to determine the time necessary to perform the work, the appropriate level of priority for the DPO's functions, and for the DPO (or the organization) to draft a work plan. 
· Adequate support in terms of financial resources, infrastructure (premises, facilities, equipment), and personnel, as required. 
· Official communication of the DPO's appointment to all staff to ensure that their existence and function are known within the organization. 
· Necessary access to other services, such as human resources, legal department, IT, security, etc., so that DPOs can receive essential support, contributions, and information from these services. 
· Continuous training. DPOs must be given the opportunity to stay up to date with developments in the field of data protection. The goal should be to continuously improve the level of knowledge of DPOs, and they should be encouraged to participate in training courses on data protection and other forms of professional development, such as participation in private forums, workshops, etc. 
· Depending on the size and structure of the organization, it may be necessary to establish a DPO team (a DPO and their staff). In such cases, the internal structure of the team and the tasks and responsibilities of each of its members must be clearly defined. Similarly, when the DPO function is performed by an external service provider, a group of individuals working for that entity may effectively carry out the DPO functions as a team, under the responsibility of a designated main contact for the client. In general, the more complex or sensitive the processing operations are, the more resources should be allocated to the DPO. The data protection function must be performed effectively and provided with sufficient resources for the processing being carried out.”

As a consequence of its character and legal nature, Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, has direct effect and applies directly in the Member States without the need for them to adopt any implementing legislation into domestic law. This character and direct effect are peacefully reconciled with the requirements derived from the principle of legality and legal certainty enshrined in the Spanish Constitution and in the regulations on common administrative procedure. 

On the other hand, as previously stated, the Guidelines of the Article 29 Working Party refer both to the need for the Data Protection Officer (DPO) to have sufficient time to fulfill their functions and to the close relationship between the functions of the DPO and the size and structure of the organizations in which they operate (section 2.3 of the Guidelines). 

In summary, the appointment of a specific DPO who provides services in relation to a multiplicity of controllers and/or processors is a decision of the controller that is considered acceptable as long as the requirements of training, adequacy of resources, availability, and independence established by the GDPR are met, and it is ensured that the DPO participates adequately and in a timely manner in all matters relating to the protection of personal data, taking into account the requirements of adequacy of resources. 

Therefore, as we indicated in our Report 100/2019, dated February 11, 2020, what is essential is not the number of DPOs, nor even that they are part of the organization of the controller (section 6 of Article 37 provides that “the data protection officer may be part of the staff of the controller or the processor or perform their functions under a service contract”), but rather that they meet the requirements of training and independence that allow them to adequately perform the functions assigned to them by the GDPR, considering that, as Recital 97 of the GDPR reminds us, “The level of specialized knowledge required should be determined, in particular, by reference to the data processing operations carried out and the protection required for the personal data processed by the controller or processor.” 

Thus, since the designation of a DPO by a public or private entity is an eminently organizational matter, and as long as their independence is adequately guaranteed, what is relevant is that the functions assigned to the DPO can be carried out effectively, also taking into account the criterion of availability, which is fundamental to ensure that data subjects can easily contact the DPO (according to Article 38.4 of the GDPR, “data subjects...”).

In conclusion, these functions can be effectively carried out if the training requirements are met when appointing the DPO and if they are provided with the necessary resources, including, as noted by the Article 29 Working Party, a DPO team (a DPO and their staff), which must be proportional to the size and structure of the organization, as well as to the sensitivity, complexity, and volume of the data that an organization processes, ensuring the availability of the DPO so that interested parties can contact them, as well as communicate with the data protection authorities.

IV
Secondly, the consultation raises the legal validity of the appointment of the DPO made by designation in favor of individuals linked to the organization itself, which, according to the consultant, could call into question the independence of the officer and lead to potential incompatibilities in the performance of their duties. 
At this point, before addressing the issues related to the independence and potential conflicts of interest of the officer, it is important to emphasize the clear differentiation, on one hand, between the figure of the data controller and that of the data protection officer, and, on the other hand, between the figure of the officer and that of the security officer. 
Regarding the first of the distinctions, the GDPR is clear in imposing on the data controller the obligation to comply with the measures it provides. It will be the controller who must maintain a record of processing activities, assess the concurrent risk in a specific data processing operation, or develop, where applicable, the impact assessment required by the regulation. Likewise, it will be the one who must determine the technical and organizational measures that must be adopted to ensure the security of the processing. Logically, these measures will be developed by those who have been assigned such responsibilities within the structure of the controller, with particular relevance in this regard to the various parties that actively participate in the implementation of information security measures and, in particular, the security officer. 
In contrast to what has just been indicated, the role of the data protection officer will be to provide the controller with the necessary assistance and advice in the process of adopting the measures and to supervise that they have been adopted and are being implemented. That is to say, the data protection officer advises the controller and monitors the
Legal Office
compliance with the obligations established by the personal data protection regulations.
In this regard, the guidelines document WP243, adopted by the Working Group established by Article 29 of Directive 95/46/EC, states that “The GDPR clearly establishes that it is the controller and not the DPO who is obliged to implement ‘appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is performed in accordance with this Regulation’ (Article 24, paragraph 1). Compliance with data protection regulations is a corporate responsibility of the data controller, not the DPO.”
Furthermore, as we pointed out in our Report 170/2018, dated November 12, 2018, it is important to clearly differentiate the designation and functions of the data protection officer from those of the security officer in public or private organizations. This Report highlighted the clear distinction between the DPO and the security officer of the National Security Scheme -ENS-, in such a way that:

The positions of the RSEG and the DPO are requirements mandated by differentiated regulations with distinct objectives and scopes of application, and the principle of independence of the DPO should be understood broadly even in relation to the figures mentioned in Article 10 of the ENS. For its part, Article 38.3 of the GDPR, in regulating the position of the Data Protection Officer, emphasizes their independence by stating that the controller and the processor shall ensure that the data protection officer does not receive any instructions regarding the performance of these functions, nor may they be removed or sanctioned by the controller or the processor for performing their functions, and shall report directly to the highest hierarchical level of the controller or processor. Furthermore, according to the document from the Article 29 Working Party “Guidelines on Data Protection Officers,” last revised and adopted on April 5, 2017 -WP243-, the element related to the independence of the DPO must be taken into account in their appointment, so that:

“Independence: Article 38.3 of the Regulation establishes basic guarantees for the delegates to act independently within the organization in which they provide their services, including that they “do not receive any instruction regarding the performance of their tasks.” Additionally, it is important to note that those obliged to comply with the GDPR are the controller or the processor, so that if they make decisions contrary to the regulation and the advice provided by the delegate, the delegate must be given the opportunity to clearly express their dissenting opinion regarding such decisions.

Removal: The aforementioned Article 38.3 also refers to the fact that data protection officers “must not be removed or penalized by the controller or the processor for carrying out their functions,” which reinforces their autonomy and independence. They could, however, be dismissed or sanctioned in accordance with the applicable contractual, labor, or criminal legislation of each country, for reasons other than the performance of their functions. It should be noted that in the context of public administrations, the regime of infringements and sanctions applicable to their personnel applies.”

In relation to the possible conflict of interest of the delegate, the guidelines on data protection officers adopted by the Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, last revised and adopted on April 5, 2017 -WP243-, state the following:

“3.5. Conflict of interest
Article 38, paragraph 6, allows DPOs to “perform other functions and tasks.” However, it requires that the organization ensures that “such functions and tasks do not give rise to a conflict of interest.”
Legal Office
The absence of a conflict of interest is closely linked to the requirement to act independently. Although Data Protection Officers (DPOs) may have other functions, they may only be entrusted with other tasks and responsibilities if these do not give rise to conflicts of interest. This particularly means that the DPO cannot hold a position in the organization that leads them to determine the purposes and means of processing personal data. Due to the specific organizational structure of each organization, this must be considered on a case-by-case basis. 
As a general rule, conflicting positions within an organization may include senior management roles (such as CEO, COO, CFO, Chief Medical Officer, head of the marketing department, head of human resources, or head of the IT department) but may also include other lower positions in the organizational structure if such roles or positions lead to the determination of the purposes and means of processing. Furthermore, a conflict of interest may also arise, for example, if a DPO is asked to represent the data controller or processor before the courts in cases related to data protection.
From the above, it can be inferred that, regardless of the formula adopted for their appointment, the designation of the data protection officer must respond to the requirements derived from the principle of independence in the performance of their activities, ensuring that the performance of their functions and responsibilities does not give rise to a conflict of interest.
The provision of a data protection officer in public or private organizations requires that the selection complies with the legally established requirements and, in particular, that specialized knowledge in law and practice of data protection as indicated by the GDPR is accredited. Moreover, the formula adopted for the appointment of the DPO will depend on the decision made by the entity in which they perform their functions, as a consequence of its organizational autonomy.
However, issues related to the autonomy of the organizations in which the delegates are framed, clearly derived from the regulations analyzed in this report, cannot be an obstacle to the necessary guarantee of the independence of the data protection officer - pursuant to Article 38 of the GDPR - within the framework of the internal and external legal relationships they maintain in the performance of their functions.
Thus, in any case, it will be required, as provided in Article 36 of the LOPDGDD, that (i) when it is a natural person integrated into the organization of the data controller or processor, the data protection officer cannot be removed or sanctioned by the controller or processor for performing their functions unless they have acted with intent or gross negligence in their exercise, that (ii) the independence of the data protection officer within the organization is guaranteed, avoiding any conflict of interest, and that (iii) when the data protection officer perceives the existence of a relevant breach in data protection, they document and communicate it immediately to the governing and management bodies of the controller or processor.
In short, while Section 4 of CHAPTER IV of the GDPR - Articles 37 to 39 - provides for broad possibilities regarding the appointment and framing of DPOs within the organizations to which their designation refers, it is equally true that such autonomy must be reconciled with the requirements derived from the principle of independence of the officer, ensuring that the exercise of their functions does not lead to situations of incompatibility or conflict of interest.
In the legal norms regulating the figure of the data protection officer, the requirement of their independence is configured as inherent to the performance of their functions.

Thus, regarding the mandatory designation of a data protection officer, the Agency has issued various sanctioning resolutions, notably, concerning public administrations, the one issued within the sanctioning procedure PS/00001/2020, directed against a municipality for the infringement of Article 37 of the GDPR, due to its lack of a DPO, and, in the private sector, the one resulting from the sanctioning file PS/00417/2019, followed against a private entity also for lacking said Officer, which has led to the imposition of a sanction of 25,000 euros on that entity, also for an infringement of Article 37 of the GDPR. 
To conclude, and without prejudice to the specific infringements related to the DPO previously mentioned, it should be emphasized that, in practice, the data protection officer mainly assumes advisory and supervisory functions for the benefit of the controller or processor. However, as clearly stated by the GDPR in various provisions, the controller will have full responsibility under the law for any failure in that regard, and in no case shall that responsibility fall on the DPO. Specifically, as provided in Article 5.2 of the GDPR, “The controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and be able to demonstrate it (‘proactive responsibility’)”, and it is the controller who is responsible for applying “appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in accordance with this Regulation” (Article 24.1 of the GDPR). 
In other words, that responsibility does not rest on the DPO, as also inferred from Article 39 of the GDPR—which emphasizes their advisory and support tasks—but rather on the controller themselves, or, where applicable, on the processor. Consequently, controllers and processors will be the main parties interested in ensuring compliance with the requirements necessary for the proper designation of the DPO and in facilitating the exercise of their functions since, otherwise, they will be the ones held accountable for any potential breach of the obligations imposed by data protection regulations. 
In this regard, in the “Guidelines on Data Protection Officers” -WP243, from the Article 29 Working Party, it is expressly stated that: “DPOs are not personally liable in the event of a breach of the GDPR. The GDPR makes it clear that it is the controller or the processor who is obliged to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is carried out in accordance with its provisions (Article 24, paragraph 1). Compliance with data protection rules is the responsibility of the controller or the processor. 
Furthermore, the controller or the processor has a fundamental role in enabling the effective performance of the DPO's tasks. The appointment of a DPO is a first step, but the DPO must also have the autonomy and sufficient resources to carry out their work effectively.”
