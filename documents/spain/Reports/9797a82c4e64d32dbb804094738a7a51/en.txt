I  
The submitted Draft Law aims to regulate remote work, understood as “a form of organization of work or performance of labor activity according to which it is carried out at the home of the worker or at a place chosen by them, during all or part of their working day, on a regular basis,” and of which “teleworking” is a subcategory, defined as “that remote work carried out through the exclusive or predominant use of computer, telematic, and telecommunications means and systems,” being applicable, according to its Article 1, to employment relationships “in which the conditions described in Article 1.1 of the consolidated text of the Workers' Statute approved by Royal Legislative Decree 2/2015, of October 23, are met.”

The proposed regulation aims to adapt the regulation of remote work to the new labor relations, influenced by the increased use of new technologies and which have been amplified as a consequence of the COVID-19 pandemic, as stated in its Explanatory Memorandum:

“2. Remote work, in its classical conception of home work, as that which is performed outside the usual workplace and without the control of the company and linked to very specific sectors and geographical areas, has been surpassed by the reality of a new framework of relations and a severe impact of new technologies.

Currently, more than home work, what exists is remote and flexible work, which allows work to be carried out in new environments that do not require the presence of the worker at the workplace.

This virtualization of labor relations disconnects or delocalizes the worker from a specific place and time, which undoubtedly brings notable advantages, among others, greater flexibility in managing working times and breaks; greater possibilities, in some cases, for self-organization, with positive consequences, in these cases, for reconciling work, personal, and family life; reduction of costs in offices and savings on commuting costs; productivity and rationalization of schedules; commitment and experience of the employee; attraction and retention of talent or reduction of absenteeism.

However, it also presents possible drawbacks: data protection, security breaches, techno-stress, continuous hours, computer fatigue, permanent digital connectivity, greater work isolation, loss of corporate identity, deficiencies in the exchange of information between those who work in person and those who do so exclusively remotely, or transferring the costs of productive activity to the worker without any compensation, among others.

The real impact of these forms of work provision or organization was exponentially increased by the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, which has highlighted their advantages and weaknesses, as well as the need for their regulation to be addressed from a legal framework of security, certainty, and transparency.”

II
Regarding the matter of personal data protection, the regulation to which the Draft Bill submitted for consultation must comply is Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (GDPR hereinafter) and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD hereinafter).
In accordance with this regulation and the strict scope of competencies of this Agency, which does not extend to all digital rights regulated in the LOPDGDD but only to those involving the processing of personal data regulated in Articles 89 to 92 of the same (therefore, it does not include the right to digital disconnection, mentioned in the Draft Bill), the text submitted raises numerous issues related to the processing of personal data, although most of them are common to other modalities of labor relations, albeit in this case with new implications due to the use of technologies in remote work.
Consequently, this report will analyze some of these implications, without being able to conduct a comprehensive analysis given the abundant case law and the urgency in issuing it, starting from the need to comply, in any case, with the personal data protection regulations, systematized in the principles set out in Article 5 of the GDPR:
Article 5. Principles relating to processing
1. Personal data shall be:
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject ("lawfulness, fairness, and transparency");

In the latter case, considering that the processing of personal data

Indeed, although the Organic Law and the Regulation do not differ excessively regarding the enumeration of the legitimizing causes for processing, a highly relevant modification occurs in the way these causes are presented in the applicable texts: thus, while from the wording of Organic Law 15/1999 it seemed to be deduced that the basic rule of legitimacy was, in general, consent, with the remaining legitimizing causes being exceptional in relation to consent, which as a general rule should govern the processing, in the text of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, consent is presented as one of the six causes of legitimacy for processing without holding greater or lesser importance than the others enumerated in the regulation.

Furthermore, the General Data Protection Regulation itself highlights that the consent of the affected party should not constitute the legal basis for processing in certain cases. Thus, Recital 42 states in its last sentence that “Consent should not be considered freely given when the data subject does not have genuine or free choice or cannot deny or withdraw consent without suffering detriment,” and Recital 43 adds that “To ensure that consent has been given freely, it should not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, particularly when the data controller is a public authority and it is therefore unlikely that consent has been given freely in all circumstances of that particular situation.”

In this way, it is not appropriate to obtain the consent of the affected party in any case where the processing is covered by any of the causes included in letters b) to f) of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

In this regard, in the relationships between employer and employee, it has been considered that the legal basis for processing cannot be consent, given the inequality that arises, which prevents it from being considered free.

Constitutional 207/1996 establishes that it is “a common and constant requirement for the constitutionality of any restrictive measure of fundamental rights, including those that imply an interference with the rights to physical integrity and privacy, and more particularly, the restrictive measures of fundamental rights adopted during a criminal process are determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality. 
In this regard, we have emphasized that, to verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions: “whether such a measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, in addition, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and, finally, whether it is balanced or proportionate, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test).”
Therefore, any control measure adopted must pass this proportionality test, determining whether the measure is adequate, necessary, and balanced, since otherwise it is disproportionate and thus contrary to data protection regulations.
Ultimately, labor control as a legitimizing cause for the processing of personal data does not imply that any data processing covered by this purpose is permissible, but rather that such processing must be proportional, also complying with the other principles established in Article 5 of the GDPR. 
In general terms, this Agency has been stating that there cannot be monitoring of the worker throughout their entire workday at the workplace, as this would constitute an intrusive measure and likely disproportionate in relation to the pursued purpose, without being a proportionate response to potential or specific risks.
In this sense, the Article 29 Working Party on data protection also expressed itself in its Opinion 2/2017 on the processing of data at work, adopted on June 8, 2017 (WP 249), stating, “The rapid adoption of new information technologies in the workplace, in terms of infrastructure, applications, and smart devices, allows for new types of systematic and potentially invasive data processing,” among which it cites “new forms of processing, such as those related to personal data on the use of online services and/or the location data of a smart device, are much less visible to workers than other more traditional types,” which is why “With new technologies, the need for transparency becomes more evident, as they allow for the collection and subsequent processing of possibly large amounts of personal data in a covert manner.” Furthermore, directly related to the subject of this report, it points out that “when workers work remotely (from their home) or while traveling for professional reasons, monitoring of activities performed outside the physical work environment may occur, which may include monitoring the individual in a private context” and reiterates the importance of observing all principles of personal data protection regulations:
“Previously, the GT29 indicated in Opinion 8/2001 that employers must take into account the fundamental principles relating to data protection of the DPD when processing personal data in the labor context. The development of new technologies and processing methods has not changed this situation; in fact, it can be said that these advances make it even more important for employers to respect these principles.
In this context, employers must:
- ensure that data is processed for specific and legitimate purposes that are proportionate and necessary;
- take into account the principle of purpose limitation and at the same time ensure that the data is adequate, relevant, and not excessive for the legitimate purpose;
- apply the principles of proportionality and subsidiarity, regardless of the applicable legal basis;
- be transparent with workers about the use and purpose of control technologies;
- allow the exercise of the rights of the data subject, including the right of access and, where appropriate, the rectification, deletion, or blocking of personal data;
- maintain the accuracy of the data and not retain it longer than necessary; and
- adopt all necessary measures to protect the data against unauthorized access, as well as ensure that staff are sufficiently aware of their obligations regarding data protection.

IV
On other occasions, the processing of personal data of workers by the employer will be determined by the fulfillment of legal obligations, being lawful under the provisions of Article 6.1(c) of the GDPR: the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller.
This would be the case for processing necessary to comply with the employer's obligation to ensure the safety and health of the workers in their service in all aspects related to work, in accordance with Law 31/1995, of November 8, on the Prevention of Occupational Risks or the obligations to ensure the security of personal data imposed by the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.

What cannot be understood is that the company can enable the system it deems most appropriate, since, as has been repeatedly stated by the AEPD, the existence of a legitimizing basis does not exempt compliance with the principles of article 5 of the GDPR, and especially, in addition to the purpose limitation mentioned earlier, with the principles of proportionality and minimization, seeking means that, given the specific purpose, are the least intrusive in the private sphere of the affected party, which can only be assessed based on the specific case. In this regard, it must be evaluated whether the record will be used solely for the purposes provided in article 34.9 of the Workers' Statute or if it will also be used as a control measure by the employer, pursuing both purposes, taking into account the specific circumstances in which remote work will be carried out.

For these cases, in the Opinion of the Group dated 29 6/2014, of April 9, on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller (WP 217), various guidelines and orientations are incorporated regarding the concurrence of “legitimate interest,” as well as the necessary safeguards in consideration of the respect and guarantee of the rights of those affected by this type of processing. 

Within the framework of these guarantees, the requirement of “balancing test” between the legitimate interest of the data controller or any third parties to whom the data is communicated and the interests or fundamental rights of the data subject stands out. 

The analysis inherent to the “balancing test” requires a comprehensive consideration of a series of factors, in order to ensure that the interests and fundamental rights of those affected are duly taken into account. At the same time, it is a modifiable test, which can vary from simple to complex. The factors that must be considered when conducting such a balancing test will include: 
- The nature and source of the legitimate interest, and whether the data processing is necessary for the exercise of a fundamental right, is of public interest, or benefits from the recognition of the affected community;
- The impact on the data subject and their reasonable expectations regarding what will happen to their data, as well as the nature of the data and the manner in which it is processed; 
- The additional safeguards that could limit an undue impact on the data subject, such as data minimization, privacy protection technologies, increased transparency, the general and unconditional right to voluntary exclusion, and data portability. 

As an example of legitimate interest, Recital 49 states that “The processing of personal data is a legitimate interest of the data controller to the extent strictly necessary and proportionate to ensure the security of the network and information, that is, the ability of a network or information system to withstand, at a determined level of trust, accidental events or unlawful or malicious actions that compromise the availability, authenticity, integrity, and confidentiality of personal data stored or transmitted, and the security of the related services offered by, or accessible through, these systems and networks, by public authorities, computer emergency response teams (CERT), computer security incident response teams (CSIRT), providers of networks and electronic communications services, and providers of security technologies and services. On the other hand, it should be noted that the processing of personal data in remote work, especially regarding worker control measures, may involve the processing of special categories of data, such as in the case of biometric data aimed at the unique identification of the worker, the processing of which is prohibited by Article 9.1 of the GDPR, unless one of the exceptions outlined in its paragraph 2 applies to lift the prohibition on such processing, which is an indispensable prerequisite for subsequently assessing the existence of a legal basis that legitimizes the processing under Article 6 of the GDPR. 
In this regard, it should be recalled the restrictive criterion that this Agency has maintained regarding facial recognition, as noted in report 31/2019 concerning the field of private security and in report 36/2020 regarding the use of facial recognition techniques in online assessment tests by universities. 
Report 36/2020 analyzed the processing of biometric data through the use of facial recognition techniques, concluding that such processing involved the processing of special categories of data for the purposes of the GDPR, with reasoning that would also apply in the case where it was intended to be used as a measure of worker control: 

In this case, as the inquiry concerns facial recognition techniques aimed at verifying the identity of the student, we would be faced with the processing of biometric data, as defined by Article 4.14 of the GDPR: 
“biometric data”: personal data obtained from a specific technical processing, related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person that allow or confirm the unique identification of that person, such as facial images or fingerprint data; 
However, it should be noted that the GDPR does not seem to consider all processing of biometric data as processing of special categories of data, since Article 9.1 refers to “biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person,” so that, from a joint interpretation of both provisions, it seems to imply that biometric data would only constitute a special category of data if subjected to specific technical processing aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person. In this sense, it seems that Recital 51 also expresses this by stating that “The processing of photographs should not systematically be considered processing of special categories of personal data, as they are only included in the definition of biometric data when the fact of being processed by specific technical means allows for the unique identification or authentication of a natural person.” 
With the same criterion, the Protocol amending the Convention for the Protection of Individuals with regard to the processing of personal data, approved by the Committee of Ministers at its 128th session in Elsinore on May 18, 2018 (Convention 108+) includes only as special categories of data, in its Article 6.1, biometric data aimed at the unique identification of a person (“biometric data uniquely identifying a person”), without including the reference to authentication. 
In order to clarify the interpretative doubts that arise regarding the consideration of biometric data as special categories of data, one can refer to the distinction between biometric identification and biometric verification/authentication established by the Article 29 Working Party in its Opinion 3/2012 on the evolution of biometric technologies: 

Biometric identification: the identification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparing their biometric data (acquired at the time of identification) with a series of...

of stored biometric templates in a database (i.e., a one-to-many matching process).
Biometric verification/authentication: the verification of an individual by a biometric system is typically the process of comparing their biometric data (acquired at the time of verification) with a single biometric template stored on a device (i.e., a one-to-one matching process).
This same differentiation is reflected in the European Commission's White Paper on Artificial Intelligence:
“With regard to facial recognition, ‘identification’ refers to the comparison of a person's facial image template with many other templates stored in a database to determine if their image is stored there. ‘Authentication’ (or ‘verification’), on the other hand, usually refers to the matching of two specific templates. It allows for the comparison of two biometric templates that, in principle, are assumed to belong to the same person; thus, the two templates are compared to determine if the person in the two images is the same. This procedure is used, for example, in automated border control gates employed at airport border checks.”
Considering the aforementioned distinction, it can be interpreted that, according to Article 4 of the GDPR, the concept of biometric data would include both cases, both identification and verification/authentication. However, in general terms, biometric data will only be considered a special category of data in cases where they are subjected to technical processing aimed at biometric identification (one-to-many) and not in the case of biometric verification/authentication (one-to-one).
Nevertheless, this Agency considers that it is a complex issue, subject to interpretation, regarding which no general conclusions can be drawn, and attention must be paid to the specific case according to the data processed, the techniques used for their processing, and the consequent interference with the right to data protection, and, as long as the European Data Protection Committee or the judicial bodies do not pronounce on this matter, the most favorable interpretation for the protection of the rights of the affected individuals should be adopted in case of doubt.

c) to provide advice as requested regarding the data protection impact assessment and to monitor its application in accordance with Article 35;
d) to cooperate with the supervisory authority;
e) to act as a contact point for the supervisory authority on issues related to processing, including the prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and to consult, where appropriate, on any other matter.
2. The Data Protection Officer shall perform their functions with due regard to the risks associated with processing operations, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing.

The appointment of the data protection delegate shall be mandatory in the cases contemplated in Article 34 of the LOPDGDD. Consequently, to determine the measures and guarantees adopted to ensure that the processing is in accordance with personal data protection regulations, the data controller must take into account, as established by Article 24 of the GDPR, the scope, context, and purposes of the processing and the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Based on the risk analysis and the DPIA, they must adopt all appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and demonstrate that the processing is compliant with the Regulation, including the corresponding security measures.

Regarding the latter, Article 32 of the GDPR does not establish a closed list of security measures that the controller and the processor must adopt, such that by adopting them, they would have fulfilled their obligations regarding security. Instead, under the principle of proactive responsibility, it establishes that “taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the variable probability and severity risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk,” which may include, among others: 
“a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data; 
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services; 
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident; 
d) a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.” 

Consequently, it is the responsibility of the data controller and, where applicable, the processor, advised, if necessary, by the DPD, to determine, within the framework of risk management for the rights and freedoms established in Article 24, all measures and guarantees, including security measures, necessary for the processing of personal data that is intended to be carried out, and it does not correspond to this Agency to comment on them, except in the exceptional case that it is necessary to make a prior consultation when the DPIA shows a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals after having applied measures to mitigate it, and without prejudice to the investigative powers that correspond to it.

Article 16. Right to privacy and data protection.
1. In this regard, it should be considered that the right to personal data protection is a fundamental right recognized in Article 18.4 of the Constitution, which is independent of the right to privacy included in the same provision, according to repeated doctrine of the Constitutional Court. In this sense, the ruling of the Constitutional Court 292/2000, of November 30, 2000, recalls how “the Court has already stated that Article 18.4 C.E. contains, in the terms of STC 254/1993, an institute of...

guarantee of the rights to privacy and honor and the full enjoyment of the remaining rights of citizens, which is, in itself, "a fundamental right or freedom, the right to freedom against potential assaults on the dignity and freedom of the person arising from the illegitimate use of the mechanized processing of data, which the Constitution calls 'informatics,' which has been referred to as 'informatic freedom' (F.J. 6, reiterated later in SSTC 143/1994, F.J. 7, 11/1998, F.J. 4, 94/1998, F.J. 6, 202/1999, F.J. 2). The guarantee of the private life of the person and their reputation today has a positive dimension that exceeds the scope of the fundamental right to privacy (art. 18.1 C.E.), and translates into a right of control over data related to the person themselves. The so-called "informatic freedom" is thus the right to control the use of the same data inserted in a computer program (habeas data) and includes, among other aspects, the citizen's opposition to certain personal data being used for purposes other than that legitimate purpose which justified its acquisition (SSTC 11/1998, F.J. 5, 94/1998, F.J. 4).”

For the High Court, “the peculiarity of this fundamental right to data protection in relation to that fundamental right so closely related as is the right to privacy lies, therefore, in its different function, which entails, consequently, that its object and content also differ. The function of the fundamental right to privacy in art. 18.1 C.E. is to protect against any invasion that may occur in that area of personal and family life that the person wishes to exclude from the knowledge of others and from the intrusions of third parties against their will (see STC 144/1999, of July 22, F.J. 8). In contrast, the fundamental right to data protection seeks to guarantee that person a power of control over their personal data, over its use and destination, with the purpose of preventing its illicit and harmful trafficking for the dignity and rights of the affected party […]”.

It also emphasizes how the object of the right to data protection is broader than that of the right to privacy, starting from the premise that “the fundamental right to data protection expands the constitutional guarantee to those data that are relevant to or have an impact on the exercise of any rights of the person, whether or not they are constitutional rights and whether or not they relate to honor, ideology, personal and family privacy, or any other constitutionally protected good […]. Therefore, it also extends to those public personal data, which by virtue of being so, by being accessible to anyone's knowledge, do not escape the disposal power of the affected party because their right to data protection guarantees it. Also for this reason, the fact that the data is of a personal nature does not mean that only those related to the private or intimate life of the person are protected, but that the protected data includes all those that identify or allow the identification of the person, which may serve for the creation of their ideological, racial, sexual, economic profile or of any other nature, or that serve for any other utility that in certain circumstances constitutes a threat to the individual.”

And finally, another fundamental difference of the right to data protection stands out, derived from its content, since unlike the right to personal and family privacy, which grants the individual the legal power to impose on third parties the duty to refrain from any intrusion into the intimate sphere of the person and the prohibition of using what is thus known, "the content of the fundamental right to data protection consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that enables the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which data this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose such possession or use.
