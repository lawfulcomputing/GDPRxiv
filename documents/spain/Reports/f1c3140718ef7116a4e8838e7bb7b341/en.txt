After examining your request for a report, submitted to this Legal Office, regarding the Draft Organic Law on the fight against doping in sports, requested from this Spanish Agency for Data Protection in accordance with the provisions of articles 57.1.c) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) and 5 b) of the Statute of the Agency, approved by Royal Decree 428/1993, of March 26, I am pleased to inform you of the following:  

I  
As stated in the Explanatory Memorandum of the draft, and in the Regulatory Analysis Report (MAIN), which is attached to the request for a report, the objective of the draft is to adapt Spanish internal regulations against doping in sports to the international framework in this area, fundamentally based on the World Anti-Doping Program. This Program has as its essential tool for ensuring clean sports worldwide the World Anti-Doping Code created by the World Anti-Doping Agency in 2003. This Code is reviewed and updated every 6 years, with new versions approved in 2009 and 2015, respectively. Consequently, a new World Anti-Doping Code will come into force on January 1, 2021, which, by incorporating relevant changes and updates, requires the adjustment of Organic Law 3/2013, of June 20, on the protection of the health of athletes and the fight against doping in sports activities, in order to align it with the new Code.  

In the regulatory framework, the International Convention against Doping in Sport of UNESCO, No. 135, of November 16, 1989, was signed by Spain in 2005 and subsequently ratified in 2006. The Convention establishes that the signatory States must use the necessary legislative measures, regulations, policies, or administrative provisions to comply with the criteria set by the World Anti-Doping Agency. In this regard, it should be noted that the World Anti-Doping Code (articles 23-24) establishes that signatories must implement the applicable provisions of the Code through policies, laws, standards, or regulations, depending on their authority and within their respective areas of competence, and that compliance with the Code and the International Standards by the signatories will be supervised by the World Anti-Doping Agency, in accordance with the International Standard for the Implementation of the Code. The aforementioned Agency may impose corresponding sanctions in the event of non-compliance with the Code by a signatory State. A failure to adapt Spanish regulations to the new version of the Code would negatively and directly affect public sports policies. Finally, the draft adds that the protection of the health and physical and moral integrity of the athlete is the general legal good in the fight against doping, and whose safeguarding is pursued with this regulatory project.

Currently, Article 4.15 of the GDPR defines "data concerning health" as personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status. Thus, as this Agency literally stated in its Report 0288/2012, (…) there is no doubt that, for example, the results of an analytical test or the detection of the presence of a certain irregular indicator are considered health-related data, as will be, of course, those related to medical treatments received, and those related to therapeutic use authorizations (…). Therefore, we find ourselves, in these cases, with the processing of special categories of data, regulated in Article 9 of the GDPR, so that not only must one of the legal bases contemplated in Article 6.1 of the GDPR be met, but also one of the grounds contemplated in paragraph 2 of Article 9 of the GDPR that lifts the prohibition on processing such data.

That is to say, the analysis of the regulatory norms for the fight against doping will essentially require taking into account that, alongside the objective of ensuring fair play, its purpose is to adopt the necessary measures to guarantee the health of the athlete and their comprehensive assistance, thus preventing the consumption of doping substances or the use of prohibited methods from causing harm, sometimes irreparable, to their health.

In this way, the existence of a reason of general interest that justifies the adoption of the measures provided in the Draft Bill that may involve the processing of data, in most cases related to the health of the athlete, must be taken into account.

To this end, it is particularly relevant to emphasize that the Draft Bill repeatedly states that its essential object, and consequently, "the legislator's intention is to include a robust health protection system for athletes engaged in any sporting activity," subsequently indicating that "The new regulation represents, therefore, a change of model compared to the previous regulations. It is true that Law 7/2006, of November 21, pointed out several of the elements that have allowed the construction of the new system, but many of them could not have an effective practical development. The new regulation is based on a fundamental criterion, which is that the general objective of the Law cannot be limited to the fight against doping, but rather exceeds this aspect significantly. Experience showed that many aspects related to the health of athletes had been neglected, not so much regarding the actions of public authorities affecting high-level athletes, but in terms of the application of effective measures in other areas of sport."

Consequently, the Draft Bill submitted for report, far from grounding its content on reasons different from those that justified the approval of Law 7/2006, emphasizes even more strongly its connection to the provisions of Article 43 of the Constitution, so that, following the argumentation maintained by this Agency in its previous reports, it can be considered that the purpose pursued by the regulation may be sufficient for the purposes of considering the applicability of the principle of necessity of processing based on "reasons of general interest" referred to in Article 7.3 of Organic Law 15/1999.

Therefore, in the present case, a public interest in the field of public health is established in accordance with the aforementioned Article 9.2. i) of the GDPR and that, in accordance with the provisions of the same and Article 9.2 of Organic Law 3/2018 of December 5, is protected by a norm with the rank of law, which establishes additional requirements related to its security and confidentiality, such as the duty of confidentiality and secrecy contained in Articles 52 and 53 of Organic Law 3/2013, as well as the duty of information in its Article 15.3.

The concurrence of reasons of public interest in these cases is expressly recognized in the GDPR itself, which, when referring to the exceptions for the transfer of personal data to third countries or international organizations regulated in its Article 49, states in Recital 112 that:

Such exceptions should apply in particular to transfers of data required and necessary for important reasons of public interest, for example in the case of international data exchanges between authorities in the field of competition, tax or customs administrations, between financial supervisory authorities, between competent services in the field of social security or public health, for example in cases of contacts aimed at locating contagious diseases or to reduce and/or eliminate doping in sport."

However, it cannot be dismissed, but rather affirmed, in light of the purposes of the law expressed both in the MAIN and in the Draft Bill itself, that the exception contained in letter g) of Article 9.2 of the GDPR is also applicable as a legal basis legitimizing the processing of special categories of data: the processing is necessary for reasons of essential public interest, based on the Law of the Union or the Member States, which must be proportional to the objective pursued, respect the right to data protection in essence, and establish adequate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the interested party.

The Explanatory Memorandum of the Draft Bill clearly delineates the aspect of health protection for athletes from the specific aspect of preventing doping in sport as a good in itself, to avoid the adulteration of competition and "promote the conditions for achieving sporting and human excellence."

The fight against doping in sports at a global level is being carried out through the World Anti-Doping Program, which encompasses all necessary aspects to collectively harmonize actions aimed at combating doping practices. Its ultimate goal is the protection of the health of athletes, although it also aims to promote conditions for achieving sporting and human excellence by eradicating the use of prohibited substances or methods that distort the rules of fair competition and equality in various sports practices.

Although the prevention and prosecution of doping in sports decisively contributes to the protection of health, that other purpose of ensuring the development of sports competitions in terms of equality and in accordance with the natural abilities and preparation of athletes requires the adoption and implementation of specific and unique measures aimed directly at tackling and eliminating the use of substances and the use of methods that fraudulently alter those natural abilities.

To achieve greater effectiveness in eliminating doping practices in competitive sports, and without prejudice to the fact that this will also improve the protection of athletes' health, it has been deemed appropriate to introduce changes in the regulatory framework of this matter, fundamentally proceeding to delineate the specific competencies in the fight against doping from the more general ones related to health protection and, consequently, to entrust and circumscribe the exercise of the former to a specialized entity in such tasks, the State Agency Spanish Commission for the Fight Against Doping in Sports.

With this law, the process of dividing tasks and responsibilities in relation to the specific fight against doping and the more generic protection of health in sports is thus completed, which, moreover, was already initiated with Royal Decree-Law 3/2017, which amended Organic Law 3/2013, of June 20, on the protection of athletes' health and the fight against doping in sports activity.

In the face of increasingly sophisticated and difficult-to-detect sports doping practices, it is necessary to adopt particularly rigorous actions to prevent them through intensified controls and, where appropriate, reactive measures in case they occur. The reinforcement and intensification of anti-doping measures thus becomes the specific and concrete objective of the law.

On the other hand, as is well known, the fight against doping has a strong international dimension. Sports practice today extends worldwide, and the fight against the scourge of doping in sports has become a shared effort among states and the sports movement.

It is worth recalling that, as early as 2005, Spain signed the UNESCO International Convention Against Doping in Sport, which it ratified in 2006, and which commits all signatory governments to the fight against doping by obliging them to implement clear and decisive policies in this area.

Article 43 of the Constitution not only recognizes the right to health protection (paragraph 1), but its paragraph 3 establishes that "public authorities shall promote health education, physical education, and sports. They shall also facilitate the appropriate use of leisure." In turn, Article 53.3 of the Constitution establishes, for both purposes, that the recognition, respect, and protection of the principles recognized in Chapter Three shall inform positive legislation, judicial practice, and the actions of public authorities. It is obvious that the promotion of sports, as a constitutional good, is exclusively that of "clean" sports, which in turn can have repercussions on the health of the athlete; thus, this is not the only objective to be achieved, but the very promotion of sports is also an end in itself. See Supreme Court ruling 348/2017, of June 1, (Civil, Rec. 2467/2016).

Therefore, this Agency considers that the existence of an essential public interest, in the sense of Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, may also serve as an exception to the prohibition of processing special categories of data established in Article 9.1 of the GDPR.

IV
Moreover, in addition to the above, the study of the draft bill (of this and any other that establishes personal data processing) must take into account the provisions of the Constitution, as the fundamental right to the protection of personal data is regulated, as such a fundamental right, in Article 18.4 thereof.

The Constitutional Court has had the opportunity to examine the requirements for laws that establish personal data processing, as restrictions on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data of the interested party, to be considered in accordance with the Constitution. Thus, this constitutional doctrine can be summarized in the ruling of the Constitutional Court (STC) 76/2019, of May 22. This ruling contains the relevant doctrine regarding the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and addresses both the characteristics and the content that the regulations intending to establish an interference in that fundamental right must have.
Legal Office
(…) By express mandate of the Constitution, any state interference in the realm of fundamental rights and public freedoms, whether it directly affects their development (Article 81.1 CE) or limits or conditions their exercise (Article 53.1 CE), requires a legal authorization (see, among others, STC 49/1999, of April 5, FJ 4). (…) This dual function of the law reserve translates into a dual requirement: on one hand, the necessary intervention of the law to authorize the interference; and, on the other hand, that legal norm "must meet all those indispensable characteristics as a guarantee of legal certainty," that is, "it must express each and every one of the premises and conditions of the intervention" (STC 49/1999, FJ 4). In other words, "it not only excludes delegations in favor of regulatory norms [...], but it also implies other requirements regarding the content of the Law that establishes such limits" (STC 292/2000, FJ 15). 
And this is because, in the realm of special categories of personal data, such as health data, which includes data collected from athletes for the verification of anti-doping regulations, (…) the General Data Protection Regulation establishes the minimum, common, or general guarantees for the processing of personal data that are not special. In contrast, it does not itself establish the legal regime applicable to the processing of special personal data, neither in the context of Member States nor for Union Law. Therefore, it does not set the guarantees that must be observed for the various possible treatments of sensitive data, appropriate to the risks of varying probability and severity that exist in each case; treatments and special categories of data that are, or can be, very diverse from one another. The regulation merely contemplates the possibility that the legislator of the European Union or that of the Member States, each within their area of competence, foresee and regulate such treatments, and indicates the guidelines that must be observed in their regulation. One of those guidelines is that the law of the Member State establishes "appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject" [Article 9.2.g) GDPR] and that "adequate guarantees are provided" (recital 56 GDPR). It is evident that the establishment of appropriate and specific measures can only be express. If the internal norm regulating the processing of personal data related to political opinions does not provide for those adequate guarantees, but rather, at most, implicitly refers to the general guarantees contained in the General Data Protection Regulation, it cannot be considered that it has carried out the normative task that the latter requires.
Consequently, and as required by the Constitutional Court, the law that establishes certain interferences in the fundamental right to the

The protection of personal data of the data subjects, such as, in the present case, the possibility of processing personal data of special categories, such as data relating to their health, requires that this, first of all, and for each processing of personal data of special categories that it contemplates:
a) specify the essential public interest that underpins the restriction of the fundamental right (FJ 7 of the STC 76/2019).
b) secondly, the law must regulate in detail the interferences with the fundamental right by establishing clear rules regarding the scope and content of the data processing it authorizes. That is to say, it must establish what the prerequisites and conditions for the processing of personal data relating to special categories of personal data that are to be processed are, through clear and precise rules (STC 76/2019, FJ 7 b).
c) And finally, the law itself must contain adequate guarantees against the collection of personal data that it authorizes. The Constitutional Court has been clear in stating that [t]he provision of adequate guarantees cannot be deferred to a later moment than the legal regulation of the personal data processing in question. The adequate guarantees must be incorporated into the very legal regulation of the processing, either directly or by express and perfectly delineated reference to external sources that have the appropriate normative rank. Only that understanding is compatible with the dual requirement that stems from Article 53.1 CE (...). It is evident that if the norm included a reference for the integration of the law with the adequate guarantees established in norms of lower rank than the law, it would be considered a deregulation that sacrifices the legal reserve under Article 53.1 CE, and for this sole reason, it should be declared unconstitutional and null. (...). Ultimately, it is about “adequate guarantees of a technical, organizational, and procedural nature, which prevent risks of different probabilities and severities and mitigate their effects, as only in this way can respect for the essential content of the fundamental right itself be ensured.” It is also not sufficient for the establishment of such adequate and specific guarantees for the law to refer to the GDPR itself or to the LOPDGDD.

Furthermore, such law must respect, in any case, the principle of proportionality, as recalled by the Judgment of the Constitutional Court 14/2003, of January 28:
“In other words, in accordance with a reiterated doctrine of this Court, the constitutionality of any restrictive measure of fundamental rights is determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality. For the purposes that matter here, it is enough to recall that, to verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions: whether the measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, in addition, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and, finally, whether it is balanced or proportionate, in that it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test; SSTC 66/1995, of May 8 [RTC 1995, 66], F. 5; 55/1996, of March 28 [RTC 1996, 55], FF. 7, 8, and 9; 270/1996, of December 16 [RTC 1996, 270], F. 4.e; 37/1998, of February 17 [RTC 1998, 37], F. 8; 186/2000, of July 10 [RTC 2000, 186], F. 6).”

In the case of the draft law presented for report, it is clear that its purpose is to regulate, among other things, the processing of personal data that will ensure cleanliness in sports, preventing practices or methods contrary to such cleanliness, which involves sample taking; data analysis, processing of the same for procedures in laboratories; communication/international transfers to national and international sports authorities; opening, monitoring, and conclusion of authorization or sanctioning procedures, etc.

As the Agency has already stated in its Report 44/2020, the competent authorities for processing the corresponding legislative project should carry out the corresponding risk analysis, in accordance with Article 24 of the GDPR, so that taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller will apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with this Regulation.

There is no doubt that the processing activities foreseen in the draft bill that will be carried out by the Spanish State Agency for the Fight Against Doping in Sports (CELAD) could fall within the provisions of Article 35.1 GDPR: When a type of processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, particularly if it uses new technologies, by its nature, scope, context, or purposes, the data controller shall carry out, prior to the processing, an assessment of the impact of the processing operations on the protection of personal data. A single assessment may address a series of similar processing operations that present similar high risks, as the scope of the processing of health data of athletes, in the context of the policy of prevention and/or repression of doping, may entail a high risk for them (false positives, leakage of negative data, consequences of the treatments, etc.). This register must contain all the information indicated below: a) the name and contact details of the data controller and, where applicable, the joint controller, the representative of the controller, and the data protection officer; b) the purposes of the processing; c) a description of the categories of data subjects and the categories of personal data; d) the categories of recipients to whom the personal data have been or will be communicated, including recipients in third countries or international organizations; e) where applicable, the transfers of personal data to a third country or an international organization, including the identification of that third country or international organization and, in the case of the transfers indicated in Article 49, paragraph 1, second sentence, the documentation of adequate safeguards; f) where possible, the envisaged time limits for the deletion of the different categories of data; g) where possible, an overview of the technical and organizational security measures referred to in Article 32, paragraph 1 GDPR.

Therefore, it would not be sufficient to have only a substantive regulation in the draft bill of the different preventive or punitive activities against doping, but for each of the processing of personal data provided for in the law concerning special categories of data, the aforementioned aspects must be regulated. This requires a systematic approach (such as the RAT and the DPIA) to reflect in the law the criteria that allow considering the interferences in the fundamental right as legitimate.

The MAIN, therefore, does not incorporate the suggested systematic analysis, and the draft bill mentions the different processing of personal data regulated in the law in a non-systematic manner, but rather according to the Title or chapter addressed. Article 10, in relation to the type of doping controls, establishes that the controls may be carried out during the competition or out of competition. Paragraph 3 of this article states that “[t]he scope and manner of conducting both modalities of control will be determined by regulation, seeking an adequate reconciliation between the fundamental rights of athletes and the material resources of the State Agency Spanish Commission for the Fight Against Doping in Sport, particularly when it comes to conducting controls out of competition.” Referring the scope and manner of carrying out these controls to the regulation could imply an interference with the right to privacy, separate from the fundamental right to the protection of personal data as established in the CE, but both rights, according to Article 53.1 CE, require a law that regulates the exercise of both rights. At least one law must establish the general principles regarding the scope and manner of conducting both modalities and must not leave the absolute determination of such scope and manner of conducting the controls to the regulation, as in that case there would be no such law, and such interference with the fundamental right could be considered unconstitutional. The norm would have a blank referral for the determination of an element, consisting of the appropriate level of guarantees, which is essential to maintain the essential content of the affected fundamental right intact and thus be able to control compliance with the principle of proportionality.” The law must determine, without the need to refer to the regulatory norm, the essential requirements and guarantees so that such a blank referral to the regulation for the treatment of data it contains cannot be established.

Article 52 proclaims that the data and records of processing activities (sic) related to doping controls may be transferred, under the terms provided in Organic Law 3/2018 and the GDPR, to public or private international organizations of which Spain is a part and that participate in the fight against doping in the sports field, within the framework of the legally binding international commitments assumed by Spain, or for conducting statistics or research studies. In this regard, it should be noted that the reference here to records of processing activities (RAT) is not very clear. This wording seems to stem from the error of considering that when in LO 3/2013 its Article 54 referred to "data and files," it now corresponds to data and "RAT." The record of processing activities is a technical expression referring to a tool that aids the proactive responsibility of the data controller, established in Article 30 GDPR. And it is not applicable to what the drafter of the draft law seems to want to state in this Article 52. "RAT" is not equivalent to "file." "File" is defined in the GDPR as any structured set of personal data, accessible according to specific criteria, whether centralized, decentralized, or distributed functionally or geographically. Consequently, a file is only a structured set of data, not comparable to a "record of processing activities." Therefore, this expression should be removed from this article.

Furthermore, in the case that such transfers are cross-border, that is, beyond the Member States of the EU or the EEA, this entails the subjection, as it cannot be otherwise, of such transfers to the provisions established regarding this in the GDPR and the LOPDGDD concerning transfers to third countries or international organizations (Chapter V of the GDPR and Title VI of the LOPDGDD). Finally, it should be noted in this article (section 3) that in accordance with Article 9.2.j) GDPR, for health-related personal data to be processed for scientific or statistical research purposes based on Union or Member State law, such processing must be proportional to the objective pursued, essentially respect the right to data protection, and establish adequate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject, which the draft law does not mention at all.

Article 55 of the draft law, regarding inspection powers, establishes that such inspectors shall be authorized to take samples of products or biological samples, in order to verify compliance with the provisions of this law and the regulations for its development. The taking of biological samples, as it must necessarily be linked to the identification of the person from whom they are taken, constitutes processing of personal data. As such, this processing is subject to the rules governing the protection of personal data, so this power to take samples must be adopted in accordance with this regulation, which includes the right to information provided for in Article 13 GDPR, as has already been mentioned earlier in this Report.
