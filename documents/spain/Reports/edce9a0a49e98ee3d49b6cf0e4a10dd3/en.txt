The inquiry raises the issue of compliance with Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons in relation to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR) and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), concerning the condition that the consultant must hold in relation to the services provided to a third party.  

Specifically, it concerns services to Homeowners' Associations, which include reading, maintenance, and conservation of meters, and issuing water consumption settlements, and requests a ruling on whether, in relation to such service provision that involves the processing of personal data, the consultant should be considered a data controller or, on the contrary, a data processor.  

I  
First, it is necessary to refer to the provisions of the GDPR, which in its Article 4, paragraphs 7 and 8, defines the concepts of data controller and processor, respectively.  
7) "data controller" or "controller": the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the data controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States;  
8) "data processor" or "processor": the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller;  

As the Article 29 Data Protection Working Party (WP29) noted in its Opinion 1/2010 on the concepts of "data controller" and "data processor," the concept of controller was a functional concept aimed at the assignment of responsibilities, indicating that "The concept of 'data controller' and its interaction with the concept of 'data processor' play a fundamental role in the application of Directive 95/46/EC, as they determine who should be responsible for compliance with data protection standards and how data subjects can exercise their rights in practice. The concept of data controller is also essential when determining the applicable national legislation and for the effective exercise of the supervisory tasks conferred on data protection authorities."  

Furthermore, the aforementioned Opinion highlighted "the difficulties in implementing the definitions of the Directive in a complex environment where many hypothetical situations may involve the actions of controllers and processors, alone or jointly, and with varying degrees of autonomy and responsibility," and that "The Group acknowledges that the concrete application of the concepts of data controller and data processor is becoming increasingly complex. This is primarily due to the growing complexity of the environment in which these concepts are used and, in particular, to an increasing trend, both in the private and public sectors, towards organizational differentiation, combined with the development of ICT and globalization, which may give rise to new and difficult questions and, at times, diminish the level of protection for data subjects."  

However, at the present time, it must be taken into account that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to the protection of personal data, which is now based on the principle of "accountability" or "proactive responsibility," as repeatedly pointed out by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and is reflected in the Preamble of the LOPDGDD: "the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model based fundamentally on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment."  

Within this new system, it is the data controller who, through the instruments regulated in the GDPR itself, such as the record of processing activities, risk analysis, or data protection impact assessment, must ensure the protection of this right by complying with all the principles set out in Article 5.1 of the GDPR, adequately documenting all decisions made in order to be able to demonstrate compliance.

Likewise, based on the principle of proactive responsibility, directed essentially at the data controller, and in order to strengthen the protection of the affected parties, the GDPR has introduced new obligations that are enforceable not only against the controller but, in certain cases, also against the processor, who may be sanctioned in the event of non-compliance with these obligations. 
Regarding the need to clearly determine the responsibilities of each participant in the processing of personal data, Recital 79 of the GDPR reminds us that  
The protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, as well as the responsibility of controllers and processors, including with respect to the supervision by the supervisory authorities and the measures adopted by them, requires a clear attribution of responsibilities under this Regulation, including cases where a controller determines the purposes and means of processing jointly with other controllers, or where processing is carried out on behalf of a controller.
Taking the above into account, currently the Guidelines 07/2020 of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) on the concepts of controller and processor in the GDPR, starting from the premise that the concepts of controller and processor in the GDPR have not changed compared to Directive 95/46/EC and that, in general, the criteria on how to attribute the different roles remain the same (paragraph 11), reiterate that these are functional concepts, aimed at assigning responsibilities according to the actual roles of the parties (paragraph 12), which implies that in most cases, the specific circumstances of the case must be considered (case by case) based on their actual activities rather than the formal designation of an actor as "controller" or "processor" (for example, in a contract), as well as autonomous concepts, whose interpretation must be carried out under the European regulations on personal data protection (paragraph 13), and taking into account (paragraph 24) that the need for a factual assessment also means that the role of a data controller does not derive from the nature of an entity processing data but from its specific activities in a specific context, so that the same entity may act simultaneously as a data controller for certain processing operations and as a processor for others, and the qualification as a controller or processor must be evaluated with respect to each specific data processing activity.

II
Having said the above, regarding the application of data protection regulations to Homeowners' Associations, the general criterion of this Agency has been that they are responsible for processing in their relationships with third-party service providers, and these, also generally and except for exceptions, will be considered processors.
This is deduced, for example, from the service provided by the Property Administrator contracted for this purpose. That is to say, when they act on behalf of the homeowners' associations, they are authorized to process and manage the data of the co-owners that are necessary for the ordinary management of the community's affairs, as they act in relation to the communities they serve as processors.
Likewise, the homeowners' associations regarding the processing of data of the co-owners are legitimized, for the purposes of the grounds set forth in the GDPR, in compliance with a legal obligation in accordance with the provisions of the Horizontal Property Law (LPH).
In this regard, it is worth mentioning Report 97/2017, which indicates
This Agency has been pointing out in its reports the status of the data controller held by homeowners' associations, as they carry out various processing of personal data to comply with the obligations imposed on them by Law 49/1960, of July 21, on Horizontal Property, as well as to ensure the proper exercise by the owners of the rights that correspond to them in the community. III
Taking the above into account, it is necessary to analyze the factual situation that arises from the consultation in relation to the criteria of the Guidelines 7/2020 of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) and the specific processing involved in the provision of the service.
Regarding the analysis of specific treatments, Report 64/2020 indicates that in any case, the legal relationship established between the parties must be analyzed carefully and in depth in order to identify who determines the purposes and means, for which the repeatedly cited EDPB Guidelines provide different criteria that can serve to establish such positions, starting from the fact that the word "determine" implies actually exercising influence over the purposes and means, for which it is not an obstacle that the service is defined in a specific way by the processor, as long as the controller is presented with a detailed description and can make the final decision on how the processing is carried out and can request changes if necessary, without the processor being able to subsequently introduce modifications to the essential elements of the processing without the approval of the controller (paragraph 28) or that a certain margin of maneuver is recognized for the processor to make some decisions regarding the processing (paragraph 35), allowing the processor to make decisions about non-essential means (paragraph 39), so that the processor must not process the data in any way other than in accordance with the instructions of the controller, without prejudice to the fact that such instructions may leave a certain degree of discretion on how to best serve the interests of the controller, allowing the processor to choose the most appropriate technical and organizational measures (paragraph 78).
Furthermore, another criterion to consider is whether the entity involved in the processing does not pursue any own purpose in relation to the processing but is simply paid for the services rendered, since, in this case, it would act, in principle, as a processor rather than as a controller (paragraph 60).
Therefore, although as noted in our Report 11/2020, the figure of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, the EDPB reminds that not all service providers that process personal data in the course of providing a service are a "processor" in the sense of the GDPR, since it does not depend on the nature of the entity that is processing the data, but on its specific activities in a specific context, so that if the processing does not constitute a key element of the service, the service provider may be in a position to independently determine the purposes and means of that processing required to provide the service, in which case it may be considered a controller and not a processor, and, conversely, but reiterating that the EDPB still considers it necessary to conduct a case-by-case analysis to determine the degree of influence that each party has.

Well, the first aspect to highlight is that the Community of Property Owners decides to contract the service and, based on that premise, exerts a determining influence on the processing.
Paragraph 24 of the Guidelines indicates that
In the absence of control derived from legal provisions, the qualification of a party as a data controller must be established based on an evaluation of the factual circumstances surrounding the processing. All relevant factual circumstances must be taken into account to reach a conclusion about whether a particular entity exerts a determining influence over the processing of the personal data in question.
Indeed, the influence arises because it chooses or determines the purpose of the processing, has agreed through the corresponding agreements that the reading of meters and cost allocators is a service it will contract, and that involves the communication of a list of personal data of the neighbors for the corresponding reading, and once this has occurred, it will receive the individual measurements and subsequently, according to certain criteria, proceed to collect them. There is therefore no doubt about the influence that the Community has on the processing of data made evident.
Secondly, in relation to the contracting of the service, its maintenance, and subsequent modification, paragraph 29 of the Guidelines 7/2020 tells us that
According to the factual approach, the word “determines” means that the entity that actually exerts influence over the purposes and means of processing is the controller. The aforementioned Guidelines provide the example of hiring a law firm to conclude that it will be considered responsible for the processing, because the processing is not the key to the service, but rather that data may be processed as part of the contracted service, although it is not fundamental, and that the law firm will act with a significant degree of independence, for example, when deciding what information to use and how to use it, and there are no instructions from the client regarding the processing of personal data. Thirdly, the consultant does not establish direct relationships with the owners, in the sense that, without prejudice to the special characteristics that Communities of Owners have in relation to the co-owners, the service is billed by the consultant to the Community of Owners, which is the recipient of the provisions of the aforementioned Royal Decrees, in the case of the horizontal property regime and which decides to contract the service. In this regard, it is important to highlight that, if the entity provided the service, for example, to a private individual, a single-family home not subject to the LPH would be considered responsible for the processing, just as indicated in report 64/2020 regarding the case of a private individual going to Correos to have a specific service rendered.
Another aspect that deserves analysis is the contract under which the service subject to analysis is governed. Regarding the contract, the Guidelines 7/2020 indicate in their section 27 that 
In many cases, an evaluation of the contractual conditions between the different interested parties can facilitate the determination of which party (or parties) acts as the controller. Even if a contract is silent on who is the controller of the processing, it may contain sufficient elements to infer who exercises a decision-making function regarding the purposes and means of the processing. It may also be that the contract contains an explicit statement about the identity of the controller. If there is no reason to doubt that this accurately reflects reality, there is nothing against what is stipulated in the contract. However, the conditions of a contract are not decisive in all circumstances, as this would simply allow the parties to assign the responsibility they deem appropriate. V
That said, it is necessary to remember that the status of data processor entails compliance with the obligations arising from the GDPR and the LOPDGDD. As indicated, the GDPR has introduced new obligations that are enforceable not only against the data controller but also, in certain cases, against the data processor, who may be sanctioned in the event of non-compliance.

In this regard, the Guidelines 07/2020 of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) on the concepts of data controller and processor in the GDPR make special reference (paragraph 91) to the obligation of the processor to ensure that persons authorized to process personal data have committed to respecting confidentiality or are subject to a statutory confidentiality obligation (Article 28, paragraph 3); to maintain a record of all categories of processing activities carried out on behalf of a controller (Article 30.2); to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk (Article 32); to appoint a data protection officer under certain conditions (Article 37); and to notify the data controller without undue delay of any personal data breaches of which it becomes aware (Article 33 (2)). Furthermore, the rules on transfers of data to third countries (Chapter V) apply to both processors and controllers. 

Therefore, the EDPB considers that Article 28 (3) of the GDPR imposes direct obligations on processors, including the obligation to assist the data controller in ensuring compliance.

Regarding compliance with the principles of processing set out in Article 5 of the GDPR, it is necessary to highlight the principles of purpose limitation and data minimization. 

Paragraph 1 b) states that data will be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and will not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; according to Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific and historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (“purpose limitation”);

Indeed, the consulting entity may not use the personal data to which it has access for purposes other than the provision of the service. In this regard, it is necessary to recall what is indicated in Article 33.2 of the LOPDGDD, which states that: 

A person will be considered a data controller and not a processor who, in their own name and without it being established that they act on behalf of another, establishes relationships with the affected parties even when there is a contract or legal act with the content set forth in Article 28.3 of the GDPR. 
(…) 
A person will also be considered a data controller who, while acting as a processor, uses the data for their own purposes. 

For its part, paragraph 1 c) states that personal data shall be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”); 

In the present case, it is necessary to highlight that in the contract provided, in clause 6 under the heading “Data Protection and Confidentiality Clause,” it is stated in paragraph 6.1 c) that the processor will process the following data: address, apartment, name of the owner, name of the payer, bank account, phone number, email.

Well, these seem to exceed the minimum necessary for the provision of the service as described by the consultant. That is to say, if the service provider only needs to know the consumption by households, it would be sufficient to either assign an identifier to each one or to know the data of the floor and door of the household. In conclusion, the processing of the data just indicated does not seem justified in relation to the service provided by the consultant in the terms reported to this Agency and, therefore, would be contrary to the principle of minimization.
