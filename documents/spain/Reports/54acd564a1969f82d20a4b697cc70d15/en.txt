The inquiry raises the issue of compliance with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (hereinafter GDPR) and with Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD), regarding access by third parties to information containing personal data of participants in public recruitment processes when one of the evaluation criteria is the condition of being a victim of gender-based violence.  

The inquiry differentiates the type of access, whether it is carried out by a participant in the process or by a third party unrelated to the process, such as even a member of the public corporation.  

I  
Prior to analyzing the issue raised, it should be noted that the applicable regulation to the public recruitment process is not indicated, that is to say, it is unknown whether we are dealing with state, regional, or municipal regulations, and specifically which regulation is being referred to, nor are the bases of the applicable call for applications known, nor the model of the application form for participation, for the purposes of analyzing the information regarding the processing of personal data that is offered to the participants.  

From the inquiry, it is necessary to analyze access to information that may be classified as personal data in a competitive bidding process, by interested parties and by third parties who are not interested.  

Furthermore, the inquiry requires an analysis of the nature of the data of victims of gender-based violence as deserving of special protection, to determine whether or not to grant them the protection established in Article 9 of the GDPR.

First, it is necessary to analyze whether the elements that have been taken into account when making a certain assessment in a competitive bidding procedure can be considered, in that context, personal data.  

Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines the concept of personal data, establishing that it considers as such “...any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person...”  

The Article 29 Working Party in its Opinion 4/2007 “On the concept of personal data” indicates the following:  
“The expression ‘any information’ used in the Directive clearly indicates the legislator's intention to give a broad meaning to the concept of ‘personal data’. This wording requires a broad interpretation. From the perspective of the nature of the information, the concept of personal data includes all types of statements about a person. Consequently, it encompasses ‘objective’ information such as, for example, the presence of a certain substance in their blood, but also ‘subjective’ information, opinions, or evaluations. This latter type of statement constitutes a considerable part of the personal data processed in sectors such as banking, to assess the reliability of borrowers (‘So-and-so is a reliable borrower’), insurance (‘So-and-so is not expected to die soon’), or employment (‘So-and-so is a good worker and deserves a promotion’).  

For such information to be considered ‘personal data’, it is not necessary for it to be true or proven. In fact, data protection regulations provide for the possibility that the information may be incorrect and grant the data subject the right to access that information and to contest it through appropriate means.  

From the perspective of the content of the information, the concept of personal data includes all those data that provide information regardless of the type of it. Of course, this includes personal information considered ‘sensitive data’ in Article 8 of the Directive due to its particularly...

Secondly, it is necessary to analyze whether the consideration of a victim of gender-based violence can be included within the "special categories of data," subject to the special protection offered by personal data protection regulations.
Regarding the conceptual determination of what is understood as special categories of data (previously sensitive data or specially protected), the evolution of the concept must be analyzed, from its origin to the present.
The Council of Europe’s Convention 108 of January 28, 1981, regulates as a specific category of data in its Article 6, under the heading "Particular categories of data," the following: "Personal data revealing racial origin, political opinions, religious beliefs or other beliefs, as well as personal data related to health or sexual life, shall not be processed automatically unless domestic law provides appropriate safeguards. The same rule shall apply in the case of personal data concerning criminal convictions."
The Judgment of the National Court of February 28, 2003, regarding the conceptualization of sensitive data, states that "the concept of sensitive data is included in Article 6 of Convention 108 of the Council of Europe (…) the Convention leans towards sensitive data per se, as inferred from point 43 of the Report where it states, 'although the risk that the processing of data poses to individuals depends, in principle, not so much on the content of the data themselves as on the context in which they are used, there are exceptional cases in which the processing of data can, as such, cause harm to the rights or interests of individuals'; that is to say, that the data is considered sensitive in and of itself, regardless of its context(…), which does not prevent there from being cases that present difficulties in determining whether they are specially protected or sensitive data and in which it is necessary to refer to the context to elucidate their conceptualization as such data, as will be referenced later.

In the present case, it is necessary to analyze the elements that allow for the accreditation of the condition of victim of gender-based violence, in the context raised by the inquiry, to determine whether such information can be subsumed under one of the categories established by Article 9 of the GDPR. 
Article 23 of the LVG establishes, under the heading "Accreditation of situations of gender-based violence," the following: 
The situations of gender-based violence that give rise to the recognition of the rights regulated in this chapter will be accredited through a conviction for a crime of gender-based violence, a protection order, or any other judicial resolution that agrees to a precautionary measure in favor of the victim, or by the report of the Public Prosecutor's Office indicating the existence of evidence that the claimant is a victim of gender-based violence. Situations of gender-based violence may also be accredited through reports from social services, specialized services, or shelters for victims of gender-based violence from the competent Public Administration; or by any other means, provided that it is foreseen in the sectoral regulatory provisions that govern access to each of the rights and resources.
Considering the above and to the extent that it is unknown what specific information is requested in the public procurement process referred to in the inquiry, it cannot be determined that the information that could arise from such a condition falls within any of the categories referred to in the aforementioned Article 9. 
This does not imply, as will be analyzed below, that the legal system grants ordinary protection, but rather that, given the particularly vulnerable nature of this group, formulas must be articulated to provide the maximum protection, since we are dealing with the scope and effectiveness of a fundamental right (Article 18.4 of the Constitution).

This implies that the interpretation of the applicable legal system must be carried out under the principle of the "greater value" of these rights. 
Regarding this principle, Francisco Fernandez Segado indicates that the objective nature of fundamental rights, which makes them essential elements of the community's legal order, in a basic decision that must inform our entire legal system, must necessarily influence the interpretation of rights and that of the legal system as a whole. 
In the opinion of the Constitutional Court (STC 66/1985, of May 23 (FJ 2.°)), the privileged place that fundamental rights and public freedoms occupy in the general economy of our Constitution is beyond doubt.
As a result, not only the unconstitutionality of all acts of power, whatever their nature and rank, that infringe them is established, but also the necessity to interpret the Law in the most favorable manner for maximizing its content (this is a constant jurisprudence of the High Court, as evidenced by, among many others, the following Judgments: 34/1983, of May 6 (FJ 3."); 67/1984, of June 7 (FJ 3.°); 32/1987, of March 10 (FJ 3.°); 117/1987, of July 8 (FJ 2.°), and 119/1990, of June 21 (FJ 4.").).
It is thus understood that the interpretation of legal provisions must be made in light of constitutional norms and especially those that proclaim and enshrine fundamental rights and public freedoms, with the interpretation that provides greater viability and strength to the fundamental right prevailing in case of doubt (STC 1/1989, of January 16 (FJ 3.").
It can therefore be affirmed that one of the constants of our constitutional doctrine is the reaffirmation of the hermeneutic principle favor libertatis, that is, the aforementioned principle that rights must be interpreted in the broadest possible manner.
But not only the aforementioned consideration of the extensive interpretation of a fundamental right should be cited here, but it is the ordinary legislator, and in their case the public authorities with normative capacity, who recognize the group of victims of gender-based violence as deserving of that special consideration.

VII
It is appropriate to address next the processing of personal data derived from access to the public contracting file of personnel. The inquiry presents two possibilities depending on whether the information that has been subject to evaluation is sought by a participant in the procedure in question (from which their status as an interested party derives) or a third party unrelated to it (distinguishing between a third party who is a member of the local corporation and an ordinary third party).
In both cases, the communication of information containing personal data of a participant in the public contracting process constitutes data processing, understood as such, in accordance with Article 4.2 of the aforementioned GDPR, as “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, limitation, erasure or destruction.”
All processing of personal data must be based on one of the legitimizing grounds provided for in Article 6 of the aforementioned Regulation, according to which:
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:
 a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes; 
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the implementation at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures;
 c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject; 
d) the processing is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person; 
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller; 
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that such interests do not override the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.
The provision in letter f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions.”
VIII
In relation to access to the administrative file by an interested party, it is advisable to first refer to Article 12.5 of the LOPDGDD which states the following:
When the applicable laws to certain processing establish a special regime affecting the exercise of the rights provided for in Chapter III of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the provisions of those laws shall apply.
In the case raised in the inquiry, the regulation of the right of access to the documentation contained in the file—according to Article 53 of the Common Administrative Procedure Law of Public Administrations (LPACAP hereinafter)—undoubtedly affects the rights provided for in Chapter III of the GDPR and Title III of the LOPDGDD.
Article 53.1 a) of the LPACAP establishes the right of the interested parties in the procedure:
Legal Office
  "To be informed, at any time, of the status of the processing of the procedures in which they have the status of interested parties; the meaning of the administrative silence that corresponds, in case the Administration does not issue or notify an express resolution within the deadline; the competent body for its instruction, if applicable, and resolution; and the procedural acts issued. Likewise, they shall also have the right to access and obtain copies of the documents contained in the aforementioned procedures."
Taking the above into account, it can be affirmed that the LPACAP recognizes in its article 53.1 a) the right of interested parties in a procedure to access the data contained therein, which may include data of other persons who may participate in the same procedure, and, consequently, the obligation of the Administration to provide such data.
It should be noted here that the effective exercise of any right implies the existence of obligations and duties for those who must guarantee such compliance; therefore, the translation in the field of data protection regulations of the right provided in article 53.1 LPACAP is a treatment that is mandatory for the responsible party, in this case, the administration responsible for the procedure.
Thus, the legal basis that legitimizes this treatment consisting of making the file available, with the personal data of the affected parties, will be that provided in article 6.1 c of the GDPR, which states that the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller;
Article 8 of the LOPDGDD under the heading "Processing of data by legal obligation, public interest, or exercise of public powers" establishes in its section 1 the following:
1. The processing of personal data may only be considered based on the fulfillment of a legal obligation enforceable against the controller, in the terms provided in article 6.1.c) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when such is provided for by a norm of European Union law or a norm with the rank of law, which may determine the general conditions of processing and the types of data subject to it as well as the transfers that may occur as a consequence of compliance with the legal obligation. Such a norm may also impose special conditions on the processing, such as the adoption of additional security measures or others established in Chapter IV of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
Therefore, the processing consisting of the communication of personal data to an interested party in the procedure to satisfy the right of access provided in article 53.1 a) LPACAP would find support in section 1.c) of article 6 GDPR, with the aforementioned LPACAP being the norm with the rank of law according to article 8.1 LOPDGDD.
However, it should be indicated that the purpose of such a right of access to the file is none other than to guarantee another fundamental right, such as the right to defense, as pointed out by the Supreme Court ruling of January 26, 2011, cassation 302/2010, regarding the identical right of access to the file established in article 35 of Law 30/1992, according to which "To decide this issue, it must first be stated that the right recognized in section a) of that so often repeated article 35 of the LRJ/PAC is certainly aimed at facilitating the right of defense, and what this means is to offer the interested party the possibility of knowing in an administrative procedure all the facts and data that may be relevant for the protection of their rights and interests that they wish to exercise by any means (that is, either administratively or judicially)."
Well, in the present case, where we are faced with a competitive bidding process, in which the participants obviously hold the status of interested parties in the same, they must be recognized the right established in article 53 of Law 39/2015.
However, this right is not absolute, as declared by the Supreme Court ruling referenced. The principles related to processing that the General Data Protection Regulation enshrines in its article 5.1 must be respected, particularly highlighting the one contained in its letter c) according to which "Personal data shall be: (...) c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ('data minimization')."
In this same sense, the ruling of the National Court of July 10, 2013 stated, "The right of access to administrative files and to obtain copies of the documents contained therein, legally recognized for interested parties, does not cover such access by any means or procedure, at the will of the interested party, nor the possibility of obtaining photocopies of all the documents it contains, but only in relation to those documents in which they show a legitimate interest and in the manner provided by the Administration, which must guarantee in all cases access to the information it contains in such a way that..."

shall not be diminished in their right to defense, which underlies the legitimate right of access to the file.
The expressed provision cannot be interpreted in an absolutely literal manner, so that any request at any time and whatever its content and scope must be immediately satisfied, since a systematic interpretation of it, in relation to Article 37.7 of the LRJPAC, leads to the conclusion that the exercise of such a right should not affect the effectiveness of the functioning of public services (in this sense, STS of May 30, 2007, Rec. 5077/2005) and should not undermine other rights deserving, likewise, of protection, as occurs with the fundamental rights to privacy and the protection of personal data.”
Consequently, access must be provided to those interested in a procedure to all the necessary data to guarantee their defense therein.
Now, Article 5.1 b) and c) of the GDPR enshrine the principles of purpose limitation and data minimization, stating that personal data shall be:
b) collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and shall not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (“purpose limitation”);
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”);
In accordance with the above, access by the interested third party to the documentation of the file must be articulated in ways that ensure compliance with the aforementioned principles.
For example, regarding the first, by clearly establishing and informing the recipient about the sole purpose for which access to the file is granted, and regarding the second, by establishing pseudonymization techniques, such as the one proposed by the inquirer to create within the call a category called “sensitive information criteria” or “special circumstances,” which encompasses the condition of being a victim of gender-based violence, etc., so that if access to the file is granted, it indicates that a score has been obtained within the mentioned category.
In similar terms, it was indicated in Report 147/2018 that (…) it is possible to limit access or the delivery of copies of the documentation contained in the file, for reasons of administrative efficiency or for the purpose of protecting the privacy of the other interested parties, to the data and relevant copies for the protection of the rights and interests of those requesting them. Likewise, they may not use the data to which they have access for a purpose other than that of defending their right in the procedure in question or subsequently in judicial proceedings. Thus, the use for other purposes, its communication to third parties, or its disclosure may constitute an infringement of the provisions of the LOPD.(…)
