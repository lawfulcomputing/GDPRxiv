The consultation raises the adequacy to Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR) and to Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), of the information request made by the Spanish Agency of Medicines and Medical Devices (AEMPS, hereinafter) to the consultant (CTAEX) regarding the registration of movements of cannabis derived from its activity within the "Research and Development Project of Cannabis sativa L. genotypes for the obtaining of cannabinoid extracts" authorized by the AEMPS. This registration may contain personal data and therefore must be carried out with sufficient legal coverage.  
CTAEX states that the AEMPS acts in the exercise of the powers attributed to it by Royal Decree 1275/2011, of September 16, which creates the state agency "Spanish Agency of Medicines and Medical Devices" and approves its Statute, and in accordance with the provisions of the current LOPDGDD in its article 8, the norm that attributes the competence and that will allow the processing of data must have the rank of law and not regulatory. Therefore, it questions the request for personal data due to lack of legal support and, secondly, raises whether such request would be in accordance with the principle of data minimization set forth in article c) of the GDPR.  

I  
Regarding the processing of personal data carried out by authorities, this Legal Office has had the opportunity to express itself on several occasions, with the general criterion being that what is indicated in article 6.1 c) or e) of the GDPR would apply depending on what is provided by the applicable norm to the data controller. (Report 74/2019).  
Article 6.1 of the GDPR states the following:  
The processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies: (…)  
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject; (…)  
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  

Recital 41 of the GDPR states that “When this Regulation refers to a legal basis or a legislative measure, this does not necessarily require a legislative act adopted by a parliament, without prejudice to the requirements of conformity with the constitutional order of the Member State in question. However, such legal basis or legislative measure must be clear and precise and its application foreseeable for its addressees, in accordance with the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter, 'Court of Justice') and the European Court of Human Rights.  
Recital 45 of the GDPR states that “When carried out in compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the controller, or if necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority, the processing must have a basis in Union or Member State law.”  

Now, the LOPDGDD establishes in its article 8 under the heading “Processing of data by legal obligation, public interest or exercise of public powers” the following:  
1. The processing of personal data may only be considered based on compliance with a legal obligation enforceable against the controller, in the terms provided in article 6.1.c) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when such is provided by a norm of European Union law or a norm with the rank of law, which may determine the general conditions of processing and the types of data subject to it as well as the transfers that may arise as a consequence of compliance with the legal obligation. Such norm may also impose special conditions on the processing, such as the adoption of additional security measures or others established in Chapter IV of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.  
2. The processing of personal data may only be considered based on compliance with a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred to the controller, in the terms provided in article 6.1 e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when it derives from a competence attributed by a norm with the rank of law.”

That is to say, the Spanish legislator has determined that the instrument which either imposes an obligation on the data controller that involves the processing of personal data, or which attributes public powers to the data controller that imply data processing, must be by a norm with the rank of law. 
It is necessary to analyze the legal regime of the lawful trafficking of narcotics and the powers that the AEMPS has in this regard. 
II
First of all, it is appropriate to cite Law 17/1967, of April 8, which updates the existing regulations on narcotics and adapts them to the provisions of the 1961 United Nations Convention, which, despite the passage of time since its approval, remains in force and is the first regulation on which the actions of the AEMPS in the case under study should be based. 
A joint analysis of several of its provisions is appropriate to interpret it in accordance with the current administrative organization, since obviously, in the year of its approval neither the form of the State was the same as it is now, nor was the administrative organization. 
Article One states the following:
The Spanish State has the right to intervene, within its territory, in the cultivation and production, manufacture and extraction, storage, transport and distribution, importation, exportation, and transit of raw materials and narcotic products, as well as their prescription, possession, use, and consumption. Likewise, the Spanish State has the right to prevent, pursue, and sanction acts that constitute an infringement or crime, as provided for in this legal regime.
Article Four states the following:
In the General Directorate of Health, and within its pharmaceutical services, the Narcotics Control Service will be established, with the duties specified in the following article, and a Technical Commission, of a consultative nature, whose composition and functions will be established by regulation.
As can be observed, a department of the State Administration is cited, the General Directorate of Health (which with that designation does not currently exist and presumably will not exist with the same powers), and the creation of a specific body is foreseen to carry out certain duties, the Narcotics Control Service, whose functions that must be indicated in relation to the subject of consultation are specified in the following provisions:
Article Five.
The duties of the Narcotics Control Service will be:
a) The authorization, intervention, supervision, and control of the cultivation, harvesting, storage, deposit, production, and manufacture of narcotic products or substances and their raw materials.
Article Seven.

III 
The AEMPS, in the requests it directed against the consultant, based its actions on the provisions of Article 7.27 of Royal Decree 1275/2011, of September 16, which creates the state agency "Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products" and approves its Statute, which attributes to it “Developing the state functions and responsibilities of inspection and control regarding the trafficking and lawful use of narcotics and psychotropic substances.” 
However, the legal provision for the creation of the current AEMPS was found in the Fifth Additional Provision of Law 28/2006 of July 18 on State Agencies for the Improvement of Public Services, which under the heading “Transformation of Public Bodies into State Agencies” authorized the Government to proceed with such transformation, and to do so by Royal Decree of the Council of Ministers at the initiative of the Minister in charge of the respective Public Body and at the joint proposal of the Ministers of Economy and Finance and Public Administrations. 
That is to say, the transformation and therefore the creation of the current AEMPS was already foreseen in a norm with the rank of law - the aforementioned law on agencies. 
Taking into account this authorization by a norm with the rank of law, it should be indicated that the current AEMPS replaces the autonomous body Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products (OAAEMPS) and subrogates and succeeds it, among other aspects, in all its competencies and functions. 
This is indicated by the first additional provision of the aforementioned Royal Decree 1275/2011, of September 16, under the designation “Abolition of the autonomous body Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products”: 
1. The autonomous body Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products is abolished. 
2. From the entry into force of this royal decree, the state agency that is created will be subrogated in all the assets, rights, and obligations of the abolished autonomous body and will succeed it in all its competencies and functions. 
3. References made by current regulations to the autonomous body Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products will be understood as references to the new state agency that is created.

IV
Another aspect that must be highlighted and is related to the succession of bodies of the State Administration is that the aforementioned Law 17/1967, of April 8, which updates the existing regulations on narcotics, attributed to the General Directorate of Pharmacy (DGF) through the Narcotics Control Service the competencies that are being analyzed in this report.
It should be noted that the aforementioned DGF, with the successive changes of government since that date and therefore, the restructuring of ministerial departments, had its functions assumed by the now-defunct General Directorate of Pharmacy and Health Products, and its express mention in Law 66/1997, of December 30, and in Royal Decree 520/1999, of March 26, should be noted for the purposes of completing the succession of competencies that has been highlighted in this report in favor of the current AEMPS.

V
Regarding the second issue raised, it pertains to whether the data requested by the AEMPS concerning the identification of the recipients listed in the accounting register complies with the principle of data minimization. The consultant raises the adequacy of this principle regarding the generic request for the list of all clients since January 1, 2020, regardless of the THC content, and the manner in which it should be provided, considering that CTAEX initially communicated it in an anonymized manner, that is, by adding an identifier, and that the AEMPS reiterated its request for the complete identification of each client. In response, CTAEX did not comply due to its implications related to data protection.

As a starting point, it should be indicated that both the GDPR and the LOPDGDD apply to the processing of data of natural persons (Article 1.1 GDPR) and, regarding legal entities, it applies to the individuals who represent them. Thus, in Report 4/2021, it was indicated that “legal entities are excluded from the application of the regulations on personal data, but their protective scope extends to the natural persons who represent them, whose personal data must be processed in accordance with the provisions of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.”

Therefore, if in the analyzed case the AEMPS requests the identification of clients listed in the register of “accounting laboratory sample services,” we are not dealing with natural persons, and thus the data protection regulations would not apply, and therefore, no exception could be raised or opposed by CTAEX related to a potential non-compliance with the GDPR or LOPDGDD. 

That is to say, CTAEX could not refuse to identify the clients on the pretext of not wanting to violate the data protection regulation, as it would not be applicable.

Having said this, it is appropriate to analyze the principles of purpose limitation and data minimization provided for in Article 5.1 b) and c) of the GDPR. Article 5.1 of the GDPR states that personal data shall be:
b) collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and shall not be further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes (…) (“purpose limitation”)

Taking the above into account, for data processing to be compliant with the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, it is not enough to comply with the principle of lawfulness, as analyzed previously, but the principles of purpose limitation and minimization must also be observed. 
Regarding the number of individuals who may be affected by the request, it should be noted here that the jurisprudence of the Constitutional Court is opposed to the mass communication of data to Public Administrations, as stated in its ruling 17/2013, dated January 31, 2013, in which it interprets Article 16.3 of the Local Government Act, relating to the communication of data from the municipal register, and whose conclusions are fully applicable to the present case. 
As interpreted by the Constitutional Court in that ruling (FJ 8), this provision refers to the non-consensual transfer of data related to residence or domicile to other public administrations that request it only in those cases where, for the exercise of their competencies, such data is relevant. In summary, this request, which does not specifically refer to the transfer of data from the register concerning foreign individuals, aims to obtain data related to the residence or domicile recorded in the municipal register, (...). Thus, in accordance with the Organic Law on data protection, the initial purpose that justified the collection of data by a public administration does not prevent the subsequent use of the data for purposes different from those that motivated its collection, while respecting, in any case, the principle of legal reservation to establish such a change, (...) the Local Government Act, in its capacity as a regulatory norm of a file such as the municipal register, may foresee data transfers between public administrations.
(...) the transferred data must be strictly necessary for the fulfillment of the functions assigned to the administrative bodies, so the request for those data that are relevant must be justified, as it is necessary to distinguish between the analysis and monitoring of an individualized situation related to a specific case and the generalized and indiscriminate provision of all the information contained in a personal record. The provision has contemplated both extremes in such a way that any transfer of data from the register must be based on the necessity by the transferring Administration acting in the exercise of its functions.

Furthermore, regarding the principle of proportionality, the Judgment of the Constitutional Court 14/2003, of January 28, reminds us of the following:
“In other words, in accordance with a reiterated doctrine of this Court, the constitutionality of any restrictive measure of fundamental rights is determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality. For the purposes relevant here, it is sufficient to recall that, to verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions: whether the measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, in addition, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and, finally, whether it is weighed or balanced, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict sense proportionality test; SSTC 66/1995, of May 8 [RTC 1995, 66], F. 5; 55/1996, of March 28 [RTC 1996, 55], FF. 7, 8, and 9; 270/1996, of December 16 [RTC 1996, 270], F. 4.e; 37/1998, of February 17 [RTC 1998, 37], F. 8; 186/2000, of July 10 [RTC 2000, 186], F. 6).”

From the above transcribed text, and from the rest of the legal reasoning contained in STC 17/2013, and from the principle of proportionality, it follows that the TC has determined that (i) indiscriminate and massive access to personal data must be avoided (ii) the data in question requested must be relevant and necessary (iii) for the purpose established in the provision (iv) the request for access to the specific personal data must be expressly motivated and justified, (v) in such a way that it allows for its control by the transferor (vi) and avoids a tortious use of that power with massive accesses. This implies (vii) that the possibility of analyzing whether in each specific case the access was supported by what is established in the law must be guaranteed.
