II
At the present time, regarding the matter of personal data protection, the regulations to which the draft submitted for consultation must comply are Regulation (EU) 2016/679, already cited (GDPR) and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD).
In this regard, it should be noted that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to personal data protection, which is now based on the principle of “accountability” or “proactive responsibility” as repeatedly pointed out by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and is included in the Explanatory Memorandum of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD): “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the data controller or processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the necessary measures based on that assessment.” Within this new system, it is the data controller who, through the instruments regulated in the GDPR itself, such as the record of processing activities, risk analysis, or data protection impact assessment, must guarantee the protection of this right by complying with all the principles set forth in Article 5.1 of the GDPR, adequately documenting all decisions made in order to demonstrate compliance.
Furthermore, based on this principle of proactive responsibility, directed essentially at the data controller, and in order to reinforce the protection of the affected individuals, the GDPR has introduced new obligations that are enforceable not only against the controller but also, in certain cases, against the processor, who may be sanctioned in the event of non-compliance.
 
Therefore, as the main addressees of the obligations set forth in the GDPR are the controllers and processors, their observance entails the necessity for the national legislator to adopt certain behaviors to ensure compliance with the GDPR and, ultimately, the adoption of sufficient guarantees for the protection of the fundamental right, particularly when it comes to processing of personal data legitimized as a result of compliance with legal obligations or for the fulfillment of a mission of public interest or the exercise of public powers.
In this regard, it should be recalled, as indicated in our Report 283/2017, that the processing of personal data necessary to verify the identity and circumstances of applicants for qualified certificates will be legitimized by Article 6.1 c) of the General Data Protection Regulation, which enables processing based on a legal obligation imposed by domestic law or European Union law.

III  
Analyzing the articulated text from the perspective of personal data protection, it is important to highlight the following:  
First, the lawfulness of the processing of personal data will require the existence of a legal basis that legitimizes the processing, taking into account that the GDPR, unlike what occurred in the previous regulation, has placed all legal bases on equal footing, so that consent is included as one of the six grounds for legitimization for processing without holding greater or lesser importance than the others, even regulating cases in which the consent of the affected party should not constitute the legal basis for processing.  
As the AEPD has been indicating since its report 65/2017, the GDPR has shifted the "principle of consent" as the central axis of the right to data protection, so that "it can no longer be said that there are exceptions to consent, but rather, since consent is one of the possible legitimate bases for processing, it will not be necessary to obtain it in cases where the processing is covered by any of the other grounds included in Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation."  
In the present case, the legal basis that legitimizes the processing, as previously indicated, is that provided for in letter c) of Article 6 of the GDPR (the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller), so that, for these purposes, it is not necessary to obtain the consent of the affected party.

For these purposes, it should be noted that the order develops Article 7.2 of Law 6/2020, as expressly stated in its Article 1, which refers to other identification methods distinct from physical presence, among which it mentions videoconferencing or video identification but does not exclude others, while the order is limited exclusively to the same, so the first thing that should be assessed is whether there are also other alternative means that could even be less invasive, such as through the remote verification of the information contained in the ID certificate itself or identification by a trusted third party.
On the other hand, within the modalities of remote video identification, Article 3 of the order states that “The remote video identification process may be carried out in an assisted manner, with the synchronous mediation of an operator, or in a non-assisted manner, without the need for online interaction between an operator and the applicant, with subsequent review by an operator.” Therefore, a non-assisted modality is accepted, which is much more invasive and will necessarily require the recording of images (as generally established by Article 11 of the order), which would require a specific assessment regarding, first of all, the necessity of such processing from the perspective of its proportionality, and in the event that it is deemed necessary, the adoption of reinforced guarantees from the perspective of personal data protection.

For these purposes, it should be emphasized that the processing of personal data
that may currently be taking place for the non-presential identification of individuals requires, in accordance with the principle of proactive responsibility, that the respective controllers have adopted and documented all necessary measures to ensure compliance with personal data protection regulations, through the appropriate risk analysis, DPIA, etc., identifying the legal bases that legitimize the processing and adopting all necessary guarantees in accordance with the principle of data protection by default and by design, thus, any non-compliance with said regulations shall be the sole responsibility of the respective controllers.
However, at the moment a legal norm regulates such processing, they will be obliged to apply said norm, so it will be necessary to determine beforehand that it complies with personal data protection regulations. This is particularly relevant if, as in the present case, it encompasses more invasive processing, such as non-assisted identification, and establishes obligations for specific processing, such as the obligation to fully record the identification process in all cases or, particularly, when it affects special categories of data, the obligation to carry out a biometric comparison, as provided for in Article 9.8.
For all these reasons, as pointed out at the beginning of this report, it is considered essential to carry out a detailed and thorough analysis of the implications that the approval of the norm has for the rights and freedoms of the affected parties, in the terms provided for in the GDPR and that have been indicated throughout this report, in the most complete manner possible within the urgency with which it has been requested, considering that this analysis is particularly relevant since the present order does not solely address the temporary situation arising from the Covid-19 pandemic, but aims to regulate these identification processes permanently, to eliminate, in the words of its MAIN, “the competitive disadvantage -derived from this regulatory gap- of trust service providers established in Spain compared to providers established in other EU Member States.”
Such analysis will allow for the correct identification of all measures and guarantees that must be adopted and, especially, those measures contemplated in the order that, due to not having a merely technical nature but regulating processing that may imply an interference with the fundamental right to personal data protection, would require a norm with the rank of law, such as those previously referred to the full recording of the identification process, in all cases, and the processing of biometric data aimed at the unique identification of the person.
For the reasons stated, a negative report is issued, pending the rectification of the observations made.
