The inquiry raises the applicability of data protection regulations to the system that the consulting entity intends to develop, consisting of the processing of the signal received by devices for capturing and analyzing Wi-Fi signals installed in certain stores, with the aim of understanding the routes and movements made by electronic terminals emitting said signal – “WiFi tracking.” This data processing would aim to detect the products that generate the most interest among customers for subsequent marketing actions.  

Before proceeding to issue this report, it should be noted that it can only analyze, based on the data provided by the consulting entity, the issues raised by it in general terms, without being able to exhaustively analyze the specific measures proposed. It is not the responsibility of this Agency to validate any type of processing in advance; it is the responsibility of the data controllers to ensure their compliance with personal data protection regulations, without prejudice to the exceptional cases in which prior consultation is appropriate and the investigative powers that correspond to it.  

I  
The first issue to be analyzed is whether the system described in the inquiry involves the processing of personal data as defined by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation -GDPR-), and therefore whether the indicated regulations should be applied.  

First of all, it should be noted that Article 4.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, defines personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  

In turn, according to the content of the “Definition” in point 2 of Article 4 of the GDPR, for there to be processing of personal data, it is required to carry out “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction.” For its part, the definition of “file” is contained in section 6 of Article 4, which refers to “any structured set of personal data, accessible according to specific criteria, whether centralized, decentralized, or dispersed in a functional or geographical manner.”  

Sections 1 and 2 of Article 2 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights -LOPDPGDD-, “Scope of application of Titles I to IX and Articles 89 to 94,” establish that:  
“1. The provisions of Titles I to IX and Articles 89 to 94 of this organic law apply to any total or partially automated processing of personal data, as well as to the non-automated processing of personal data contained or intended to be included in a file. (…)”  

As stated in the inquiry, it involves deploying a system of scanners inside facilities and establishments -either by utilizing the owner’s Wi-Fi infrastructure or implementing a new one- that detects all signals emitted by electronic devices when their Wi-Fi is turned on -WiFi tracking-. Thus, knowing which areas generate the most interest among customers, through Wi-Fi location, can allow for the creation of new marketing strategies or promotions for customers in order to enhance their market participation and position brands more competitively.

Well then, following the definition of “data processing” from Article 4 of the GDPR, it is inferred that only when the information containing personal data has been subjected to a prior dissociation procedure will the provisions of said Regulation not apply, thus nullifying its provisions regarding the “anonymized” information subject to processing. In summary, data protection regulations will not apply to processing subjected to a prior dissociation procedure, but will apply in the event that such dissociation does not occur.
Thus, in Recital (26) of the GDPR, the interpretation of what is to be understood as anonymized information is contained, clearly delineating this concept from that related to “pseudonymization” of data, namely:
“(26) The principles of data protection must apply to all information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Pseudonymized personal data, which could be attributed to a natural person by the use of additional information, must be considered information about an identifiable natural person. To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means, such as singling out, that may reasonably be used by the data controller or any other person to identify the natural person directly or indirectly must be taken into account. To determine whether there is a reasonable likelihood that means will be used to identify a natural person, all objective factors, such as the costs and time required for identification, must be considered, taking into account both the technology available at the time of processing and technological advancements. Therefore, the principles of data protection should not apply to anonymous information, that is, information that does not relate to an identified or identifiable natural person, nor to data rendered anonymous in such a way that the data subject is not identifiable, or ceases to be so. Consequently, this Regulation does not affect the processing of such anonymous information, including for statistical or research purposes.”

Conversely, Recital (28) of the GDPR interprets the effective application of data protection regulations to the processing of pseudonymized data, stating that “The application of pseudonymization to personal data can reduce risks to the affected data subjects and help controllers and processors meet their data protection obligations. Thus, the explicit introduction of ‘pseudonymization’ in this Regulation is not intended to exclude any other measures related to data protection.”

In light of what we have just indicated, for an anonymization procedure to be considered sufficient for the purposes of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, of April 27, 2016, it will be necessary that the application of such a procedure makes it impossible to identify a specific piece of data with its identified subject. In this sense, international provisions regulating the protection of personal data consider that the affected individual will not be determinable when their identification requires a disproportionate effort that makes it practically impossible to identify the natural person who is the owner of the personal data previously processed by the controller.

Notwithstanding the above, it is important to emphasize the distinction between both figures. Thus, while dissociation prevents the identification of the affected subject and places the information subject to processing outside the scope of data protection regulations, pseudonymization requires the adoption of necessary technical and organizational measures to ensure that the GDPR applies to the corresponding processing while keeping the additional information separate for the attribution of personal data to a specific person (see Recital 29 GDPR), in which case the data processing is subject to the regime of the GDPR.

Note that, even due to its own nature and regulatory fit—see Articles 25.1, 32.1 a) of the GDPR—pseudonymization constitutes in itself a technical and organizational security measure, which would involve the substitution of one unique identifier for another unique identifier, between which there is a biunique relationship, which could allow the profiling of identified or identifiable natural persons, especially if combined with data obtained from other sources. Recital 30 of the GDPR reminds us of this: “Natural persons can be associated with online identifiers provided by their devices, applications, tools, and protocols, such as internet protocol addresses, session identifiers in the form of ‘cookies’ or other identifiers, such as radio frequency identification tags. This can leave traces that, in particular, when combined with unique identifiers and other data received by servers, can be used to create profiles of natural persons and identify them.”

- Hash function: This is a function that returns a fixed-size result from an input value of any size (this input can consist of a single attribute or a set of attributes). This function is not reversible, meaning there is no risk of reversing the result, as is the case with encryption. However, if the range of input values for the hash function is known, these values can be passed through the function in order to obtain the actual value of a specific record. For example, if the hash function is applied to the national identification number to pseudonymize a dataset, that attribute can be obtained simply by running the function with all possible input values and comparing the results with the values of the dataset. The use of a "salted" hash function (in which a random value, known as "salt," is added to the attribute to which the hash function is applied) can reduce the likelihood of obtaining the input value. In summary, from what is established in the General Data Protection Regulation, it follows that, as soon as it applies and at least from the time it comes into force, both the anonymization and pseudonymization of personal data will entail the existence of two successive processes: the one that involves the actual anonymization or pseudonymization based on the personal data held by the controller and the one that is subsequently carried out with the already anonymized or pseudonymized data. The difference between both scenarios will lie in the fact that while data protection regulations will not apply to this second processing if the data has been anonymized, they will be applicable in the event that only pseudonymization has occurred.

This difference will also affect the balancing required by Article 6.1(f) regarding the first processing carried out, given that if the anonymization is complete, making it impossible to link the information directly or indirectly to a specific data subject, the impact of the processing on the rights and interests of that individual will be substantially lower than in the case where the data can still identify them if the pseudonymization process is reversed.

Even this impact will be even lower if the anonymization involves, from its origin, an aggregation of personal data from a specific universe of affected individuals, since, from that moment on, if the aggregation mechanisms are adequate, it would be impossible to disaggregate from the aggregated data the information related to a specific subject.

This means that in the proposed scenario, if the result of the processing were indeed the obtaining of anonymous data or, even more so, if the resulting data is aggregated, in such a way that it cannot, in the terms mentioned in Recital 26 of the Regulation, be reassociated with a specific affected individual, the minimal impact that could exist on the rights of the affected individuals as a consequence of applying an anonymization or aggregation procedure to their data could yield to the legitimate interest that could justify that processing, which, in the terms of the consultation, would be the development by the entity of new products or services. In our previous reports -0310/2010 and 0060/2011, both dated June 3, 2011, 0149/2014, dated July 30, 2014, and 0175/2015, dated March 3, 2016-, it is recalled that the right to data protection constitutes a fundamental right, and therefore, before adopting a restrictive measure regarding it, such as the data processing referred to in the inquiry, the proportionality of such a measure must be analyzed. 
Currently, Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR enshrines the “principle of data minimization,” meaning that only those personal data processing activities that are adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which such data are processed are protected.
In this regard, it is pertinent to bring to attention what the Constitutional Court stated in its Judgment 186/2000, in which it declared that no fundamental right is absolute “and may yield to constitutionally relevant interests, provided that the limitation it undergoes is necessary to achieve the legitimate purpose intended, proportionate to achieve it, and, in any case, respects the essential content of the right" (SSTC 57/1994, FJ 6, and 143/1994, FJ 6, among others).
From this, it follows that the processing of a specific personal data, in this case, location data, must be proportionate to the purpose that motivates it. Thus, if such a purpose could be achieved by carrying out an activity different from the aforementioned processing, without altering or harming that purpose, the latter activity should be chosen, given that the processing of personal data implies, as our Constitutional Court enshrines in Judgment 292/2000, dated November 30, a limitation of the individual's right to control information related to themselves.
From the terms in which the inquiry has been formulated, we can only conclude that the processing of the MAC -as a unique and permanent identifier of a device- constitutes the processing of personal data, and therefore must comply with this regulation. In Opinion 6/2014, of April 9, on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller, from the working group established by Article 29 of Directive 95/46/EC -WP 217-, various guidelines and orientations are incorporated regarding the concurrence of "legitimate interest," as well as the necessary safeguards in light of the respect and guarantee of the rights of the affected parties by this type of processing. 

Within the framework of these guarantees, the requirement of the "balancing test" between the legitimate interest of the data controller or any third parties to whom the data is communicated and the interests or fundamental rights of the interested party stands out. 

The analysis inherent to the "balancing test" requires the complete consideration of a series of factors, in order to ensure that the interests and fundamental rights of the affected parties are duly taken into account. At the same time, it is a modifiable test, which can vary from simple to complex. The factors that must be considered when conducting this balancing test will include: 
- The nature and source of the legitimate interest, and whether the processing of data is necessary for the exercise of a fundamental right, is of public interest, or benefits from the recognition of the affected community;
- The impact on the interested party and their reasonable expectations regarding what will happen to their data, as well as the nature of the data and the manner in which it is processed; 
- The additional safeguards that could limit undue impact on the interested party, such as data minimization, privacy protection technologies, increased transparency, the general and unconditional right to voluntary exclusion, and data portability. 

Therefore, only in cases where, as a result of the balancing test, the interests and fundamental rights of the affected parties do not prevail, may processing be carried out on the legal basis of Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR. 

On the other hand, an adequate assessment of the balance under the "balancing test" – sometimes facilitating the possibility of voluntary exclusion from processing – may lead to other alternatives or advise resorting to other legal bases grounded in "consent" or the execution of a "contract."

Article 39 - Functions of the Data Protection Officer - 
1. The Data Protection Officer shall have at least the following functions: 
a) to inform and advise the controller or the processor and the employees involved in the processing of the obligations that they have under this Regulation and other data protection provisions of the Union or the Member States; 
b) to supervise compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, other data protection provisions of the Union or the Member States, and the policies of the controller or the processor regarding personal data protection, including the allocation of responsibilities, awareness-raising and training of staff involved in processing operations, and the corresponding audits; 
c) to provide advice as requested regarding the data protection impact assessment and to supervise its application in accordance with Article 35; 
d) to cooperate with the supervisory authority; 
e) to act as a contact point for the supervisory authority on issues related to processing, including prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and to consult, where appropriate, on any other matter. 
2. The Data Protection Officer shall perform their functions with due regard to the risks associated with processing operations, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing." 

Consequently, the consulting entity must have the services of a Data Protection Officer to inform, advise, and supervise them in compliance with personal data protection regulations and to serve as an interlocutor with data protection authorities.

VIII
To determine that the measures and guarantees adopted ensure that the processing is in compliance with personal data protection regulations, the controller must take into account, as established by Article 24 of the GDPR, the scope, context, and purposes of the processing and the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Based on the risk analysis and the DPIA, they must adopt all appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the Regulation, including the corresponding security measures.

Regarding the latter, Article 32 of the GDPR does not establish a closed list of security measures that the controller and the processor must adopt, such that by adopting them, they would have fulfilled their obligations regarding security. Instead, under the principle of proactive responsibility, it establishes that “taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the varying probabilities and severity of risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk,” which may include, among others:
“a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;
d) a process for regular verification, evaluation, and assessment of the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of the processing.”

Consequently, it is the responsibility of the data controller and, where applicable, the processor, advised, if necessary, by the DPO, to determine, within the risk management framework for the rights and freedoms established in Article 24, all measures and guarantees, including security measures, necessary for the processing of personal data that is intended to be carried out, and it is not the responsibility of this Agency to rule on them, except in the exceptional case that it is necessary to submit a prior consultation when the DPIA shows a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals after having applied measures to mitigate it, and without prejudice to the investigative powers that correspond to it.
