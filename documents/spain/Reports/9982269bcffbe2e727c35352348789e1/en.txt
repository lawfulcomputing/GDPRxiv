The consulting party understands that the processing of data of its clients for marketing, advertising, and commercial communication purposes, in line with the development of the business it conducts for its own products through its Distance Selling Division, is covered by the legitimate interest provided and regulated in Article 6.1(f) of the General Data Protection Regulation, taking into account the reasonable expectations of the data subjects (consisting of continuing to receive the commercial communications that were sent to them until the application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679), and based on the prior relationship with our company, requesting confirmation of the feasibility of using this avenue of legitimization.  

I  
To adequately respond to the inquiry raised, it is necessary to differentiate the cases in which commercial communications are made by electronic means from those in which other means are used.  
In the first case, the sending of commercial communications by electronic means is regulated by Article 21 of Law 34/2002, of July 11, on Information Society Services and Electronic Commerce, whose paragraph 1 clearly states that “The sending of advertising or promotional communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means that have not been previously requested or expressly authorized by the recipients is prohibited.” This rule would only be exempted in the case of an existing contractual relationship and “provided that the provider has lawfully obtained the contact details of the recipient and uses them to send commercial communications regarding products or services of its own company that are similar to those that were initially the subject of the contract with the client,” as established in the first paragraph of paragraph 2 of the provision.  
This provision was amended by Law 32/2003, of November 3, General Telecommunications Law as a consequence of the transposition of Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of July 12, 2002, concerning the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on privacy and electronic communications). This Directive introduced the principle of “opt-in” throughout the European Union, that is, the need to obtain the prior consent of the recipient for sending emails for commercial purposes. Thus, any sending of advertising or promotional communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means is subject to the prior provision of consent, unless there is a prior contractual relationship and the subject does not express their opposition.  

This means that in the matter at hand, the principle of specialty applies, such that the legitimizing grounds for the processing of data referred to in Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation, which generally regulates the processing of personal data, must yield to the special rule contained in Law 34/2002 for these specific treatments, since otherwise, this latter regulation would be rendered devoid of content.  

In this regard, it is appropriate to bring to attention what was stated in the report of this Agency number 195/2017:  

This requires, before resorting to the applicability of Article 6.1(f) of the General Data Protection Regulation, that consideration be given to the application to the case, when communications are carried out by electronic means, of what is established in Law 34/2002, of July 11, on Information Society Services and Electronic Commerce, whose Article 21 provides as follows:  

“1. The sending of advertising or promotional communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means that have not been previously requested or expressly authorized by the recipients is prohibited.  

2. The provisions of the previous paragraph shall not apply when there is a prior contractual relationship, provided that the provider has lawfully obtained the contact details of the recipient and uses them to send commercial communications regarding products or services of its own company that are similar to those that were initially the subject of the contract with the client.  

In any case, the provider must offer the recipient the possibility to oppose the processing of their data for promotional purposes through a simple and free procedure, both at the time of data collection and in each of the commercial communications sent to them.  

When the communications have been sent by email, this means must necessarily consist of the inclusion of an email address or another valid electronic address where this right can be exercised, and the sending of communications that do not include this address is prohibited.”

Law 34/2002 constitutes a special regulation in relation to these activities, which means that the provisions of the General Data Protection Regulation cannot be invoked to resolve the issue raised in this point, but rather the stipulations of this special regulation must be taken into account when communications are carried out through electronic means.

This Agency is certainly aware of the existence of a proposed Regulation on the respect for private life and the protection of personal data in the electronic communications sector, which repeals Directive 2002/58/EC (Regulation on Privacy and Electronic Communications), whose Article 16 would refer to unsolicited communications. However, aside from the fact that this provision also starts from the principle of consent with an exception similar to that contained in the reproduced Article 21.2, it is necessary to point out that, even though it is intended for this Regulation to apply on the same date as the full applicability of the General Data Protection Regulation, it is currently just a proposal for a provision whose final content cannot yet be known, so the currently applicable regulation must be adhered to.

Thus, the aforementioned Article 21 operates as a limit that must be observed in all cases when marketing or advertising actions are carried out through electronic means, as it establishes the general rule of the express consent of the interested party for their execution, unless such actions refer to “products or services of their own company that are similar to those that were initially contracted with the customer.”

This means that the new regime of the General Data Protection Regulation will be irrelevant regarding these actions, which would also not affect the factual assumptions analyzed by the consultation, since, as required by Law 34/2002, the express consent of the customers is necessary, with the only exception mentioned. Therefore, it has not been possible, under the currently applicable regime, to carry out this type of actions based on consent obtained in accordance with the procedure provided for in Article 14 of the development regulation of Organic Law 15/1999, which is also not applicable.

III

In another case, that is, when communications are made by non-electronic means, it will be necessary for the processing to be based either on the provision of new consent that complies with the requirements established in the General Data Protection Regulation or on the application of Article 6.1 f) of the Regulation, according to which processing may take place, in the private sector, if it "is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that those interests do not prevail over the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject requiring the protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child."

The recitals of the Regulation also provide certain interpretative elements regarding the application of the rule contained in the provision just transcribed.

Thus, Recital 47 recalls that the legitimate interest of a controller, an assignee, or a third party "may constitute a legal basis for processing, provided that the interests or the rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail, taking into account the reasonable expectations of the data subjects based on their relationship with the controller," adding subsequently that "in any case, the existence of a legitimate interest would require a meticulous assessment, including whether a data subject can reasonably foresee, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that processing for such purpose may occur. In particular, the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject could prevail over the interests of the data controller when personal data is processed in circumstances where the data subject does not reasonably expect further processing to take place."

Furthermore, the General Data Protection Regulation lists some scenarios that may be taken into consideration to determine the applicability of this rule.

Thus, firstly, it is noted that legitimate interest "could arise, for example, when there is a relevant and appropriate relationship between the data subject and the controller, such as in situations where the data subject is a customer or is in the service of the controller." Likewise, in the recital itself, it states that "the processing of personal data strictly necessary for the prevention of fraud also constitutes a legitimate interest of the controller."

Indeed, if the regulations governing privacy in electronic communications establish a particularly strict regime for obtaining the consent of the interested party, and exempt from such consent the case concerning communications related to “products or services of their own company that are similar to those initially contracted with the client,” it can be inferred that this rule would be applicable by analogy to cases where such requirements are less stringent; that is, to actions carried out through other communication channels.

Now, for such weighing to be carried out in favor of the responsible entity, it will be necessary to make a reasonable interpretation of what should be considered a product or service similar to that previously contracted by the client, so that the authorization that could be covered by the rule being referenced should also be linked to the nature of the products and services previously contracted, not extending to those for which a logical identification based on the reasonable expectation of the client cannot be applied.

In this way, there would be no doubt that it would be possible to offer other products related to savings or credit, but it would be necessary to establish a first restrictive analysis when the advertising action referred to services that could fit within the broad concept of “financial services,” as would be the case with insurance. Finally, the weighing we are referring to would not operate when it comes to advertising or offering products or services that are not related to the activity of the entity, but rather when the advertising action arises from the existence of a specific agreement with the advertiser to which the advertising refers or affects non-financial products or services but offered by group companies or those in which the entity has a stake.

On the other hand, the weighing just carried out would be applicable to cases where the interested party maintains a relationship with the entity, without affecting those in which the client has ceased that relationship.
