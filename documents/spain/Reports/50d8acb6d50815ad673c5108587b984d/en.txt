The inquiry raises the question of whether, from the perspective of Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (GDPR) and the Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), the express authorization of a participant in a selection process for the Local Police of the consulting municipality is sufficient for the results of the medical examination to be made available to the Qualifying Tribunal, beyond the assessment of FIT or NOT FIT, and specifically to know the content of the evaluated assessment, its height measurement.  

The local corporation, through a minor contract, as provided for in Law 9/2017 of November 8, on Public Sector Contracts, contracted with a company for the performance of the third medical examination in the selection process in question, and this company sent the results of the medical examination of 13 candidates, specifically regarding the affected individual with the result of FIT. The local corporation understands that the company awarded the minor contract may have breached said contract (Article 211 LCSP) by not providing the complete content of the evaluated assessment, and has requested a report from the State Public Procurement Advisory Board. It asserts that the authorization granted by the participants in the selection process encompasses the complete content of the evaluation.  

For its part, the company has stated that this authorization is insufficient, as to communicate the complete content of the evaluated assessment, the express consent of the affected individual is necessary, since for these purposes, the authorization granted by the affected individual refers only to "the result," that is, whether they are FIT or NOT FIT, and to communicate the complete content of the medical test, express consent granted for that purpose is necessary.  

For all the above, a report is requested from this Agency, regarding whether the authorization of the affected individual is sufficient for the local corporation to know the content of the medical evaluation conducted by the contracted company.

Article 4.11 GDPR states that for the purposes of the Regulation, "consent of the data subject" shall mean:

Article 7 GDPR states:  
1. When processing is based on the consent of the data subject, the controller shall be able to demonstrate that the data subject has consented to the processing of their personal data.

In accordance with the above, the first conclusion reached is that consent must be given freely, which implies that the choice of the data subject is not conditioned by various factors, such as an imbalance between them and the data controller, or that they suffer a detriment in case of not providing it. There must also be the possibility of revoking consent without undermining the situation of the affected party.
Taking the above into account, the articulation of the method of obtaining consent for these purposes derived from the selective process in question must be analyzed.
Both in the general bases of the selective process and in the participation request, it is observed that it is intended to obtain such consent through an authorization system.
In the general bases, it is stated in section 6.2 under the heading Third Test, medical examination. This aims to verify compliance with the conditions required in the medical exclusion table (Annex III). Candidates will be classified as fit or unfit.
The performance of the medical examination implies the consent of the candidates for the results of the medical examination to be made available to the Qualifying Tribunal for the stated purposes and to serve as a basis for the evaluation and grading of the same.
In the participation request, it is stated that I expressly authorize the results of the medical examination to be made available to the Qualifying Tribunal. In this case, there is no designated space for marking this consent.
From the formal and circumstantial analysis of these elements, it follows that by merely participating in the selective process, consent is granted for the processing of health data, without providing the candidate with the possibility of not giving such consent or even revoking it at a later time. It is deduced that if consent is not given, a person cannot be admitted as a candidate in the selective process.
Therefore, it can be asserted that the granting of consent lacks the characteristic of having been given freely and thus, neither the assumption of lifting the prohibition of Article 9.2 a) nor the legal basis of Article 6.1 a) of the GDPR applies to this processing, nor to that carried out by the medical service responsible for conducting the medical test, nor to a supposed communication of the complete content of the medical examination to the Qualifying Tribunal.
In conclusion, requesting participation in a selective process, of any kind, entails the candidate's adherence to the rules governing that process. However, such participation cannot be interpreted as consent in the field of data protection, which can be given freely, either totally or partially, or with conditions, or even revoked for certain matters, since the regulatory rules of the process do not allow for such circumstances if one wishes to continue in the selective process.
Thus, consent, from the perspective of the right to data protection, will be inadequate to legitimize the processing of health data.
However, this circumstance does not imply per se that health data cannot be processed, but rather that another assumption must be found that exceptions the prohibition set forth in Article 9.1 of the GDPR and that is applicable as analyzed in subsequent sections.

Indeed, participation in a selection process is a legal act from which a legal relationship obviously arises between the candidate and the convening administration. As such, in addition to being regulated by the applicable norms due to the subject matter and specialty, it must be governed by the general principles of law provided in the Civil Code and reflected in a very extensive jurisprudence, among which the principle of good faith (Article 7.1 CC) and the doctrine derived from it regarding the prohibition of acting against one's own acts should be highlighted.

When participation in a selection process is requested, it shows the acquiescence to submit to its rules, that is, to the bases of the process, which on one hand, turn out to be the rules of the game, the law of the contract or the legal relationship that has just been perfected, and on the other hand, if they have not been challenged within the legally established deadlines, they become firm and unassailable, such that their content (except in cases of nullity of full right, STS No. 1040/2019 of July 19) cannot be disputed in any way once the result of the selection process is known.

This is reflected in the jurisprudence (for example, the Judgment of the Supreme Court, Third Chamber, of March 8, 2006, Rec. 6077/2000) stating that "the bases of the call for a competition or selection tests constitute the law to which the procedure and resolution of the same must be subject, in such a way that, once firm and accepted, they bind both the participants and the Administration, as well as the Courts and Commissions responsible for evaluating the merits, and can only be modified in accordance with the provisions established in Law 30/1992." This approach had its normative reflection in Article 3 of Royal Decree 1411/1968, of June 27, which approved the General Regulation for Admission to the Public Administration, and was embodied in Articles 13.4 and 5 of the Regulation approved by Royal Decree 364/1995, of March 10, which implies that the selection procedure must conform to the provisions contained in the bases.

Considering the binding nature arising from the legal relationship, it is not lawful to prevent the consequences derived from its execution from occurring, neither by the candidate nor by the convening administration (the parties in this contract). Among those consequences, the treatment of health data deserves to be highlighted, as it would contravene the doctrine of the prohibition of acting against one's own acts, which informs any legal relationship, derived from the principle of good faith (Article 7.1 Civil Code).

The Supreme Court, through extensive jurisprudence, establishes the bases, requirements, and content of this rule. Thus, "it is a reiterated doctrine of...

This Chamber holds that the general principle of law which asserts the inadmissibility of acting against one's own acts constitutes a limit on the exercise of a subjective right or a power, as a consequence of the principle of good faith and, particularly, the requirement to observe, within legal transactions, a coherent behavior, provided that the presupposed requirements that such doctrine demands for its application are met, which are that the own acts must be unequivocal, in the sense of creating, defining, fixing, modifying, extinguishing, or clarifying without any doubt a specific legal situation affecting its author, and that there exists an incompatibility or contradiction between the previous conduct and the current claim according to the meaning that in good faith should be attributed to the previous conduct." 
“This Chamber has been requiring, for the so-called own acts to be binding, that they cause a state, unalterably defining the legal situation of their author, or that they are aimed at creating, modifying, or extinguishing some right opposed to itself (Supreme Court rulings, among many others, of July 27 and November 5, 1987; June 15, 1989; January 18 and July 27, 1990), in addition to the fact that the act must be endowed with certain solemnity, be express, unambiguous, and perfectly delineated, unequivocally defining the intention and situation of the one who performs it (Supreme Court rulings of September 22 and October 10, 1988), which cannot be predicated of the situations where there is error, ignorance, mistaken knowledge, or mere tolerance” (for all, Supreme Court ruling 30/10/1995).
Therefore, it must be concluded that the participant in a selective process for public service freely establishes a legal relationship with the administration, and thus, undoubtedly assumes the consequences of the acts that derive from such relationship, among which is submitting to the exercises and tests provided in the bases that regulate the process and which involves the processing of personal data. 
This conclusion can be explained, for mere dialectical purposes and not to lift the prohibition under Article 9.2 of the GDPR, from the perspective of the essential core of the assumption provided in Article 6.1 b) of the GDPR, which legitimizes the processing of data when it is necessary for the execution of a contract, that is, for a legal relationship. In this case, once the legal relationship derived from the signing of the opposition has been accepted, the processing of data that derives from it is inherent and necessary to carry out the aforementioned relationship.
To this, concepts such as the foreseeability of the processing and the (subjective and objective) expectation of privacy must be added, which converge in the candidate regarding the processing of their personal information - including health information - derived from their participation in the selective process according to rules that expressly include such types of processing and which have been voluntarily accepted.
V
It is appropriate to address next and jointly the assumptions provided in sections b) and h) of Article 9.2 of the GDPR, as we are faced with the potential emergence of an employment relationship since, although public service is not governed, in purity, by labor regulations but by the statutory administrative law of public officials, the interpretation derived from it shares, in general terms, the same legal nature.
The psychophysical or medical aptitude test in selective processes serves the purpose of verifying the existence in the candidate of requirements that are demanded according to the job to be performed, that is, they aim to evaluate the worker's labor capacity and, therefore, their suitability for professional exercise.
Section b) requires that this processing be authorized by the law of the Union of the Member States or a collective agreement in accordance with the law of the Member States that establishes adequate guarantees for the respect of fundamental rights and the interests of the data subject, and section h) requires that guarantees be offered when the processing is carried out by a professional subject to the obligation of professional secrecy, or under their responsibility, in accordance with the law of the Union or of the Member States or with the rules established by the competent national bodies, or by any other person also subject to the obligation of secrecy in accordance with the law of the Union or of the Member States or the rules established by the competent national bodies.

Regarding section g) and the essential public interest that must characterize the processing of health data, specifically that derived from psychophysical fitness medical tests, it should be noted that members of the local police force, in addition to being committed to carrying weapons, perform a variety of functions that serve society and the general interest, making it reasonable to assert that there exists an essential public interest in ensuring that they possess a certain psychophysical fitness.

Therefore, we can also find in section g) of Article 9.2 of the GDPR another circumstance that lifts the general prohibition on processing special categories of data.

IX
Once the prohibition on processing special categories of data has been lifted, and the legal bases that legitimize the processing of personal data in public employment access processes have been identified, it is necessary to analyze whether only the medical services responsible for the medical tests should process health data, or whether, on the contrary, the qualification tribunals can also access such data, and if so, under what circumstances and to what extent.
As a starting point, it should be noted that access to public employment is part of the content of the fundamental right provided for in article 23.2 of the Spanish Constitution (hereinafter CE), which recognizes the right of citizens to access public functions and positions under conditions of equality, with the requirements established by law. The realization of this right is carried out through the various procedures for access to public employment that the administrative legal system provides, and as such, with the guarantees inherent to the administrative procedure and the rights that assist the interested parties in accordance with the provisions of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (LPACAP).
At this point, it is necessary to recall that among the rights of the interested parties in administrative procedures, article 53 e) of the LPACAP recognizes, among others, the right to use the means of defense admitted by the Legal System, reflecting the fundamental right provided for in article 24 of the Constitution, which guarantees the use of means of evidence in defense of the claims of the litigant.
Although this is a right that is primarily effective in judicial proceedings and in the field of sanctioning administrative law, considering the extensive interpretation of the content and scope of fundamental rights under the "greater value" of these rights (STC 66/1985, of May 23), in the case of administrative procedures that concern another fundamental right such as that provided for in article 23.2 CE, it could also have effects.

That is to say, the exclusion framework contemplates two combined requirements that must concur: certain pathologies or injuries and that these hinder or limit the development of police functions.

Therefore, and taking into account the cited jurisprudence, the medical tests in the selection processes serve the purpose of verifying whether the applicant has any of the exclusion causes listed in the medical report and, secondly, to what extent these causes hinder professional practice. This will essentially be the purpose of the health data processing carried out by the Qualifying Tribunal.

Thus, the processing of health data conducted by the Qualifying Tribunal must be limited to that purpose, that is, when the applicant must be excluded from the selection process, when the qualification must be "not suitable," that body may access health data in order to fulfill that task and to be able to explain (justify) the reason for it. However, such knowledge must be limited to what is strictly necessary, that is, to be able to explain to what extent the applicant is hindered in performing the professional function (purpose limitation and data minimization).

The cited principles prevent the processing from having another purpose and that the knowledge of health data exceeds that which refers solely to the cause of exclusion, so the Qualifying Tribunal will not be able to know the complete content of the report or record issued by the medical service. 

Furthermore, the principles and guarantees in the processing are complemented by the observance of the principles of confidentiality and integrity provided for in Articles 5.1 f) of the GDPR and Article 5 of the LOPDGDD, previously cited.

XII
Finally, it should be noted that the obligations arising from Articles 12 and 13 of the GDPR related to transparency and the right to information in data processing are particularly relevant when we are dealing with the processing of special categories of data. 

Therefore, as a complement to the observance of the aforementioned principles, the public administrations calling for the selection processes must exercise exquisite diligence in complying with these principles, considering that the applicant assumes the rules of the game derived from their participation in the selection process and that will sustain the legal relationship to which they are bound. 

In this regard, it should be remembered that neither the processing carried out under a legal obligation nor that which is necessary for the exercise of public powers is exempt from the duty of transparency and the right to information, nor can its intensity be modulated simply because we are dealing with competitive selection procedures, where there is voluntary participation that implies adherence to rules set by the other party in the legal relationship, or because it is public authorities who will process the data to exercise a power attributed by law.

The information provided must leave no doubt about the processing that will occur, especially regarding the data that will be collected and the recipients, without prejudice to the other elements that make up Article 13 of the GDPR.

XIII
According to what has been indicated, in the present case, the local corporation intends to know the complete content of the report issued by the medical service contracted for this purpose, without it being stated in the inquiry that the interested party was excluded from the selection process; on the contrary, it is stated that they achieved the qualification of "SUITABLE." 

In any case, as previously indicated, access to the complete content of the medical report would not be justified, but only in cases of justifying the exclusion of the applicant and to the extent necessary for such justification. 

Therefore, in the case presented, there is no justification for the consulting local corporation to know the complete content of the report issued by the medical service.
