Directive (EU) 2018/1972 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of December 11, 2018, which establishes the European Code of Electronic Communications, consolidates into a single text the package of Community Directives approved in 2002 and amended in 2009, which are part of the regulatory framework for electronic communications networks and services (Access, Authorization, Framework, and Universal Service Directives) with the exception of Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of July 12, 2002, on the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on privacy and electronic communications), which remains in force until the approval of what is known as the E-Privacy Regulation.

Regarding the concept of commercial communication, it was included in the Annex of definitions of Law 34/2002, of July 11, on information society services and electronic commerce:
f) “Commercial communication”: any form of communication aimed at the promotion, directly or indirectly, of the image or of the goods or services of a company, organization, or person engaged in commercial, industrial, artisanal, or professional activities.

In order to better protect the legitimate interests of consumers and users, it is prohibited for operators to make unsolicited telephone calls to the consumer and/or user with the aim or effect of promoting or selling goods or services. IV
Once the applicable legal regime and the evolution of the legislative process have been studied, taking into account the competencies that correspond to this Agency regarding the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, specifically recognized in Article 114.1.b) of the current General Telecommunications Law, it is appropriate to interpret Article 66.1.b) of the same, considering the criteria set forth in Article 3 of the Civil Code: “The norms shall be interpreted according to the proper meaning of their words, in relation to the context, historical and legislative background, and the social reality of the time in which they are to be applied, fundamentally taking into account the spirit and purpose of those.”
Thus, the text approved by the General Courts has not limited the processing of personal data for making calls for commercial communication purposes to the prior provision of consent, but has included the possibility that the communication may be based on another legal basis provided for in Article 6.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 on the processing of personal data.
Among these legal bases is the legitimate interest in its letter f):
f) the processing is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that those interests do not prevail over the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject requiring the protection of personal data, particularly when the data subject is a child.

The legitimate interest of a data controller, including that of a controller to whom personal data may be communicated, or of a third party, may constitute a legal basis for processing, provided that the interests or the rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail, taking into account the reasonable expectations of the data subjects based on their relationship with the controller. Such legitimate interest could arise, for example, when there is a relevant and appropriate relationship between the data subject and the controller, such as in situations where the data subject is a customer or is in the service of the controller. In any case, the existence of a legitimate interest would require a meticulous assessment, including whether a data subject can reasonably foresee, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that processing for such purpose may occur. In particular, the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject could prevail over the interests of the data controller when processing personal data in circumstances where the data subject does not reasonably expect further processing to occur. Since it is up to the legislator to establish by law the legal basis for the processing of personal data by public authorities, this legal basis should not apply to the processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions. The processing of personal data strictly necessary for the prevention of fraud also constitutes a legitimate interest of the data controller in question. The processing of personal data for direct marketing purposes may be considered to be carried out for legitimate interest. It also recognizes an absolute right of opposition when it comes to processing for direct marketing purposes, as expressed in its Recital 70:

As the Court of Justice of the European Union stated in its judgment of November 24, 2011 (joined cases C-468/10 and C-469/10, Case Asociación Nacional de Establecimientos Financieros de Crédito (ASNEF) and others, the application of this legitimizing basis requires the concurrence of two cumulative requirements: on the one hand, that the processing of personal data is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data relate, and, on the other hand, that the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail, which requires a weighing of the conflicting rights and interests, which will depend, in principle, on the specific circumstances of the particular case at hand, criteria that have also been reiterated in more recent judgments, such as the judgment of May 4, 2017 (Case C-13/16, "Rigas Satskime", paragraphs 28 to 34), or the CJEU judgment of July 29, 2019 (Case C-40/17, "Fashion ID", paragraph 95).
The Article 29 Working Party, in its extensive Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC, carefully analyzed this legal basis and provides interesting examples in this regard, in order to assess when, under the "balancing test," the legitimate interest of the controller can be considered prevailing.
From its conclusions, the following is of interest:
d) First, that the legitimate interest of the data controller should not be considered a legal basis that can only be used sparingly to cover gaps in rare or unforeseen situations as "a last resort," or as a last possibility if other grounds for legitimacy cannot be applied. It should also not be perceived as a preferred option nor should its use be improperly extended because it is considered less restrictive than other legal bases. On the contrary, it is a means as valid as any other to legitimize the processing of personal data.
An appropriate use of Article 7, letter f), in the correct circumstances and subject to the relevant safeguards, can help prevent misuse and avoid excessive reliance on other legal bases.
An adequate assessment of the balance under Article 7, letter f), sometimes with a possibility of voluntary exclusion from processing, can in some cases be a valid alternative to inappropriate use, for example, of the legal basis based on "consent" or on the "necessity of performing a contract." Considered in this way, Article 7, letter f), presents complementary guarantees compared to other previously determined grounds for legitimacy. It should therefore not be considered as "the weakest link" or an open door to legitimize all data processing activities that are not covered by any of the other grounds for legitimacy.
b) Secondly, that the interest of the data controller, understood in the broadest possible sense, in order to be considered "legitimate" must be lawful, that is, in accordance with national and EU legislation. It must also be articulated with sufficient clarity and must be specific enough to allow the "balancing test" to be carried out in contrast to the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject. It must also represent a real and current interest, that is, it must not be speculative.
c) The processing must also be "necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest" pursued by the data controller or, in the case of disclosure of the data, by the third party. Therefore, less invasive means to serve the same purpose should always be preferred.
d) Taking into account the difficulties posed by the application of the "balancing test," key factors are identified that must be considered when carrying it out:
• the nature and source of the legitimate interest, including the fact of:
- whether the processing of data is necessary for the exercise of a fundamental right, or
- whether it is otherwise a matter of public interest or benefits from legal or regulatory, social, or cultural recognition by the affected community.
• the impact on the data subjects, including:
- the nature of the data, for example, whether the processing affects data that may be considered sensitive or that are contained in public sources;
- the manner in which the data is processed, for example, whether the data has been disclosed to the public or otherwise made available to a large number of people, or whether large amounts of personal data are processed or combined with other data (for example, in the case of profiling for commercial purposes, law enforcement, or others);

It should also be noted the doctrine established by the Supreme Court regarding the obligation to inform the affected party of their right to expressly object, as provided in Article 21.4 of the GDPR, whenever the legal basis invoked is the legitimate interest under Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR. According to this doctrine, the Supreme Court requires that, in cases where the controller uses "legitimate interest" as the basis, the data subject must have had the opportunity to "expressly object," as stated, among others, in Judgment 1477/2020, of November 10, 2020 (Rec. 4713/2019), related to a marketing case:
“However, the use of personal data for that legitimate purpose requires, according to the Union Regulation, that the data subject has had the opportunity to object to such processing, and that this right has been explicitly communicated to them, as provided in Recital 70 and in Article 21 of that regulation.”
Therefore, legitimate interest can be a legal basis that legitimizes the making of calls for commercial purposes, provided that the duty of transparency is fulfilled and the exercise of the right to object is facilitated.
In this regard, advertising exclusion systems remain a valid method to exercise the right to object. The General Telecommunications Law has not repealed Article 23 of the LOPDGDD, and it should be remembered that even the law itself, following the Opinion of the Council of State, has considered them to be of public interest. Thus, the obligation to consult these systems remains in force, unless the consent of the affected party is obtained.
However, unlike the regime contained in Article 48.1(b) of the General Telecommunications Law, which directly legitimized the making of calls without prejudice to the right to object, the reference to the bases of Article 6.1(f) requires that, beforehand, the controller has conducted the corresponding balancing, in the terms outlined in this report.
On the other hand, the legislator has contemplated a “iuris tantum” presumption that legitimizes the processing of personal data of individuals providing services for legal entities, as well as that of individual entrepreneurs and liberal professionals, on the legal basis of legitimate interest, as outlined in Article 19 of the LOPDGDD:
Article 19. Processing of contact data of individual entrepreneurs and liberal professionals.
1. Unless proven otherwise, it shall be presumed, based on the provisions of Article 6.1(f) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, that the processing of contact data and, where applicable, data related to the function or position held by individuals providing services in a legal entity is legitimate, provided that the following requirements are met:
a) That the processing refers only to the data necessary for their professional location.
b) That the purpose of the processing is solely to maintain relationships of any kind with the legal entity in which the affected party provides their services.
2. The same presumption shall apply to the processing of data related to individual entrepreneurs and liberal professionals, when it refers to them solely in that capacity and is not processed to establish a relationship with them as natural persons.
3. The controllers or processors referred to in Article 77.1 of this organic law may also process the data mentioned in the previous two sections when this arises from a legal obligation or is necessary for the exercise of their competencies.
In any case, the considerations made in this report regarding the application of the legal basis of legitimate interest are limited exclusively to the processing of personal data necessary for making the call for commercial communication purposes.
Any other processing of personal data that may be carried out by the controller linked to the same commercial purpose, such as obtaining contact data from third parties, possible profiling of contacts and their segmentation, data enrichment with, among others, the responses being given by the client or their possible recording, will require having the corresponding legitimizing basis in accordance with Article 6.1 of the GDPR and complying with all the principles and obligations established by the data protection regulations.
V
Consequently, as analyzed throughout this report, considering the literal wording of Article 66.1(b) of Law 11/2022, of June 28, General Telecommunications Law, the historical and legislative background, and especially the spirit and purpose of the regulation, which has not exclusively limited the making of calls for commercial communication purposes to the prior consent of end users, but rather...
Legal Office
that has included the possibility of being able to rely on other legal bases of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, it must be concluded that the provision admits, as a legitimizing basis, in addition to consent, legitimate interest, in accordance with what was stated in the previous section. 
Regarding the possible contradiction between said article and the sixth final provision of the LGTel itself, which was not modified during the parliamentary process despite the substantial modification made to the text of Article 66.1.b), the regime contained in said provision must prevail, as substantive regulation, over a provision of a temporary nature. 
Given the doubts raised in the interpretation of this provision, and in order to contribute to legal certainty, this Legal Office considers it appropriate for a circular to be processed and approved, in accordance with the provisions of Article 55 of the LOPDGDD and Article 6 of Royal Decree 389/2021, of June 1, which approves the Statute of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection.
