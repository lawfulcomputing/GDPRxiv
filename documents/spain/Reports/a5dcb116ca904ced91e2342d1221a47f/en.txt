The consultation raises the legal position that, in relation to the processing of personal data, may correspond to the companies subcontracted by the State-owned Company Correos y Telégrafos SA SME (hereinafter, Correos) for the transportation of mail from one Correos logistics center to another, in order to determine whether the subcontracted company holds the status of data processor or if it would be a service without access to personal data by the transporter.  

As noted in the consultation, Correos, in its capacity as a postal operator, provides its clients (legal entities and individuals) with the following postal services: general shipping services, certified shipping services, and declared value shipping services. The operations of the postal service as a whole are as follows: collection, admission, classification, management, routing, transportation, distribution, and delivery.  

The provision of mail transportation service by road is often subcontracted to transportation companies (companies or self-employed individuals). This service carried out by the transporter consists of collecting the mail (letters and packages) to be transported from one Correos logistics center to another Correos logistics center; that is, in no case does this transportation service involve the delivery of correspondence to the recipients of the shipments, but rather the merchandise is always collected and delivered at a Correos center.  

The loading and unloading of the merchandise from the transporter's vehicle is mostly carried out by Correos' own personnel, but on other occasions (on provincial routes and at intermediate stops along the route), this activity is performed by the subcontracted transporter. In any case, whether the loading and unloading is carried out by Correos personnel or by the transporter, it is the latter (the transporter) who organizes the loading within the vehicle for safety and preservation purposes.  

The merchandise (letters and packages) is loaded onto the vehicle properly categorized and arranged using pallets, carts, cages, and trays. Although in most cases the merchandise is not packaged, meaning it is not covered by packaging or any other system that effectively prevents viewing the labeling information of the merchandise, in the case of letters, the data on their labels is not visible as they are placed one behind the other (they are not placed "lying down," but arranged vertically one behind the other in such a way that it does not allow for the visualization of personal data on their labels). The same does not occur with packages, which, in some exceptional circumstances, the transporter may indeed view the label with the personal data of the sender and recipient (for example, if the transporter needs to rearrange the merchandise during the route or the last package placed in the cart allows for its label to be visible because, exceptionally, it was placed that way).

Before proceeding to study the position that corresponds to the subcontractors, it is necessary to determine the different legal positions that, in relation to the processing of personal data, may be held by Correos, taking into account the different services it provides. For these purposes, there are various reports from this Agency in which, based on the service provided and the specific data presented by the consultant, the consideration of either data controller or data processor has been appreciated for postal, messaging, and parcel service companies.  

In this regard, in Report 331/2017, issued at the request of DHL and in accordance with the regulations then in force, it was concluded that messaging companies held the status of data controller for the personal data transferred to them (data of the sender and recipient) to deliver the envelope to its destination, with the legal basis legitimizing the processing being, at least, that established in Article 11.2 c) of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, since, for the development, compliance, and control of the legal relationship between the sender and the consultant, the connection of such processing with the files of the consultant is necessarily required, which will allow it to carry out the service. Likewise, in accordance with the provisions of Law 43/2010, of December 30, on the universal postal service, user rights, and the postal market, it was noted that “the law seems to establish specific obligations regarding data protection for the postal operator derived from the fundamental rights at stake, which do not align well with the figure of the data processor.”

As can be observed from the various reports issued by this Agency, there are different scenarios that may arise, depending on the circumstances of the specific case, the legal relationship established between the intervening parties and their specific obligations, as well as the obligations that may be imposed by the legal system for the correct provision of the service, which will be decisive in assessing whether one acts in the capacity of data controller or data processor.

As the Article 29 Working Party already pointed out in its Opinion 1/2010 on the concepts of "data controller" and "data processor," the concept of controller was a functional concept aimed at assigning responsibilities, indicating that "The concept of 'data controller' and its interaction with the concept of 'data processor' play a fundamental role in the application of Directive 95/46/EC, as they determine who must be responsible for compliance with data protection rules and how data subjects can exercise their rights in practice. The concept of data controller is also essential when determining the applicable national legislation and for the effective exercise of the supervisory tasks conferred on data protection authorities."
Furthermore, the aforementioned Opinion highlighted "the difficulties in implementing the definitions of the Directive in a complex environment where many hypothetical situations may arise involving the actions of controllers and processors, either alone or jointly, and with varying degrees of autonomy and responsibility" and that "The Group recognizes that the concrete application of the concepts of data controller and data processor is becoming increasingly complex. This is primarily due to the growing complexity of the environment in which these concepts are used and, in particular, to an increasing trend, both in the private and public sectors, towards organizational differentiation, combined with the development of ICT and globalization, which may give rise to new and difficult questions and, at times, may diminish the level of protection for data subjects."
However, at the present time, it must be taken into account that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to personal data protection, which is now based on the principle of "accountability" or "proactive responsibility," as repeatedly pointed out by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and is reflected in the Preamble of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD): "the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or the processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to, based on that assessment, adopt the
Legal Office
measures that may proceed.” Within this new system, it is the data controller who, through the instruments regulated in the GDPR itself, such as the record of processing activities, risk analysis, or data protection impact assessment, must ensure the protection of this right by complying with all the principles set forth in Article 5.1 of the GDPR, adequately documenting all decisions made in order to demonstrate compliance.

Furthermore, based on this principle of proactive responsibility, aimed essentially at the data controller, and in order to strengthen the protection of the affected individuals, the GDPR has introduced new obligations that are enforceable not only against the controller but also, in certain cases, against the processor, who may be sanctioned in the event of non-compliance.

In this regard, the Guidelines 07/2020 of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) on the concepts of data controller and processor in the GDPR (currently pending final adoption after the public consultation process has concluded) make special reference (paragraph 91) to the obligation of the processor to ensure that persons authorized to process personal data have committed to respecting confidentiality or are subject to a statutory obligation of confidentiality (Article 28, paragraph 3); to maintain a record of all categories of processing activities carried out on behalf of a controller (Article 30.2); to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk (Article 32); to appoint a data protection officer under certain conditions (Article 37); and to notify the controller without undue delay of any personal data breaches of which it becomes aware (Article 33 (2)). Additionally, the rules on transfers of data to third countries (Chapter V) apply to both processors and controllers. Therefore, the EDPB considers that Article 28 (3) of the GDPR imposes direct obligations on processors, including the obligation to assist the controller in ensuring compliance.

Without prejudice to the attribution of direct obligations to the processor, the aforementioned Guidelines, based on the fact that the concepts of controller and processor in the GDPR have not changed compared to Directive 95/46/EC and that, in general, the criteria for attributing the different roles remain the same (paragraph 11), reiterate that these are functional concepts, aimed at assigning responsibilities according to the actual roles of the parties (paragraph 12), which implies that in most cases, the specific circumstances of the case must be considered (case by case) based on their actual activities rather than the formal designation of an actor as "controller" or "processor" (for example, in a contract), as well as autonomous concepts, whose interpretation must be carried out under the framework of the GDPR.

Notwithstanding, in these cases, the specific case must be addressed, taking into account that Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines "processing" as “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not…” and as stated in the Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union (Second Chamber) of July 29, 2019 (Case C-40/17 Fashion ID, paragraph 74), a “natural or legal person cannot be considered responsible, in the sense of that provision, for the previous or subsequent operations in the processing chain regarding which they do not determine the purposes or the means.”

In any case, the legal relationship established between the parties must be analyzed carefully and in depth in order to identify who determines the purposes and the means, for which the repeatedly cited Guidelines of the EDPB provide different criteria that can serve to establish such positions, starting from the premise that the word “determine” implies actually exercising influence over the purposes and means, for which it is not an obstacle that the service is defined in a specific manner by the processor, provided that the controller is presented with a detailed description and can make the final decision on how the processing is carried out and can request changes if necessary, without the processor being able to subsequently introduce modifications to the essential elements of the processing without the approval of the controller (paragraph 28) or that a certain degree of discretion is recognized for the processor to make some decisions regarding the processing (paragraph 35), allowing the processor to make decisions about non-essential means (paragraph 39), so that the processor must not process the data in any way other than in accordance with the instructions of the controller, without prejudice to the fact that such instructions may leave a certain degree of discretion on how to best serve the interests of the controller, allowing the processor to choose the most appropriate technical and organizational measures (paragraph 78).

Furthermore, another criterion to consider is whether the entity involved in the processing does not pursue any own purpose in relation to the processing, but is simply paid for the services rendered, since in this case, it would act, in principle, as a processor rather than as a controller (paragraph 60).

II
Once the legal position regarding the processing of personal data that Correos may hold, which will generally act as the data controller except for the cases indicated in which it may hold the status of processor, has been analyzed, it is necessary to examine the specific issue raised in the inquiry, relating to the legal position of the companies that Correos subcontracts for the transportation of mail by road from one Correos logistics center to another, in order to determine whether the transport company holds the status of processor or whether we are dealing with a service without access to personal data by the transporter. 
For the proper resolution of the inquiry, it must be analyzed whether the provision of the subcontracted service requires the processing of personal data, according to the definition in Article 4.2 of the GDPR: 
“processing”: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction; 
As can be seen, the GDPR contains a very broad definition of processing, as well as of personal data, in order to ensure the greatest protection of the fundamental right.

However, in the present case, as defined by the inquirer regarding the provision of the service, it does not require access in any way to the personal data contained on the outside of letters and packages, stating that “The goods (letters and packages) are loaded onto the vehicle properly categorized and arranged using pallets, carts, cages, and trays. Although in most cases the goods are not packaged, that is, they are not covered by packaging or any other system that effectively prevents viewing the labeling information of the goods, in the case of letters, the data on their labels are not visible as they are placed one behind the other (they are not placed ‘lying down’, but arranged vertically one behind the other in such a way that it does not allow for the visualization of personal data on their labels). The same does not occur with packages, where, in some exceptional circumstances, the transporter may indeed visualize the label with the personal data of the sender and recipient (for example, if the transporter needs to rearrange the goods during the route or the last package placed on the cart allows for the visualization of its label because, exceptionally, it has been placed that way).” 
Therefore, the provision of the service does not require the processing of personal data, with the inquirer acknowledging that any possible access to personal data only occurs under exceptional circumstances, such that the proper execution of the subcontracted task can be carried out without accessing the aforementioned personal data and without that exceptional access implying a fully or partially automated processing of the data or being intended to include them in a file, so it cannot be understood that access to them should be carried out acting on behalf of Correos, as in this case it would be considered excessive processing in terms of necessity and contrary to the principle of minimization. 
Thus, transporters must be considered as third parties, which Article 4.10 of the GDPR defines by exclusion as “a natural or legal person, public authority, service, or body other than the data subject, the data controller, the data processor, and the persons authorized to process personal data under the direct authority of the data controller or the processor,” with Correos, as the data controller (or, in the case that it acts as a processor, having been authorized for such subcontracting by the controller and under the terms in which it has been authorized), being required to adopt all necessary security measures to prevent them from accessing the data and to establish a duty of confidentiality in case they accidentally access personal data, in accordance with the provisions of Article 32 of the GDPR.
