I  
Having outlined the terms of the inquiry and its various interpretations, it is necessary to analyze the role that each actor plays from the perspective of data protection, generally in the exercise of their functions, and specifically regarding the proposed processing.  
That is to say, whether they can be considered as the data controller and processor, in which case it would not be considered a communication of data, or whether, on the contrary, they are two independent controllers.  
Article 4.7 of the GDPR defines the data controller as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the data controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States.”  
It also refers - in paragraph 8 of its Article 4 - to the data processor as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller.”  
In general, the figure of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, so that in those cases where the data controller entrusts a third party with the provision of a service that requires access to personal data.

However, in practice, more complex situations may arise depending on the different functions of the actors and the processing itself considered, which require resorting to interpretative criteria such as those established by the Article 29 Working Party, in its Opinion 1/2010 on the concepts of "data controller" and "data processor," where it is indicated that the concept of controller was a functional concept aimed at the assignment of responsibilities, stating that “The concept of ‘data controller’ and its interaction with the concept of ‘data processor’ play a fundamental role in the application of Directive 95/46/EC, as they determine who should be responsible for compliance with data protection rules and how data subjects can exercise their rights in practice. Furthermore, the aforementioned Opinion highlighted “the difficulties in implementing the definitions of the Directive in a complex environment where many hypothetical situations may arise involving the actions of controllers and processors, alone or jointly, and with different degrees of autonomy and responsibility” and that “The Group recognizes that the concrete application of the concepts of data controller and data processor is becoming increasingly complex. This is primarily due to the growing complexity of the environment in which these concepts are used and, in particular, to an increasing trend, both in the private and public sectors, towards organizational differentiation, combined with the development of ICT and globalization, which may lead to new and difficult questions arising and, at times, a decrease in the level of protection for data subjects.”

This implies that the legal status of an actor as a “controller” or “processor” should be determined primarily by their actual activities in a specific situation, and not by the formal designation of an actor as a “controller” or “processor” (for example, in a contract).

II
Article 6 of the GDPR establishes the circumstances that legitimize the processing of personal data, and since the legal regime of both actors has been analyzed in relation to the processing subject to inquiry, it is appropriate to exclude from the analysis the circumstances provided in letters b) referring to processing that is necessary for the performance of a contract in which the data subject is a party or for the application at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures, since in the agreement mentioned in the inquiry, the members cannot be considered part of a potential contractual relationship, but rather HNA and the Professional College will be. 

The circumstances provided in letters c) and e) referring to compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller, or the fulfillment of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred on the data controller, must also be excluded, to the extent that from the legal regime analyzed so far, the processing proposed in the inquiry cannot be considered included. 

And finally, for the sake of obviousness, the one indicated in letter d) referring to protecting vital interests of the data subject or another natural person must be excluded. 

Therefore, the circumstances provided in letters a) the data subject has given their consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes; and f) the processing is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that such interests do not prevail over the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject requiring the protection of personal data, particularly when the data subject is a child, will be subject to analysis. 

III
Before analyzing the legitimizing circumstances that could legitimize the processing, it is necessary to recall the content of the principle of purpose limitation, which, like the principle of lawfulness, must be complied with in all data processing. Without prejudice to the fact that the degree of compatibility of the purpose may be an element to consider in the balancing judgment required by the analysis of legitimate interest as a legal basis for processing. 

If the principle of purpose limitation is not complied with, the processing could not be carried out on the legal basis that legitimized the initial collection of the data, as it would no longer comply with the provisions of Article 5.1 of the GDPR, which requires the concurrence of all the principles contained therein. 

The intended communication implies processing the data for a purpose different from that for which it was initially collected and processed by the College. 

Article 5.1 b) of the GDPR states the principle of purpose limitation, according to which personal data shall be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and shall not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; 

Article 6.4 of the GDPR provides certain parameters that may be taken into account when establishing the compatibility of the purpose, when the processing is not based on the consent or on the application of Union law or the laws of the Member States, specifically stating the following: 
4. When the processing for a purpose other than that for which the personal data were collected is not based on the consent of the data subject or on Union law or the laws of the Member States that constitute a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society to safeguard the objectives set out in Article 23, paragraph 1, the data controller, in order to determine whether the processing for another purpose is compatible with the purpose for which the personal data were initially collected, shall take into account, among other things:

In turn, the Article 29 Working Party in its “Opinion 3/2013 on Purpose Limitation” states the following:
(…) Further processing for a different purpose does not necessarily mean that it is incompatible: compatibility must be assessed on a case-by-case basis. A substantial compatibility assessment requires an evaluation of all relevant circumstances. In particular, the following key factors must be taken into account:
- the relationship between the purposes for which the personal data was collected and the purposes of its further processing;
- the context in which the personal data was collected and the reasonable expectations of the data subjects regarding its further use;
- the nature of the personal data and the impact of the further processing on the data subjects;
- the safeguards adopted by the data controller to ensure fair processing and prevent any undue impact on the data subjects.

The availability of alternative methods to achieve the objectives pursued by the data controller, with a less negative impact on the data subject in this context, should undoubtedly be a relevant consideration.

For its part, in Opinion 4/2014 "on Legitimate Interest," it is indicated that: In general, the more negative and uncertain the impact of the processing may be, the less likely it is that the processing will be considered, as a whole, legitimate. Finally, regarding the safeguards or guarantees of the proposed further processing, nothing has been indicated in the consultation. 

That is to say, the retention periods or the criteria for determining them are unknown (it could be the case that a person is no longer registered, and they continue to receive offers for products and services), nor is it known whether HNA could communicate that data to third parties with whom it has corresponding commercial agreements, nor have other guarantees such as security measures to be applied, like encryption or pseudonymization, been disclosed. In order to be considered "legitimate" and relevant under Article 7, letter f), the interest must be lawful, that is, in accordance with national and EU legislation. It must also be articulated with sufficient clarity and be specific enough to allow for the balancing test to be conducted against the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject. It must also represent a real and current interest, meaning it should not be speculative.

If the data controller or the third party to whom the data is communicated has such a legitimate interest, this does not necessarily mean that Article 7, letter f), can be used as the legal basis for the processing. Whether Article 7, letter f), can be used as a legal basis will depend on the outcome of the subsequent balancing test. The processing must also be "necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest" pursued by the data controller or, in the case of data disclosure, by the third party. Therefore, less invasive means to achieve the same end should always be preferred.

The concept of "interests" of the affected parties is defined even more broadly, as it does not require the element of "legitimacy." If the data controller or the third party can pursue any interest, as long as it is not unlawful, the data subject will in turn have the right to have all categories of interests that affect them taken into account and weighed against the interests of the data controller or the third party, as long as they fall within the scope of the Directive.

In assessing whether consent has been freely given, consideration shall be given to the extent possible to whether, among other things, the execution of a contract, including the provision of a service, is conditional on consent to the processing of personal data that is not necessary for the execution of that contract. 
In Recital 42 of the GDPR it is stated that: (…) Consent should not be considered freely given when the data subject does not have genuine or free choice or cannot refuse or withdraw consent without suffering detriment. (…).
In general terms, consent will be invalidated by any undue influence or pressure exerted on the data subject (which may manifest itself in very different ways) that prevents them from exercising their free will. 
In Recital 43 of the GDPR it is stated that: (…) To ensure that consent has been freely given, it should not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, particularly when the controller is a public authority and it is therefore unlikely that consent has been freely given in all the circumstances of that particular situation. Consent is presumed not to have been freely given when it does not allow for separate authorization of the different processing operations of personal data despite being appropriate in the specific case, or when the fulfillment of a contract, including the provision of a service, is dependent on consent, even when this is not necessary for such fulfillment. (…).
According to the above, consent must be given freely, which implies that the choice of the data subject is not conditioned by various factors, such as an imbalance between them and the data controller, or that they suffer detriment in case of not providing it. There must also be the possibility of revoking consent without undermining the situation of the affected individual.
