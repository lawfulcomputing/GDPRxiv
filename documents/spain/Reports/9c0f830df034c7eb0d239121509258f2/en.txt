The inquiry raises the question of which regulation should be applied in the procedure for accessing personal data contained in documents held in the archives of the Ministry of the Interior, following the entry into force of Law 20/2022, of October 19, on Democratic Memory (LMD), in the sense of whether this regulation prevails or if Law 16/1985, of June 25, on the Spanish Historical Heritage (LPHE), to which the former refers in some of its provisions, prevails. 
And according to the regulatory application that is determined, how to proceed in cases of access requests when they are intended to be incorporated into a procedure for declaration of reparation and personal recognition provided for in the LMD, in the sense of whether they should be depersonalized (anonymized) or not.
To this end, the inquiry describes the procedure that has been carried out so far in the Ministry, which can be summarized as follows:
Starting from the fact that both the LPHE and its implementing regulation, Royal Decree 1708/2011, of November 18, (RDSEA) establish that, when a request for access to documents contains personal data of third parties and certain circumstances are met, the authorization of the holders of the personal data will be required (articles 57.1 c) and 28.2 respectively), and that the usual practice indicates that, (i) in most cases they do not grant authorization either to researchers or to relatives of the victims, (ii) that considering that the mere fact of allowing the data holders to express themselves on the matter (sometimes it is very difficult to communicate with them) is complicated and when they do express themselves, (iii) it involves arduous work of anonymization, partial or total, depending on the overwhelming case law, it has been opted to apply the provisions of article 28.4 of the RDSEA which allows access to documentation, without the need for consent, when prior appropriate dissociation of the data is carried out in such a way as to prevent the identification of the affected individuals.

I
Considering the competencies of this Agency, this report is issued solely with respect to the scope of the protection of personal data, for which the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR), and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and guarantee of digital rights (LOPDPGDD) must be observed. 
The GDPR extends its protection, as established in its article 1.2, to the rights and fundamental freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, their right to the protection of personal data, defined in its article 4.1 as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”

II
Having stated the above, the consulting party raises a first question regarding which regulation prevails when access to the files of the Ministry of the Interior is sought, whether the LMD or the LPHE.
The first element to analyze is the nature of the file to which access is sought. The LPHE establishes in its Article 49.2 the following:
2. The Documentary Heritage includes documents of any era generated, preserved, or gathered in the exercise of their function by any public body or entity, by legal persons in which the State or other public entities hold a majority stake, and by private individuals, whether natural or legal persons, managing public services in relation to the management of such services.
For its part, the RDSEA establishes in its Article 10 under the heading “General or Central Archives of the Ministries and of the public bodies dependent on them” the following:
1. These are the archives existing in the Ministries and public bodies for the custody of documents, once their processing has been completed and the time limits established by current regulations or in the conservation calendars have elapsed.
Taking into account the information provided in the consultation, access requests would be made to the General Archive of the Ministry of the Interior (AGMIR hereinafter), and therefore the LPHE and its implementing regulations will generally apply.
However, the consulting party focuses the debate on access requests to the AGMIR under the provisions of the LMD, which in its Article 27.3 establishes the following regulatory reference:
3. The consultation of documents regarding the coup d'état, the War, and the Dictatorship generated and gathered by the different ministerial departments of the General State Administration and other entities of the state public sector, regardless of the public administration or institution managing them, is governed by the provisions of Article 57 of Law 16/1985, of June 25, and the regulatory norms that govern the Archive System of the General State Administration and its Public Bodies and their access regime.
Well, that regulatory reference made by the LMD in favor of the LPHE leads us to conclude that there is, a priori, no antinomy, and that, in general, access to the indicated documents will be governed by the LPHE, all without prejudice to the application of the principle of normative specialty that will be addressed next.
III
The principle of normative specialty, qualified by jurisprudence as a general principle of Law (see STS of January 16, 1998), along with the principles of hierarchy and chronology of norms, has been considered a traditional criterion for resolving antinomies, understanding these as the normative contradictions that occur when, under the same factual conditions, legal consequences are attributed that cannot be observed simultaneously.

And it refers to the content of the regulated matter and implies the transition from a broader rule, which affects an entire category, to a less extensive rule, which affects exclusively a specific type of that category. VILLAR PALASÍ argues that the rule of specialty presupposes and does not eliminate the simultaneous validity of the general norm and the special norm. The special law will apply preferentially to the general law when its factual assumption aligns more closely with the specific fact, as otherwise it would become ineffective, since it would never be applicable (…). Conversely, the general law will apply to all assumptions that cannot be classified under the special law and will therefore also be effective within its scope. 

Even the principle of specialty can be found within the same normative text (STS of April 17, 1997) and, it is worth noting, this is what underlies the inquiry posed considering the literal, systematic, and teleological interpretation of Article 27 of the LMD, which will be analyzed further on.

Considering the non-application of the LOPDGDD in Article 2.2 and the partial exception to this non-application proposed in Article 3, it is necessary to refer to the Judgment of the Constitutional Court 292/2000, of November 30, to determine to what extent that provision affects the inquiry raised. 
The Judgment specifies the content of the right to data protection, indicating that “the right to data protection grants its holder a set of powers consisting of various legal powers whose exercise imposes legal obligations on third parties and serves the function that this fundamental right performs, which is to guarantee the individual a power of control over their personal data, which is only possible by imposing the aforementioned duties on the third party. These include the right to require prior consent for the collection and use of personal data, the right to be informed about the destination and use of that data, and the right to access, rectify, and cancel such data. In short, the power of disposition over personal data.”
From the reading of STC 292/2000, we can affirm that the content of the right to data protection encompasses, on one hand, a set of powers of the right holder, and on the other, a series of legal obligations that fall on third parties who will process personal data. As powers, we can cite the intended effects in this inquiry, such as the right of access, and among the legal obligations or duties, we can mention that the processing of data must find its legal basis in some provision of Article 6 of the GDPR, and otherwise classify it as unlawful.
Well, the aforementioned Article 3 LOPDGDD does nothing but expand or extend over time some of the powers that are part of the content of the right to data protection, and Article 2.2 LOPDGDD, which addresses the non-application of the regulation when it concerns personal data of a deceased person, implies the decay of the legal obligations (of third parties) that also form part of the content of the right to data protection. 
Considering the above, in the case presented, it does not seem that the applicant falls within the assumptions established by Article 3 of the LOPDGDD, so they are not the holder of the powers provided in the provision, as it is not possible to equate the condition of designated person provided in paragraph 2, with a mere assertion that the applicant is the literary executor of the deceased. 
However, based on the non-application of the LOPDGDD to the processing of personal data of deceased individuals, it would also not be necessary to seek a legal basis in accordance with Article 6 of the GDPR to support the communication of personal data. The responsible party of the RMC would not be obliged to fit that communication, which involves data processing, into any assumption provided in the regulations of the GDPR or LOPDGDD. 
In other words, the denial of access to the data of the deceased by the applicant cannot be based on a potential breach of the LOPDGDD, as it is not the applicable norm.

Already in Guidelines 5/2014, of April 10, 2014, on “Anonymization Techniques,” from the Article 29 Working Group of Directive 95/46, it was indicated that: 
Anonymization involves subsequent processing of personal data. Therefore, it must meet the compatibility requirement considering the circumstances and legal grounds of such processing. Furthermore, although anonymized data is outside the scope of data protection legislation, it is possible that the interested parties have a right to protection under other legal provisions (such as those that protect the confidentiality of communications).
(…)
When a dataset is truly anonymized and individuals can no longer be identified, European data protection legislation does not apply.
More recently, in the Basic Guide to Anonymization1 prepared by the National Data Protection Authority of Singapore and published by the AEPD for its eminently educational character, essential concepts were clarified, and the same conclusion regarding the inapplicability of personal data protection regulations was reached: 
Anonymization consists of converting personal data into data that cannot be used to identify any individual. Anonymization should be considered a risk-based process, which includes both the application of anonymization techniques and safeguards to prevent re-identification.
De-identification involves the removal of identifiers (for example, name, address, national identity document number) that directly identify an individual. De-identification is sometimes mistakenly understood as anonymization; however, it is only the first step of anonymization. A de-identified dataset can be easily re-identified when combined with publicly available or easily accessible data.
Re-identification refers to the identification of individuals from a dataset that was previously de-identified or anonymized.
Anonymized data is not considered personal data and, therefore, is not governed by data protection regulations.

Furthermore, regarding documents similar to those of access proposed in this consultation, such as those from the Civil War and the Dictatorship, anonymization was also advocated as an alternative to the request for consent in several consultations regarding the publication on the internet of rulings from the Public Order Court, where the LPHE was also applicable. Thus, in reports 171/2010, 330/2010, and 61/2017, the following was stated:
“Taking into account everything that has been pointed out up to this moment, it can be concluded that, from the perspective of the application of personal data protection regulations, the publication on the portal referred to in the consultation of data relating to individuals who are victims of the Civil War or reprisals from Francoism, and in particular, those linked to the proceedings processed by the [Public Order Courts] will only be possible if the following conditions are met:

1. That they are deceased individuals, and the data held by the requester must be contrasted with the results from other records that allow this circumstance to be verified when necessary.
2. In the case of living individuals, that consent has been obtained from the interested parties for their data to be included in the portal, and the interested party must be informed of all aspects related to that processing in the terms provided by Article 5 of Organic Law 15/1999.
3. In the absence of consent, the information on the portal regarding individuals who have not provided consent must be dissociated in such a way that it is not possible to identify the interested party directly or indirectly.”

Therefore, the criterion of this Agency has been favorable to the application of anonymization techniques in those cases where consent referred to by the historical heritage and Spanish documentary fund regulations is not available.

VIII
Now, in the present case, we are faced with requests made under the LMD, where, in addition to an underlying right on the part of third parties with an evident legitimate interest, the principle of normative specialty to which we have previously referred would apply, even within the same law.

It should be added that the systematic establishment of anonymization, as the requester states is carried out in practice when resolving access requests, could occur to the detriment of the holders of the right expressly recognized in the LMD, as will be explained below.

And the subjective aspect – the potential holders of the right –, the material aspect – the integrity of the document and the express recognition of the possibility of accessing personal data of third parties –, and the teleological aspect – certifying the status of victim –, are the elements that will enable the application of the principle of specialty – even within the same norm – to which we referred earlier, in contrast to the regulatory reference made in section 3, related to the application of the LPHE and its development regulations.
That is to say, the person responsible for the archive must weigh whether, strictly applying the LPHE and specifically the RDSAE in its Article 28.4 on anonymization, when resolving access requests for documents under Article 6 LMD, the right of a person to certify their status as a victim may be affected in such a way that its effective realization is hindered if such anonymization of third-party data were to be carried out.

And following the systematic interpretation of the provision, when considering the principle of specialty, it must be taken into account that section 3 of Article 27 LMD no longer expressly refers to those requests with the aforementioned teleological element, but rather has a more transversal vocation, and aims to regulate, in general terms, “the consultation of documents regarding the coup d'état, the War, and the Dictatorship.”
Therefore, there is an evident difference that the legislator established between the access referred to in Article 27.2 LMD that is sought by individuals who are potential holders of the right under Article 6 LMD to be considered “victims,” and the rest of the individuals who wish to access the documents regarding the coup d'état, the War, and the Dictatorship for different reasons. 
This is the difference that allows the application of the principle of specialty, and, consequently, that the person responsible for the archive does not have to anonymize per se all the documentation requested from them, no matter how much Article 28.4 RDSAE allows it; for otherwise, it would not make sense that in section 2 of the cited Article 27 LMD, access to personal data is expressly permitted, and it even literally refers to “regardless of the date of the same,” which obviously dispenses with the temporal criteria of 25 or 50 years, depending on the case, established by the historical heritage regulations. 
Therefore, it is possible for the person responsible for the archive to apply strictu sensu section 2 of Article 27 LMD, without the limitations or options provided by the LPHE and its development regulations.
Furthermore, it cannot be overlooked that individuals who may request the declaration of victim and reparation have a legitimate interest in the processing – ex Article 6.1.f) GDPR – (in the event that the data subjects are living persons, thus applying the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, since if they were deceased persons, as previously explained in this report, personal data regulations do not apply). This, as indicated earlier, must be taken into account by the person responsible for the archive when evaluating the access request.

IX
Consequently, a generalized practice of anonymization based on the regulatory reference of Article 27.3 LMD, without analyzing case by case the content of the requests, could effectively hinder or make impossible the exercise of the right expressly recognized in Article 6 of the LMD. 
In this regard, it is appropriate to recall what is indicated in the document published by the AEPD “Guidelines and Guarantees in the Procedures for Anonymization of Personal Data,” which states that anonymized data must ensure that any operation or processing that may be carried out after anonymization does not lead to a distortion of the actual data. In other words, if excessive anonymization occurs, it could affect the integrity of the document, which is a fundamental element in the realization of the right provided for in Article 6 of the LMD.
Therefore, in light of the particular situations cited in the inquiry, it must be concluded that it is the responsibility of the archive controller and not this Agency to determine on a case-by-case basis, taking into account data protection principles such as minimization and purpose limitation, the appropriateness or not of depersonalization, considering that excessive use, both qualitatively and quantitatively, may undermine the effectiveness of the recognition of the right to declare victim status and reparation referred to in the norm.
