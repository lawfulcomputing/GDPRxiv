The Municipal Register of inhabitants is an administrative record regulated by Law 7/1985, of April 2, on the Bases of the Local Regime, whose article 16, first paragraph, states that “The municipal register is the administrative record where the residents of a municipality are recorded. Its data constitutes proof of residence in the municipality and of the habitual domicile therein. Regarding the nature of the data contained in the municipal register, jurisprudence has repeatedly declared that: “The data of the register is confidential as it contains information pertaining to the privacy of the registered individuals, as inferred from a simple reading of article 16.2 of the Law on the Bases of the Local Regime, where the data that must be included in the Register is exposed and which is subject to the Organic Law on the Protection of Personal Data with the only exception contained in article 16.3 of said Law on the Bases.”
In relation to the possible transfer of their data, the third paragraph of the cited provision includes the principles governing the transmission and use of the data from the Municipal Register, stating that “The data from the Municipal Register will be transferred to other public administrations that request it without prior consent from the affected individual only when it is necessary for the exercise of their respective competencies, and exclusively for matters in which residence or domicile are relevant data. Regarding the reason for the inquiry, it is necessary to recall, as has been indicated on numerous occasions by this Agency, that the reform carried out by the General Data Protection Regulation concerning the regime contained in Organic Law 15/1999 requires a change of perspective regarding the principles that articulate the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and, in particular, the one that made the "principle of consent" the central axis of the right to data protection.

Indeed, while the Organic Law and the Regulation do not differ excessively regarding the enumeration of the legitimizing causes for processing, there is a highly relevant modification in the way these causes are presented in the applicable texts: thus, while from the wording of Organic Law 15/1999 it seemed to be deduced that the basic rule of legitimization was, in general, consent, with the remaining legitimizing causes being exceptional in relation to consent, which as a general rule should govern processing, in the text of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, consent is included as one of the six legitimizing causes for processing without holding greater or lesser importance than the others enumerated in the regulation.

Furthermore, the General Data Protection Regulation itself makes it clear that the consent of the affected party should not constitute the legal basis for processing in certain cases. Thus, Recital 42 states in its last sentence that "Consent should not be considered freely given when the data subject does not have genuine or free choice or cannot refuse or withdraw consent without suffering detriment," and Recital 43 adds that "To ensure that consent has been given freely, it should not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, particularly when the controller is a public authority and it is therefore unlikely that consent has been freely given in all circumstances of that particular situation."

c) The possibility of exercising the rights established in Articles 15 to 22 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
If the data obtained from the affected party is to be processed for profiling, the basic information will also include this circumstance. In this case, the affected party must be informed of their right to object to the adoption of automated individual decisions that produce legal effects concerning them or similarly significantly affect them, when this right applies in accordance with the provisions of Article 22 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
3. When personal data has not been obtained from the affected party, the data controller may comply with the information duty established in Article 14 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 by providing them with the basic information indicated in the previous paragraph, indicating an electronic address or other means that allows easy and immediate access to the remaining information.
In these cases, the basic information will also include:
a) The categories of data subject to processing.
b) The sources from which the data originated.
Regarding the right to object, it is regulated in Article 21 of the GDPR:
“1. The data subject shall have the right to object at any time, on grounds relating to their particular situation, to the processing of personal data concerning them based on the provisions of Article 6, paragraph 1, letters e) or f), including profiling based on such provisions. The data controller shall cease processing the personal data unless it demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds for the processing which override the interests, rights, and freedoms of the data subject, or for the establishment, exercise, or defense of legal claims. 
2. When the processing of personal data is for the purpose of direct marketing, the data subject shall have the right to object at any time to the processing of personal data concerning them, including profiling to the extent that it is related to such marketing. 
3. When the data subject objects to processing for direct marketing purposes, the personal data shall no longer be processed for such purposes. 
4. At the latest at the time of the first communication with the data subject, the right indicated in paragraphs 1 and 2 shall be explicitly mentioned to the data subject and shall be presented clearly and separately from any other information.

5. In the context of the use of services of the information society, and notwithstanding the provisions of Directive 2002/58/EC, the data subject may exercise their right to object by automated means that apply technical specifications. 
6. When personal data is processed for scientific or historical research purposes or for statistical purposes in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, the data subject shall have the right, for reasons related to their particular situation, to object to the processing of personal data concerning them, unless such processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out for reasons of public interest.
Consequently, the data subject must be informed at the time of obtaining their personal data, among other circumstances, of the legal basis for the processing, to which it should be added what is established in letter d), that is, when the processing is based on Article 6, paragraph 1, letter f) - legitimate interest - the data subject must be informed of the legitimate interests of a third party that are claimed as the legal basis for the processing, as well as of the right they have to object to such processing.
This is of great importance because it demonstrates that the GDPR aims for the data subject to be aware at that moment (at the time of collection of their personal data) of what the legitimate interests of third parties are that could hypothetically legitimize the processing of their data without their consent, as well as for the controller to take into account the rights, freedoms, and interests of the affected party, so that the controller can weigh the legitimate interests that may be claimed by the third party against the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject that require the protection of their personal data, particularly when the data subject is a child.
Therefore, such weighing must be carried out:
a) By the controller
b) Taking into account the circumstances of the specific case.
Notwithstanding the above, nothing prevents the Registration Council, in the exercise of the competence attributed to it by Article 17.4.c) of Law 7/1985, Regulating the Bases of Local Regime (to propose the approval of the necessary technical instructions for the management of municipal registers) from establishing general criteria for weighing that will not exempt the controller from carrying out the same according to the specific purpose for which the certificates and registration documents are requested and the circumstances that apply to the individuals whose personal data may be included in them.

Thus, for example, in the case referred to in the inquiry, of a request for the certificate or registration document by one of those who is currently registered at the residence, the weighing judgment could favor the legitimate interests invoked by the third party, when this results from the purpose for which it is requested and the absence of other circumstances concerning the affected individuals. The same applies in the case where the certificate is requested by the owner of the residence and is necessary for the intended purpose (for example, to take legal action against those residing in it without a valid legal title, being necessary for the plaintiff to provide all data that facilitate the identification and location of the defendant in accordance with Article 155 of the Civil Procedure Law, whose paragraph three expressly provides that “for the purposes of communication acts, the address appearing in the municipal register may be designated […]).
On the contrary, in other cases, the rights and interests of the affected individuals may prevail in the weighing judgment, as would be the case raised in the inquiry of individuals who are registered at a residence and have ceased to reside there, for whom an ex officio deregistration procedure must be initiated, always again taking into account the specific circumstances of the case and the purpose invoked by the third party (which, again, could be, for example, for the exercise of legal actions).
Similarly, it could be the case of individuals who are victims of gender-based violence or minors, when the request is not made by those holding their legal representation.
In any case, it is necessary to emphasize that, without prejudice to the general criteria that the Registration Council may propose taking into account the diverse case law and its experience, the weighing judgment, taking into account the circumstances of the specific case, must be carried out by the data controller, assisted, in case of doubt, by the data protection officer, whose appointment is mandatory in the scope of Public Administrations.
