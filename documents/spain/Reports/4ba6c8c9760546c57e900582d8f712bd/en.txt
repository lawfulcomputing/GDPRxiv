The inquiry raises the question of whether the adoption of an agreement by the State Commission against Violence, Racism, Xenophobia, and Intolerance, within the scope of its competencies, establishing measures for compliance by clubs consisting of the installation of biometric systems for controlling all access to the stands of support that allows for the unambiguous identification of fans accessing said stands, would be legally viable in accordance with the data protection regulatory framework.  

This possibility would be supported, according to the inquiry, by the legally attributed competence under Article 13.1 of Law 19/2007, of July 11, against violence, racism, xenophobia, and intolerance in sports, which empowers it to decide on the implementation of additional security measures for competitions or sporting events classified as high risk, or for venues that have been subject to closure sanctions in accordance with Titles II and III of this Law, including in particular:  
b) Promote systems for verifying the identity of individuals attempting to access sports venues.  

Consequently, in the opinion of the inquirer, the processing of personal data of fans, including their biometric data, would be carried out in application of Article 6.1.e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), that is, the processing of the data would be necessary "for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller." In this case, the task carried out by the Clubs/SAD in the public interest would be to ensure the safety and integrity of individuals attending football stadiums, as well as to prevent and avoid violations of fundamental rights of individuals, such as hate crimes and discrimination, through the aforementioned measures.  

Furthermore, since the requested measure refers to the processing of special categories of data, the exception regulated in Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR would apply, meaning that the processing of biometric data "is necessary for reasons of substantial public interest, based on Union law or the law of the Member States, which must be proportionate to the objective pursued, respect in essence the right to data protection, and establish appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject." In this regard, the agreement that the CEVRXID may adopt would also establish the appropriate and specific measures that Clubs/SAD must adopt to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subjects concerning the implementation of the additional required measure, as required by Article 9.2.g) of the aforementioned GDPR.  

It is also indicated that considering that the additional measure would require the processing of special categories of data, the agreement adopted by the CEVRXID regarding the obligation to adopt the requested measure will also require that, before implementing it, the following be carried out:  
- A proportionality assessment, analyzing from the perspective of data protection, both the suitability of the measure and the necessity of the processing and its proportionality in a strict sense.  
- A Data Protection Impact Assessment that meets the requirements of Article 35 of the GDPR.  

In summary, the measure would ensure that the processing of identifying personal data (including biometric data) carried out by Clubs/SAD is conducted in compliance with the principles of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, security, as well as proactive responsibility, as established by Article 5 of the GDPR.  

Thus, according to the inquirer, the measure would ensure that the processing of identifying personal data (including biometric data) carried out by Clubs/SAD is conducted in compliance with the principles of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, security, as well as proactive responsibility, as established by Article 5 of the GDPR.  

I  
Regulation (EU) 2016/679, of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) defines in its Article 4.14 biometric data as "personal data obtained through a specific technical processing, related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person that allow or confirm the unique identification of that person, such as facial images or fingerprint data."  

Article 9 of this regulation governs the processing of special categories of data, among which biometric data are included, establishing a general prohibition on their processing in the following terms:

“The processing of personal data revealing ethnic or racial origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union affiliation is prohibited, as well as the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, data related to health, or data related to the sexual life or sexual orientation of a natural person.”
In relation to the processing of biometric data, in our Report 36/2020, analyzing Article 9.1 in relation to Recital 51 of the GDPR, as well as the Protocol amending the Convention for the Protection of Individuals with regard to the processing of personal data, adopted by the Committee of Ministers at its 128th session in Elsinore on May 18, 2018 (Convention 108+), we pointed out that 
“In order to clarify the interpretative doubts that arise regarding the consideration of biometric data as special categories of data, one can refer to the distinction between biometric identification and biometric verification/authentication established by the Article 29 Working Party in its Opinion 3/2012 on the evolution of biometric technologies:
 Biometric identification: the identification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparing their biometric data (acquired at the time of identification) with a set of biometric templates stored in a database (i.e., a one-to-many matching process).
 Biometric verification/authentication: the verification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparison between their biometric data (acquired at the time of verification) with a single biometric template stored on a device (i.e., a one-to-one matching process).
This same differentiation is reflected in the White Paper on Artificial Intelligence by the European Commission:
“With regard to facial recognition, ‘identification’ is understood as the comparison of a person's facial image template with many other templates stored in a database to determine whether their image is stored in it. ‘Authentication’ (or ‘verification’), on the other hand, usually refers to the search for matches between two specific templates. It allows the comparison of two biometric templates that, in principle, are assumed to belong to the same person; thus, the two templates are compared to determine whether the person in the two images is the same. Nevertheless, this Agency considers that this is a complex issue, subject to interpretation, regarding which no general conclusions can be drawn, and attention must be paid to the specific case according to the data processed, the techniques used for their processing, and the consequent interference with the right to data protection, and, as long as the European Data Protection Committee or the judicial bodies do not pronounce on this matter, the most favorable interpretation for the protection of the rights of the affected parties should be adopted in case of doubt.”
Consequently, in that report, this Agency already highlighted the difficulty of delineating the concepts of identification and authentication, which requires attention to the specific case and the particular techniques employed in relation to the purpose pursued by the processing, as well as the need to grant maximum protection to the rights of the affected parties against the use of techniques that may be more invasive of their privacy and generate greater risks to their rights and freedoms.

For the purposes that matter here, it is sufficient to recall that, to verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions: whether the measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, in addition, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and, finally, whether it is balanced or weighed, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test; SSTC 66/1995, of May 8 [RTC 1995, 66], F. 5; 55/1996, of March 28 [RTC 1996, 55], FF. 7, 8, and 9; 270/1996, of December 16 [RTC 1996, 270], F. 4.e; 37/1998, of February 17 [RTC 1998, 37], F. 8; 186/2000, of July 10 [RTC 2000, 186], F. 6).”

The conclusions reached in the cited case are transferable to the present, given that the processing of special categories of data aims to rely on the authority of the Commission to promote systems for verifying the identity of individuals attempting to access sports venues, under the terms provided in Article 13.1 of Law 19/2007, of July 11.

As can be seen, Article 13.1 of Law 19/2007, of July 11, refers to systems for verifying identity, but does not contemplate the possibility that such systems may involve the processing of biometric data, nor does it establish the pertinent and adequate guarantees for the protection of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data. 
This possibility is also not foreseen in Article 15.3 of Royal Decree 203/2010, of February 26, although it should be noted that this regulation would lack, as has been stated, the appropriate legal rank to proceed with the regulation of the processing of special categories of personal data. 
Therefore, intending to process personal data included in the special categories of data referred to in Article 9.1 of the GDPR, since these are biometric data aimed at identifying natural persons, it is a prerequisite that one of the circumstances contemplated in its paragraph 2 occurs to lift the prohibition on processing such data, established generally in its paragraph 1, requiring Article 9.2 of the LOPDGDD to state that “The data processing referred to in letters g), h) and i) of Article 9.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 based on Spanish law must be supported by a regulation with the rank of law, which may establish additional requirements regarding its security and confidentiality.” There is no legal norm, as indicated, that enables such processing under Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, since Article 13.1 does not meet the requirements established legally and jurisprudentially, as has been analyzed in this report. 
And without this gap being able to be filled by an agreement of the CEVRXID, as it does not have the appropriate normative rank. In this regard, as has already been indicated, the jurisprudence of the Constitutional Court is clear regarding the norm that must contain the adequate guarantees that cannot be deferred to a later moment in the legal regulation of the processing of personal data in question. The adequate guarantees must be incorporated into the legal regulation of the processing itself, whether directly or by express and perfectly delineated reference to external sources that possess the appropriate normative rank (Judgment 76/2019 of May 22, FJ 8). 
Therefore, it must be concluded that the adoption of an agreement by the State Commission against Violence, Racism, Xenophobia, and Intolerance, within the scope of its competencies, establishing measures for compliance by clubs consisting of the installation of biometric systems for controlling all access to the stands of animation that allows for the unambiguous identification of fans accessing such stands, is not in accordance with the regulatory framework for data protection.
