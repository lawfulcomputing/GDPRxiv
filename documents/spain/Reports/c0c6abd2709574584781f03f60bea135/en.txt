The inquiry raises whether it is compliant with Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR) and with Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), from its status as the data controller of a private hospital (hereinafter referred to as the Hospital), to allow access to the medical records of patients in various scenarios, in application of the provisions of Article 9.2 f) GDPR.  

I  
The scenarios for which the interpretation and scope of Article 9.2 f) of the GDPR are requested can be summarized as follows:  

A) When healthcare personnel (either employed or contracted) request access to the medical records of patients to whom they provided healthcare, for the purpose of preparing their legal defense, both against a claim and against the initiation of legal proceedings (preliminary proceedings, lawsuit, etc.) initiated by the patient, such access being essential for the defense of their medical practice.  

B) When healthcare personnel (either employed or contracted) request access to the medical records of patients to whom they provided healthcare, for the purpose of sending such documentation to the insurance companies that cover their civil liability in relation to possible damages caused in the performance of their professional activity, following the receipt of a claim or the initiation of legal proceedings.  

C) When direct action as provided in Article 76 of the Insurance Contract Law has been exercised against the insurer, if the Hospital or the healthcare professional can access the medical records.  

Also, regarding the type of professionals providing their services in the Hospital, the inquiry indicates that those with a commercial relationship "do not form part of the staff nor maintain any hierarchical or dependent relationship with it. In this case, the Hospital acts as the data controller and the commercial healthcare personnel as the data processor, signing the corresponding contract."  

II  
Access to the data of medical records constitutes a communication of data that must be considered as processing of personal data, according to what is indicated in Article 4.2 GDPR, which defines it as: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.  

The information referred to in the medical record must be considered health data (Article 4.15 GDPR), and therefore falls within the special categories of data whose processing is prohibited by paragraph 1 of Article 9 of the GDPR, unless one of the exceptions set out in paragraph 2 applies.  

The LOPDGDD in its seventeenth additional provision in paragraph 1 indicates that the processing of data related to health, which is regulated in the laws it mentions, is covered by letters g), h), i), and j) of Article 9.2 of the GDPR, among which it is worth mentioning, in relation to the inquiry raised, Law 41/2002, of November 14, the basic law regulating patient autonomy and rights and obligations regarding information and clinical documentation (hereinafter LAP) and Law 20/2015, of July 14, on the organization, supervision, and solvency of insurance and reinsurance entities (hereinafter LOSSEAR).  

However, the inquiry raises the application of paragraph 2.f) of Article 9 of the GDPR for the purpose of lifting the general prohibition on the processing of health data in relation to access to the medical record by the hospital's healthcare professionals to defend themselves against claims made by patients regarding the medical assistance received.  

Considering the above, it is appropriate to refer to the aforementioned provision which indicates that the general prohibition on the processing of special categories of data shall not apply when:

From what has been indicated so far, the following conclusions can be drawn: the first is that (i) the GDPR separately mentions extrajudicial claims of various natures and administrative claims, and on the other hand, those claims that are promoted through judicial bodies. The second is that (ii) the lifting of the prohibition on processing special categories of data must be understood as exceptional, subsidiary, and the interpretation of its application must be restrictive, in accordance with the special protection to which this type of data is entitled due to its legal nature. 

Thirdly, (iii) the national or European Union law regulating these treatments must provide sufficient guarantees to protect the rights of the affected individuals. 

And fourthly, (iv) while the GDPR establishes certain assumptions that exempt the prohibition on processing special categories of data, through the law of the Member States, ad hoc regulations can be introduced in order to adapt the reality of the sectors involved to ensure effective protection of the rights of Union citizens.

III
Based on the premises just indicated, it is appropriate to address the assumptions raised in the consultation, differentiating, first, whether we are dealing with judicial or extrajudicial claims, and in turn, whether we are dealing with claims involving an insurance company or not. 

Taking the above into account, in general terms, the law regulating the medical history is Law 41/2002, of November 14, the basic law regulating patient autonomy and rights and obligations regarding information and clinical documentation, which defines the medical history as the set of documents containing data, assessments, and information of any kind about the situation and clinical evolution of a patient throughout the care process. 

In its article 15.2, it establishes that “The medical history shall primarily aim to facilitate healthcare, documenting all those data that, under medical criteria, allow for a truthful and updated knowledge of the state of health.” 

And in article 16 under the heading “uses of the medical history,” it indicates in section 1 that “The medical history is an instrument primarily intended to guarantee adequate assistance to the patient. The healthcare professionals at the center who perform the diagnosis or treatment of the patient have access to the medical history of the patient as a fundamental instrument for their adequate assistance.” 

For its part, in section 3 it is established that “Access to the medical history for judicial, epidemiological, public health, research, or teaching purposes is governed by the provisions of the current legislation in the matter of...

The exceptions are the research cases provided for in section 2 of the seventeenth additional provision of the Organic Law on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights. 
Also excluded are the research cases by the judicial authority in which the unification of identifying data with clinical assistance data is deemed essential, in which case the provisions of the judges and courts in the corresponding process shall apply. From what has been indicated so far, it can be deduced that, as a general rule, the purpose of using the medical history corresponds to the assistance function that primary and specialized care physicians carry out, without prejudice to the exceptions provided for in sections 3 and 5.

In relation to extrajudicial claims and within which the consultant raises access to the medical record to prepare the “legal defense,” the following must be indicated:
As a starting point, it should be noted that the requested access does not fall within the cases in which the LAP itself allows access to the medical record outside the patient's healthcare purpose. (paragraphs 3 and 5 of Article 16).
However, the direct effect of the Regulations must be remembered. Indeed, Article 288 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU states that regulations are directly applicable in the EU countries. Likewise, the direct effect is a jurisprudential principle established by the CJEU in the Judgment of February 5, 1963, (Case 26-62, Van Gend Loos v. Dutch Tax Administration) or in the Judgment of December 14, 1971 (Case 43-71 Politi v. Ministry of Finance of the Italian Republic.)

This means that what is indicated in Article 9.2 f) of the GDPR is directly applicable and therefore requires an interpretation and application that allows it to coexist with national law, in this case, the LAP, in the sense that from the reading of the aforementioned provision and Recital 53 of the GDPR, judicial and extrajudicial claims must be understood to be included, and therefore, the latter would fall outside the legal regime regarding access to medical records established by national sectoral regulations. 

In general terms, the cases that lift the general prohibition on processing provided for in Article 9.2 of the GDPR only serve that purpose, that is, they act as exceptions to the provisions of paragraph 1, which does not mean that whenever one of them occurs, the processing can or should be carried out, as the remaining obligations derived from the GDPR itself must be fulfilled. In other words, the mere existence of a claim under Article 9.2 f) of the GDPR does not, by itself, legitimize the processing of special categories of data, but must be accompanied by other elements that ensure that the processing is compliant with the GDPR. 

Having said the above, regarding the purpose and uses of the medical record as determined in the LAP, the application of Article 9.2 f) referred to in the consultation must be constructed in the sense that the "healthcare purpose" served by the medical record will operate as a premise and limit for applying the aforementioned provision to the specific case. In other words, the interpretation of the scope of Article 9.2 f) of the GDPR in relation to access to the medical record must be restrictive. 

In the sense that the extrajudicial claims referred to in Article 9.2 f) of the GDPR, in relation to the use of the medical record, must have a direct origin in the healthcare process between the patient and the healthcare professional. 

That is to say, the object of the claim must refer to one or more services provided by the professional, and the parties to the claim will be, on one side, the patient and, on the other side, the doctor (without prejudice to the legal representatives of each or the insurance companies with which the professional insures their civil liability). 

This means that the information from the medical record could not be used for extrajudicial claims that serve the interests of a third party unrelated to the healthcare relationship. Or even if they are parties to the healthcare relationship, when the claim does not concern events derived from it. 

Furthermore, it should be noted that once the general prohibition on processing is lifted, there must be some circumstance that legitimizes the processing. That is, in addition to the circumstance provided for in Article 9.2 f), some legal basis from Article 6.1 of the GDPR must apply. 

In this sense, it should be indicated that, on one side, there could be a concurrence of the legitimate interest of the professional in knowing aspects that will serve to address the claim, and on the side of the data subject, their reasonable expectation of privacy would not be breached regarding the professional's access to their medical record, as the claimant is the affected party and can reasonably foresee that the "defendant" would want to know what events the claim concerns. 

Ultimately, it could be that we are facing the concurrence of paragraph 6.1 f) of the GDPR to the extent that there is a legitimate interest of a third party, in this case, the healthcare professional, without prejudice to the fact that in certain cases arising from the relationship with the healthcare center, the professional is not considered a third party but as the data controller, as analyzed at the beginning of this report. 

Likewise, a regulated procedure must be established by the party that bears the duty of custody and the condition of data controller, which guarantees the principles established in Article 5 of the GDPR (legal basis, purpose of processing, proportionality, limitation and minimization, security in processing, retention period, planned communications, etc.). 

For example, the procedure should establish mechanisms that guarantee the following aspects: 
- Verify that there is indeed a claim and that it refers to events related to the healthcare processes for which the affected professional is responsible (purpose limitation and prior existence of the healthcare process concerning the claim).
- That access serves the purpose of addressing the claim, both in qualitative terms (events related to the assistance that is the subject of the claim) and quantitative terms (no more information than necessary). (purpose limitation and minimization).
- The establishment of an access log that allows knowing who, how, when, and for what purpose the access occurred. And that such log offers guarantees of accuracy, resilience, and authenticity. (confidentiality and integrity).

That the professionals who access this information assume the legal commitments arising from the use of the information to which they will have access, establishing models or forms that outline the purpose and limitation of the use of such information, as well as adherence to the duty of professional secrecy (limitation of purpose and confidentiality).

Law 50/1980, of October 8, on Insurance Contracts (hereinafter LCS) establishes the following:

Article 16.
The policyholder or the insured or the beneficiary must notify the insurer of the occurrence of the loss within a maximum period of seven days from having knowledge of it, unless a longer period has been established in the policy. In case of non-compliance, the insurer may claim damages caused by the lack of declaration. This effect will not occur if it is proven that the insurer has become aware of the loss by other means.

The policyholder or the insured must also provide the insurer with all kinds of information regarding the circumstances and consequences of the loss. In case of violation of this duty, the loss of the right to compensation will only occur if there was intent or gross negligence.

Article 18.
The insurer is obliged to pay the compensation at the end of the necessary investigations and assessments to establish the existence of the loss and, if applicable, the amount of the damages resulting from it. In any case, the insurer must make the payment of the minimum amount that the insurer may owe, according to the circumstances known to them, within forty days from the receipt of the loss declaration. When the nature of the insurance allows it and the insured consents, the insurer may replace the payment of compensation with the repair or replacement of the damaged object.

Article 73.
For civil liability insurance, the insurer is obliged, within the limits established by law and in the contract, to cover the risk arising from the insured's obligation to indemnify a third party for damages caused by an event provided for in the contract for which the insured is civilly liable, according to law.

From this provision, it follows that the insurer has a legal obligation to conduct the necessary investigations and assessments regarding both the existence of the incident and the amount of damages if they have occurred. This obligation has a direct connection to another legal obligation, which is to pay compensation at the conclusion of those investigations and assessments.

In turn, this latter legal requirement is reinforced in the very regulation by imposing on the insurer, in any case, the obligation to make, within a maximum period from the receipt of the claim declaration, the payment of a minimum amount of what may be owed, according to the circumstances known to them.

On the other hand, Law 30/1995, of November 8, on the Organization and Supervision of Private Insurance, imposes on insurance entities "the obligation to establish and maintain at all times sufficient technical provisions for the entirety of their activities." (Article 16.1).

In calculating the technical provisions, understood as the amount of obligations assumed by insurance and reinsurance entities derived from direct insurance and reinsurance contracts entered into, there is a part of the aforementioned obligations that is related to the amount of obligations arising from the payment of compensation, which appears as the main obligation of the insurance entity once the incident has occurred. Specifically, the provision for benefits includes the total amount of the insurer's pending obligations arising from incidents that occurred prior to the closing date of the fiscal year.

In the coverage of a civil liability insurance, the insurer is obliged to cover the risk of the insured's obligation to compensate a third party for damages caused by an event provided for in the contract for which the insured is civilly liable, according to law, with the particularity that the third-party victim, who is external to the contractual relationship established between the insured and the insurer, is granted by law, specifically by Article 76 of Law 50/1980, of October 8, on Insurance Contracts, the possibility of direct action against the insurer to demand compliance with the obligation to indemnify.

In this case, the assessment of damages refers to the data provided by a third party external to the business relationship, but without which it is not possible to know the amount of the compensation and, if applicable, the portion corresponding to the technical provisions. The lack of knowledge of this data also makes it impossible for the insurance entity to fulfill its obligation.

Furthermore, in relation to the aforementioned data, insurance entities must maintain a claims register in the terms established in Article 65 of Royal Decree 2486/1998, of November 20, which approves the Regulation on the Organization and Supervision of Private Insurance, in which, among other data, the initial assessment recorded and the payments made in relation to it must be included. To determine the amount of such concepts, reference will again be made to data that does not correspond to any of the parties appearing in the insurance contract, but rather to the data of the third-party victim and the damages suffered by them, duly proven.

The aforementioned obligations are complemented by those arising from the control of insurance entities. Thus, Articles 70 to 72 of the same Law establish the competencies of the Ministry of Economy in the control of insurance entities, specifically providing that "financial control will consist, in particular, of verifying the entirety of the activities of the insurance entity, the state of solvency, and the establishment of technical provisions..." (Article 71.2) and subject them to Insurance Inspection. (Article 72.1).

To this end, Insurance Inspectors, in the exercise of their functions, will verify all necessary documentation to check the technical-financial solvency of the insurance entities under their supervision, for which they will request from them all documentation that substantiates the calculations reached by the entity, as determined by Article 72 of the Law. Among these documents are those on which the entity bases its calculation of the amount of indemnities, such as medical reports when the incident results in personal injuries.

It is therefore necessary for the Insurance Inspection to have access to the data of the third-party victim in order to fulfill the supervisory obligations assigned to it and to ensure that the company has the necessary solvency to meet the obligations assumed under the insurance contract.

In conclusion, all information may be requested by the Directorate General of Insurance and Pension Funds in the exercise of its supervisory duties as provided by Article 71.4 of the Law, such that the failure to submit the same may be considered a very serious, serious, or minor infringement in the terms established in Article 40 of the Law, which may lead to the imposition of administrative sanctions. 

From all of the above, it is concluded that, both from the perspective of the obligations of the insurer contained in Law 50/1980, and from those derived from Law 30/1995, insurance entities must collect and retain, where applicable, information related to the health of third parties who must be compensated as a result of a civil liability insurance. Therefore, it should be understood that exceptions to the processing of such data without the consent of the affected party, as provided in Articles 6.1, 7.3, and 11.2.a) of the LOPD, apply.

However, it should be noted that Articles 4 and 5 of the same Organic Law regulate other principles applicable to the processing of personal data, such as data quality and information quality, respectively. 

Furthermore, it is necessary to take into account that the exclusion of consent by virtue of a law does not generally exclude the validity of the principles set forth in the LOPD. 

Therefore, while the processing of health data will be permissible, as outlined, without the consent of the affected party, they must be informed in the precise terms of Article 5 of the LOPD, and the processing carried out must comply with the requirements of Article 4 of that regulation, particularly the principle of purpose. 

In particular, what is indicated in the aforementioned Article 4 of Organic Law 15/1999 is especially relevant in relation to the case raised in the consultation, whose paragraph 1 states that “Personal data may only be collected for processing, as well as subjected to such processing, when they are adequate, relevant, and not excessive in relation to the scope and the determined, explicit, and legitimate purposes for which they have been obtained.” 

This means that the communication of data, which the consulting Health Center may make to the insurance company without needing to specify the patient's consent, must be limited to the data that are necessary in relation to the purposes that, as previously indicated when reproducing the resolution of this Agency, justify and enable such communication. 

That is to say, the consultant may communicate to the insurance company the data that allow justifying the necessity of carrying out the medical acts referred to in the consultation, although in no case will it be necessary to communicate the results derived from such medical acts, provided that they do not lead to the need to carry out other subsequent actions that affect the amount of the compensation covered by the insurance subscribed by the party responsible for the damage.

Regarding legitimate interest as a legal basis for processing, Recital 47 provides interpretative elements to determine its application, stating that “it may constitute a legal basis for processing, provided that the interests or the rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail, taking into account the reasonable expectations of the data subjects based on their relationship with the controller,” adding subsequently that “in any case, the existence of a legitimate interest would require a meticulous assessment, including whether a data subject can reasonably foresee, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that processing for such purpose may occur. In particular, the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject could prevail over the interests of the data controller when processing personal data in circumstances where the data subject does not reasonably expect further processing to take place.”
It is important to underline the rule of (i) reasonable expectation as a parameter to be assessed in the balancing judgment and to integrate it along with (ii) the fulfillment of the purpose of the processing, for the purposes of determining the prevalence of the legitimate interest of the data controller and the impact of such processing on the rights and freedoms of the affected party:
A basic analysis regarding the subjects of the liability of public administrations leads us to distinguish two fundamental parties, the claimant and the party being claimed against. Based on this premise, the claimant has a reasonable expectation that by filing the claim, their personal data will be processed in the necessary terms to resolve it, and this expectation is not undermined by the fact that the other party in the litigation, by having insurance, communicates their data to the insurance entity so that, in the event of obtaining the right in their favor, the payment of the compensation occurs.
In the case presented, the claimant positions themselves as a part of the litigation and perceives the opposing party, that is, the administration being claimed against – whether or not it has an insurance contract – as “a whole,” and the reasonable expectation of processing remains intact, that their data will be processed by that party when carrying out the necessary actions (among others, access to the final resolution of the liability procedure by the insurance entity) so that, after recognizing the right to compensation, it becomes effective.
Nor can their rights and interests be understood as diminished or impaired by such communication; rather, the opposite is true, as this processing serves the purpose for which the claimant has provided their data when filing the claim, that is, for the collection of the compensation once their right is recognized.
