“Article 58 bis. Use of technological means and personal data in electoral activities.  
1. The collection of personal data related to the political opinions of individuals carried out by political parties within the framework of their electoral activities will be protected in the public interest only when adequate guarantees are provided.  

2. Political parties, coalitions, and electoral groups may use personal data obtained from websites and other publicly accessible sources for political activities during the electoral period.

The inquiry raised refers to the interpretation and application of the new Article 58 bis of the LOREG, regarding which the following considerations are made:  

1.- Introduction.  

The collection and processing of data by political organizations for political communication purposes, along with the use of modern techniques such as big data and artificial intelligence, have generated extensive debate and concern regarding the limits that must be applied, among which is the right to the protection of personal data.  

Hence, at the 27th International Conference of Data Protection and Privacy Authorities held in Montreux (Switzerland) from September 14 to 16, 2005, the “Resolution on the Use of Personal Data for Political Communication” was approved, which stemmed from two fundamental concerns regarding data processing and profiling by political organizations, as reflected in its fifth and sixth Recitals:  
“Considering that, in this framework, political organizations continuously collect a large amount of personal data and, at times, process it using aggressive modalities, applying various techniques, including surveys, collecting email addresses through software/search engines, soliciting votes in cities, or forms of political decision-making through interactive television and voter isolation files; considering that this data sometimes illegally includes (in addition to email addresses, phone numbers, email accounts, information related to professional activities and family relationships) sensitive data related to actual or alleged political or moral convictions or activities, or voting activities;  
Considering that there is invasive profiling of various individuals who are currently classified (sometimes inaccurately or based on superficial contact) as sympathizers, supporters,

MOTIVATION 
To adapt the Regulation to national specificities and establish safeguards to prevent cases like the one linking Cambridge Analytica to the unlawful use of data from 50 million Facebook users for electoral marketing. 

As will be indicated later, this amendment was negotiated, highlighting the removal of the term “processing” in section 1 of article 58.bis, which thus only allows political parties to collect political opinions and the removal of the second section, which expressly contemplated the “systematic and comprehensive profiling of individuals for electoral purposes.” 

2.- Exception to the general regime. Restrictive interpretation. 

The processing of personal data by political parties is subject to the general regulations on personal data protection, currently constituted by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) and the Organic Law on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights, including the amendments it has made to the LOREG. 

In this regard, article 2.3 of the Organic Law provides as follows: 

“The processing that is not directly applicable to Regulation (EU) 2016/679 due to affecting activities not covered by the scope of Union Law shall be governed by its specific legislation if any, and subsidiarily by the provisions of the aforementioned regulation and this organic law. This situation includes, among others, the processing carried out under the organic legislation of the general electoral regime, the processing carried out in the field of penitentiary institutions, and the processing derived from the Civil Registry, the Property Registries, and Commercial Registries.” 

As stated in the original text of the amendment, the new article 58 bis of the LOREG is issued “in accordance with the provisions of Recital 56 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679”: 

“If, in the context of electoral activities, the functioning of the democratic system requires in a Member State that political parties collect personal data about the political opinions of individuals, the processing of such data may be authorized for reasons of public interest, provided that adequate safeguards are offered.” 

This Recital includes an exception to the general regime for the processing of political opinions, which are considered a special category of personal data, and which would be covered by letter g) of article 9.2 of the GDPR: 

g) the processing is necessary for reasons of essential public interest, based on Union or Member State law, which must be proportional to the objective pursued, respect the right to data protection in essence, and establish adequate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject; 

Therefore, it is a case in which the EU legislator has considered that there is a public interest that legitimizes the processing, albeit limited to the conditions established by the aforementioned provision, and which, in any case, remains subject to the rest of the principles and requirements of the GDPR. Among these conditions, it is particularly noteworthy that it is a requirement derived from the functioning of the democratic system and that adequate safeguards are offered. 

Moreover, since it is an exception to the processing of special categories of personal data, it must be subject to restrictive interpretation, for the following reasons: 

a) It is an exception to the general rule set out in article 9 of the GDPR, which prohibits the processing of special categories of personal data: 

“The processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, is prohibited.”

3.- Collection and processing of personal data by political parties. 

Firstly, Article 58 bis of the LOREG regulates the collection and processing of personal data by political parties in its paragraphs 1 and 2, which must be subject to a joint interpretation in accordance with Recital 56 and Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, requiring an analysis of the prerequisites for its application: 

a) Legal basis for processing: The processing of personal data related to political opinions by political parties under Article 58 bis is legitimized by the existence of a “public interest,” as indicated in paragraph 1 and Recital 56 and Article 9.2.b) of the GDPR (“essential public interest”). 

Political parties may process other types of personal data provided that one of the legal bases in Article 6 of the GDPR applies, and in the case of special categories, one of the exceptions in Article 9.2 of the GDPR, except for the consent of the data subject in accordance with Article 9.1 of Organic Law 3/2018. In particular, the provisions of Article 9.2 of the GDPR in its letter d) will apply: 

“the processing is carried out, within the scope of its legitimate activities and with the necessary safeguards, by a foundation, an association, or any other non-profit organization, whose purpose is political, philosophical, religious, or trade union, provided that the processing refers exclusively to current or former members of such organizations or to persons who maintain regular contacts with them in relation to their purposes and provided that the personal data are not communicated outside of them without the consent of the data subjects.” 

However, the situation regulated in Article 58 bis refers exclusively to the public interest, which thus acts as both a foundation and a limit. Therefore, its application must always be interpreted in the most favorable sense for the achievement of that public interest, which is the “functioning of the democratic system,” so that in no case may it justify processing, such as microtargeting, that may be contrary to the principles of transparency and free participation that characterize a democratic system. 

In this regard, it should be noted that the very Explanatory Memorandum of the LOREG states the following: 

The present Organic Law of the General Electoral Regime aims to achieve a stable framework so that political decisions reflecting the right to vote are made in full freedom. This is, without a doubt, the essential objective within which all Electoral Law of a democracy must be framed. 

We are faced with the development of one of the fundamental norms of a democratic State, as we can only affirm ourselves in democracy when the people can freely constitute the majority decision on government matters. 

The Spanish Constitution is unequivocally inscribed among the most advanced democratic constitutions in the Western world, and therefore establishes the foundations of a mechanism that makes possible, within the full guarantee of the rest of the political freedoms, the alternation in power of the different options derived from the political pluralism of our society. 

These principles are embodied in a norm such as the present one, which articulates the procedure for the expression of the majority will of the people in the various representative instances in which the Spanish State is articulated. 

b) Authorized subjects for processing: Recital 56 of the GDPR refers generically to political parties, while Article 58 bis refers in its paragraph 1 to political parties and in its paragraph 2 to political parties, coalitions, and electoral groups. 

The term political parties must be interpreted, to avoid any type of discrimination, in a broad sense according to our electoral legislation regarding those who can participate in the electoral process and present the corresponding candidacies: parties, federations, coalitions, and groups of voters (Articles 43 and 44 LOREG). It is understood that only those who present the corresponding candidacies and, consequently, participate in the electoral process are authorized to process such data. 

On the other hand, since it is an exceptional authorization to these subjects and for purposes specific to their participation in the electoral process, such data may not be communicated or transferred to third parties. 

However, the processing may be carried out directly by these subjects, in their capacity as data controllers, as they will determine the purposes and means of processing, or they may entrust it to another subject in the capacity of data processor, which must strictly adhere to the instructions of the controller, and the contract provided for in Article 28 of the GDPR must be signed.

c) Framework in which the processing is authorized: According to 
Recital 56, it is limited to “electoral activities,” which should be understood as those carried out by the authorized subjects during the electoral period, as recognized by paragraph 2 of Article 58 bis: “conducting political activities during the electoral period.” This is also evident from the systematic interpretation of Article 58 bis, located in Section 5 “Propaganda and electoral campaign acts” of Chapter VI “Electoral procedure” of Title I “common provisions for elections by direct universal suffrage” of the LOREG.

Consequently, the first conclusion drawn is that such activities are confined to the electoral period defined in the LOREG and for those who present the corresponding candidacies and, consequently, participate in the electoral process. Therefore, Article 58 bis does not authorize political parties to process personal data outside the electoral period. In the event that such processing is intended, it must be based on a different legal basis in accordance with the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.

The second conclusion reached is that such activities are limited to those regulated in the aforementioned Section 5 of Chapter VI of Title I of the LOREG: propaganda and campaign acts.

d) Purpose: Consequently, the authorized subjects must determine the purpose or purposes pursued with the processing, which in any case must relate to their electoral activity, which, as we have seen, is confined to propaganda and electoral campaign acts, and therefore, within the generic purpose of garnering votes referred to in Article 50.4 LOREG.

d) Personal data that may be subject to processing: are the “political opinions of individuals” obtained “from websites and other publicly accessible sources.” Only the processing of this data can be understood as covered by reasons of public interest in accordance with Recital 56 in relation to Articles 58 bis paragraphs 1 and 2:

- Political opinions: Recital 56 expressly refers to them as one of the special categories of data in Article 9.1 of the GDPR, and regarding which, as we have seen, the general rule is the prohibition of their processing. Such political opinions must have been freely expressed by individuals in the exercise of their right to freedom of expression enshrined in Article 20 of the Spanish Constitution and their ideological freedom enshrined in Article 16. However, what the provision does not cover is the processing of other types of personal data (whether they are special categories of data or not) to which, by applying modern technologies for mass data processing and artificial intelligence, one can...

The processing of any other type of personal data or obtained from other sources by the legitimized subjects must be based on one of the legitimizing bases of Article 6 of the GDPR and fall under one of the exceptions of Article 9.2 of the GDPR if it concerns special categories of data.  

e) Type of processing: It will be up to the legitimized subjects to determine the processing activities to be carried out, which may consist, in principle, of any of those provided for in Article 4.2 of the GDPR:  

any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated or non-automated procedures, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or modification, extraction, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, limitation, erasure or destruction;  

But in any case, the processing carried out must be “proportional to the objective pursued” (Article 9.2 g GDPR), which is to ensure the proper functioning of a democratic system, and as stated in Recital 2 of the GDPR, “the processing of personal data must be designed to serve humanity.”

Consequently, treatments that are not proportional, such as the aforementioned microtargeting, cannot be justified, nor can they aim to coerce or divert the will of the voters. At this point, although the Constitutional Court has stated that “in strict terms, in the context of electoral processes, only in very extreme cases can the possibility be admitted that a message has sufficient capacity to coerce or divert the will of the voters, given the intimate nature of the voting decision and the existing legal means to guarantee the freedom of suffrage” (STC 136/1999, of July 20), modern behavioral analysis techniques based on the mass processing of data and artificial intelligence, combined with aggressive behavioral modalities towards the interested parties, could potentially produce such a harmful effect.

Therefore, if profiling is intended, it must be excessively rigorous regarding the level of detail and comprehensiveness, since while the functioning of a democratic system may require the creation of general profiles, so that political parties can understand the political concerns of the citizenry, even by generic categories such as age, gender, population, etc., what cannot be pursued in any case is the creation of individual profiles or those made based on very specific categories that would infringe upon the aforementioned fundamental rights.

At this point, it is important to recall, once again, how the legislator himself did not admit in the drafting of the legal text the regulation of “the systematic and exhaustive creation of electoral profiles of natural persons.”

In any case, all principles set forth in Article 5 of the GDPR will apply to the processing:
- Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency, requiring that affected individuals be adequately informed of the processing, the data being processed, and the purpose thereof, which is established as an essential guarantee and to which we will refer later.
- Purpose limitation, as previously indicated, without the possibility of further processing in a manner incompatible with that purpose.
- Data minimization, limited strictly to what is necessary and without allowing, in any case, the ideological qualification of a specific person as outlined.
- Accuracy, taking all reasonable measures to avoid processing inaccurate data.

5.- Powers of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection.

Without prejudice to the competencies that may correspond to other bodies, particularly to the Central Electoral Board, the Court of Auditors, the Ministry of the Interior, and, where applicable, the Constitutional Court, and in order to guarantee compliance with personal data protection regulations, the AEPD may exercise the functions attributed to it by Article 57 of the GDPR and the investigative, corrective, authorization, and advisory powers of Article 58.

Specifically, the AEPD may adopt, among other measures, the following:

a) Approve an instruction with "ad extra" effectiveness, in accordance with the provisions of the Supreme Court in its ruling of February 16, 2007 or, where applicable, a circular under the provisions of Article 55 of the LOPDGDD, which collects the appropriate guarantees to which the treatments provided for in Article 58.bis LOREG must be subject.

b) Exercise its investigative and corrective powers, which may include all types of sanctioning and corrective measures under Article 58 of the GDPR, as the regime contemplated in Article 77 of the Organic Law on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights does not apply to political parties. Likewise, it may exercise such powers against the data processors.
