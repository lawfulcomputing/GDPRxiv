**I**

Before analyzing the specific issue raised in the inquiry, this Legal Office finds it appropriate to make a prior reference to the substantive and competency differentiation that exists between the field of information security and that of personal data protection.

Regarding information security, it encompasses the set of techniques and measures aimed at ensuring the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information and data important to any organization, regardless of their format. In the context of Spanish Public Administrations and in relation to systems that handle information in electronic format (commonly referred to as "Information and Communication Technologies (ICT)"), Article 42.2 of Law 11/2007, of June 22, on electronic access of citizens to Public Services, currently replaced by Article 156.2 of Law 40/2015, on the legal regime of the public sector, created the National Security Framework, which aims to establish the security policy for the use of electronic means within the scope of the aforementioned Law, and is constituted by the basic principles and minimum requirements that adequately guarantee the security of the processed information.

This provision finds its regulatory development in Royal Decree 3/2010, of January 8, which regulates the National Security Framework in the field of Electronic Administration, whose Preamble states that "the purpose of the National Security Framework is to create the necessary conditions of trust in the use of electronic means, through measures to guarantee the security of systems, data, communications, and electronic services, allowing citizens and public administrations to exercise rights and fulfill duties through these means," adding that "in this context, security of networks and information is understood as the ability of networks or information systems to withstand, with a certain level of trust, accidents or unlawful or malicious actions that compromise the availability, authenticity, integrity, and confidentiality of the data stored or transmitted and of the services that such networks and systems offer or make accessible."

In this area, there are multiple bodies that hold competencies, notably, according to what is stated in the recent Royal Decree-Law 12/2018, of September 7, on the security of networks and information systems, the National Cryptologic Center (CCN) responsible for the aforementioned NSG, the National Cybersecurity Institute (INCIBE), and the Ministry of Defense.

On the contrary, the protection of personal data of individuals is configured as a true fundamental right that finds its basis in Article 18.4 of the Spanish Constitution, according to which "the law shall limit the use of computing to guarantee the honor and personal and family privacy of citizens and the full exercise of their rights." This has been recognized by our Constitutional Court, highlighting in Judgment 94/1998, of May 4, that the aforementioned Article 18.4 "not only entails a specific instrument for the protection of citizens' rights against the tortious use of computing technology, but also enshrines an autonomous fundamental right to control the flow of information concerning each person - to 'privacy' according to the neologism stated in the Preamble of the LORTAD - whether or not they belong to the strictest realm of intimacy, in order to preserve the full exercise of their rights."

This is reflected in the Preamble of the Organic Law Project on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights, which for its clarity is transcribed below:

"The protection of individuals in relation to the processing of personal data is a fundamental right protected by Article 18.4 of the Spanish Constitution. Thus, our Constitution was a pioneer in recognizing the fundamental right to personal data protection when it provided that 'the law shall limit the use of computing to guarantee the honor and personal and family privacy of citizens and the full exercise of their rights.' The Constitutional Court indicated in its Judgment 94/1998, of May 4, that we are faced with a fundamental right to data protection, which guarantees individuals control over their data, any personal data, and over its use and destination, to prevent the illicit trafficking of such data or harm to the dignity and rights of those affected; thus, the right to data protection is configured as a citizen's power to oppose the use of certain personal data for purposes other than those that justified its acquisition. At the legislative level, the specification and development of the fundamental right of protection for individuals in relation to the processing of personal data took place in its origins through the approval of Organic Law 5/1992, of October 29, regulating the automated processing of personal data, known as LORTAD. In this same regard, Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR), states that its purpose is “to protect the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, their right to the protection of personal data” (Article 1.2). It emphasizes in Recital 1 that “the protection of natural persons in relation to the processing of personal data is a fundamental right” and in Recital 10 that “to ensure a consistent and high level of protection for natural persons and to eliminate obstacles to the free movement of personal data within the Union, the level of protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons in relation to the processing of such data must be equivalent in all Member States. Consequently, the right to the protection of personal data of natural persons is a fundamental right and, therefore, situated at the highest level of legal protection, which is currently regulated directly by community legislation, with the aforementioned GDPR establishing a set of principles, rights, obligations, and an organizational structure aimed at guaranteeing this fundamental right. Among these, information security appears as an additional obligation for the data controllers and processors, who must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which will include, among other factors, “the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services” and “the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident” (Article 32.2.b) and c) of the GDPR).

Therefore, there is no doubt that the guarantee of the security of personal data acquires special significance regarding its protection, but this is not limited exclusively to the realm of the security of such information, as the protection of personal data has a much broader scope that encompasses, as we mentioned, a set of principles, rights, and obligations that are much more extensive.

II

Now, entering into the analysis of the issue raised in the inquiry regarding the functional compatibility of the “data protection officer under the GDPR” and the “security officer of the National Security Scheme,” it is necessary to analyze the functions of each of these figures according to their specific regulations and the possible existence of a conflict of interest that would prevent such appointment.

Regarding the data protection officer, this figure acquires essential importance in the new protection model established in the GDPR, assuming the role of advising and supervising the processing activities of the controllers or processors, and whose appointment may be mandatory or voluntary, depending on the cases. The GDPR regulates it in Section 4 of Chapter IV, Articles 37 to 39, a regulation that must be completed, once it comes into force, with the provisions in Chapter III of Title III, Articles 34 to 37, of the Draft Organic Law on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (PLOPDPGDD), currently under consideration in the Senate.

As for its functions, Article 39 GDPR establishes the following:

“1. The data protection officer shall have at least the following functions:

Regarding the designation of the data protection officer, Article 37 of the GDPR establishes the cases in which it will be mandatory for controllers and processors, and regarding the qualifications, it states in its paragraph 5 that “they shall be appointed based on their professional qualities and, in particular, their specialized knowledge of data protection law and practice and their ability to perform the functions indicated in Article 39,” adding Article 35 of the Organic Law Project on Data Protection that compliance with these requirements “may be demonstrated, among other means, through voluntary certification mechanisms that will particularly take into account the obtaining of a university degree that certifies specialized knowledge in data protection law and practice.”  

Moreover, Article 37.5 of the GDPR provides that “the data protection officer may be part of the staff of the controller or the processor or perform their functions under a service contract,” and Article 38.3, when regulating the position of the data protection officer, emphasizes their independence by stating that “the controller and the processor shall ensure that the data protection officer does not receive any instructions regarding the performance of those functions. They shall not be dismissed or sanctioned by the controller or the processor for performing their functions. The data protection officer shall report directly to the highest hierarchical level of the controller or processor,” and its paragraph 6 adds that “the data protection officer may perform other functions and tasks. The controller or processor shall ensure that such functions and tasks do not lead to a conflict of interest.” In this same sense, Article 36.2 of the Organic Law Project on Data Protection specifies that “when it comes to a natural person integrated into the organization of the controller or processor, the data protection officer may not be removed or sanctioned by the controller or the processor for performing their functions unless they have acted with intent or gross negligence in their exercise. The independence of the data protection officer within the organization shall be guaranteed, and any conflict of interest must be avoided.”

Article 38, paragraph 6, allows DPOs to "perform other functions and tasks." However, it requires that the organization ensures that "such functions and tasks do not lead to a conflict of interest."  
The absence of a conflict of interest is closely linked to the requirement to act independently. Although DPOs may have other functions, they may only be assigned other tasks and responsibilities if these do not lead to conflicts of interest. This means, in particular, that the DPO cannot hold a position in the organization that leads them to determine the purposes and means of processing personal data. Due to the specific organizational structure of each organization, this must be considered on a case-by-case basis.  
As a general rule, conflicting positions within an organization may include senior management positions (such as CEO, COO, CFO, medical director, head of marketing, head of human resources, or head of the IT department) but also other lower positions in the organizational structure if such positions lead to the determination of the purposes and means of processing. Furthermore, a conflict of interest may also arise, for example, if a DPO is asked to represent the data controller or processor before the courts in cases related to data protection.  
Depending on the activities, size, and structure of the organization, it may be advisable for data controllers and processors to:  
 determine the positions that could be incompatible with the DPO function;  
 develop internal rules for this purpose to avoid conflicts of interest;  
 include a more general explanation about conflicts of interest;  
 declare that their DPO has no conflict of interest regarding their functions as DPO, as a means of raising awareness about this requirement;  
 include safeguards in the organization's internal rules and ensure that the job announcement for the DPO position or the service contract is sufficiently precise and detailed to avoid a conflict of interest. In this context, it should also be noted that conflicts of interest can take various forms depending on whether the DPO is hired internally or externally.

III 

Necessarily starting from the differentiation between information security and the protection of personal data mentioned earlier, this Legal Office considers that the roles of the Data Protection Officer (DPO) and the Security Officer should be distinguished for the following reasons:

1.- The segregation of functions outlined in Article 10 of the ENS (National Security Scheme) would also extend to the role of the DPO, as provided by the GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) in its Article 38.3:

“The controller and the processor shall ensure that the data protection officer does not receive any instructions regarding the performance of those tasks. He or she shall not be dismissed or penalized by the controller or the processor for performing his or her tasks. The data protection officer shall report directly to the highest management level of the controller or the processor.”

Unlike the roles defined in Article 10 of the ENS, which receive orders from the information controller, the DPO cannot receive instructions from any of the aforementioned roles and will act with total independence from them. This, logically, prevents the functions of the DPO from being carried out by any of the roles involved in compliance with the ENS, who can receive instructions from the information controller. We can say that the principle of independence of the DPO has a more restrictive scope than that of the RSEG (Security Officer) within the framework of the ENS.

2.- In line with the above, the DPO informs and advises the controller, supervises compliance, carries out awareness-raising and training activities for personnel involved in the processing of personal data, and cooperates with the supervisory authority independently of the other roles involved in the information security of Public Administrations.

The role of the RSEG is to ensure the information security of Public Administrations, while the role of the DPO can be summarized as ensuring the rights and freedoms of individuals whose data is processed, independently reporting directly to the highest management level of the controller or the processor. Therefore, these are distinct advisory functions in their principles and scope, which is why these functions correspond to differentiated regulations.

Consequently, the RSEG provides guidelines aimed at ensuring information security, whether personal data or simply information from Public Administrations, while the guidelines that the DPO must provide (consideration 77) are aimed at ensuring the rights and freedoms of individuals and not the security of information. Unlike the RSEG, who can receive instructions from the RINF (Information Controller), the DPO should not receive instructions in the performance of his or her functions.

The appointment of the DPO as the same person or entity that holds the position of RSEG would imply a denial of the principle of independence and segregation of functions of the ENS (Article 10) and a denial of the principle of independence established by the GDPR (Article 38.3).

Regarding the DPO's advice to the controller in impact assessments, such advice cannot be interpreted as a way for the DPO to receive instructions from the controller or to participate in decision-making regarding the processing activities. The DPO's advice concerning the data processor should be understood as follows:

- The DPO advises the controller, both in impact assessments and in any aspect of the processing activities carried out.
- The controller makes decisions based on, or not based on, the DPO's advice, as it is the controller who ultimately determines the purposes and means and who will assume the possible consequences that the processing activities may have for the rights and freedoms of individuals.

Therefore, it is solely and ultimately the responsibility of the controller to decide how the data will be processed. The DPO advises but does not decide on the manner in which the data will be processed and much...

It would be more appropriate for the RSEG to carry out such advisory services since its scope of action focuses on ensuring information security.

3.- On the other hand, although similarities could be appreciated in part of the functions of both figures, it should be noted that, as already indicated, they pertain to different areas of action, with objectives that should be differentiated. Specifically, the RSEG acts with the aim of ensuring information security, while the functions of the DPD must be directed towards guaranteeing the rights and freedoms of individuals in the processing carried out by the controller.

It would be a mistake to consider that the risk analysis activities necessary for conducting impact assessments on personal data protection coincide with the risk analysis activities carried out to determine security measures to mitigate risks to information security. Impact assessments on data protection are aimed at determining the risks of a processing operation for the rights and freedoms of individuals and are a distinct activity from that required to carry out risk analysis for information security concerning the risks of information and communication technologies.

The coincidence of the risk approach of the ENS with the GDPR cannot be used to highlight the identity of roles and functions of the RSEG with the DPD. The risk analysis has different purposes, as already mentioned; in the ENS, it aims to determine the risks to the information of Public Administrations, while in the GDPR, it aims to determine the risks that personal data processing entails for the rights and freedoms of individuals.

It also makes no sense to equate the positions of the RSEG and the DPD simply because neither of them is responsible for the information or the data processing, and in the event of regulatory non-compliance, neither of them should assume the responsibilities for such non-compliance. Such a statement would be equivalent to assuming that any person who is not the controller could take on the role of the DPD or that of the RSEG. As already indicated, it is the controller who, in the event of non-compliance, must assume the possible consequences of it, but the fact that both the RSEG and the DPD do not assume this responsibility cannot be understood as an equivalence of roles or functions.

It would also make no sense to equate the role and position of the RSEG and the DPD simply because neither of them can hold a position in the organization that leads them to determine the purposes and measures of personal data processing. That both are subject to this principle of independence does not imply that they act with the same objectives; it should be noted again that the RSEG has a scope of action differentiated from that of the DPD, the former must ensure information security while the latter must guarantee the rights and freedoms of individuals whose data are processed.

4.- Based on the above, the "brotherhood" of both figures makes no sense as it would imply that there would never be a possible conflict of interest, or that there would be risks to the independence of the DPD as expressed in Article 38.3 of the GDPR.

IV  

The positions of the RSEG and the DPD are requirements mandated in differentiated standards with distinct objectives and scopes of application, and the principle of independence of the DPD should be understood broadly, even in relation to the figures mentioned in Article 10 of the ENS. In the same vein, it should be considered that the very principle of segregation of functions of the ENS takes into account the separation of the indicated roles (RINF, RSEG, RSER) concerning the functions of the DPD.  

It should be understood that the information security function is a tool that allows addressing compliance with the provisions of Article 32 of the GDPR, but it cannot be understood as a tool that guarantees full and complete compliance with the GDPR. Consequently, the functions of the RSEG have a limited scope in the GDPR compared to the scope of the competencies of the DPD.  

It makes no sense to specify the differentiation of the three roles related to information security in Public Administrations and now seek to assign an additional role, that of DPD, to the information security officer. It is clear that a DPD will provide requirements, advise, and supervise the three responsible parties: information, service, and security. If the security officer assumes the tasks of the DPD, they are directly assigned tasks of the other two responsible parties, which contradicts the ENS itself and would undoubtedly generate potential conflicts of interest that could affect the rights and freedoms of individuals or even the very security of the information.  

In short, this differentiation of tasks that guarantees the effectiveness of the work of the security officer makes sense to extend to ensure that non-specific tasks of their function are not assigned to them. Just as the necessary independence of the DPD and the need to avoid conflicts of interest prevent assigning them direct responsibilities in an area they will have to supervise and in which they will be subject to instructions from other bodies.  

This has been understood in organizations with significant responsibilities in information security. In this regard, in the Ministry of Defense, where information “constitutes a strategic resource of the Department on which superiority must be sought to facilitate compliance and achieve the success of the tasks entrusted to the Ministry of Defense and the missions of the Armed Forces” (Article 2 of Order DEF/1196/2017, of November 27, which establishes the Information Strategy of the Ministry of Defense), it is equipped with a structure that depends on the Secretary of State for Defense as the responsible body for the direction, promotion, and management of the policies of technologies, systems, and information security (Article 4 of Royal Decree 998/2017, of November 24, which develops the basic organic structure of the Ministry of Defense) and which is developed in different ministerial orders, in addition to the aforementioned Order DEF/1196/2017: Ministerial Order 76/2006, of May 19, which approves the information security policy of the Ministry of Defense, Order DEF/2639/2015, of December 3, which establishes the Policy of Information and Communication Systems and Technologies of the Ministry of Defense, and Ministerial Order 5/2017, of February 9, which approves the Electronic Document Management Policy of the Ministry of Defense.

V

In conclusion, it is the opinion of this Legal Office that, as a general rule, there must be a necessary separation between the data protection officer regulated under the GDPR and the security officer of the ENS, such that their functions cannot fall on the same person or collegiate body.

Only exceptionally, in those organizations that, due to their size and resources, cannot observe such separation, would it be acceptable to designate as the data protection officer the person who performs the functions of the security officer of the ENS, provided that the requirements for training and qualification established in the GDPR are met. Furthermore, it would be essential to adopt all organizational measures, duly reflected in its Information Security Policy, that guarantee the necessary independence and the absence of conflict of interest, so that they cannot receive instructions regarding the performance of their functions as data protection officer, must report directly to the highest hierarchical level, and cannot participate in decisions related to the purposes and means of processing. In any case, this circumstance, which as we mentioned, is exceptional, must be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, and such designation must be documented, stating the reasons why the corresponding body has not been able to observe such separation of functions as well as the measures that guarantee the necessary independence of the data protection officer.
