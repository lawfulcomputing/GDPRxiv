II
Having outlined the terms of the consultation raised, given the magnitude of the data transfer in question, the first thing to highlight is the sensitive nature of the data whose transfer is requested, as it concerns health data. The legislation distinguishes, among all personal data, a set of them that it qualifies as special categories of data and that are traditionally considered sensitive data, the processing of which is subject to a specific regime regulated in Article 9 of the GDPR. These are the data that reveal: "Ethnic or racial origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, data related to health or data related to the sexual life or sexual orientation of a natural person" (Article 9.1 GDPR).
In this regard, we must bring to mind the ruling of the Constitutional Court (STC) 76/2019, of May 22, which contains the relevant doctrine on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, and addresses both the characteristics and the content that the regulation intending to establish an interference in that fundamental right must have.
(...) By express mandate of the Constitution, any state interference in the realm of fundamental rights and public freedoms, whether it directly affects their development (Article 81.1 CE) or limits or conditions their exercise (Article 53.1 CE), requires a legal authorization (see, among others, STC 49/1999, of April 5, FJ 4). (...) This dual function of the legal reserve translates into a double requirement: on one hand, the necessary intervention of the law to enable the interference; and, on the other hand, that legal norm "must meet all those indispensable characteristics as a guarantee of legal certainty," that is, "it must express each and every one of the assumptions and conditions of the intervention" (STC 49/1999, FJ 4). In other words, "it not only excludes delegations in favor of regulatory norms [...], but also implies other requirements regarding the content of the Law that establishes such limits" (STC 292/2000, FJ 15).
And this is because, in the realm of special categories of personal data, (...) the General Data Protection Regulation establishes the minimum, common, or general guarantees for the processing of personal data that are not special. In contrast, it does not itself establish the legal regime applicable to the processing of special personal data, neither in the context of Member States nor for Union Law. Therefore, it does not set the

guarantees that must be observed for the various possible treatments of sensitive data, appropriate to the risks of varying probability and severity that exist in each case; treatments and special categories of data that are, or may be, very diverse from one another. The regulation merely contemplates the possibility that the legislator of the European Union or that of the Member States, each within their scope of competence, foresee and regulate such treatments, and indicates the guidelines that must be followed in their regulation. One of those guidelines is that the law of the Member State establishes "appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject" [Article 9.2.g) GDPR] and that "adequate guarantees are provided" (recital 56 GDPR). It is evident that the establishment of appropriate and specific measures can only be explicit. If the internal regulation governing the processing of personal data related to political opinions does not provide for those adequate guarantees, but rather, at most, implicitly refers to the general guarantees contained in the General Data Protection Regulation, it cannot be considered that it has carried out the regulatory task that the latter requires.

Consequently, and as required by the Constitutional Court, the norm that establishes certain interferences in the fundamental right to the protection of personal data of data subjects of sensitive categories requires that such norm, first, be a norm with the rank of law, and that, in addition:
a) specify the essential public interest or the cause that justifies the lifting of the prohibition on processing that underpins the restriction of the fundamental right (FJ 7 of STC 76/2019). The law must explicitly state the cause among those provided for in Article 9.2 GDPR that underpins the interference with the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, and this, the Constitutional Court, citing its STC 292/2000, rejects that such identification of the legitimate purposes of the restriction can be made through generic concepts or vague formulas.
b) secondly, such law must regulate in detail the interferences with the fundamental right by establishing clear rules regarding the scope and content of the data processing it authorizes. That is to say, it must establish what the prerequisites and conditions are for the processing of personal data related to the special categories of personal data that may be included in such records through clear and precise rules (STC 76/2019, FJ 7 b)
c) And, finally, the law itself must contain adequate guarantees against the collection of personal data that it authorizes. The Constitutional Court has been clear in stating that: 
"[t]he provision of adequate guarantees cannot be deferred to a later moment than the legal regulation of the personal data processing in question. The adequate guarantees must be incorporated into the legal regulation of the processing itself, either directly or by express and perfectly delineated reference to external sources that possess the appropriate normative rank. Only that understanding is compatible with the dual requirement that arises from Article 53.1 CE (...). It is evident that if the norm included a reference for the integration of the law with the adequate guarantees established in norms of lower rank than the law, it would be considered a delegalization that sacrifices the legal reserve under Article 53.1 CE, and, for this sole reason, it should be declared unconstitutional and null. (...) 
It is also not sufficient that for the establishment of such adequate and specific guarantees it is intended to refer to the GDPR itself or to the LOPDGDD.
The same doctrine is upheld by the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU). Thus, if Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union...

recognizes the right of every person to the protection of personal data concerning them, and Article 52.1 acknowledges that this right is not unlimited and allows for the limitation of the exercise of those rights and freedoms recognized by the Charter, a limitation that must be established by law and respect the essential content of the same.

The CJEU ruling of October 6, 2020, in the joined cases C-511/18, C-512/18, and C-520/18, La Quadrature du Net and others, in paragraph 175, states: 
Regarding the justification for such interference, it should be specified that the requirement, provided for in Article 52, paragraph 1, of the Charter, that any limitation on the exercise of fundamental rights must be established by law implies that the legal basis permitting it must itself define the scope of the limitation on the exercise of the right in question (see, in this regard, the judgment of July 16, 2020, Facebook Ireland and Schrems, C 311/18, EU:C:2020:559, paragraph 175 and cited case law).

Similarly, paragraph 65 of the CJEU Judgment of the same date, October 6, 2020 (C-623/17), Privacy International against Secretary of State for Foreign and Commonwealth Affairs and others, citing, as before, the Schrems 2 judgment, states: 
65      It should be added that the requirement that any limitation on the exercise of fundamental rights must be established by law implies that the legal basis permitting interference with those rights must itself define the scope of the limitation on the exercise of the right in question (judgment of July 16, 2020, Facebook Ireland and Schrems, C 311/18, EU:C:2020:559, paragraph 175 and cited case law).

In summary, paragraph 175 of the CJEU ruling of July 16, 2020, C-311/2020, Schrems 2, states: It should be added, regarding this last aspect, that the requirement that any limitation on the exercise of fundamental rights must be established by law implies that the legal basis that allows interference with those rights must itself define the scope of the limitation on the exercise of the right in question [Opinion 1/15 (EU-Canada PNR Agreement), of July 26, 2017, EU:C:2017:592, paragraph 139 and cited case law].

Thus, it is the same law that establishes the interference with the fundamental right that must determine the conditions and guarantees, that is, the scope and limitation, that must be observed in such processing.

And in that CJEU ruling of July 16, 2020, Schrems 2, it is added (and reiterated subsequently in the aforementioned judgments of October 6, 2020):
176    Finally, to meet the requirement of proportionality according to which exceptions to the protection of personal data and limitations of that protection must not exceed what is strictly necessary, the controversial regulation that entails interference must establish clear and precise rules governing the scope and application of the measure in question and impose minimum requirements, so that individuals whose data has been transferred have sufficient guarantees to effectively protect their personal data against the risks of abuse. In particular, such regulation must indicate under what circumstances and according to what requirements a measure that contemplates the processing of such data may be adopted, thus ensuring that the interference is limited to what is strictly necessary. The need for such guarantees is particularly important when personal data is subject to automated processing [see, in this regard, Opinion 1/15 (EU-Canada PNR Agreement), of July 26, 2017, EU:C:2017:592, paragraphs 140 and 141 and cited case law].

The processing of personal data may only be considered based on compliance with a legal obligation enforceable against the controller, in the terms provided in Article 6.1.c) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when such is provided for by a norm of European Union law or a norm with the rank of law, which may determine the general conditions of processing and the types of data subject to it as well as the transfers that may occur as a consequence of compliance with the legal obligation. Requests for communication from public authorities must always be presented in writing, with justification and on an occasional basis, and should not refer to the entirety of a file nor give rise to the interconnection of several files. The processing of personal data by such public authorities must comply with the applicable data protection regulations depending on the purpose of the processing.”

“33. As the Advocate General pointed out in paragraph 74 of his conclusions, the requirement to inform the individuals concerned is particularly important as it is a necessary condition for the exercise by them of their right of access to the data being processed and to rectify it, established in Article 12 of Directive 95/46, and of their right to object to the processing of that data, as provided for in Article 14 of the same Directive.
34. Consequently, the requirement for fair processing of personal data provided for in Article 6 of Directive 95/46 obliges a public administration to inform the individuals concerned of the transmission of that data to another public administration for processing by it as the recipient of such data.
35. It follows from the explanations of the referring court that the appellants in the main proceedings were not informed by the ANAF of the transmission to the CNAS of the personal data relating to them.
36. The Romanian Government argues, however, that the ANAF is obliged, specifically under Article 315 of Law No. 95/2006, to transmit to the regional health insurance funds the data necessary for the CNAS to determine the insured status of individuals earning income from self-employment.
37. It is true that Article 315 of Law No. 95/2006 expressly states that “authorities, public institutions, and other institutions shall transmit free of charge to the health insurance funds, based on a protocol, the data necessary to determine the insured status.” However, it follows from the explanations provided by the referring court that the data necessary to determine the insured status, in the sense of that provision, does not include data relating to income, as the law also recognizes the insured status of those who do not have taxable income.
38. In such circumstances, Article 315 of Law No. 95/2006 cannot constitute, in the sense of Article 10 of Directive 95/46, prior information that can exempt the data controller from their obligation to inform the individuals from whom they collect data relating to their income about the recipients of that data. Therefore, it cannot be considered that the transmission in question was carried out in compliance with the provisions of Article 10 of Directive 95/46.
39. It must be examined whether this omission of information to the individuals concerned may be covered by Article 13 of the Directive. It follows, indeed, from paragraph 1, letters e) and f), of that Article 13, that Member States may limit the scope of the obligations and rights provided for in Article 10 of the Directive when such limitation constitutes a necessary measure for the safeguarding of “an important economic and financial interest of a Member State [...], including monetary, budgetary, and fiscal matters” as well as “a control, inspection, or regulatory function related, even if only occasionally, to the exercise of public authority in the cases referred to in letters c), d), and e).” However, the aforementioned Article 13 expressly requires that such limitations be adopted through legal measures.

Well, in addition to the circumstance highlighted by the referring judicial body that the data related to income does not form part of the personal data necessary for determining the insured status, it should be emphasized that Article 315 of Law No. 95/2006 merely contemplates, as a principle, the transmission of such personal data held by authorities, public institutions, and other institutions. 42. As for the second point regarding Article 11 of the aforementioned Directive, it provides, in paragraph 1, that the data controller who has not collected data from the data subject must communicate to them the information listed in letters a) to c). This information refers to the identity of the data controller, the purposes of the processing, and any other information necessary to ensure fair processing of the data. Among this other information, Article 11, paragraph 1, letter c), of the Directive expressly mentions "the categories of data concerned" and "the existence of rights of access and rectification of the data concerning them."
43. From the above, it follows that, according to Article 11, paragraph 1, letters b) and c), of Directive 95/46, in the circumstances of the main case, the processing by the CNAS of the data transmitted by the ANAF implied that the individuals to whom such data referred were to be informed of the purposes of such processing and the categories of data concerned.
44. However, from the explanations provided by the referring judicial body, it is clear that the CNAS did not communicate to the appellants in the main proceedings the information listed in Article 11, paragraph 1, letters a) to c), of that Directive.
45. It should be added that, according to Article 11, paragraph 2, of Directive 95/46, the provisions of Article 11, paragraph 1, of the Directive shall not apply, in particular, when the recording or communication of data to a third party is expressly prescribed by law, in which case Member States must establish appropriate safeguards. For the reasons set out in paragraphs 40 and 41 of this judgment, the provisions of Law No. 95/2006 invoked by the Romanian Government and the 2007 Protocol cannot be covered by either the exceptional regime of Article 11, paragraph 2, or that of Article 13 of the aforementioned Directive. As can be seen from its reading, the CJEU concludes that the exceptions and limitations that may be agreed upon in the field of data protection must be provided for in a norm with the rank of law, which must also establish the appropriate safeguards. In the case examined in the partially transcribed judgment above, neither the provisions of the invoked Romanian law nor the invoked protocol could be covered or enable an exceptional regime in the field of data protection, as the former lacks such scope and content, and the latter lacks the required normative value. 
Jurisprudential doctrine, in light of the provisions of the GDPR and the LOPD mentioned above, remains in force today and is extrapolable to the circumstances of the case subject to this consultation.

III
It is established peacefully by both the current legislation and the jurisprudence of the Constitutional Court (TC) and the Court of Justice of the European Union (TJUE) that the same law which establishes interference in the fundamental right must determine the conditions and guarantees, that is, the scope and limitation, that must be observed in such treatments. It should be noted that the request for data transfer that is the subject of this consultation does not arise from a legal norm with such scope and content but from the aforementioned ACM, whose provisions lack the necessary normative value to enable the requested data treatments. 
In the same way, it is also not possible to consider the provisions contained in LO 6/2013, of November 14, creating the Independent Authority for Fiscal Responsibility, as a sufficient enabling title to allow the realization of this consultation. Article 4 of this law establishes in its second paragraph that it will have at its disposal economic-financial information related to the different Administrations, and in its paragraph 3 that “The subjects included within the scope of this Law shall be obliged to provide the economic-financial information required by the Independent Authority for Fiscal Responsibility, which is necessary for the performance of its functions, within the deadline that it specifies for this purpose. In determining said deadline, the volume and complexity of the requested information must be taken into account.” First of all, it should be noted that the information referred to in this provision is economic-financial information and not related to health data, which are considered, as indicated above, as sensitive data, with the implications that this entails and which we have been discussing in this report. Secondly, it should be added that the aforementioned law does not authorize interference in the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, nor does it regulate the conditions and guarantees, nor the scope, nor the limitation, under which such hypothetical treatments could be carried out. 
In the same line, and as we have anticipated, the provision contained at the end of the aforementioned ACM cannot be considered an enabling title either, for reasons including its lack of necessary value and normative rank: 
“For these purposes, public administrations and entities of the institutional, business, or foundational public sector at the state, regional, and local levels shall provide, at the request of the AIReF, all available information and documentation necessary for the fulfillment of the assignment, in accordance with the information transfer regime established in the current regulations. For the purposes provided for in the regulations on the protection of personal data and Article 4.4 of Organic Law 6/2013, of November 14, creating the AIReF, the transfer of personal data shall be considered to be carried out in compliance with a legal obligation enforceable against the AIReF and for a mission carried out in the public interest, for its subsequent processing for statistical and scientific purposes, without the need for prior consent, and the AIReF shall maintain due confidentiality.”
IV
Furthermore, in the hypothesis that there were a legal enabling provision to carry out the data treatments subject to this consultation, which as we have been indicating is not established as of the date of this report, it should be recalled that in accordance with Article 35.1 of the GDPR regarding impact assessment related to data protection: “When it is likely that a type of treatment, particularly if it uses new technologies, by its nature, scope, context, or purposes, entails a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall carry out, prior to the treatment, an assessment of the impact of the processing operations on the protection of personal data. A single assessment may address a series of similar processing operations that entail similar high risks.” The impact assessment whose scope and content is specified in Article 35.7 of the GDPR, and whose preparation it is not established has been addressed by the party that would be called to be responsible for these treatments.
