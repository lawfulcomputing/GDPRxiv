In the consultation document, two general questions are essentially raised: the first, the possible exclusion of private security activities, as they are subordinate to public security, from the scope of application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) according to its Article 2.2, letter d); and the second, the legality of incorporating facial recognition systems in video surveillance services under Article 42 of the Private Security Law.  

I  
The first question raised in the consultation concerns whether, considering that private security activities are subordinate to public security, certain data processing carried out by private security companies and personnel could or could not fall under Article 2.2 letter d) of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
The complementary nature of private security services and their subordination to the Security Forces and Corps, with which they collaborate, is repeatedly recognized in Law 5/2014, of April 4, on Private Security. In this regard, its Explanatory Memorandum highlights that “In the special relationship that private security maintains with the Security Forces and Corps, true guarantors of the system of freedoms and rights that are constitutionally protected, it is necessary to advance in legal formulas that recognize the auxiliary and particularly collaborative role played by private security, so that, in addition to functionally integrating its capabilities into the public security system, it makes them participants in the information that is necessary for the better fulfillment of their duties” and that “In summary, it can be said that the set of changes proposed in the new law, in addition to improving and resolving technical, management, and operational problems, decisively deepens the current Spanish model of private security (complementary, subordinate, collaborative, and controlled by public security), betting on its preventive role for the benefit of general security, and it does so by taking advantage of and functionally integrating all its informational potential, human resources, and material means, at the service of the protection and security of the entire citizenry, in a manner compatible with the legitimate interest pursued by private security entities.”  
Starting from the above, Article 1 defines the object of the law as follows:  
1. This law aims to regulate the performance and provision by private individuals, whether natural or legal persons, of private security activities and services that, developed by them, are contracted, voluntarily or mandatorily, by natural or legal persons, public or private, for the protection of persons and property. It also regulates private investigations carried out on them. All these activities are considered complementary and subordinate to public security.  
2. Likewise, this law, for the benefit of public security, establishes the framework for the most efficient coordination of private security services with those of the Security Forces and Corps, of which they are complementary.  
For its part, Article 2 defines private security as “the set of activities, services, functions, and security measures adopted, voluntarily or mandatorily, by natural or legal persons, public or private, carried out or provided by security companies, private detective offices, and private security personnel to address deliberate acts or accidental risks, or to conduct inquiries about persons and property, with the aim of guaranteeing the safety of individuals, protecting their assets, and ensuring the normal development of their activities.”  
And Article 4 establishes its specific purposes:  
Private security has the following purposes:  
a) To satisfy the legitimate security or information needs of private security users, ensuring the safety or privacy of the persons or property whose security or investigation is entrusted to it against possible violations of rights, deliberate threats, and accidental risks or those arising from nature.  
b) To contribute to guaranteeing public security, to prevent offenses, and to provide information for procedures related to its actions and investigations.  
c) To complement the monopoly of security that corresponds to the State, functionally integrating its means and capabilities as an external resource of public security.

Developing this duty of collaboration, Articles 14 and 15 of the Law regulate the data communications that may be carried out between security companies, detective agencies, and private security personnel and the Security Forces and Corps, which must, in any case, respect the provisions established in the personal data protection regulations:

Article 14. Professional Collaboration.
1. The special obligation of collaboration of security companies, detective agencies, and private security personnel with the Security Forces and Corps will be carried out in accordance with the principle of legality and will be based exclusively on the necessity to ensure the proper outcome of actions aimed at preserving public safety, guaranteeing due reserve and confidentiality when necessary.
2. Security companies, detective agencies, and private security personnel must communicate to the competent Security Forces and Corps, as soon as possible, any circumstances or relevant information for the prevention, maintenance, or restoration of citizen security, as well as any criminal act of which they become aware in the exercise of their activity or functions, making available to them the presumed offenders, as well as the instruments, effects, and evidence related to them.
3. The Security Forces and Corps may provide private security personnel, in the exercise of their functions, with information that facilitates their risk assessment and subsequent implementation of protection measures. If this information contains personal data, it may only be provided in the event of a real danger to public safety or to prevent the commission of criminal offenses.

Article 15. Access to Information by the Security Forces and Corps.
1. The transfer of data that is deemed necessary to contribute to the safeguarding of citizen security is authorized, as well as access by the Security Forces and Corps to the systems installed by private security companies that allow for the verification of information in real-time when necessary for the prevention of a real danger to public safety or for the repression of criminal offenses.
2. The processing of personal data, as well as the files, automated or not, created for the fulfillment of this law will be subject to the provisions of the personal data protection regulations.
3. The good faith communication of information to the Security Forces and Corps by entities and private security personnel will not constitute a violation of the restrictions on the disclosure of information imposed by contractual means or by any legal, regulatory, or administrative provision, when necessary for the prevention of a real danger to public safety or for the repression of criminal offenses.

Based on this configuration of private security in our legal system, the consultation raises the possible application of the exclusion established in Article 2.2.d) of the GDPR, according to which:
2. This Regulation does not apply to the processing of personal data:
d) by competent authorities for the purposes of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses, or for the execution of criminal sanctions, including protection against threats to public safety and their prevention.

This exclusion is justified as a consequence of the existence of a specific regime applicable to such processing, as indicated in Recital 19:

The protection of natural persons regarding the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses or executing criminal sanctions, including protection against threats to public security and the free movement of such data and its prevention, is the subject of a specific legal act at the Union level. Member States may assign to the competent authorities, as defined in Directive (EU) 2016/680, functions that are not necessarily carried out for the purposes of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses or executing criminal sanctions, including protection against threats to public security and their prevention, in such a way that the processing of personal data for these other purposes, to the extent that it falls within the scope of Union law, falls within the scope of this Regulation.

Regarding the processing of personal data by such competent authorities for purposes that fall within the scope of this Regulation, Member States must have the possibility to maintain or introduce more specific provisions to adapt the application of the rules of this Regulation. This applies, for example, in the context of the fight against money laundering or the activities of scientific police laboratories.

Consequently, the processing of personal data by the competent authorities for the purposes referred to in Article 2.2.d) of the GDPR is subject to Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses or executing criminal sanctions, and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Framework Decision 2008/977/JHA of the Council.  
This Directive was approved on the same date as the GDPR and Directive (EU) 2016/681 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the use of Passenger Name Record (PNR) data for the prevention, detection, investigation, and prosecution of terrorism and serious crime, together forming what is known as the data protection reform package.  
Directive 2016/680, which has not yet been transposed into our legal system, is currently undergoing the corresponding Draft Law process. After stating in its Article 1.1 that “This Directive establishes the rules relating to the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses or executing criminal sanctions, including the protection and prevention against threats to public security,” it delineates its subjective scope of application in Article 2.1, in a clearly differentiated manner from that contemplated in the GDPR:  
“1. This Directive applies to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes established in Article 1, paragraph 1.”  
As this Agency has repeatedly pointed out, Directive 2016/680 establishes a special regime, to which only the processing regulated by it would be subject, as opposed to the general data protection regime set forth in the General Data Protection Regulation. For this reason, the provisions of the GDPR will apply to all processing carried out within the scope of Union law that is not specifically regulated by the Directive, as can be inferred from the scope of application established in Article 2 of the Regulation. Likewise, the special nature of the rule is equally applicable to the rule that adapts Spanish law to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), currently constituted by Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD).

Biometric identification: the identification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparing their biometric data (acquired at the time of identification) with a set of biometric templates stored in a database (i.e., a one-to-many matching process).
Biometric verification/authentication: the verification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparison between their biometric data (acquired at the time of verification) with a single biometric template stored on a device (i.e., a one-to-one matching process).

“In relation to facial recognition, ‘identification’ is understood as the process where the facial image template of a person is compared with many other templates stored in a database to determine if their image is stored therein. ‘Authentication’ (or ‘verification’), on the other hand, typically refers to the search for matches between two specific templates. Considering the aforementioned distinction, it can be interpreted that, according to Article 4 of the GDPR, the concept of biometric data would include both scenarios, namely identification and verification/authentication. However, in general terms, biometric data will only be considered a special category of data in cases where they are subjected to technical processing aimed at biometric identification (one-to-many) and not in the case of biometric verification/authentication (one-to-one). 

Nevertheless, this Agency considers that this is a complex issue, subject to interpretation, from which no general conclusions can be drawn, and it must be addressed on a case-by-case basis according to the data processed, the techniques used for their processing, and the consequent interference with the right to data protection. In the absence of a pronouncement on this matter by the European Data Protection Committee or the judicial bodies, the most favorable interpretation for the protection of the rights of the affected parties should be adopted in case of doubt.”

In the present case, it is undeniable that the use of facial recognition in the video surveillance systems employed in the field of private security would involve the processing of biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, in a one-to-many matching process, constituting the processing a special category of data whose processing is, in principle, prohibited by Article 9.1 of the GDPR.

III
Therefore, for the processing of biometric data by facial recognition systems integrated into a video surveillance system to be lawful, one of the exceptions that lift the prohibition of their processing must apply, in accordance with paragraph 2 of Article 9 of the GDPR. In this regard, the inquirer considers that the exclusion or prohibition of Article 9 of the GDPR, in relation to the processing of special categories of personal data, would not apply to the sectoral field of Private Security, governing the exception of paragraph 2 of this article, an exception that would be provided for the processing of data for private security companies and personnel in the private security regulations within the internal law of Spain.

However, this assertion cannot be shared by this Agency as there is no essential legal protection, in the terms required by Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR and by constitutional doctrine.

As we mentioned earlier, the processing of personal data necessary for the provision of the public service of higher education is generally legitimized by the existence of a public interest under the provisions of Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR. However, regarding special categories of data, the provision contemplated in letter g) of Article 9.2 does not refer solely to the existence of a public interest, as is the case in many other provisions of the GDPR, but is the only provision of the GDPR that requires that it be "essential," an adjective that qualifies said public interest, given the importance and need for greater protection of the processed data. 
This provision finds its precedent in Article 8.4 of Directive 95/46/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of October 24, 1995, on the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data: "4. Provided that they have adequate safeguards, Member States may, for reasons of substantial public interest, lay down other exceptions, in addition to those provided for in paragraph 2, either by means of their national legislation or by decision of the supervisory authority." However, from its reading, there is a greater rigor in the new regulation by the GDPR, as the adjective "substantial" is replaced by "essential," and it is not allowed for the exception to be established by the supervisory authorities. 
In relation to what should be understood as essential public interest, the case law of the European Court of Human Rights must also be taken into account, which, under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, has considered that the processing of personal data constitutes a lawful interference with the right to respect for private life and may only be carried out if it is in accordance with the law, serves a legitimate aim, respects the essence of the rights and fundamental freedoms, and is necessary and proportionate in a democratic society to achieve a legitimate aim (D.L. v. Bulgaria, no. 7472/14, May 19, 2016, Dragojević v. Croatia, no. 68955/11, January 15, 2015, Peck v. United Kingdom, no. 44647/98, January 28, 2003, Leander v. Furthermore, the doctrine of the Constitutional Court regarding restrictions on the fundamental right to data protection must be taken into account, which it summarizes in its ruling 292/2000, of November 30, in which, after establishing the fundamental right to the protection of personal data as an autonomous and independent right that consists of a power of disposal and control over personal data that empowers the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose that possession or use, analyzes its limits, stating the following:
More specifically, in the aforementioned rulings regarding data protection, this Court has declared that the right to data protection is not unlimited, and although the Constitution does not expressly impose specific limits on it, nor refers to the Public Authorities for its determination as it has done with other fundamental rights, there is no doubt that they must be found in the remaining fundamental rights and constitutionally protected legal goods, as required by the principle of unity of the Constitution (SSTC 11/1981, of April 8, F. 7; 196/1987, of December 11 [RTC 1987, 196], F. 6; and regarding art. 18, the STC 110/1984, F. 5). These limits may either be direct restrictions on the fundamental right itself, as previously mentioned, or they may be restrictions on the manner, time, or place of exercising the fundamental right. In the first case, regulating these limits is a way of developing the fundamental right. In the second, the limits set are to the specific manner in which the bundle of powers that makes up the content of the fundamental right in question can be exercised, constituting a way to regulate its exercise, which the ordinary legislator can do in accordance with the provisions of art. 53.1 CE. The first observation that must be made, which is no less crucial for being evident, is that the Constitution has intended that the Law, and only the Law, can set the limits to a fundamental right. Fundamental rights can yield, of course, to goods, and even constitutionally relevant interests, as long as the reduction they experience is necessary to achieve the legitimate aim provided, proportionate to achieve it, and, in any case, respectful of the essential content of the restricted fundamental right (SSTC 57/1994, of February 28 [RTC 1994, 57], F. 6; 18/1999, of February 22 [RTC 1999, 18], F. 2).

Indeed, if the Law is the only authority enabled by the Constitution to set limits on fundamental rights and, in the present case, on the fundamental right to data protection, and those limits cannot be different from those constitutionally provided, which in this case are none other than those derived from the coexistence of this fundamental right with other rights and legal goods of constitutional rank, the legal empowerment that allows a Public Authority to collect, store, process, use, and, where appropriate, transfer personal data is only justified if it responds to the protection of other fundamental rights or constitutionally protected goods. Therefore, if those operations with the personal data of a person are not carried out with strict observance of the rules that regulate them, the right to data protection is violated, as constitutionally illegitimate limits are imposed on its content or on the exercise of the bundle of powers that compose it. As that limiting Law will also infringe upon this right if it regulates the limits in such a way that makes the affected fundamental right impracticable or the guarantee that the Constitution grants ineffective. The issue can only have a constitutional answer. The provision of adequate guarantees cannot be deferred to a moment after the legal regulation of the processing of personal data in question. The adequate guarantees must be incorporated into the legal regulation of the processing itself, either directly or by express and perfectly delineated reference to external sources that have the appropriate normative rank. Only that understanding is compatible with the dual requirement that emanates from Article 53.1 of the Spanish Constitution (RCL 1978, 2836) for the legislator of fundamental rights: the reservation of law for the regulation of the exercise of the fundamental rights recognized in Chapter Two of Title One of the Constitution and the respect for the essential content of those fundamental rights. 
According to reiterated constitutional doctrine, the reservation of law is not limited to requiring that a law enables the restrictive measure of fundamental rights, but it is also necessary, according to both the so-called requirements of normative predetermination and the quality of the law, as well as respect for the essential content of the right, that in that regulation the legislator, who is primarily obliged to weigh the conflicting rights or interests, predetermines the assumptions, conditions, and guarantees under which the adoption of restrictive measures of fundamental rights is appropriate. On the contrary, the current regulation is considered insufficient to allow the use of facial recognition techniques in video surveillance systems employed by private security, as it does not meet the previously mentioned requirements. It is necessary for a law to be approved that specifically justifies to what extent and under what circumstances the use of such systems would respond to an essential public interest, defining such legal norm, after weighing by the legislator the conflicting interests according to the principle of proportionality, all and each of the material prerequisites of the limiting measure through precise rules, which make it foreseeable for the interested party the imposition of such limitation and its consequences, and establishing the appropriate technical, organizational, and procedural guarantees that prevent risks of varying probability and severity and mitigate their effects.

Should such a norm be processed, it must be mandatorily informed by this Agency, at which point it could be assessed whether it aligns with the stated criteria, without a priori establishing a definitive criterion on our part. However, it can be anticipated that, considering the principle of proportionality and the necessity judgment, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness, the existence of other measures that allow the protection of persons, property, and facilities with less intrusion into the rights of the affected would require a special justification for the necessity of opting for facial recognition over such other measures.

Thus, this Agency considers that there are exceptional cases in which the use of facial recognition systems could be justified, provided that the legislation, in the terms previously mentioned, so provides, as could be the case for critical infrastructures, understood as those whose “operation is indispensable and does not allow alternative solutions, so that their disruption or destruction would have a serious impact on essential services,” according to Law 8/2011, of April 28, which establishes measures for the protection of critical infrastructures. In this case, the adequate protection of the same aims to guarantee the safety of citizens and the proper functioning of essential services, so the authorization by the legislator for the use of facial recognition techniques, establishing the appropriate guarantees, could be considered proportional.

However, the general authorization for the use of facial recognition systems in video surveillance systems employed by private security, as proposed in the consultation, would be considered, by this Agency, as disproportionate, given the intrusion and risks it poses to the fundamental rights of citizens.
