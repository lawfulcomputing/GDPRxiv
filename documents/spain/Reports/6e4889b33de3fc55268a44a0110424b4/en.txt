The inquiry refers to the adequacy of data protection regulations concerning the monographic work titled “Francoist Criminal Law and Homosexuality: From Sin and Sexual Aberration to the State of Dangerousness,” which the Ministry of Justice is interested in publishing, as it includes names, surnames, and other personal data of judges, magistrates, forensic doctors, and other officials present in various judicial cases that, under the application of the criminal regulations in force at the time, were pursued during the Franco regime against various individuals accused of homosexuality. This inquiry arises after the issuance of a preliminary report by the Data Protection Delegate of the aforementioned ministry and after the names and surnames of the accused have been anonymized and the reproductions of the documents from the judicial files have been removed from the work, questioning the appropriateness of publishing the personal data of the others mentioned in the work in their capacity as professionals (judges, magistrates, forensic doctors, and other officials) without the need to obtain their consent or that of their relatives in cases of the interested parties' death.  

This inquiry is presented in light of the report issued by this Legal Office on May 25, 2010, highlighting that it is unknown who among the affected may have passed away and that they are officials and professionals in the exercise of their functions, and that at least in the case of judges and magistrates, their appointments are public.  

I  
Considering the competencies of this Agency, this report is issued solely concerning the field of personal data protection, for which it must adhere to the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons concerning the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR), fully applicable since May 25, 2018, and to Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDPGDD).  

The aforementioned Regulation extends its protection, as established in its Article 1.2, to the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, their right to the protection of personal data, defined in its Article 4.1 as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  

Furthermore, Article 4.1 defines “processing” as “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction.”  

Therefore, the publication of the work “Francoist Criminal Law and Homosexuality: From Sin and Sexual Aberration to the State of Dangerousness,” insofar as it contains personal data relating to natural persons, is subject to the provisions of the GDPR.  

However, since its initial reports, the AEPD has maintained that the right to data protection, as a personality right, ceases upon the death of the individual in accordance with Article 32 of the Civil Code. This was already noted in a report dated May 20, 1999 (when the LORTAD was in force) and has been reiterated in numerous reports since then, which was included in Article 2.4 of the Regulation implementing Organic Law 15/1999 (Royal Decree 1720/2007, of December 21):  

“This regulation shall not apply to data relating to deceased persons. However, individuals linked to the deceased, for family or analogous reasons, may contact the data controllers or processors containing data about them to notify the death, providing sufficient proof thereof, and request, where appropriate, the deletion of the data.”  

The same criterion is followed by the GDPR, whose Recital 27 states: “This Regulation does not apply to the protection of personal data of deceased persons. Member States are competent to establish rules regarding the processing of personal data of such persons,” a criterion that is reiterated in Recitals 158 and 160.  

Based on this provision, the draft Organic Law, after excluding from its scope of application in its Article 2.2.b “the processing of data of deceased persons,” regulates the personal data of deceased persons in its Article 3:

II 

Regarding living individuals, the right to the protection of personal data recognized in Article 18.4 of the Constitution must be respected, which guarantees them, as declared by the Constitutional Court in its ruling 292/2000 of November 30, “a power of control over their personal data, over its use and destination, with the purpose of preventing its illicit trafficking and harm to the dignity and rights of the affected party.” However, as declared by the highest interpreter of the Constitution, it is not an unlimited right “and although the Constitution does not expressly impose specific limits on it, nor refers to the Public Authorities for its determination as it has done with other fundamental rights, there is no doubt that they must...

In this regard, the ruling of the National Court dated January 22, 2019, can be cited, whose fourth legal basis summarizes the constitutional doctrine regarding the balancing between the right to data protection and the rights to freedom of information and expression recognized by Article 20 of the Constitution, highlighting the differences that exist in cases involving individuals exercising public functions:

FOURTH.- As we have stated in similar cases to the present one (rulings of May 15, 2017 (R. 30/16); ruling of June 19, 2017, (R.1842/15) and ruling of July 18, 2017, (R. 114/16)), for the correct approach to the issues raised in the present appeal, it is important to emphasize that the Constitutional Court, in ruling 39/2016, of March 3, recalling what was already reasoned in ruling 292/2000, declares that: “[...] the right to data protection is not unlimited, and although the Constitution does not expressly impose specific limits on it, nor refers to public authorities for its determination as it has done with other fundamental rights, there is no doubt that they must find them in the remaining fundamental rights and constitutionally protected legal goods, as required by the principle of unity of the Constitution [...]”.

Based on the above, and in light of the parties' arguments, the issue raised in the present procedure is confined to the balancing of rights and interests in confrontation. To this end, it is considered necessary, first, to delineate the object and content of the fundamental rights at stake, as this Chamber has done on previous occasions when identical legal controversies have arisen.

Following the aforementioned Constitutional Court ruling, it must be affirmed that the fundamental right to data protection, enshrined in Article 18.4 of the Spanish Constitution, unlike the right to privacy in Article 18.1 CE, which shares the objective of providing effective constitutional protection of personal and family privacy, excluding external knowledge and intrusions by third parties against their will, seeks to guarantee that individual a power of control over their personal data, its use and destination, with the purpose of preventing its illicit and harmful trafficking to the dignity and rights of the affected individual.

The right to data protection, therefore, has a broader object than that of the right to privacy, since the fundamental right to data protection extends its guarantee not only to privacy in its constitutionally protected dimension by Article 18.1 CE, but also to the sphere of personal goods that belong to the realm of private life, inseparably linked to the respect for personal dignity, such as the right

to the honor, and to the full exercise of the rights of the person. The fundamental right to data protection extends the constitutional guarantee to those data that are relevant or have an impact on the exercise of any rights of the person, whether or not they are constitutional rights and whether or not they relate to honor, ideology, personal and family privacy, or any other constitutionally protected good.

Thus, the object of the fundamental right to data protection is not limited solely to the intimate data of the person, but to any type of personal data, whether intimate or not, whose knowledge or use by third parties may affect their rights, whether or not fundamental—such as those that identify or allow the identification of the person, which may serve to create their ideological, racial, sexual, economic profile, or of any other nature, or that serve any other utility that under certain circumstances constitutes a threat to the individual—because its object is not only individual privacy, already protected by Article 18.1 of the Spanish Constitution (CE), but personal data. Consequently, it also encompasses those public personal data that, by being so, accessible to anyone's knowledge, do not escape the power of disposition of the affected individual because their right to data protection guarantees it.

Regarding the right to freedom of expression, in light of the doctrine of the Constitutional Court (SSTC 23/2010, of April 27, and 9/2007, of January 15) enshrined in Article 20 of the Constitution, it includes, along with the mere expression of thoughts, beliefs, ideas, opinions, and value judgments, the criticism of the conduct of another, even when it may be harsh and could annoy, disturb, or upset the person it is directed to, as pluralism, tolerance, and a spirit of openness require, without which there is no democratic society.

Freedom of expression is broader than freedom of information as it does not operate under the internal limit of truthfulness applicable to the latter, which is justified by the fact that it aims to present subjective ideas, opinions, or value judgments that are not subject to demonstration of their accuracy, nor by their abstract nature are they susceptible to proof, and not to establish facts or assert objective data. Nevertheless, this difference does not prevent affirming that both constitute individual rights held by all natural persons and that can be exercised through speech, writing, or any other means of reproduction, without prejudice to the fact that when such freedoms are exercised by information professionals through an institutionalized vehicle for the formation of public opinion, their degree of protection reaches its maximum level (STC 165/1987, of October 27).

In short, the recognition of freedom of expression guarantees the development of free public communication that allows the free circulation of ideas and value judgments inherent to the principle of democratic legitimacy. In this sense, the dissemination of ideas deserves special constitutional protection.

However, just as with other fundamental rights, the exercise of the right to freedom of expression is subject to constitutional limits that the Constitutional Court has progressively defined.

Thus, it does not protect the presence of injurious, offensive, and insulting phrases and expressions that are unrelated to the ideas or opinions being expressed and, therefore, unnecessary for this purpose, nor does it protect the dissemination of facts that are merely rumors, fabrications, or unfounded insinuations, nor does it provide shelter for slanders or insults, as it does not recognize a purported right to insult.

In addition, the expansive trend of freedom of expression also finds its limit in respect for the normative content guaranteed by other fundamental rights, whose infringement is not necessary for the constitutional realization of the right. This delimitation can only be achieved through the appropriate balancing of the conflicting constitutional values, among which the guarantee of the existence of public opinion stands out, inextricably linked to political pluralism. It should be noted that, as recognized by section 4 of Article 20 of the Spanish Constitution (CE), all the freedoms recognized in the provision have their limit in the right to honor, privacy, personal image, and the protection of youth and childhood, which serve a "limiting function" in relation to these freedoms.

Therefore, the protection of these other constitutional rights recognized by Article 20.4 CE is weakened in the face of the freedoms of expression and information when exercised in connection with matters of general interest, due to the subjects involved and the individuals contributing to the formation of public opinion, as occurs when it affects public figures who perform public functions or are involved in matters of public relevance, thus obliged to bear a certain risk that their subjective personal rights may be affected by opinions or information of general interest (Constitutional Court Judgments 107/1988, of June 8, 20/2002, of January 28, 160/2003, of September 15, 151/2004, of September 20, and 9/2007, of January 15).

III 

Considering that the work intended for publication aims at historical research, it is appropriate to bring to attention what the Constitutional Court has stated regarding the scientific freedom of historians in its ruling 43/2004 of March 23: 

However, the peculiarities surrounding this constitutional process advise against limiting our analysis to the possible confrontation of the aforementioned fundamental right with the freedoms of expression and information; rather, it is necessary, in line with what has been expressed by the Civil Chamber of the Supreme Court, to also refer to the scientific freedom of the historian (fifth legal basis). 

In fact, it is pertinent to recall, first of all, that the documentary refers to a judicial process (sic) conducted and judged in 1937, in the context of the Civil War, a tragedy whose effects have shaped “the harsh reality of history” (STC 28/1982, of May 26 [RTC 1982, 28], F. 2). The questioned documentary deals with historical facts that present undeniable public relevance, in which the life journey of Mr. Carrasco i Formiguera is recounted, including how he was arrested and executed. It is important to note, in order to confine our assessment of the case to its proper terms, that this is not about examining the report in question as if it were a chronicle of a judicial process of our days, as the appellants claim and as the first and second instance rulings have done. In reality, the manner in which a journalist or a media outlet has carried out a judicial chronicle and the greater or lesser accuracy with which they technically qualified the actions of a witness in the process is not being discussed here. Addressing the case at hand in this way would constitute a serious distortion of its precise object. What is being discussed here is the way in which information has been provided about an event in our recent history, as well as the opinions that the journalist who prepared the script for the television report had regarding the participation in that historical event of several individuals, including the father of the current appellants for protection. We are not being asked, therefore, to now judge information and opinions expressed about an event of the present, but rather to examine statements.

Well, it is possible to deduce that scientific freedom – in what is now of interest, the historical debate – enjoys in our Constitution an enhanced protection compared to that which operates for the freedoms of expression and information, since while the latter refer to current facts involving individuals of the present, the former, also participating in contents inherent to the freedoms of expression and information – as it is still a narration of facts and an expression of opinions and evaluations and, consequently, information and free expression for the purposes of art. 20.1 a) and d) CE – always refers to facts of the past and involving individuals whose personality, in the constitutional sense of the term (their free development is the foundation of the political order and social peace: art. 10.1 CE), has necessarily diluted over time and cannot therefore be opposed, as a limit to scientific freedom, with the same scope and intensity with which the dignity of the living opposes the exercise of the freedoms of expression and information of their contemporaries. Moreover, only in this way is historical research made possible, which is always, by definition, controversial and debatable, as it is built around assertions and value judgments about whose objective truth it is impossible to achieve full certainty, given that this uncertainty inherent to the historical debate represents what is most valuable, respectable, and worthy of protection, due to the essential role it plays in the formation of a historical consciousness appropriate to the dignity of the citizens of a free and democratic society.

As we stated in our STC 214/1991, of November 11 (RTC 1991, 214), F. 7, the "requirement of truthfulness cannot, as is obvious, be demanded regarding personal and subjective judgments or evaluations, no matter how erroneous or ill-intentioned they may be, about historical facts." To which we have added in our STC 176/1995, of December 11 (RTC 1995, 176), F. 2, that "freedom of expression includes the freedom to err, and any other attitude in this regard enters the realm of dogmatism... The assertion of absolute truth, conceptually distinct from truthfulness as a requirement of information, is the permanent temptation of those who yearn for prior censorship... Our judgment must at all times be detached from the correctness or incorrectness in the approach to the issues or the greater or lesser accuracy of the proposed solutions, devoid of any possibility of absolute certainty or unanimous agreement by their very nature, without making any value judgment on intrinsically debatable issues, nor sharing or disagreeing with opinions in a controversial context." This must be even more so for the freedoms of expression and information inherent in the exercise of scientific freedom in the historical field.

On one hand, because, as we have just said, the distance in time dilutes the obstructive condition of personality in relation to the exercise of the freedoms of Article 20 CE. On the other hand, because the framing of an activity within the scope of historical research and, therefore, in the scientific field already implies a reinforcement of the requirements established by Article 20 CE regarding the truthfulness of the information provided by the researcher, that is, regarding their diligence. For all these reasons, research on events involving deceased individuals must prevail, in its public dissemination, over the right to honor of such individuals when it effectively conforms to the uses and methods characteristic of historiographical science.

As we have said regarding the freedom of information, scientific freedom also entails a subjective participation of its author, both in the way of interpreting the sources that serve as the basis for their narrative and in the choice of how to do so (ECtHR case Lingens, of July 8, 1986 [ECHR 1986, 8], § 41; STC 171/1990, of November 12 [RTC 1990, 171], F. 5; 173/1995, of November 21 [RTC 1995, 173], F. 2; 192/1999, of October 25 [RTC 1999, 192], F. 4; 297/2000, of December 11 [RTC 2000, 297], F. 10). This Court has already stated that the truthfulness constitutionally required of information does not impose in any way that the possibility of investigating the origin or cause of the facts be excluded, nor could it be done without violating the freedom of expression of Article 20.1 a) CE, nor the formulation of hypotheses in this regard, nor the evaluation of those same hypotheses or conjectures (STC 171/1990, of November 12 [RTC 1990, 171]). The same must be said of the scientific freedom of Article 20.1 b) CE.

Starting from this constitutional configuration of historical research, and from the perspective of data protection, the processing of personal data of living individuals for scientific or historical research purposes is subject to the GDPR, as expressly stated in its Recital 160: “This Regulation shall also apply to the processing of personal data carried out for historical research purposes. This also includes historical research and research for genealogical purposes, considering that this Regulation does not apply to deceased persons.” However, a series of exceptions and limitations to the general rules are established in order to facilitate the same, based on the provisions of Article 89 and Recital 156 of the GDPR: 

  
Article 89 Guarantees and exceptions applicable to processing for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes. 

1. Processing for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall be subject to adequate safeguards, in accordance with this Regulation, for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. Such safeguards shall ensure that technical and organizational measures are in place, in particular to ensure compliance with the principle of data minimization. Such measures may include pseudonymization, provided that these purposes can be achieved in this way. Whenever those purposes can be achieved through further processing that does not allow or no longer allows the identification of the data subjects, those purposes shall be achieved in that manner.  
2. When personal data are processed for scientific or historical research or statistical purposes, Union or Member State law may provide for exceptions to the rights laid down in Articles 15, 16, 18, and 21, subject to the conditions and safeguards referred to in paragraph 1 of this Article, provided that it is likely that those rights would seriously impede or obstruct the achievement of the scientific purposes and that such exceptions are necessary to achieve those purposes.  
3. When personal data are processed for archiving purposes in the public interest, Union or Member State law may provide for exceptions to the rights laid down in Articles 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, and 21, subject to the conditions and safeguards mentioned in paragraph 1 of this Article, provided that those rights may seriously impede or obstruct the achievement of the scientific purposes and that such exceptions are necessary to achieve those purposes.  
4. In the event that the processing referred to in paragraphs 2 and 3 also serves another purpose at the same time, the exceptions shall only apply to the processing for the purposes mentioned in those paragraphs.

V

For all the reasons stated above, given that it concerns a historical investigation and personal data relating to authorities and public officials connected with the exercise of their functions that clearly have public interest, the publication of such data is not contrary to the regulations on the protection of personal data.
