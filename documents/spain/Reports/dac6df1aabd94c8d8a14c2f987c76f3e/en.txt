The inquiry raises a series of issues that should be analyzed separately, although all are connected by the common thread of the exercise of the competencies of the consulting entity.  
I  
In the first of the issues raised, reference is made to the preparation of the records containing data on both fatal and non-fatal victims in cases of gender-based violence. It is explained what the content and "journey" (iter) of the personal data collected in these records is. The question posed is whether this content and its journey comply with the principles of the LOPD, and particularly with the security standards established for data related to gender-based violence (high-level security measures).  
Starting with this last aspect, it should be noted that a substantial modification has occurred between what is prescribed (i) in Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data (LOPD), and its implementing regulation, approved by Royal Decree 1720/2007, of December 21 (RLOPD), and (ii) Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR). In this latter regulation, fully applicable from May 25, 2018, there is no longer a list of specific security measures that the data controller must establish in all cases, as was done in the LOPD and RLOPD, but rather, under the principle of active responsibility, Article 32 of the GDPR establishes:  
Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the varying likelihood and severity of risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a)  
the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b)  
the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c)  
the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d)  
a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of technical and organizational measures to ensure processing security.  

Therefore, it is the responsibility of the data controller, with the assistance, where appropriate, of the Data Protection Officer (DPO), to determine the specific measures necessary for the security and confidentiality of the data handled.  

Regarding these security measures, considering the journey of the data as it has been presented in the inquiry, from the moment sensitive data is processed (disability status, if applicable; identification of victim and aggressor, etc.), it appears that the records are sent via email to a corporate address managed by a specific sub-directorate; nothing is mentioned about the technical or organizational measures applied to that email to prevent its opening, reading, or use by those who do not need access to such data. Nor are there any technical and organizational measures mentioned that are applied to the processing carried out in the General Sub-Directorate of awareness, prevention, and knowledge to also prevent access by individuals whose functions do not include the processing of such data.  

As for the content of the records, and given that inquiries are made regarding the applicable principles in personal data protection regulations, it should be noted that in this case, the applicable principle is the principle of "data minimization." According to Article 5.1 c) of the GDPR, personal data shall be c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ("data minimization"). It is up to the data controller to decide exactly what data should be included in these records from the perspective of the data minimization principle, who must examine the content of the records, now that it seems they will be modified in format, to ensure that no data that is superfluous or unnecessary for the activities, purposes, and objectives for which they serve in these records is included. In that case, the processing of personal data that is not necessary in relation to the purposes for which it will be processed would be considered contrary to personal data protection regulations.  

Regarding the journey of the data, it is difficult to determine whether there could be a shorter journey that minimizes the risks associated with the processing of sensitive category personal data in a chain of multiple links. II

Regarding statistical confidentiality, it is obvious that the regulations related to statistical confidentiality apply when we are dealing with statistical data. Thus, Article 13 of Law 12/1989, of May 9, on the Public Statistical Function establishes:

Article 13.

1. Personal data obtained by statistical services, both directly from informants and through administrative sources, shall be protected and covered by statistical confidentiality.

2. Personal data refers to information concerning natural or legal persons that either allows for the immediate identification of the interested parties or leads, due to its structure, content, or degree of disaggregation, to their indirect identification.

3. Statistical confidentiality obliges statistical services not to disclose personal data under any circumstances, regardless of its origin.

Therefore, statistical regulations also protect data considered "statistical" even if it pertains to deceased individuals or relates to legal entities.

However, this generally applies to any data that has the character of "statistical"; but Article 14 establishes a possible exception for the communication of data and refers to Article 15. In its paragraph 1, Article 15 refers to communication for statistical purposes, and in its paragraph 2 refers to communication for "non-statistical" purposes of information contained in public records, which will not be subject to statistical confidentiality but to the specific legislation applicable in each case. Therefore, for communication for "non-statistical" purposes, the provisions established in the applicable regulations will apply, and such data processing will, of course, be subject to personal data protection regulations when applicable.

IV

Next, the inquiry addresses certain data requests made to the system control center (COMETA) by the State Security Forces and Corps. It refers to inquiries that the security forces make regarding the personal data recorded in the telematic tracking system in the context of gender-based violence; that is, data of users (victims or accused) of the telematic tracking system (bracelets with GPS and a warning system if entering a security radius around the victim). Examples of these requests are provided.

The data processing by the security forces and corps for the investigation and prevention of crimes is governed by Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offenses or the execution of criminal sanctions, and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Framework Decision 2008/977/JHA of the Council (Directive 2016/80). This directive has not yet been transposed into Spanish law, so the fourth transitional provision of the organic bill on data protection currently under parliamentary processing in the Cortes establishes that such processing will continue to be governed by the LOPD, and in particular by its article 22, and by its development provisions, until the norm that transposes the provisions of the aforementioned directive into Spanish law comes into force.

Consequently, the data processing carried out by the security forces and corps would be subject to Article 22 of the LOPD, and therefore the doctrine established by this Agency in the repeatedly cited reports (among others) 0133/2008 and 0297/2005 would also continue to apply, which essentially state:

[…] those actions of the Judicial Police that are carried out in compliance with a judicial mandate or a request made by the Public Prosecutor's Office must be distinguished from those others that are carried out on their own initiative or at the request of their hierarchical superior.

Regarding the former, Article 11.2 d) of Organic Law 15/1999 applies, and the consent of the data subject is not required for the transfer, since the Judicial Police officers requesting the data are merely transmitters of the request made by the Public Prosecutor's Office or the Judicial Body, acting in compliance with the functions that have been legally attributed to them, and the Court or Tribunal or the Public Prosecutor's Office is the recipient of the transferred data, as required by the referenced article.

The problem arises, however, in relation to those cases where the Judicial Police request the transfer of data in order to exercise the functions of investigating the crime and apprehending the responsible party, as there is no judicial mandate or request from the Public Prosecutor's Office that covers the transfer in that case.

In this case, we find ourselves, in our opinion, before the exercise by the Judicial Police officers of functions that, while expressly recognized by their regulatory provisions, are identified with those attributed, in general, to all members of the State Security Forces and Corps.

Consequently, what is provided in Article 22.2 of Organic Law 15/1999 will apply to this second case, according to which "The collection and processing for police purposes of personal data by the Security Forces and Corps without the consent of the affected persons are limited to those cases and categories of data that are necessary for the prevention of a real danger to public safety or for the repression of criminal offenses, and must be stored in specific files established for this purpose, which must be classified by categories based on their degree of reliability."

The aforementioned article empowers, in our opinion, the members of the Judicial Police to obtain and process the required data, which will entail the legitimacy of the requested transfer, provided that, as indicated in the report of the General Commissariat of the Judicial Police attached to the inquiry and as this Spanish Agency for Data Protection has repeatedly indicated, the following conditions are met:

a) That it is duly accredited that the obtaining of the data is necessary for the prevention of a real and serious danger to public safety or for the repression of criminal offenses and that, in the case of specially protected data, they are absolutely necessary for the purposes of a specific investigation.

With reference to the last of the conclusions mentioned, it should be indicated that, in the context of actions carried out within the scope of the competencies enshrined in section a) of Article 445.1 of the Organic Law of the Judiciary, the Judicial Police is therefore obliged to report the facts to the Judicial and Prosecutorial Authority immediately, and the destruction of the record of the obtained data must proceed once that communication has been made.

Furthermore, it should be recalled that, as provided in Article 11.2 d) of Organic Law 15/1999, the transfer will proceed if it is addressed to the Public Prosecutor's Office or to Judges or Courts, which, as noted, occurs in the present case, given the obligation of the members of the Judicial Police to inform the Judicial or Prosecutorial Authority of the data obtained. Therefore, the requested transfer will be supported not only by Article 22.2 of Organic Law 15/1999 but also by Article 11.2 d) of the same law.

Based on the doctrine contained in these reports, it is then possible to respond to the questions raised in the consultation.

To the first, it is that, indeed, requests from the Judicial Police units of the security forces must be addressed by the control center, in accordance with the grounds set forth, and provided that the required conditions are met. This implies, on the other hand, that it must be reflected, at least minimally in a way that does not jeopardize the investigation but allows the data controller to be certain that the information request complies with the stated requirements, that is, that they can determine (since they remain responsible for the processing and the transfer of data that involves providing information to the police from the perspective of data protection regulations) whether the obtaining of the data is necessary for the prevention of a real and serious danger to public safety or for the repression of criminal offenses and that, in the case of specially protected data, it is absolutely necessary for the purposes of a specific investigation; that it is a concrete and specific request, not generic or merely diffuse; that the request is expressly justified in relation to the previous circumstances; and that subsequently the data obtained by the Judicial Police will be deleted when they are no longer necessary for the inquiry that motivated their request.

This specific regime applies exclusively to the security forces acting within the scope of the stated requirements or characteristics, fundamentally when their action aims at either the prevention of a real danger to public safety or the repression of criminal offenses; not in any other case, in which case Article 22.1 of the LOPD establishes that the data collected for administrative purposes by the security forces will be subject to the general regime provided in that law.

The third question is answered with what was responded to in the first of these: indeed, they must sufficiently justify, within the scope of the mentioned requirements, the conditions and purpose of their request, substantiating it according to the already reiterated characteristics.

Regarding whether it is necessary for requests from the security forces to be formalized in writing, it should be noted that the GDPR establishes that it is the responsibility of the data controller to demonstrate the characteristics and conditions of the same. Thus, Article 24.1 of the GDPR states:

Taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with this Regulation. Such measures shall be reviewed and updated when necessary.

Consequently, it is up to the controller to determine whether a written request is necessary or, depending on the circumstances, whether a telephone request will suffice. From a prudential standpoint, it should be said that a written request detailing the circumstances required in the cited reports to determine whether the processing/transfer of personal data to the security forces meets the mentioned requirements is generally considered better. Certainly, in certain urgent cases involving a real danger or the prevention of a criminal offense, there could be instances where data is requested by phone, but these must necessarily be exceptions to the general rule. In any case, and even if they were made by phone exceptionally, this does not preclude the requirement that the reasons justifying the processing must also be presented.

V

Regarding the issues related to the right of users to access personal data existing in the telematic control system, in relation to data protection regulations, this Agency considers, based on the exposition made in the consultation, that the actions of the consultant in this regard have been correct in the three issues raised.

The GDPR regulates the right of access of the data subject in its Article 15, specifying in its paragraph 3 that the data controller shall provide a copy of the personal data subject to processing, but its paragraph 4 establishes that the right to obtain a copy mentioned in the previous paragraph shall not adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others.
