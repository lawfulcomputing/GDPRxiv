The inquiry is made in light of the 50th anniversary of the terrorist attack perpetrated against the then President of the Government and refers to the application of regulations on the protection of personal data concerning the request for access to the summary instructed for this purpose by a production company with the aim of illustrating a television documentary series.  
The inquiry is accompanied by the Report issued by the Secretary of Government of the Superior Court of Justice of Madrid, as the summary is deposited among the existing documentary funds in the Judicial Archive of the Community of Madrid, dependent on that Secretary of Government.  
In this inquiry, the following considerations regarding access to certain personal data contained in the judicial proceedings that make up the aforementioned judicial process are included:  
1) Regarding the officials involved in the aforementioned judicial process, such as judges, police inspectors, doctors, experts, etc. As for the names and surnames of public officials who have intervened in the process in that capacity, such as Judges and Magistrates, Lawyers of the Administration of Justice, Police, Forensic Doctors, etc., we believe that in these specific cases anonymization would not be necessary as they are public officials acting in the exercise of the functions assigned to them.  
However, in the case of police or Civil Guard officials, if they appear in the proceedings without their names and surnames but with their professional identification number, anonymization of this data would not be necessary. Conversely, if these public officials appear in the proceedings with their names and surnames, anonymization would be necessary.  
On the other hand, in the case of Judges and Lawyers, many of the resolutions they have issued throughout the process (Orders, Judgments, Decrees, etc.) have been published in full, so it would not be logical to anonymize such data.  
Regarding other types of participants who may have intervened in the aforementioned judicial process, for example, as experts who are not public officials, it would be appropriate to anonymize their personal data.  
2) In relation to the deceased persons in the attack, the personal data of the deceased parties fall outside the scope of application of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and guarantee of digital rights, according to its article 2, 2 b).  
However, article 3 of the aforementioned legal norm contains regulations on access, rectification, or deletion of personal data of deceased persons.  
Now, since more than twenty-five years have passed since their death, their personal data could be consulted, in accordance with the provisions of article 57, 1 c) of the Spanish Historical Heritage Law.

I
Before outlining this Agency's criteria regarding access by interested third parties to personal data in cases analogous to the present one, a reference must be made to the specific legal regime applicable to judicial archives and the nature of the corresponding personal data processing, based on the regulations contained in Organic Law 6/1985, of July 1, on the Judiciary (LOPJ).

The legal regime of these archives derives from the provisions contained in Article 235 of the LOPJ, which includes an express reference to the application of the regulations on personal data protection:

Article 235.
Access to judicial resolutions, or to certain aspects of them, or to other procedural actions, by those who are not parties to the proceedings and can demonstrate a legitimate and direct interest, may be carried out prior to dissociation, anonymization, or other protective measures of the personal data contained therein, and with full respect for the right to privacy, the rights of individuals who require a special duty of protection, or the guarantee of anonymity for victims or affected parties, when appropriate.

This regime is further developed by Royal Decree 937/2003, of July 18, on the modernization of judicial archives, whose Article 4 distinguishes between a) Management Judicial Archives, b) Territorial Judicial Archives, and c) Central Judicial Archive. Its Article 1.2 defines a judicial archive as both the organic set of judicial documents and the place where judicial documents are duly safeguarded and classified, in accordance with the operational rules established in Chapter II.

Article 12 regulates access to the documentation of the Territorial Judicial Archive in the following terms:

Article 12. Access to the documentation of the Territorial or Central Judicial Archive.
1. The documentation preserved in the Territorial Judicial Archives and in the Central Archive shall at all times be available to the judicial body to which it belongs.
2. The judicial body from which the document originates, through a request from its secretary, may require the Territorial or Central Judicial Archive to provide its original, a copy, or a certification issued by the archive manager, as well as any information it deems necessary. If originals of documents are provided, they must be returned to the Territorial or Central Judicial Archive as soon as the reason for the request ceases to exist.
3. The right to data protection, therefore, has a broader scope than the right to privacy, as the fundamental right to data protection extends its guarantee not only to privacy in its constitutionally protected dimension under Article 18.1 of the Spanish Constitution (CE), but also to the sphere of personal rights that belong to the realm of private life, inseparably linked to the respect for personal dignity, such as the right to honor, and to the full exercise of individual rights. The fundamental right to data protection broadens the constitutional guarantee to those data that are relevant or have an impact on the exercise of any individual rights, whether or not they are constitutional rights and whether or not they relate to honor, ideology, personal and family privacy, or any other constitutionally protected good.  
In this way, the object of the fundamental right to data protection is not limited only to the intimate data of the individual, but to any type of personal data, whether intimate or not, whose knowledge or use by third parties may affect their rights, whether or not they are fundamental—such as those that identify or allow the identification of the individual, which may serve to create their ideological, racial, sexual, economic profile, or of any other nature, or that serve any other purpose that in certain circumstances constitutes a threat to the individual—because its object is not only individual intimacy, already protected by Article 18.1 CE, but personal data. Consequently, it also encompasses those public personal data that, by being public and accessible to anyone, do not escape the affected individual's power of disposition because such is guaranteed by their right to data protection.

Freedom of expression is broader than freedom of information as it does not operate under the internal limit of truthfulness applicable to the latter. This is justified by the fact that it aims to present ideas, opinions, or subjective value judgments that cannot be demonstrated for their accuracy, nor are they susceptible to proof due to their abstract nature, and not to establish facts or assert objective data. Nevertheless, this difference does not prevent us from asserting that both constitute individual rights held by all natural persons and can be exercised through speech, writing, or any other means of reproduction, without prejudice to the fact that when such freedoms are exercised by information professionals through an institutionalized vehicle for the formation of public opinion, their level of protection reaches its maximum (STC 165/1987, of October 27).

Ultimately, the recognition of freedom of expression guarantees the development of free public communication that allows the free circulation of ideas and value judgments inherent to the principle of democratic legitimacy. In this sense, the dissemination of ideas that contribute to the formation of public opinion and facilitate citizens' ability to freely form their opinions and participate responsibly in public affairs deserves special constitutional protection.

However, just as with other fundamental rights, the exercise of the right to freedom of expression is subject to constitutional limits that the Constitutional Court has progressively outlined. 

Thus, it does not protect the presence of injurious, outrageous, and offensive phrases and expressions unrelated to the ideas or opinions being presented and, therefore, unnecessary for this purpose, nor does it protect the dissemination of facts that are merely rumors, inventions, or unfounded insinuations, nor does it provide shelter for slanders or insults, as it does not recognize a purported right to insult.

Alongside this, the expansive tendency of freedom of expression also finds its limit in respect for the normative content guaranteed by other fundamental rights, whose infringement is not necessary for the constitutional realization of the right. This delimitation can only be made through the appropriate balancing of conflicting constitutional values, among which the guarantee of the existence of public opinion stands out, inextricably linked to political pluralism. It is important to note, in order to confine our judgment of the case to its proper terms, that we are not here examining the report in question as if it were a chronicle of a judicial process of our days, as the appellants claim and as the first and second instance judgments did.

In reality, what is not being discussed here is how a journalist or a media outlet has carried out a judicial chronicle and the greater or lesser accuracy with which they technically qualified the actions of a witness in the process. Addressing the case at hand in this manner would constitute a serious distortion of its precise object. What is being discussed here is the way in which information has been provided about an event in our recent history, as well as the opinions that, in light of its dissemination, the journalist who prepared the script for the television report had regarding the participation in that historical event of several individuals, including the father of the current appellants for protection. We are therefore not being asked to judge information and opinions expressed about a current event, but rather to examine statements that were aimed at a fact that is already historical, and this not only in the sense of a fact that tragically related to the public life of the country, and not to the intimate biography of its protagonists, but also, in the most proper sense of historicity, of a fact whose effects – at least the immediate ones – are already completely removed from the actions of living generations.

It is important to point out that evaluations and judgments about historical facts, and not only about current events or the more recent past, are as inevitable as they are necessary, notwithstanding the difficulty in reaching consensus or unanimous valuation. This also applies to the scientific reconstruction of the past that we call "historiography," a knowledge recognizable in relation to its adherence to certain methods, and not by virtue of such a purity, which is, on the other hand, unattainable, that disregards any ideological or moral perspective in the exposition of the past.

Well, it is possible to infer that scientific freedom – in what is now relevant, historical debate – enjoys increased protection under our Constitution compared to that which operates for the freedoms of expression and information, since while the latter refer to current facts involving present individuals, the former, also participating in contents inherent to the freedoms of expression and information – as it is still a narration of facts and an expression of opinions and evaluations and, consequently, information and free expression for the purposes of Article 20.1 a) and d) of the Spanish Constitution (CE) – always refers to past facts and those involving individuals whose personality, in the constitutional sense of the term (their free development is the foundation of political order and social peace: Article 10.1 CE), has necessarily faded as a consequence of the passage of time and cannot, therefore, be opposed as a limit to scientific freedom with the same scope and intensity with which the dignity of the living opposes the exercise of the freedoms of expression and information of their contemporaries. The Judgment specifies the content of the right to data protection, indicating that "the right to data protection grants its holder a set of powers consisting of various legal powers whose exercise imposes legal obligations on third parties and serves the function that this fundamental right performs, which is to guarantee the person a power of control over their personal data, which is only possible by imposing on the third party the aforementioned duties. From the reading of STC 292/2000, we can affirm that the content of the right to data protection encompasses, on one hand, a set of powers of the right holder, and on the other, a series of legal obligations that fall on third parties who will process the personal data. The partially transcribed regulation offers two possibilities to the applicant depending on whether the knowledge of the dates of entries and exits to Spain that the deceased made are considered data affecting the privacy of the deceased or not.
At this point, it is worth recalling that privacy, according to the Royal Spanish Academy, is the “Intimate and reserved spiritual zone of a person or a group, especially of a family,” and its legal specification is found in the recognition of the right to privacy in accordance with Article 18.1 of the Spanish Constitution. The right to privacy is linked to the most reserved sphere of individuals, to the realm that they always preserve from the gaze of others, that which they wish to keep hidden from others as it belongs to their most private sphere (STC 151/1997, of September 29), associated with dignity and the free development of personality (Article 10.1 CE). In this way, the right to an inaccessible core of privacy is also recognized for individuals who are more exposed to the public (STC 134/1999, of July 15).
Notwithstanding the above, the extent of the right is conditioned by certain qualities and circumstances of the person or the specific aspect of their life that is affected, also in accordance with the particular circumstances of the case. Thus, the Constitutional Court has interpreted that the scope of privacy is marked by the affected party themselves (STC 115/2000, of May 5, STC 83/2002, and STC 196/2004).
In the case presented, the applicant is the author of several books that deal with the life of the deceased, who was also a writer, athlete in various sports disciplines, poet, and political activist, with some public projection. Additionally, it should be noted that multiple journalistic articles about her have been published, both in online and print versions. There are also numerous links on the internet that cover various aspects of her life. Television programs have also been made about the life of the deceased.
Considering the special circumstances of the specific case, it does not seem that the knowledge of the information to which the applicant intends to access affects the privacy of the deceased. The dates of departure from Spain can be considered merely nominative or identifying data, as it is notoriously known—through multiple publications—that after the Spanish Civil War, the deceased lived in exile in France and the United States. That is to say, the information to which access is sought would be specific dates, which do not provide anything more than a temporal specification of manifestly public facts.
Notwithstanding the above, it is up to the competent authority granting access to weigh whether it affects privacy, and if not, access could be granted as one of the assumptions required by Article 28.3 of RD 1708/2011, of November 18, is met, namely, that the data subject has passed away, without any indication of a temporal limit.

Regarding the principle of proportionality, the doctrine of the Constitutional Court has highlighted a triple meaning, as recalled in the Constitutional Court Judgment 14/2003, of January 28:
“In other words, in accordance with a repeated doctrine of this Court, the constitutionality of any measure restricting fundamental rights is determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality. For the purposes that are relevant here, it is sufficient to recall that, to verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions: whether the measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, furthermore, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and finally, whether it is balanced or proportionate, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test; SSTC 66/1995, of May 8 [RTC 1995, 66], F. 5; 55/1996, of March 28 [RTC 1996, 55], FF. 7, 8, and 9; 270/1996, of December 16 [RTC 1996, 270], F. 4.e; 37/1998, of February 17 [RTC 1998, 37], F. 8; 186/2000, of July 10 [RTC 2000, 186], F. 6).”

represented a fundamental change in the understanding of the right to
personal data protection, with one of the most relevant new features
being proactive responsibility, as contemplated in Article 5.2 of said
Regulation: “2. The data controller shall be responsible for compliance
with the provisions of paragraph 1 and able to demonstrate it

data controller is responsible for the data processing it carries out. It
must not only comply with the principles enshrined in Article 5.1, but
also be able to demonstrate compliance. This responsibility entails the
need to make decisions -determining the purposes and means of the
processing to be carried out-, as well as to be accountable for the
decisions made. In this regard, Recital 74 of the GDPR provides the
following: “The responsibility of the data controller for any processing
of personal data carried out by itself or on its behalf must be established.
In particular, the controller must be obliged to implement appropriate
and effective measures and must be able to demonstrate compliance of
the processing activities with this Regulation, including the effectiveness
of the measures. Such measures must take into account the nature, scope,
context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risk to the rights
and freedoms of natural persons.” (the underlining is ours). In cases
where there is a “chain of processing,” that is, distinct and subsequent
processing carried out by different data controllers, each controller will
be responsible for the decisions it makes within its corresponding scope
regarding its processing. It cannot rely on the actions of the previous
data controller to exempt itself from its liability, just as it will not be
held responsible for the decisions made by the data controller that is
next in the chain. In this regard, it is worth mentioning the Judgment
of the Court of Justice of the European Union in the case Fashion ID,
C-40/17, ECLI:EU:2018:1039, which rules on a case in which an
e-commerce company embedded the “like” social module from the
Facebook social network on its website, which meant that personal data
of the visitors to the e-commerce company's website were transmitted
to Facebook regardless of whether the visitors were members of the
aforementioned social network or if they clicked on the “like” button
on Facebook. In its paragraph 74, it establishes that “On the other hand,
and without prejudice to any potential civil liability provided for in
national law in this regard, that natural or legal person cannot be
considered responsible, in the sense of that provision, for the previous
or subsequent operations in the chain of processing for which it does
not determine the purposes or means.” That is to say, any processing
operation carried out within the scope of the respondent (in this case,
the dissemination of personal data in connection with the news) must
be attributed solely to it, regardless of processing operations that have
been carried out previously by other parties and which, in no case,
exempt it from its responsibility. The media, as data controllers of
multiple data they handle in the exercise of their journalistic work,
must know and comply with data protection regulations, applying,
among others, the principle of data minimization enshrined in Article
5.1.c) of the GDPR.
