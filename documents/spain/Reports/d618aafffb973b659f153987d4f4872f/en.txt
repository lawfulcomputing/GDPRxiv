The inquiry raises the issue of compliance with data protection regulations regarding the publicity of the scrutiny of electoral processes derived from the provisions of Organic Law 5/1985, of June 19, on the General Electoral Regime - established in order to ensure the transparency and objectivity of such processes - as this publicity may conflict with the "group privacy" inherent to the status of certain groups of voters.  

Accompanying the inquiry is the complaint submitted to the Ombudsman by a group particularly affected by the effects inherent to the publication and general knowledge of the results obtained in electoral processes within certain "polling stations" and/or "electoral colleges," as well as the response from the Central Electoral Board dated May 4, 2021, regarding the issues raised in the aforementioned complaint, issued at the request of the General Directorate of Internal Policy of the Ministry of the Interior.  

According to the documents provided, the affected group claims that the information on electoral results disseminated via the internet, including the details of the votes received by each candidacy in each polling station and electoral college, clearly impacts the identification of the political orientation of such group, to the detriment of the secrecy of the right to vote. Additionally, the inquiry is accompanied by a dossier containing various press clippings, which particularly refer to the political orientation of the affected group, potentially impacting the "group privacy" and that of its individual members.

II
It should also be noted that the data protection regulations contemplate different legal bases for legitimacy that may allow for the processing of personal data. According to Article 6—“Lawfulness of processing”—of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), such processing is lawful, and therefore legitimate when:

“1. The processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes; 
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract; 
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject; (the bold is ours)
d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person; 
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller; (the bold is ours)
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child. 
The provision in paragraph f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions.”

Thus, the Agency—among others—in its Report 0175/2018, states that “In general, the legal basis for processing in relationships with the

“2. The processing of personal data may only be considered based on the fulfillment of a mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred to the controller, under the terms provided in Article 6.1 e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when it derives from a competence attributed by a norm with the rank of law.”
Thus, the processing of personal data derived from the provisions of Articles 31 to 33 of Organic Law 5/1985, of June 19, in relation to the electoral roll, is clearly in compliance with data protection regulations, constituting its legitimizing basis as provided in Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR.  
In summary, on one hand, it is clear that the data of the electoral roll - as referred to identified natural persons by their name, surname, and ID number, in accordance with the provisions of Articles 31 to 33 of Organic Law 5/1985, of June 19 - are personal data, and processing is carried out on them within the physical framework of a structured support according to criteria related to identified or identifiable persons (note that the final provision third-One of the LOPDGDD has modified section 3 of Article 39 of the LOREG, without such modification implying any alteration in relation to the previous considerations).
The Electoral Census Office, framed within the National Statistics Institute, is the body responsible for the formation of the electoral roll and exercises its competencies under the direction and supervision of the Central Electoral Board, carrying out the competencies assigned to it by electoral law in the preparation of the Electoral Roll and in the development of electoral processes. 
And, consequently, on the other hand, the actions of this Office in relation to the preparation of the electoral roll and the processing carried out on it respond to the legal basis established in section e) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, acting in pursuit of the “necessary fulfillment of a mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred to the data controller.”

Furthermore, regarding the data from the electoral census, as evidenced in various reports issued by this Agency - notably report 29/2020 - the national legislator has been particularly rigorous in regulating access to such data, establishing additional guarantees that involve the intervention of the judicial authority when it comes to accessing said data outside the expressly contemplated cases in the electoral regulations for electoral purposes. On the other hand, the third paragraph of this provision includes the possibility of communicating anonymized data, stating that “However, the Electoral Census Office may provide statistical data that does not reveal personal circumstances of the voters.”

For its part, the purpose of the publicity of the scrutiny of electoral processes, derived from the provisions of Organic Law 5/1985, of June 19, of the General Electoral Regime, is concretized in achieving transparency and objectivity of such processes that serve as the basis for the democratic legitimization of the political system. 

From the above, it can be inferred that the actions of the Electoral Census Office —in relation to the electoral census—, exercising its competencies under the direction and supervision of the Central Electoral Board, and in accordance with the purposes established by the legal system, are fully compliant with the provisions of data protection regulations, obtaining legitimacy for the processing of personal data from the electoral census based on the legitimacy of processing provided in Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR.

III
The General Data Protection Regulation extends its protection, as established in its Article 1.2, to the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, to their right to the protection of personal data, defined in its Article 4.1 as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”

Regarding its "Material Scope of Application," Article 2.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation states that "This Regulation applies to the total or partial automated processing of personal data, as well as to the non-automated processing of personal data contained or intended to be included in a file. (...)". For its part, Article 4.1 defines "processing" as "any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or modification, extraction, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, limitation, erasure or destruction." 

In this context, it is clear that, although the data from the electoral roll—regarding identified individuals by their name, surname, and ID number, in accordance with the provisions of Articles 31 to 33 of Organic Law 5/1985, of June 19—are personal data, and processing is carried out on them within the physical framework of a structured medium according to criteria related to identified or identifiable persons, the same does not apply to the disaggregated data obtained as a result of the regulatory mandate regarding "Counting at Polling Stations," Articles 95 to 102 of the same Organic Law. 

Thus, as provided in the aforementioned provisions, such data—which must be announced aloud—correspond solely to the "number of registered voters, the number of census certifications provided, the number of voters, the number of invalid ballots, the number of blank votes, and the number of votes obtained by each candidacy." 

Regarding the disaggregated data obtained as a result of the aforementioned regulatory mandate concerning "Counting at Polling Stations"—Articles 95 to 102 of the LOREG—the necessary requirements for their consideration as "personal data" are not met; and, concerning the operations that may be performed on such data—even in the event of being incorporated into a physical medium—one cannot assert the consideration of "processing" in the sense required by data protection regulations. 

That is, the operations performed on the disassociated information resulting from the publicity of the electoral counting data would not be subject to data protection regulations, as it would be anonymized information provided to third parties and/or entities—not included in the definition of recipients and/or third parties in paragraphs 9 and 10 of Article 4 of the GDPR (specified for the purposes of its application)—offered as a result of the legal mandate, expressly regulated in the provisions of the LOREG mentioned.

Thus, in accordance with the "Definitions" of Article 4 of the GDPR, for there to be processing of personal data—as provided in its point 2—there must be the performance of "any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, (...)". Consequently, when such personal information has previously undergone a dissociation procedure, the provisions of said Regulation -GDPR- will not apply, and its provisions regarding the "anonymized" information subject to processing will lapse. 
In Recital (26) of the GDPR, the interpretation of what is to be understood as anonymized information is contained, clearly delineating this concept from that related to the "pseudonymization" of data, namely: 
“(26) The principles of data protection must apply to all information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Pseudonymized personal data, which could be attributed to a natural person through the use of additional information, must be considered information about an identifiable natural person. To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means, such as singling out, that the data controller or any other person may reasonably use to identify the natural person directly or indirectly must be taken into account. To determine whether there is a reasonable likelihood that means will be used to identify a natural person, all objective factors, such as the costs and time required for identification, must be considered, taking into account both the technology available at the time of processing and technological advancements. Therefore, the principles of data protection should not apply to anonymous information, that is, information that does not relate to an identified or identifiable natural person, nor to data rendered anonymous in such a way that the data subject is not identifiable, or ceases to be so. Consequently, this Regulation does not affect the processing of such anonymous information, including for statistical or research purposes.”
Conversely, Recital (28) of the GDPR interprets the effective application of data protection regulations to pseudonymized data processing, stating that “The application of pseudonymization to personal data can reduce risks to the affected data subjects and help data controllers and processors comply with their data protection obligations. Thus, the explicit introduction of ‘pseudonymization’ in this Regulation is not intended to exclude any other measures related to data protection.”
Considering what we have just indicated, for an anonymization procedure to be deemed sufficient for the purposes of the GDPR, it will be necessary that the application of such a procedure makes it impossible to identify a specific data with its determined subject. In this regard, international provisions regulating the protection of personal data consider that the affected individual will not be determinable when their identification requires a disproportionate effort that makes it impossible, in the
Legal Office
practice, to identify the natural person who is the owner of the personal data previously processed by the data controller. 
As has been stated, it is advisable to emphasize the clear distinction between both figures. Thus, while dissociation prevents the identification of the affected subject and places the information subject to processing outside the scope of data protection regulations, pseudonymization requires the adoption of the necessary technical and organizational measures to ensure that the GDPR applies to the corresponding processing, but keeps the additional information separate for the attribution of personal data to a specific person (see Recital 29 GDPR), with the data processing remaining subject to the regime of the GDPR.
From what is indicated in the consultation, it can be inferred that the processed data -referring to "polling stations and electoral colleges"- are not linked to personal data that in any way identifies the voters within the framework of the electoral process, so in this case, the regulations governing the protection of personal data would not be applicable to the analyzed situation concerning the information subject to processing, whose communication and/or publication occurs once the counting is completed.
In summary, the data processed in relation to the results of the polling stations and electoral colleges within the framework of the electoral process do not refer to personal information of the voters, so the data protection regulations would not apply to the analyzed situation, given that such data is subject to a prior dissociation procedure. First of all, it should not be forgotten that - although the concept of privacy is often confined to the sphere of the individual, as it is the dimension addressed by the regulations, resulting in such data protection regulations being applicable only in relation to data concerning identified and/or identifiable natural persons - on multiple occasions, individuals are not the target of processing as individuals but as members of specific groups, where the groups are the focus of interest (https://www.aepd.es/es/prensa-y-comunicacion/blog/privacidad-de-grupo). 
Thus, for example, the profiling of individuals and their processing by the State or Internet services can be considered one of the most significant risks to privacy. The approach to privacy that takes the individual in isolation has a historical origin, as the technological level in the past limited the possibilities for the mass processing of the population's data. However, the advancement of information processing techniques, such as Big Data or Artificial Intelligence, has introduced new aspects to consider. 
At this point, in accordance with the definition of "profiling" contained in Article 4.4) of the GDPR, we must reiterate that it is understood as “any form of automated processing of personal data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects of a natural person, in particular to analyze or predict aspects related to professional performance, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behavior, location, or movements of that natural person.” (the bold is ours) 
And, in line with the above, although data protection regulations - as has been repeatedly stated - apply only to identified or identifiable natural persons, it should not be forgotten that a third interested party (third party) can define groups by establishing, for example, that individuals who have the same type of car, who buy a certain type of food, who share the same purchasing power, who select the same online content, who live in the same neighborhood, who carry a specific gene, who suffer from the same infection, etc., belong to a certain group. 
These groups - which are called ad-hoc - may contain a specific list of members, and their members are not always aware of their belonging to them. A further step, qualitatively more intrusive, occurs when these groups are established not from the positive action of a person, but automatically from the abstract definition of an AI that “algorithmically groups” individuals based on a set of data. 
Thus, once the ad-hoc group is established, a profile associated with it could be fed with aggregated data about that group of people.

various forms. One of them is to make use of open data sources, such as, for example, the electoral results of a district. A profile could also be created using Big Data techniques on aggregated data from the digital technology that people use (apps, IoT, browsing data, geolocation, etc.). 
Related to the above, it is also possible to enrich the information of the group by making inferences about the aggregated information within it through predictive models developed using machine learning. The profile can even be enriched by tracking and analyzing specially selected individuals assigned to these groups, whose results are extrapolated to the entire group.
In this way, the data used to generate the group profile, without being considered ab initio as "personal data," could include information even related to special categories of data. At the same time, the ad-hoc groups generated would not benefit from any effective protection concerning the generated profiles because they would not be established as such and would not be subject to any legal form. Profiling and inferences about these groups would thus lack any legal protection.
At this point, entities interested in these processes could make decisions about individuals taking into account qualities that have been determined -globally- within the framework of the group in which that individual has been included. Thus, regardless of the perception that the person has of their own individuality, of their disconnection from the interests or characteristics of other people, the subject would be evaluated based on the profile assigned to the group.
These treatments carry risks for individuals, not only due to their implicit inclusion in a group of which they are unaware, but also because decisions are made that affect them and/or they may be affected by biases of which the scope and possible consequences are unknown. We could encounter discriminatory outcomes based on gender, race, opinions, habits, or specific geographical locations. Members of a group could be attacked or discriminated against without them even knowing it.
However, the General Data Protection Regulation does not explicitly address "group privacy," specifying only the rights of the data subjects and the duties of the data controllers in relation to automated decision-making. Consequently, the interpretation of the applicable regulations is based solely on the consideration of the inherent risks of making this type of decisions, without prejudice to the mandatory adoption of technical measures and

Furthermore, the other principles of Article 5 of the GDPR must be respected, especially those of purpose limitation and data minimization. Thus, in relation to the principle of data minimization, it is required that the data processed by the competent authority be “adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed” (Article 5.1.c of the GDPR), establishing the necessary measures and guarantees to minimize the risks of identification.
Recital (71) of the GDPR states that:
“The data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision, which may include a measure, that evaluates personal aspects concerning him or her, and which is based solely on automated processing and produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her, such as the automatic denial of an online credit application or online contracting services in which there is no human intervention. This type of processing includes profiling consisting of any form of automated processing of personal data that evaluates personal aspects concerning a natural person, in particular to analyze or predict aspects related to work performance, economic situation, health, personal preferences or interests, reliability or behavior, the situation or movements of the data subject, to the extent that it produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her. In any case, such processing must be subject to appropriate safeguards, including specific information to the data subject and the right to obtain human intervention, to express his or her point of view, to receive an explanation of the decision reached after such assessment, and to contest the decision. Such a measure must not affect a minor.
In order to ensure fair and transparent processing regarding the data subject, taking into account the specific circumstances and context in which personal data are processed, the data controller must use appropriate mathematical or statistical procedures for profiling, apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure, in particular, that factors introducing inaccuracies into personal data are corrected and that the risk of error is minimized, secure personal data in such a way that possible risks to the interests and rights of the data subject are taken into account, and prevent, among other things, discriminatory effects on natural persons based on race or ethnic origin, political opinions, religion or beliefs, trade union membership, genetic condition or health status or sexual orientation, or processing that leads to measures producing such effects. Automated decisions and profiling based on particular categories of personal data shall only be permitted under specific conditions.”

While, at the present time, the collective guarantee and protection of groups of individuals is not expressly recognized, the Article 29 Working Party - created by Directive 95/46/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of October 24, 1995 - (now GDPR), has analyzed this issue in various documents. In light of its recent publication and the detailed analysis contained therein - by all - the "Guidelines on Automated Individual Decision-Making and Profiling for the Purposes of Regulation 2016/679 (wp251rev.01)", adopted on October 3, 2017, and last revised on February 6, 2018, has addressed this issue.

In these Guidelines, the risks arising from the identification of natural persons are foreseen, whose individual rights - due to their incorporation into a certain group - may be violated, with particular impact on their privacy and the protection of their personal data. However, neither in the GDPR nor in the interpretation made of its content by the GDPR is the "Group" identified as a subject of "protection" in relation to the provisions of data protection regulations.

In summary, the General Data Protection Regulation does not explicitly address "group privacy." The adoption of appropriate technical and organizational measures to better guarantee the protection of the personal data of potential affected individuals — with full respect for the principles of Article 5 of the GDPR, especially those of purpose limitation and data minimization — refers to the data of identified and/or identifiable natural persons.

VI  
Finally, it remains to analyze the processing that may be carried out by other parties regarding the published information. In this regard, as outlined in the documents of the Article 29 Working Party —now the European Data Protection Board (EDPB)—, the risks arising from the identification of individuals are evident, whose individual rights —due to their inclusion in a specific group— may be compromised, particularly concerning their privacy and the protection of their personal data.  
It should be particularly noted the special regulation regarding this type of data —related to political opinions— contained in Article 9 of the REPD itself:  
“Article 9 Processing of special categories of personal data  
1. The processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health, or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation is prohibited. (…)”  
In general, the processing of this category of data is prohibited by the transcribed Article 9.1 of the GDPR, unless one of the circumstances set out in paragraph 2 applies, which lifts the prohibition on such processing, and which is an essential prerequisite for subsequently assessing the existence of a legal basis that legitimizes the processing in accordance with Article 6 of the GDPR. However, in the case at hand, regarding the processing by third parties of data relating to identified or identifiable individuals, none of the grounds in paragraph 9.2 of the GDPR that serve to lift the initial prohibition established by paragraph 1 of that provision are present.  
Consequently, special attention must be paid to the monitoring and control of the potential actions of other parties —natural and/or legal persons— who, in contravention of the provisions of the applicable regulations, particularly stringent in relation to “special categories of personal data,” carry out personal data processing —derived from the information provided by the competent body regarding the counting and proclamation of disaggregated results— lacking the corresponding legal authorization for such processing.  
In relation to this type of actions, which will constitute violations of the provisions of data protection regulations, the sanctioning regime of Title IX of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, shall apply. Thus, in its Article 71, after stating that “Acts and behaviors referred to in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 of Article 83 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, as well as those that are contrary to this organic law, constitute violations,” the list of violations by data controllers and processors is identified - Articles 72, 73, and 74 - along with the legal consequences arising from any potential non-compliance.  
In accordance with the above, in the event of a potential violation of the provisions of data protection regulations affecting one or more identified or identifiable individuals —as a result of their belonging to a specific group or collective— the regime of Chapter VIII, “Resources, liability, and sanctions,” contained in Articles 77 to 84 of the GDPR, shall apply.
