The inquiry raises the issue of compliance with data protection regulations regarding the guide on maximum retention periods for personal data that is attached, related to the activities of the consulting entity and developed in relation to the different purposes for which data is collected by the various entities that make up its corporate group.  

As stated by the consultant, in the preparation of this guide, the retention periods derived from legal obligations and the specific circumstances of the consulting entity—identified by the data controllers of each entity within the corporate group—have been taken into account, with the aim of ensuring the protection of the personal data of the affected individuals.  

In addition to the guide prepared, the consultant attaches a report justifying the retention periods of documentation, prepared by the Human Resources department.  

As a preliminary matter, it is important to note that at the time of formulating the inquiry, one must start from the new regime established by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) and in Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD).  

Indeed, as indicated in the Explanatory Memorandum of Law 3/2018, “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.”  

Therefore, it is the data controller who must comply with the principles set out in Article 5 of the GDPR, among which, as seen, is the principle of proactive responsibility, included in its paragraph 2, “the controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and be able to demonstrate it (‘proactive responsibility’).” And among the principles of paragraph 1 is the “limitation of the retention period,” included in its letter e).  

In turn, Article 38.1 of the GDPR clearly states that “The controller and the processor shall ensure that the data protection officer is involved appropriately and in a timely manner in all issues related to the processing of personal data,” and Article 39.2 provides that “The data protection officer shall perform their tasks with due regard to the risks associated with processing operations, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing.”  

Finally, Article 39.1 enumerates the functions of the data protection officer, among which are “to inform and advise the controller or the processor and the employees involved in the processing of their obligations under this Regulation and under other Union or Member State data protection provisions” (paragraph a), “to monitor compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, other Union or Member State data protection provisions, and the policies of the controller or processor regarding the processing of personal data, including the allocation of responsibilities, awareness-raising and training of staff involved in processing operations, and the corresponding audits” (paragraph b), and “to provide advice as requested regarding the data protection impact assessment and to monitor its implementation in accordance with Article 35” (paragraph c).  

Moreover, it is the responsibility of the data protection officer “to act as a contact point for the supervisory authority on issues related to processing, including prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and to consult, where appropriate, on any other matter” (paragraph e).  

In the present case, it is the data protection officer themselves who raises the inquiry, generally citing the doubts that arise as a result of the application of the new legal framework for the protection of personal data. Thus, as stated, they request the opinion of this Agency in the interest of the legal certainty necessary for the performance of their personal data processing, attaching to their request the report issued by the Human Resources department regarding certain processing included in the Guide, but without providing their own report or reasoning what the doubts are that arise and may have a general scope, seeking with their inquiry the validation, by this Agency, of the Guide prepared, which exceeds the competencies of a supervisory authority and does not correspond with the principle of proactive responsibility introduced by the GDPR.

At this point, it is worth recalling that there are numerous reports issued by this Agency regarding the provisions of Article 16 of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, whose paragraph 5 referred to the retention of data and its paragraph 3 to the obligation of blocking.

In this regard, our report of July 30, 2004, reiterated in many others, stated the following:
“(...) Organic Law 15/1999 regulates the blocking of personal data in its Article 16.3, establishing that ‘cancellation will result in the blocking of the data, being kept solely at the disposal of Public Administrations, Judges, and Courts, for addressing possible liabilities arising from the processing, during the statute of limitations for these. Once the aforementioned period has elapsed, deletion must proceed.’

This provision, in turn, is complemented by the provision contained in Article 16.5, which indicates that ‘personal data must be retained for the periods established in the applicable provisions or, where appropriate, in the contractual relationships between the person or entity responsible for the processing and the data subject.’

From the joint analysis of the aforementioned norms, it follows that there will be cases in which, although the cancellation of the data must proceed, having ceased to be necessary for the purpose that justified its processing, as will occur when the complete fulfillment of the contract binding the data processor to its clients has taken place, such cancellation must occur through the blocking of the personal data being processed, which, producing effects similar to the physical deletion of the data, except in certain circumstances described by Article 16.3 of the Organic Law, will not automatically imply that deletion.

Thus, Article 16.3 recognizes, in accordance with the provisions of Article 16.5 of the Law, that there will be certain cases in which the very legal relationship that binds the affected party to the data controller and that ultimately determines the processing of the personal data whose cancellation is sought, as well as the obligations of all kinds that may arise from the aforementioned legal relationship and that are imposed by the Law, will prevent the cancellation from materializing immediately into a physical deletion of the data.

On the contrary, the data controller will be obliged, either by the content of that legal relationship or by what is established in an imperative norm, to maintain the data, albeit subject to certain conditions that ensure and guarantee the affected party's right to the protection of their personal data, not being able to dispose of such data to the same extent as they could if the cancellation of the same did not proceed (ex officio – for having ceased to be necessary for the fulfillment of the purpose of the file – or at the request of the affected party).

Regarding the reasons that may motivate the retention of the data, subject to its prior blocking, and aside from the legal relationship with the affected party, referred to in Article 16.5 of Organic Law 15/1999, these must be based on what is provided for in the ‘applicable provisions’ or for the ‘addressing of possible liabilities arising from the processing,’ as the aforementioned Law provides.

In this sense, for the determination of the blocking period of the data, it must be taken into account that the Judgment of the Constitutional Court 292/2000, of November 30, expressly imposes the principle of legal reservation regarding limitations on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, so that any limitation on that right (as would be derived from Article 16.3 of the Law) must be stated in a provision with the rank of Law for the blocking of the data to be considered lawfully carried out. Thus, by way of example, it could be considered that the blocking must be carried out during the statute of limitations for actions arising from the legal relationship that underpins the processing, in the terms provided by the civil or commercial legislation that is applicable, as well as the four-year statute of limitations for tax debts, insofar as the data may have significance from a tax perspective (given the retention obligation imposed by Article 111 of the General Tax Law and the four-year legal statute of limitations provided in Article 24 of the Law on Rights and Guarantees of Taxpayers).

In any case, it should be noted that the maintenance of the blocked data constitutes an exception to the physical deletion of the same, which, ultimately, is the ultimate goal of cancellation (as provided for in Article 16.3 itself, indicating that ‘once the aforementioned period has elapsed, deletion must proceed’).

Consequently, in our opinion, cancellation does not automatically imply in all cases a physical deletion or suppression of the data, but may determine, in the event that a provision with the rank of Law establishes it or is inferred from the very legal relationship that binds the data controller to the affected party (and that motivates the processing itself), the blocking of the data being processed.

From these latter requirements arises the need to complement the regulatory provisions regarding the retention, minimization, and accuracy of data with those relating to the rights of the data subjects affected by the processing, and, in particular, with the right to erasure, as set out in Article 17 of the GDPR, which aims at the deletion—without undue delay—of personal data when any of the circumstances regulated in that provision occur, the main cause being the disappearance of the purpose that motivated the processing for which the data was collected.

However, there are exceptions in which it is possible to retain and process the data for longer than necessary to achieve the originally pursued purpose. Thus, Articles 5.1, letters b) and e), and Article 17 of the GDPR itself mention—by way of exception—the cases of further processing for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific and historical research, and statistical purposes, with the guarantees set out in Article 89. Additionally, among other exceptions that are important to highlight at this moment, are those contemplated in paragraph 3 of Article 17 in its letters b) (when the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation requiring the processing of data imposed by Union law or the law of the Member States applicable to the data controller, or for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller) and e) (when the processing is necessary for the establishment, exercise, or defense of legal claims).

Consequently, the suppression leads to the blocking of the data, which prevents processing for the purpose that justified its collection, being retained solely (i) for making the data available to judges and courts, the Public Prosecutor's Office, or the competent Public Administrations, particularly data protection authorities, and (ii) for the enforcement of possible liabilities arising from the processing and only for the statute of limitations for these - pursuant to Article 32.2 of the LOPDGDD -.
The teleological interpretation of this provision points to the consideration of a single possibility - and not two - regarding the concurrence of the requirements that justify this blocking. Thus, on one hand, (i) the mention of possible subjects - potential assignees of the blocked data - and, on the other hand, (ii) the reference to the factual assumption - the requirement of possible liabilities arising from the processing - under which the blocked data could be made available to them, constitute cumulative requirements. Finally, Article 32.2 contains an express reference to the necessary consideration of the statute of limitations for liabilities, which must be interpreted as the statute of limitations for actions aimed at enforcing them.
This interpretation aligns with the provisions that - prior to the entry into force of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD - were contained in Article 16.3 of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13 - LOPD - and in its implementing regulation, approved by Royal Decree 1720/2007, of December 21.
Specifically, Article 16.3 of the LOPD states that:
“The cancellation will lead to the blocking of the data, being retained solely at the disposal of the Public Administrations, Judges, and Courts, for addressing possible liabilities arising from the processing, during the statute of limitations for these.”

On the other hand, there will be certain situations in which the retention of personal data will be determined by the applicable legal regulations. Thus, by way of exception, section 3, letter b), of Article 17 of the General Data Protection Regulation excludes the right to erasure and, consequently, allows the retention of personal data subject to processing in those cases where it must be maintained for the proper fulfillment of a legal obligation requiring data processing imposed by Union Law or the laws of the Member States applicable to the data controller.

Therefore, in cases where the retention of data is imposed by special law, the right to erasure does not apply in accordance with Article 17.3.b) of the aforementioned GDPR, nor, consequently, does the blocking of the same, without prejudice to the obligation of the data controller to respect the principles set out in Article 5 of the GDPR and, in particular, those of purpose limitation, data minimization, and integrity and confidentiality, as will be analyzed later when discussing the obligations imposed by occupational risk prevention regulations.

In this regard, the report of this Agency number 1/2019, relating to the retention obligations imposed by regulations on the prevention of money laundering and the financing of terrorism, has stated:
Article 5.1, letter e) of the GDPR, within the principles relating to processing, determines that personal data shall be maintained in a manner that allows the identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of processing personal data; personal data may be retained for longer periods provided that they are processed exclusively for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89, section 1, without prejudice to the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures imposed by this Regulation to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject (“limitation of the retention period”);

Here, a question of retention for archiving purposes or for scientific purposes is not raised, so the cited principle obliges the data controller to retain such data for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which they were collected.

In accordance with this principle, Article 17 of the GDPR grants data subjects a right to erasure of their personal data, and a corresponding obligation for the data controller to erase such data, among other circumstances when the personal data are no longer necessary in relation to the purposes for which they were collected or processed in another manner (letter b). However, the GDPR itself provides that this right may have limitations. Article 17.3 of the GDPR establishes that sections 1 and 2 [that is, there will be no grounds for erasure] shall not apply when processing is necessary: b) for compliance with a legal obligation requiring data processing imposed by Union Law or the laws of the Member States applicable to the data controller, or for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller.

In interpreting this section, Recital 65 of the GDPR states:
(…) In particular, data subjects should have the right to have their personal data erased and no longer processed if they are no longer necessary for the purposes for which they were collected or processed in another manner, if the data subjects have withdrawn their consent to processing or object to the processing of personal data concerning them, or if the processing of their personal data otherwise infringes this Regulation. (…) However, further retention of personal data must be lawful when it is necessary for the exercise of freedom of expression and information, for compliance with a legal obligation, for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller, for reasons of public interest in the area of public health, for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes, or for the formulation, exercise, or defense of legal claims.

In these cases, the further retention of such data is lawful, says the GDPR. That is, there would be no right of the data subject to the erasure of personal data.

VI
Furthermore, as previously stated, within the framework of this report, it is impossible to provide a comprehensive response to each and every one of the scenarios contained in the "Guide to Maximum Retention Periods for Personal Data" of the consulting party, who, in relation to the blocking of personal data, must consider, on one hand, the limitation periods for actions that may arise from legal relationships of all kinds - commercial, civil, social, etc. - that link them to the individuals affected by the processing of their data, and, on the other hand, the sectoral regulations that may apply to their activity. 

In short, it is the responsibility of the data controller - who has determined its purpose - to decide when the data is no longer necessary for the purpose for which it was collected - thus ending the possibility of its processing - although, in some cases, it is the legislator who sets a specific retention period in relation to specific scenarios or subjects. 

Consequently, it is necessary for the consulting entity to conduct a detailed examination of each and every one of the processing activities included in its "record of processing activities," determining for each of the scenarios raised, taking into account the set of concurrent circumstances (purpose of processing, types of data processed, affected subjects and their relationship with the entity, applicable regulations, etc.) the specific period during which the data must be retained, adopting the appropriate guarantees or, if applicable, keeping them blocked as a prior stage to their destruction and physical deletion. 

In this regard, the sanctioning consequences arising from the failure to comply with their obligations regarding data retention by the data controller have recently been highlighted in the context of various sanctioning proceedings initiated by the Agency. Among them, the sanctioning procedure PS/00076/2020, under which a financial entity was imposed a fine of 50,000 euros for retaining the personal data of the complainant in its files in violation of the provisions of article 5.1.b of the GDPR. 

In the aforementioned resolution, it is considered proven that the entity in question retained the personal data of the complainant in its records, despite 16 years having passed since their last commercial relationship, as it had the same when the complainant approached that financial entity again to contract a new financial service in September 2019. Consequently, the entity was charged with the commission of the aforementioned infringement of the principle of purpose limitation, according to which personal data shall be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and shall not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; it is also established that the data controller has a proactive responsibility to demonstrate compliance. 

At this point, it should be noted, as a novelty introduced by the GDPR, that the infringement of the principle of limitation of the retention period is classified in article 83.5 a) of the GDPR, which states that the violation of the basic principles for processing, including the conditions for consent under articles 5, 6, 7, and 9, shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 20,000,000 EUR or, in the case of a company, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 4% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount. 

In this regard, article 72.1 of the LOPDGDD includes among the infractions considered very serious and which shall prescribe after three years - in its letter a) - "The processing of personal data violating the principles and guarantees established in article 5 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679," and, in its letter n), "The failure to comply with the obligation to block the data established in article 32 of this organic law when it is enforceable." 

Regarding the determination of the initial day for the computation of the statute of limitations, it should be noted that, in accordance with the provisions of article 1969 of the Civil Code, "The time for the prescription of all kinds of actions, when there is no special provision determining otherwise, shall be counted from the day on which they could be exercised," with the period being interrupted in the cases provided for in the Code itself.
