I  
This Legal Office has repeatedly highlighted, in the various inquiries submitted for its report, the paradigm shift that the full application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons in relation to the processing of their personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR), has entailed, as it is based on the principle of "accountability" or proactive responsibility, as stated in the Preamble of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD): “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or processor of the risks that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.”  

Consequently, by virtue of this principle, the data controller must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the Regulation.  

Among the various instruments provided by the GDPR itself to ensure compliance with this principle are the codes of conduct, which were already foreseen in the previous regulations as a self-regulation mechanism aimed at facilitating compliance with the regulations on personal data protection. In this regard, the Article 29 Working Party, in its working document on the examination procedure for community codes of conduct, approved on September 10, 1998 (WP13), had already defined the essential elements of such codes, emphasizing that the codes should have sufficient clarity and internal coherence and provide a sufficient added value, in terms of being sufficiently focused on the specific issues and problems of data protection in the organization or sector for which they are intended to be applied and offering sufficiently clear solutions for those questions and problems, facilitating, to the extent possible, examples of best practices, preferably prepared in consultation with the affected stakeholders or their representatives.

The role of codes of conduct and their connection to the principle of proactive responsibility, as a means to demonstrate compliance with the GDPR, from which arises the need to review all previously approved codes, is outlined in the Guidelines of the European Data Protection Committee 1/2019 on codes of conduct and supervisory bodies under Regulation 2016/679, highlighting, likewise, the benefit they can provide to supervisory authorities by allowing them to obtain a better understanding and knowledge of the data processing activities of an industry, profession, or other specific sector.
These guidelines identify some of the areas where codes of conduct can be useful, such as fair and transparent processing, legitimate interests, security and data protection by design and by default, and obligations of the data controller, starting from the premise that codes can have a broader or narrower scope, depending on the particular sector, without the need to encompass compliance with all legislation. It also identifies elements of their content that can help give them added value, such as the establishment of detailed guidelines for specific processing activities, best practices, practical solutions to problems identified in a specific sector, consideration of public concerns in general or perceived within the sector itself, etc.
Furthermore, the aforementioned Guidelines detail the criteria that must be taken into account by the supervisory authorities for the approval of the codes:
1) That it meets a particular need of that sector or processing activity, with the proposed solutions being beneficial not only for the controllers but also for the affected parties.
2) That it facilitates the application of the GDPR, identifying specific needs (for example, adapting the terminology of the sector).
3) That it specifies the application of the GDPR, focusing on the sector's issues and providing added value, without merely reproducing the provisions of the GDPR.
4) That it provides effective mechanisms for monitoring compliance with the Code, both in terms of structures and procedures, with the existence of an accredited supervisory body being mandatory, except in the case of authorities and public bodies.

III
Regarding the content of the code of conduct, the GDPR has identified certain areas that it may cover in Article 40.2:
2. Associations and other representative bodies of categories of controllers or processors may develop codes of conduct or modify or expand such codes in order to specify the application of this Regulation, as regards: 
a) the fair and transparent processing; 
b) the legitimate interests pursued by the controllers in specific contexts;
c) the collection of personal data; 
d) the pseudonymization of personal data; 
e) the information provided to the public and to the data subjects; 
f) the exercise of the rights of the data subjects; 
g) the information provided to children and their protection, as well as the manner of obtaining consent from the holders of parental responsibility or guardianship over the child; 
h) the measures and procedures referred to in Articles 24 and 25 and the measures to ensure the security of processing referred to in Article 32; 
i) the notification of personal data breaches to the supervisory authorities and the communication of such breaches to the data subjects; 
j) the transfer of personal data to third countries or international organizations, or 
k) the extrajudicial procedures and other dispute resolution procedures that allow resolving disputes between the controllers and the data subjects regarding processing, without prejudice to the rights of the data subjects under Articles 77 and 79.
At this point, it should be noted that the GDPR does not identify a minimum content for codes of conduct, but rather it is an enumerative list of subjects that they may address and does not exhaust all the cases that may arise to facilitate the application of the GDPR, unlike what was done in the Development Regulation of the LOPD of 1999, which required that model codes included the content referred to in Article 73 of the LOPD Regulation, which involved the entire lifecycle of personal data, a supervision procedure, and a list of adherents, as provided by Articles 75 and 76.
Additionally, there was the option to include additional commitments.
  
What must be included mandatorily are the mechanisms for monitoring compliance as required by Article 40.4 of the GDPR in order to allow the supervisory body to carry out the mandatory control of compliance with its provisions by the controllers or processors that commit to applying it.
Consequently, regardless of the material content of the codes of conduct, they cannot overlook including the mechanisms for monitoring compliance, whether they are codes applicable to controllers or processors in the private sector, or those promoted by authorities and public bodies.
Therefore, the analysis of the content of the code of conduct for the purpose of its approval will not be determined by its greater or lesser scope, but by the extent to which it contributes to the better application of personal data protection regulations, providing a “genuine added value” and sufficient guarantees, as indicated at the beginning of this report.

Therefore, any activity involving the processing of personal data will be attributable to a subject that meets the requirements of the various categories provided by the GDPR. 
The GDPR defines in its Article 4.7 the figure of the data controller or controller as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the controller or the specific criteria for their appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States.”  
And in its Article 4.8, it defines the figure of the data processor or processor as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the controller.” 
In this regard, it should be noted that the figure of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, so that in those cases where the data controller entrusts a third party with the provision of a service that involves the processing of personal data, we will be dealing with processing carried out on behalf of the controller. 
This does not necessarily imply that the data subject to processing is owned by the controller, but rather that the processing operations, including, for example, collection, are attributed to the controller. 
This means that the processing of the data is carried out by the processor on behalf of the controller as if it were the controller itself carrying it out. 
As another manifestation of the principle of proactive responsibility, the GDPR imposes on the data controller an obligation of diligence when choosing a data processor, indicating in Recital 81 the following: (…) To ensure compliance with the provisions of this Regulation regarding the processing carried out by the processor on behalf of the controller, the controller, when entrusting processing activities to a processor, must only engage processors that provide sufficient guarantees, in particular with regard to specialized knowledge, reliability, and resources, for the implementation of technical measures and...

Regarding the formal support of the relationship between the controller and the processor, Article 28 of the GDPR requires in its third paragraph the existence of a contract or another legal act in accordance with the law of the Union or the Member States that binds the processor to the controller. As the Article 29 Working Party noted in its Opinion 1/2010 on the concepts of "data controller" and "data processor," the concept of controller was a functional concept aimed at assigning responsibilities, indicating that “The concept of ‘data controller’ and its interaction with the concept of ‘data processor’ play a fundamental role in the application of Directive 95/46/EC, as they determine who must be responsible for compliance with data protection rules and how data subjects can effectively exercise their rights in practice. The concept of data controller is also essential for determining the applicable national legislation and for the effective exercise of the supervisory tasks conferred on data protection authorities.”

Furthermore, the aforementioned Opinion highlighted “the difficulties in implementing the definitions of the Directive in a complex environment where many hypothetical situations may arise involving the actions of controllers and processors, either alone or jointly, and with varying degrees of autonomy and responsibility” and that “The Group acknowledges that the concrete application of the concepts of data controller and data processor is becoming increasingly complex. This is primarily due to the growing complexity of the environment in which these concepts are used and, in particular, to an increasing trend, both in the private and public sectors, towards organizational differentiation, combined with the development of ICT and globalization, which may give rise to new and difficult questions and, at times, may diminish the level of protection for data subjects.”

However, at the present time, it must be taken into account that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to personal data protection, which is now based on the principle of "accountability" or "proactive responsibility" as has been
Legal Office
repeatedly indicated by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and is
included in the Explanatory Memorandum of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD): “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or the processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.” Within this new system, it is the data controller who, through the instruments regulated in the GDPR itself, such as the record of processing activities, risk analysis, or data protection impact assessment, must guarantee the protection of this right by complying with all the principles set forth in Article 5.1 of the GDPR, adequately documenting all decisions made in order to be able to demonstrate it.

Furthermore, based on this principle of proactive responsibility, aimed essentially at the data controller, and in order to reinforce the protection of the affected parties, the GDPR has introduced new obligations that are enforceable not only against the controller but, in certain cases, also against the processor, who may be sanctioned in the event of non-compliance.

In this regard, the Guidelines 07/2020 of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) on the concepts of data controller and processor in the GDPR make special reference (paragraph 91) to the obligation of the processor to ensure that persons authorized to process personal data have committed to respecting confidentiality or are subject to a statutory obligation of confidentiality (Article 28, paragraph 3); to maintain a record of all categories of processing activities carried out on behalf of a controller (Article 30.2); to apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk (Article 32); to designate a data protection officer under certain conditions (Article 37); and to notify the data controller without undue delay of any personal data breaches of which it becomes aware (Article 33 (2)). Moreover, the rules on transfers of data to third countries (Chapter V) apply to both processors and controllers. Therefore, the EDPB considers that Article 28 (3) of the GDPR imposes direct obligations on processors, including the obligation to assist the data controller in ensuring compliance.

Without prejudice to the attribution of direct obligations to the processor, the aforementioned Guidelines, based on the fact that the concepts of controller and processor in the GDPR have not changed compared to Directive 95/46/EC and that, in general, the criteria for attributing the different roles...

These guarantees are articulated through specific obligations that are enforceable through the control mechanisms established by the code, which reinforces its binding nature, as highlighted in our Report 89/2020:
In this regard, it must be noted that the configuration of codes of conduct as a self-regulation mechanism derived from the autonomy of will becomes a source of legally binding norms for those who voluntarily adhere to it, thus contributing to specifying the application of the current legal framework, which they must respect at all times.

Therefore, this Legal Office understands that the need to establish legally enforceable obligations is inherent to the very nature of codes of conduct, binding those who have freely subscribed to it. The necessity for codes of conduct to have mandatory content also derives from the GDPR itself and from the important role it assigns to them regarding compliance with the principle of proactive responsibility. In this regard, Article 40.4 of the GDPR states that “The code of conduct referred to in paragraph 2 of this article shall contain mechanisms that allow the body mentioned in Article 41, paragraph 1, to carry out the mandatory control of compliance with its provisions by the controllers or processors who commit to applying it, without prejudice to the functions and powers of the supervisory authorities that are competent under Article 51 or 56.” Therefore, mere recommendations are not acceptable in a code of conduct and must be removed from it, as their mandatory control is not possible. Similarly, Article 40.3, when providing for the adherence to the code by controllers or processors to whom the GDPR does not apply, requires them to assume “binding and enforceable commitments, by contractual means or through other legally binding instruments, to apply such adequate guarantees,” making the requirement for a legally binding commitment regarding mere recommendations meaningless. In this same sense, paragraph 37 of Guidelines 1/2019 refers to norms and rules, which denotes their mandatory nature: “The agreed norms and rules must be unequivocal, concrete, accessible, and applicable (verifiable). The establishment of specific norms in a specific area is an acceptable method through which a code can provide added value. The use of unique and relevant terminology for that sector and the provision of specific cases or examples of best practices can help meet this requirement.”

Finally, to conclude the analysis of certain aspects that contribute to considering that the code provides genuine added value, special reference must be made, without prejudice to the rest of the extensive content of the code, to the establishment of a prior procedure for extrajudicial resolution of disputes, which is voluntary and free of charge.

IV
Regarding the mechanisms for controlling compliance with the code, it has already been noted that they must be included as a requirement in the codes of conduct, in accordance with the provisions of Article 40.4 of the GDPR: “The code of conduct referred to in paragraph 2 of this article shall contain mechanisms that allow the body mentioned in Article 41, paragraph 1, to carry out the mandatory control of compliance with its provisions by the controllers or processors who commit to applying it, without prejudice to the functions and powers of the supervisory authorities that are competent under Article 51 or 56.”

For these purposes, the code includes the corresponding control and supervision measures that the supervisory body will apply and that are detailed in the report of the General Subdirectorate of Promotion and Authorizations, among which stand out the attention to inquiries from the adhering insurance entities, the collection of information from the adhering entities about the functioning of the systems regulated by the code, the establishment of an annual program of systematic and random reviews, and the application of the sanctioning regime.
