The inquiry submitted raises numerous questions regarding the processing of personal data in the university context, many of which are framed in an excessively generic and underdeveloped manner. Therefore, in those cases, only equally generic responses can be provided, which must be tailored to the specific case by the data controller, in accordance with the principle of proactive responsibility on which the current regulations on personal data protection are based.  

However, considering the opinions expressed in the inquiry regarding the legal bases for processing, a first general reflection must be made concerning them, starting from what is indicated in paragraph 1 of Article 6 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation):  

1. The processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:  
   a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
   b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
   c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;  
   d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
   e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller;  
   f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.  

The provision in letter f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.  

Based on the above, it is necessary to recall, as has been indicated on numerous occasions by this Agency, that the reform carried out by the General Data Protection Regulation regarding the regime contained in Organic Law 15/1999 requires a change of perspective concerning the principles that underpin the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, and in particular, that which made the "principle of consent" the central axis of the right to data protection.  

Indeed, although the Organic Law and the Regulation do not differ excessively regarding the enumeration of the legitimizing grounds for processing, there is a highly relevant modification in the way these grounds are presented in the applicable texts: thus, while from the wording of Organic Law 15/1999 it seemed to be deduced that the basic rule of legitimacy was, in general, consent, with the remaining legitimizing grounds being exceptional in relation to consent, which as a general rule should govern processing, in the text of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, consent is included as one of the six grounds for legitimacy for processing without holding greater or lesser importance than the others enumerated in the regulation.  

Furthermore, the General Data Protection Regulation itself makes it clear that the consent of the data subject should not constitute the legal basis for processing in certain cases. Thus, Recital 42 states in its last sentence that "Consent should not be regarded as freely given when the data subject does not have genuine or free choice or cannot refuse or withdraw consent without detriment," and Recital 43 adds that "To ensure that consent has been given freely, it should not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, particularly when the data controller is a public authority and it is therefore unlikely that consent has been given freely in all circumstances of that particular situation."  

Thus, it is not appropriate to seek the consent of the data subject in any case where the processing is covered by any of the grounds included in letters b) to f) of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.  

However, regarding the existence of a public interest as a legitimizing basis for the processing of personal data, which is repeatedly invoked in the inquiry, it must be strictly in accordance with what is indicated in letter e) of 6.1 in relation to its paragraph 3: "The basis for the processing referred to in paragraph 1, letters c) and e), shall be established by:  
   a) Union law, or  
   b) the law of the Member States applicable to the data controller."

Regarding private universities, the publication of productivity data of employees could find its legal basis in what is provided in Article 6.1.f) of the Regulation, according to which such processing will be possible if it "is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that those interests do not prevail over the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject requiring the protection of personal data," as long as it responds to a legitimate purpose of the university, such as incentivizing the productivity of its workers, as well as a legitimate interest of the employees themselves, who in this way will be able to know their own performance in comparison with that of their colleagues, also ensuring the transparency of this data which has its corresponding economic impact, provided that the necessary guarantees are adopted by the controller to ensure that workers are not identified by their name and surname, and that the exposure takes place in an environment to which only the employees themselves have access.

"Notwithstanding the existence of such a legitimate interest, the appreciation of the same requires, as already pointed out by the Judgment of the CJEU of November 24, 2011, and the Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC prepared by the Article 29 Working Party on data protection, that a weighing or 'balancing test' be carried out with the rights and interests of the affected parties, indicating for these purposes the aforementioned Opinion that 'it is important to highlight the special role that guarantees can play in reducing undue impact on the data subjects and, therefore, in shifting the balance of rights and interests to the point that the legitimate interest of the data controller prevails,' citing among such possible guarantees the minimization of data, privacy protection technologies, increased transparency, the general and unconditional right to voluntary exclusion, and data portability.

In the present case, each worker is identified by their registration number, a piece of information known only to the employee themselves and the personnel in the Human Resources Department, and the necessary organizational and technical measures must be adopted to prevent access to the rest of the personal data and the individual identification of each worker by unauthorized persons, ensuring that the secrecy of such data is sufficiently guaranteed.

On the other hand, it must be guaranteed that the publication is carried out in a manner that is least harmful to the data subject. In this sense, this Agency, in relation to the publicity of the productivity of public officials, has deemed it necessary that access to the data does not lead to subsequent treatments that may be contrary to the provisions of data protection legislation or generate situations that could jeopardize the rights of employees, with a general exposure of the data being preferable, avoiding its publication on a network, even if it is private, or its transmission by email. In any case, the exposure must take place in an environment to which only the employees themselves have access, thus avoiding its public dissemination to persons who are not part of the corresponding employment relationship, a requirement that seems to be met in the present case as the publication is limited to the notice board of the room where the work activity takes place.

Outside of the indicated cases, the transfer to third parties of data related to productivity, bonuses, or allowances will require the consent of the affected parties, and this Agency does not agree that it can be carried out, in general, on the basis of a public interest in scrutinizing the proper use of public funds, beyond the cases expressly included in a norm with the rank of law, such as Law 19/2013, of December 9, on transparency, access to public information, and good governance.

IV 

Regarding access to the contents of final projects (bachelor's thesis, master's thesis, or doctoral thesis), the inquirer considers that the express and unequivocal consent of the author is necessary, without prejudice to the application of intellectual property regulations.

Focusing exclusively on the matter of data protection, in the case of doctoral theses, it must comply with the provisions established in Royal Decree 99/2011, of January 28, which regulates official doctoral education, whose Article 11.2 states that “Individuals enrolled in a Doctoral program shall be subject to the legal regime, and if applicable, contractual, resulting from the specific legislation that applies to them,” providing in its Article 13.3 that “The university shall guarantee the publicity of the completed doctoral thesis so that during the evaluation process, and prior to the defense act, other doctors may submit observations regarding its content” and Article 14.5 that “Once the doctoral thesis is approved, the university shall ensure its archiving in an open electronic format in an institutional repository and shall send, in electronic format, a copy of it as well as all necessary supplementary information to the Ministry of Education for the appropriate purposes.”

Therefore, the publication of the thesis is provided for in these provisions, thus it would be covered under the provisions of Article 6.1.b of the GDPR: the processing is necessary for the execution of a contract in which the data subject is a party or for the application at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures.

All of this is without prejudice to the provisions of Article 14.6 of the aforementioned royal decree: “In exceptional circumstances determined by the academic committee of the program, such as, among others, the participation of companies in the program or school, the existence of confidentiality agreements with companies, or the possibility of generating patents that pertain to the content of the thesis, universities shall enable procedures to develop the previous sections 4 and 5 that ensure the non-publicity of these aspects.”

In the case of final projects for bachelor's and master's degrees, in the absence of a similar regulation, it must adhere to the provisions of each university's regulations to determine whether access is appropriate as part of the legal or contractual regime of the program in question. Otherwise, consent from the affected party should be obtained.

In cases where access is requested under the Law 19/2013, of December 9, on transparency, access to public information, and good governance, the provisions therein must be applied, particularly those in Article 15.

VII 

It is then questioned whether the recording of oral exams and teaching sessions can also be based on Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR. 

At this point, Article 46.3 of the LOU provides that “Universities shall establish procedures for verifying students' knowledge.” Furthermore, in its paragraph 2, the aforementioned provision recognizes students' right to “the publicity of the rules of the Universities that must regulate the verification of students' knowledge” and to “the guarantee of their rights, through appropriate procedures and, where applicable, the action of the University Ombudsman,” with the Statutes and the rules of organization and functioning being those that will develop the rights and duties of students, as well as the mechanisms for their guarantee. 

Therefore, it is the Statutes and the rules of organization and functioning that regulate the procedures for reviewing students' evaluations, which sometimes includes their review before collegiate bodies (Appeals Tribunal, Review Commission, or other names), as well as establishing retention periods for exams so that students can submit the appropriate claims. 

Consequently, the recording of oral exams may be necessary as a means of proof for the exercise of students' rights, as well as for the professor to justify the evaluation carried out, without prejudice to the acceptance of other evidentiary means (for example, requiring the student to provide an outline of what they will present). 

Therefore, for the stated purpose, and provided that the internal rules of the University foresee the recording of oral exams, the processing will be grounded in what is provided in Article 6.1(e): the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller. 

Regarding the recording of teaching sessions, it is necessary to differentiate, as the inquiry does, depending on whether the recording is carried out by the teacher themselves, which may be convenient or even necessary (for example, in distance learning universities) for the exercise of the educational function, whose basis would also be found in Article 6.1(e), and access should be limited to teaching staff and the students to whom it is directed, without it being used subsequently for other purposes, such as public disclosure, which would require the express consent of the affected individuals. 

On the other hand, in the case that the recording is carried out by the University itself for labor control purposes, its basis would be found in Article 6.1(b): the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract in which the data subject is a party or for the application at their request of pre-contractual measures, with the provisions of Article 89 of Organic Law 3/2018 being applicable. 

Regarding the recording of the session by students present in the teaching session, the specific purpose of such recording should be identified, which will require, in any case, the consent of the teacher, as well as the other students if their image or voice could be subject to recording.

Although the right to privacy of the deceased ceases with their death, the right to privacy of third parties who were related to them may still exist, or even of those who were mentioned in their information or messages, and who could be infringed upon as a consequence of the knowledge of such information, according to the aforementioned jurisprudential doctrine.

There is no "secret" for the person to whom the communication is directed, nor does the retention by any means of the content of the message contravene the provisions of Article 18.3 of the Constitution. This formal condition of the secrecy of communications (the presumption "iuris et de iure" that what is communicated is "secret" in a substantial sense) sheds light on the identity of the generic subject upon whom the duty imposed by the constitutional norm weighs. The lifting of the prohibition by Article 9.2.j) does not exempt the requirement that, for the processing to be lawful, one of the legal bases provided in Article 6.1 must also be met, which requires differentiating whether the data was previously obtained for another purpose or if it is being obtained for the first time.

If the data was lawfully obtained for another purpose, what is stated in Article 5.1 b) applies, so the further processing of personal data for scientific and historical research purposes will not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes, and a separate legal basis, distinct from that which allowed the obtaining of the personal data, is not required.

If the data is being obtained for the first time, one of the bases in Article 6.1 must be met, among which, as indicated in the consultation, is letter e), which would be applicable, as this Agency has repeatedly pointed out, regarding the research included in the scientific research plans regulated by Law 14/2011, of June 1, on Science, Technology, and Innovation, and provided that the community or national regulations do not require the express consent of the data subject.

However, as we indicated earlier, it will also be necessary to take into account what the special regulations may foresee, establishing the appropriate guarantees referred to by the GDPR. Thus, for example, Law 16/1985, of June 25, regulating the Spanish Historical Heritage, provides in its Article 57.1 that “The consultation of the documents constituting the Spanish documentary heritage referred to in Article 49.2 shall be subject to the following rules:... b) documents containing personal data of a police, procedural, clinical, or any other nature that may affect the safety of individuals, their honor, the privacy of their personal and family life, and their own image, may not be publicly consulted without the express consent of the affected parties or until 25 years have passed since their death if their date is known or, in other cases, 50 years from the date of the documents.”

Therefore, it will be necessary to assess in each specific case whether the research affects special categories of data, whether the data in question had already been obtained for other purposes or is being obtained for the first time, whether Law 14/2011 applies, or whether special rules are established in the laws regulating the processing, the specific data, or the sources of origin. This assessment must be made by the responsible party on a case-by-case basis, without prejudice to adopting the other guarantees deemed appropriate, unless it concerns anonymized data, in which case the GDPR does not apply.

The aforementioned Judgment of the Court of Justice has expressly declared the direct effect of Article 7 f) of Directive 95/46/EC, according to which:

“Member States shall provide that the processing of personal data may only be carried out if (...) it is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data are communicated, provided that the interests or the rights and fundamental freedoms of the data subject requiring protection under paragraph 1 of Article 1 of this Directive do not prevail.”

Therefore, this provision must be taken directly into account in the application of personal data protection regulations by Member States, and consequently by this Spanish Agency for Data Protection, given that, as the Supreme Court points out in its judgment of February 8, 2012, “it produces immediate legal effects without the need for national rules for its application, and therefore can be invoked before administrative and judicial authorities when its transgression is observed.”

As the Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union recalls in its paragraph 38, Article 7 f) of the Directive “establishes two cumulative requirements for the lawful processing of personal data, namely, on the one hand, that such processing of personal data is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data are communicated, and, on the other hand, that the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail” and, in relation to the aforementioned balancing, paragraph 40 recalls that it “will depend, in principle, on the specific circumstances of the particular case in question and within which the person or institution carrying out the balancing must take into account the importance of the rights conferred on the data subject by Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union.”

For this reason, the judgment states in its paragraph 46 that Member States, when adapting their legal systems to Directive 95/46, must “seek to base themselves on an interpretation of this that allows them to guarantee a fair balance between the different rights and fundamental freedoms protected by the legal order of the Union, so that, according to its paragraph 47, ‘nothing prevents Member States, in exercising the margin of appreciation conferred on them by Article 5 of Directive 95/46, from establishing the principles that must govern such balancing.’”

Therefore, to determine whether the application of the aforementioned provision would be appropriate, the balancing rule provided for therein must be applied; that is to say, it will be necessary to assess whether in the specific case under analysis there exists a legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data are communicated that prevails over the interest or the rights and fundamental freedoms of the data subject requiring protection in accordance with the provisions of Article 1 of the LOPD, according to which “this Organic Law aims to guarantee and protect, with respect to the processing of personal data, the public freedoms and fundamental rights of individuals, and especially their honor and personal and family privacy,” or whether, on the contrary, such fundamental rights or interests of the data subjects referred to in the processing of the data must prevail over the legitimate interest on which the data controller or the third party intends to base the processing or transfer of personal data.

In this way, for the purpose of carrying out the necessary balancing required by the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice, it must be considered whether, taking into account the specific circumstances occurring in the present case, the interest of the third party in accessing the requested data should prevail over the right to data protection of the affected individuals whose data are subject to transfer.

Therefore, this article establishes the principle that personal data may not be used for purposes incompatible with those for which they were collected. 
 
The ruling of the National Court dated February 14, 2014 (Rec. 97/2013) has captured the doctrine of this court regarding the concept of "incompatible purposes," citing the ruling of this court dated March 17, 2004 (Rec. 641/2001). 
 
"Regarding the interpretation of the term incompatible purposes, the SAN of March 17, 2004 (Rec. 641/2001) states, 'When Article 4.2 of Organic Law 15/1999 refers to incompatible purposes, it is using an expression inspired by Article 1.b) of Directive 95/46, EC, of the Parliament and the Council, of October 24, 1995, but the transposition of the term incompatible is confusing and ambiguous and has been reflected as such in Spanish law. It suffices to point out that a piece of personal data collected for a specific purpose may be improperly used for another different activity, which, however, is not strictly used for that (sic). Applying Article 4.2 of the Organic Law in a literalist manner would render it meaningless and devoid of content, and to avoid this undesirable outcome, this Chamber considers that what the provision prohibits is the use of personal data for a purpose different from that for which it was collected.' 
 
That is to say, if the collection or provision of personal data was carried out for specific purposes, any subsequent use or processing that is not in line with the purposes for which they were provided and for which the affected party did not consent is incompatible with the purpose that determined the provision, using the LOPD the expression "incompatible purposes" in the sense of "different purposes." 
 
Thus, for the analysis of the possibility of the transfer, it will be necessary to determine whether the purpose for which the data was collected is different from the purpose intended to be achieved with the transfer. In this case, the inquirer states that "the purpose of the aforementioned video surveillance system is solely to guarantee security in the facilities and of the elements and persons present therein." From the public information available on the AEPD website (www.agpd.es), it can be inferred that of the 12 files registered as of this date by the inquirer, one of them fits the description provided: it is the file called "physical access control," whose description and typification of the purpose is as follows, according to the description provided by the inquirer in the file registration: physical access control to buildings and facilities and management of general services (travel and transfers, allocation of parking spaces): management of clients, accounting, tax and administrative, human resources, security and access control to buildings, video surveillance, statistical, historical, or scientific purposes. In light of both the description and typification provided for this file in the data of its registration with this Agency and the mere description of it in the inquiry made, we see that the purposes related to "security," "video surveillance," "allocation of parking spaces," and "human resources," are included, which serve to determine that in this case the purpose for which the data was collected is compatible, or not different, in accordance with the provisions of Article 4.2 LOPD, with the purpose for which its transfer is requested. 
 
That is to say, in general terms, it must be concluded that the transfer of the data whose inquiry is presented for report does not pursue a different or incompatible purpose with the purpose for which they were collected, since the expressions used to describe and typify the purpose of the processing, which have already been noted in the previous paragraph, are compatible with the purpose for which the transfer is intended, as there is no doubt that the purpose of transferring the data for the purpose of filing a civil lawsuit or criminal complaint for certain acts that may constitute a criminal or civil offense falls within the broad term of "security." 
 
However, it would not be feasible to authorize the transfer of data for purposes that are different or incompatible with those that served to describe or typify the purpose of the file, so that if the transfer were aimed at something different from the purposes for which the data was collected, its transfer would not comply with data protection legislation without the consent of the affected party.

Therefore, it will be appropriate to uphold the appeal and annul the contested resolution based on the prevalence of the general interest, taking into account that, as is natural, such use will only be protected within the purposes related to the same competitive selection process in application of the limits set forth in Article 4 of the LOPD (...). 
The cited legal regulations support publication and contemplate a regime aimed at protecting a public legal good such as the right of participants in the process to obtain the necessary information to assert their rights, and the principle of publicity and transparency becomes essential, as a guarantor of the principle of equality. The National Court has weighed the principle of publicity against the protection of personal data, concluding that during the processing of the selection process, the former must prevail.

“5.1    Personal data shall be:

Well, the seventh additional provision of the LOPDDD refers to the manner of identifying data subjects in cases such as the one submitted for consultation, specifically regulating those cases in which publication aims to inform the addressee of the act, without pursuing public knowledge of said act. Conversely, if—for example—a sanctioning resolution condemns an offender with a public warning through publication in an official bulletin, that publication must fully identify them and not seek to avoid complete identification.  
What the legislator intends with the introduction of the seventh additional provision of the LOPDDD is not only to guarantee compliance with the principles of data protection but also to ensure the integrity of individuals' identities. It is not about limiting transparency but about ensuring that identifying data of a person is not published in full, so as to prevent situations of identity theft, undue interference in personal freedom and the free development of personality, persecution, stalking, or, ultimately, other types of actions constituting criminal or administrative offenses.  
Through this provision, a uniform criterion is incorporated into our legal system, without prejudice to the fact that, for special situations—such as those occurring with victims of gender-based violence (see paragraph 2 of the seventh additional provision itself)—a higher degree of confidentiality may be established.
