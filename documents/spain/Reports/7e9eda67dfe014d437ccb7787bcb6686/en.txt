The inquiry refers to compliance with the obligations arising from Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (hereinafter GDPR), in relation to the consultant's involvement in providing services to entities that carry out biomedical research actions.  

I  
In general terms, research activities in the field of biomedicine and health sciences are regulated by Regulation 536/2014 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 16, 2014, by Law 14/2007, of July 3, on Biomedical Research, and by Royal Decree 1090/2015, of December 4, which regulates clinical trials with medicines, the Ethics Committees for Research with medicines, and the Spanish Registry of Clinical Studies.  

Regarding the processing of personal data derived from such activities, in addition to the provisions contained in the aforementioned regulation, they are governed by the GDPR and the provisions of the seventeenth additional provision of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), under the heading "Processing of health data."  

The consultant defines their activity as a mere collaborating intermediary in the clinical trial with the trial sponsor and the medical researcher, solely for the purpose of facilitating the patient’s receipt of the financial support provided by the Laboratory, and to maintain the patient's anonymity with respect to the laboratory, asserting that they do not have access at any time to the patient's medical data.  

The purpose of the inquiry is to determine the need to regulate their relationship with the Hospital or Center where the clinical trial is conducted, given that it is the holder of the patients' data.  

From the information provided by the consultant, (i) it cannot be determined that their activity could fall into any category of participants in a clinical trial according to the applicable regulations on biomedical research and, in particular, on clinical trials, but rather that their activity is limited to providing a service to one of the participants in the trial, in this case, the sponsoring laboratory.  

Nor (ii) can it be inferred that they handle special categories of data (Article 9 GDPR) such as health data of the participants in the trial.  

Therefore, it is appropriate to analyze their activity in relation to the general categories of participants provided by the GDPR and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD).  

II  
As a starting point, we must refer to what is indicated in Recital 79 of the GDPR, which states that "(...) The protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, as well as the responsibilities of controllers and processors, including with respect to the supervision by the supervisory authorities and the measures adopted by them, requires a clear attribution of responsibilities under this Regulation, including cases where a controller determines the purposes and means of processing jointly with other controllers, or where processing is carried out on behalf of a controller. (...)"

The GDPR emphasizes the need to clearly establish the map of participants in any data processing, in order to accurately determine the attribution of responsibilities according to the aforementioned regulation.  

This regulation aims to ensure that there are no scenarios of action outside its scope of application, in order to provide supervisory authorities with the necessary elements to carry out their function and ultimately to provide European citizens with the protection that their personal data deserves. Therefore, any activity that involves the processing of personal data will be attributable to some subject that meets the requirements of the different categories offered by the GDPR.  

In accordance with what has been indicated, the GDPR generally establishes (and without prejudice to the figures of the recipient and third party) three scenarios of participants in the processing of personal data: the data controller, the joint controllers, and the data processor.

The GDPR defines in its Article 4.7 the figure of the data controller or controller as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the controller or the specific criteria for their appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States.”  
Alongside this figure, the GDPR defines in its Article 26 the joint controller, determining the legal regime to which it must be subject: “1. When two or more controllers jointly determine the objectives and means of processing, they shall be considered joint controllers. The joint controllers shall transparently and by mutual agreement determine their respective responsibilities in complying with the obligations imposed by this Regulation, in particular regarding the exercise of the rights of the data subject and their respective obligations to provide information referred to in Articles 13 and 14, unless, and to the extent that, their respective responsibilities are governed by the law of the Union or of the Member States applicable to them. Such an agreement may designate a point of contact for the data subjects. 
2. The agreement referred to in paragraph 1 shall duly reflect the respective functions and relationships of the joint controllers in relation to the data subjects. The essential aspects of the agreement shall be made available to the data subject. 
3. Regardless of the terms of the agreement referred to in paragraph 1, data subjects may exercise the rights conferred upon them by this Regulation against, and in relation to, each of the controllers.” 
Therefore, to determine the compliance with obligations and the assumption of responsibilities of each joint controller, one must refer to the agreement that covers their relationship. 
In the same vein, to determine the responsibility of each joint controller, Article 29 of the LOPDGDD offers another additional parameter to the aforementioned agreement, indicating that the determination of the responsibilities referred to in Article 26.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 shall be carried out taking into account the activities actually carried out by each of the joint controllers of the processing. 
Ultimately, the relationship between joint controllers will be formally determined by the agreement provided for in Article 26 of the GDPR and materially according to the activities they perform in relation to the processing being carried out. 
III
The GDPR defines in its Article 4.8 the figure of the data processor or processor: the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller. 
In this regard, it should be noted that the figure of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, so that in those cases where the data controller entrusts a third party with the provision of a service that involves the processing of personal data, we will be dealing with processing carried out on behalf of the controller. 
This does not necessarily imply that the data subject to processing is owned by the controller, but rather that the processing operations, including, for example, collection, are attributed to the controller. 
This means that the processing of the data is carried out by the processor on behalf of the controller as if it were the controller itself carrying it out. 
As another manifestation of the principle of proactive responsibility, the GDPR imposes on the data controller an obligation of diligence when choosing a data processor, indicating in Recital 81 the following: “To ensure compliance with the provisions of this Regulation regarding the processing carried out by the processor on behalf of the controller, the controller, when entrusting processing activities to a processor, must only use processors that provide sufficient guarantees, in particular regarding specialized knowledge, reliability, and resources, with respect to the implementation of technical and organizational measures that meet the requirements of this Regulation, including the security of processing.” 
Regarding the formal support of the relationship between the controller and the processor, Article 28 of the GDPR requires in its third paragraph the existence of a contract or other legal act under Union law or the law of the Member States that binds the processor to the controller. The contract or legal act must be in writing, including in electronic format, as indicated in paragraph 9 of that article.

Among the determinations that must be included in such a contract, the first stipulation is that the processor "will process personal data only in accordance with documented instructions from the controller, including with respect to the transfers of personal data to a third country or an international organization, unless required to do so by Union law or the laws of the Member States applicable to the processor; in such a case, the processor shall inform the controller of that legal requirement prior to processing, unless such law prohibits it for important reasons of public interest." Furthermore, item 10 of Article 28 establishes that “Without prejudice to the provisions of Articles 82, 83, and 84, if a processor infringes this Regulation by determining the purposes and means of processing, it shall be considered responsible for the processing in relation to that processing.”

On its part, the LOPDGDD in its Article 33 regulates the figure of the processor and provides clarifications to determine when we are faced with this figure, indicating the following:
1. Access by a processor to personal data that is necessary for the provision of a service to the controller shall not be considered a communication of data as long as the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, this organic law, and its implementing regulations are complied with.
2. A person who establishes relationships with data subjects in their own name and without it being recorded that they act on behalf of another shall be considered a controller and not a processor, even if there is a contract or legal act with the content set forth in Article 28.3 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679. This provision shall not apply to processing assignments made within the framework of public sector procurement legislation.

As can be observed, the LOPDGDD aims to provide solutions to scenarios that are practically backed by a contract and that in reality correspond to a false processor or simulated assignment, since materially, the contracted entity decides on the use and purpose of the processing by establishing direct relationships with the data subjects, thus exceeding the assignment stated in the contract and becoming a controller.

IV
Regarding the general obligations of the controller and the processor, it is necessary to consider, in addition to those arising from compliance with the general principles provided in Article 5 of the GDPR, the right to information provided in Articles 13 and 14 of the GDPR, and the obligations arising from the principle of proactive responsibility, the provisions of Article 28 of the LOPDGDD, which states the following:
1. Controllers and processors, taking into account the elements listed in Articles 24 and 25 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, shall determine the appropriate technical and organizational measures that must be applied in order to ensure and demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the aforementioned regulation, this organic law, its implementing regulations, and the applicable sectoral legislation. In particular, they shall assess whether it is appropriate to carry out a data protection impact assessment and the prior consultation referred to in Section 3 of Chapter IV of the aforementioned regulation.
2. For the adoption of the measures referred to in the previous paragraph, controllers and processors shall take into account, in particular, the higher risks that could arise in the following cases:
a) When the processing could generate situations of discrimination, identity theft or fraud, financial losses, damage to reputation, loss of confidentiality of data subject to professional secrecy, unauthorized reversal of pseudonymization, or any other significant economic, moral, or social harm to the data subjects.
b) When the processing could deprive data subjects of their rights and freedoms or could prevent them from exercising control over their personal data.
c) When the processing involves non-merely incidental or accessory processing of special categories of data referred to in Articles 9 and 10 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and Articles 9 and 10 of this organic law or data related to the commission of administrative offenses.
d) When the processing involves an evaluation of personal aspects of the data subjects in order to create or use personal profiles of them, particularly through the analysis or prediction of aspects related to their work performance, economic situation, health, personal preferences or interests, reliability or behavior, financial solvency, location, or movements.
e) When the processing is carried out on data of groups of data subjects in a situation of special vulnerability, particularly minors and persons with disabilities.

f) When there is a massive processing that involves a large number of affected individuals or entails the collection of a large amount of personal data.
g) When personal data is to be transferred, on a regular basis, to third countries or international organizations for which an adequate level of protection has not been declared.
h) Any other circumstances that, in the opinion of the data controller or the processor, may be relevant, particularly those provided for in codes of conduct and standards defined by certification schemes.

V
In the case presented by the consultant, if they act within the framework of providing a service to the pharmaceutical laboratory/promoter of the trial and process personal data on its behalf, they will qualify as a data processor and, therefore, will be subject to the described obligations. From the information and documentation provided in the consultation, it is observed that through an agreement-contract, they collect personal data from the participants, for the provision of the service as an intermediary between them and the promoting laboratory of the trial, a circumstance that is expressly stated in the data collection document. That is to say, despite collecting data directly from the data subject for the completion of the agreement, they do so on behalf of the laboratory, and therefore in this case will be considered a data processor.

VI
Regarding the obligations arising from the relationship with the Hospital Center where the clinical trial is conducted, and to the extent that the terms on which it is based are unknown—since nothing is indicated in this regard nor is documentation provided in the consultation—it is not possible to conduct an in-depth analysis regarding the adequacy of the processing to the GDPR and LOPDGDD; attention can only be drawn to the fact that the consultant claims that the data belongs to them, so it could happen that the personal data of the participants is provided by the Hospital Center so that the consultant signs an agreement with them to cover the costs of the trial and guarantee anonymity from the laboratory. However, as inferred from the information provided in the consultation, this service—of anonymization and payment of expenses—is provided by the consultant to the laboratory and not to the Center.

Therefore, even if the consultant may be the recipient of the personal data of the participants whose ownership corresponds to the Center, it cannot be overlooked, as previously mentioned, that the service of managing the expenses and guaranteeing the anonymity of the patients is provided to the laboratory, that is, as a data processor for it. 

Thus, this processing is attributable to the laboratory, which will have the appropriate contract or agreement with the Hospital Center that establishes the legal framework regulating its activity—information that is also unknown as nothing is indicated or provided in the consultation. This legal framework is where the obligations related to the protection of the data of the participants in the clinical trial are stipulated, and they could even be considered joint controllers of the processing if the circumstances provided for in the aforementioned Article 26 of the GDPR are met. 

Another issue would be if the consultant provides any service to the Center, and in that case, just like in the previous scenario, it will be necessary to consider the service relationship between both parties. That is, if personal data of the patients is processed in the development of a service provided to the Center, they will qualify as a data processor, but on this occasion, they would be the data processor of the Center, and therefore will be subject to the indicated obligations.
