The inquiry raises whether the request for information made by the General Directorate of Gaming, General Secretariat of Consumption and Gaming, of the Ministry of Consumption, to the Royal Spanish Football Federation (RFEF hereinafter) under the Law 13/2011, of May 27, on the regulation of gaming (LRJ) is compliant with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (GDPR hereinafter) and with Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD hereinafter).  

The RFEF considers that complying with the information request may contravene the GDPR as it could be contrary to the principles of lawfulness, purpose, and minimization.  

In support of this consideration, it attaches a report from the Data Protection Officer, which, in summary, states that the request for information has not been sufficiently justified, as it should be related to a specific and concrete investigation.  

The General Directorate of Gaming (DGOJ hereinafter) does not explain why it is necessary for its investigation to know the identity of a large number of individuals as would be required to respond to the request. It also refers to what is indicated in Organic Law 7/2021, of May 26, on the protection of personal data processed for the purposes of prevention, detection, investigation, and prosecution of criminal offenses and enforcement of criminal sanctions, regarding the duty of cooperation provided for in Article 7 and how requests must be made, that is, in a reasoned, concrete, and specific manner, specifying the situation of real and serious danger that justifies such a request, and the DGOJ's request does not take these aspects into account.  

It also considers that the request, in addition to using ellipses that create ambiguity and vagueness, is made concerning individuals not obligated by the LRJ (Article 6.2) such as attendees and equipment managers.  

Finally, it considers that it would also be excessive to provide the addresses of all possible affected individuals, as requested in the requirement.  

I  
Having outlined the terms of the inquiry, it should be indicated that for the processing of personal data to be compliant with the GDPR, among other elements of the data protection system, the principles of data protection contained in Article 5.1 of the GDPR must be fulfilled, according to which personal data shall be:  
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (“lawfulness, fairness, and transparency”);  
b) collected for specified, legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (“purpose limitation”);  
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”);  
d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable steps must be taken to ensure that personal data that are inaccurate are erased or rectified without delay (“accuracy”);  
e) kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of the processing of personal data; personal data may be stored for longer periods as long as they are processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, without prejudice to the application of the appropriate technical and organizational measures required by this Regulation to safeguard the rights and freedoms of the data subject (“storage limitation”);  
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage.

II
The principle of lawfulness determines under what circumstances or conditions a processing operation should be considered lawful.
In general, the circumstances or legal bases that legitimize the processing of personal data are found in Article 6 of the GDPR, and regarding special categories of data, in Article 9.2 of the GDPR, which outlines the circumstances under which the general prohibition on processing such data established in paragraph 1 does not apply, i.e., it is exempted.
This means that in order to process these latter types of data, coverage must be found in Article 9.2 GDPR, and once the general prohibition is exempted, one must refer to the circumstances of Article 6 GDPR to legitimize the processing in question. This was indicated by the Article 29 Working Party (whose functions have been assumed by the European Data Protection Board) in its opinion “Guidelines on Automated Individual Decision-Making and Profiling for the Purposes of Regulation 2016/679” by stating that (…) Data controllers may only process special category personal data if one of the conditions laid down in Article 9, paragraph 2, is met, as well as a condition from Article 6. (…).

Regarding the motivation of administrative acts, the Supreme Court has declared, as in the Judgment of April 4, 2012, that “the duty of the Administration to motivate its acts, as stated by the Supreme Court, among others, in the Judgment of November 19, 2001, has its constitutional link in the principle of legality proclaimed in Article 103 of the Constitution, as well as in the effectiveness of judicial control over the actions of the Administration recognized in Article 106 of the same Constitution.
