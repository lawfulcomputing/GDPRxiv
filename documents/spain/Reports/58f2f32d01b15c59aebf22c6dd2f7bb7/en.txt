I  
The submitted text aims to transpose Directive (EU) 2019/1153 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of June 20, 2019, which establishes rules intended to facilitate the use of financial and other information for the prevention, detection, investigation, or prosecution of criminal offenses and repealing Decision 2000/642/JAI of the Council.  

The aforementioned Directive, based on the premise that the fight against serious forms of crime, particularly against financial fraud and money laundering, remains a priority for the Union (Recital 5) and that facilitating the use of financial information is necessary to prevent, detect, investigate, or prosecute serious crimes (Recital 1), aims to improve access to information by Financial Intelligence Units (FIUs) and public authorities responsible for the prevention, detection, investigation, or prosecution of serious crimes, enhance their capacity to conduct financial investigations, and improve cooperation among them (Recitals 2 and 31).

Thus, the Directive establishes measures aimed at facilitating access to financial information and information about bank accounts, as well as its use by the competent authorities, for the prevention, detection, investigation, or prosecution of serious criminal offenses (Chapter II). It also establishes measures aimed at facilitating the exchange of information between the competent authorities and the Financial Intelligence Units (FIUs) and among the FIUs (Chapter III) and the exchange of information with Europol (Chapter IV). 

During the processing of the aforementioned Directive, various bodies reported on the need to reconcile the objectives pursued by it with the necessary respect for the fundamental right to the protection of personal data. In this regard, the Regulatory Control Committee particularly highlighted that the scope of this initiative was not well defined, especially regarding the expansion of cross-border cooperation and the justification for operating without a judicial authorization, and that the impacts on fundamental rights were not exhaustively examined, particularly given the extent of its scope to serious offenses. Furthermore, the European Data Protection Supervisor, in its opinion of September 10, 2018, emphasized the importance of highlighting the full application of European legislation on the protection of personal data, making special reference to the principles of purpose limitation, necessity, and proportionality, and positively evaluating some of the guarantees established, such as access logs or information request logs.

The approved Directive has partially incorporated these observations, containing numerous references to the need to respect the right to the protection of personal data (Recitals 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, and 28) and establishing some specific guarantees, including, in addition to the continuous references to necessity and proportionality, the conditions that personnel must meet to access the centralized bank account records or the need to adopt the necessary technical and organizational measures to ensure data security (Article 5), or the establishment by the authorities managing the centralized bank account records of a log each time the designated competent authorities access and consult information about bank accounts, which will be regularly examined by data protection delegates and will be available to the competent supervisory authority (Article 6). It also includes a specific chapter relating to supplementary provisions regarding the processing of personal data (Chapter V), applicable to the cases of information exchanges regulated in Chapters III and IV that refer to the processing of sensitive personal data (Article 16), the logging of information requests (Article 17), and the restrictions on the rights of data subjects (Article 18).

II
The draft bill subject to this report proceeds with the transposition of Directive (EU) 2019/1153, including most of the guarantees related to the protection of personal data previously referenced. However, within the margin of appreciation allowed by the Directive and for the purpose of its proper incorporation into our legal system, it must be taken into account, in certain cases that will be referenced later, the doctrine of the Court of Justice of the European Union and our Constitutional Court regarding the restrictive measures on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data. 

Therefore, before proceeding to the specific analysis of the submitted text, it is deemed appropriate to recall this doctrine, as well as the criteria maintained by this Agency on issues directly related to the content of the regulation, such as those concerning the processing of personal data by the Judicial Police or the requirement for judicial authorization for access to the Financial Holdings File. 

In this regard, and based on the objectives pursued by the Directive, it should be noted that in the Judgment of the Court of Justice (Grand Chamber) of April 8, 2014, in the case of Digital Rights Ireland (joined cases C-293/12 and C-594/12), it recognized that the fight against international terrorism and serious crimes constitutes a general interest objective; however, when the legal tools enacted to pursue that objective interfere with fundamental rights to privacy and data protection, it is necessary to analyze the proportionality of such measures (paragraphs 41 to 48).

More specifically, in the aforementioned rulings regarding data protection, this Court has declared that the right to data protection is not unlimited, and although the Constitution does not expressly impose specific limits on it, nor refers to the Public Powers for its determination as it has done with other fundamental rights, there is no doubt that these limits must be found in the remaining fundamental rights and constitutionally protected legal goods, as required by the principle of unity of the Constitution (SSTC 11/1981, of April 8, F. 7; 196/1987, of December 11 [RTC 1987, 196], F. 6; and regarding art. 18, the STC 110/1984, F. 5). These limits may either be direct restrictions of the fundamental right itself, as previously mentioned, or they may be restrictions on the manner, time, or place of exercising the fundamental right. In the first case, regulating these limits is a way of developing the fundamental right. In the second, the limits set are to the specific manner in which the bundle of powers that constitutes the content of the fundamental right in question can be exercised, constituting a way to regulate its exercise, which ordinary legislators can do in accordance with the provisions of art. 53.1 CE. The first observation that must be made, which is no less crucial for being evident, is that the Constitution has intended for the Law, and only the Law, to set the limits to a fundamental right. Fundamental rights can yield, of course, to goods, and even constitutionally relevant interests, as long as the reduction they experience is necessary to achieve the legitimate purpose provided, proportional to achieve it, and, in any case, respects the essential content of the restricted fundamental right (SSTC 57/1994, of February 28 [RTC 1994, 57], F. 6; 18/1999, of February 22 [RTC 1999, 18], F. 2). 
Indeed, if the Law is the only entity authorized by the Constitution to set limits on fundamental rights and, in the present case, on the fundamental right to data protection, and these limits cannot be different from those constitutionally provided, which in this case are none other than those arising from the coexistence of this fundamental right with other rights and constitutionally ranked legal goods, the legal empowerment that allows a Public Power to collect, store, process, use, and, where appropriate, transfer personal data is only justified if it responds to the protection of other fundamental rights or constitutionally protected goods. Therefore, if those operations with the personal data of a person are not carried out with strict observance of the rules that regulate them, the right to data protection is violated, as constitutionally illegitimate limits are imposed on it, whether to its content or to the exercise of the bundle of powers that compose it. The limiting Law will also violate this if it regulates the limits in such a way that makes the affected fundamental right impracticable or the guarantee ineffective.

To this end, we must make a first observation. The fact that the invalidity of Directive 2006/24/EC has been declared does not mean that the national transposition laws that developed it in each country follow the same fate.
A Directive is an instrument for harmonizing national legislations but allows for margins of discretion. This is evident in relation to data retention, where the legislations of each Member State show notable differences. Hence, once the national norm is in force, if it respects Union law, it has autonomy from the Directive that justifies its existence and can only be repealed by a subsequent norm. A second observation is necessary. At this moment, the European Union, once Directive 2006/24/EC has been annulled, lacks an instrument for harmonizing national legislations. The absence of a community norm requires focusing attention on the doctrine of the CJEU, and we cannot fail to highlight that each new judgment from the high court, as we have tried to summarize previously, adds nuances, establishes exceptions, designs new requirements and modulations, creating doctrines that add and accumulate normative concepts that increase its legal complexity.

The problem arises, however, in relation to those cases in which the Judicial Police requests the transfer of data in order to exercise the functions of investigating the crime and apprehending the responsible party, as there is no judicial mandate or request from the Public Prosecutor that covers the transfer in that case.

The doctrine that has just been highlighted requires that the
massive processing of data for the prosecution of crime is clearly
delimited from a threefold perspective: on the one hand, the data
subject to processing should be minimized; on the other hand, the
circumstances in which access to the data may occur should be
limited, specifying, for example, the nature of the crimes whose
seriousness justifies such access; and finally, there should be
control, which in the case of Spain should be judicial, prior to the
effective access to the information.
The text now under analysis would indeed comply with the first of the
mentioned requirements, as it minimizes, in correlation with the
projected Article 32 bis of the Directive, the amount of data that will
be incorporated into the financial ownership file.
At the same time, in its currently valid wording, the analyzed norm,
Article 43 of Law 10/2010, would also meet the remaining requirements
demanded by jurisprudence, as access is limited in principle to the
prevention, investigation, and prosecution of money laundering and
terrorism financing, and judicial or Prosecutor's Office authorization is
foreseen for the data to be accessible by the Security Forces and
Corps.
However, the text now submitted for report alters the two guarantees
that have just been mentioned.
Thus, firstly, an expansion of the purpose of the file is provided for
the prosecution of crimes preceding money laundering or terrorism
financing, without specifying whether the investigation carried out for
these preceding crimes is independent of that which is actually the
subject of the Law and constituted the initial purpose of the processing.
In this way, access to the file could, in practice, occur in any
investigation of crimes with economic content, regardless of whether
or not there was an investigation regarding the prevention of money
laundering and terrorism financing, since if this were linked to the
preceding crimes, it would not be necessary to carry out an expansion
of the purpose of the file in the legal text.
Similarly, and even more evidently, all reference to prior judicial or
Prosecutor's Office control prior to access to the file disappears from
paragraph 3 of the text, and it is not even indicated that the Security
Forces and Corps accessing the data will do so in their capacity as
judicial police.
This leads to two necessary consequences to ensure the compliance of
the provision with the jurisprudence that has been analyzed
previously: on the one hand, the reference to the preceding crimes
should be removed, maintaining the currently valid text, and, on the

Processing of sensitive personal data
1. The processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs of a person, or membership in a union, or data related to the health of a natural person, or to the sexual life or sexual orientation of a person shall only be authorized subject to the relevant guarantees concerning the rights and freedoms of the data subject, in accordance with the applicable data protection regulations.
2. Only individuals who have received specific training and have been specifically authorized by the data controller may access and process the data referred to in paragraph 1, in accordance with the guidelines of the data protection officer.

As can be seen, paragraph 1 conditions the authorization for the processing of such data “subject to the relevant guarantees concerning the rights and freedoms of the data subject, in accordance with the applicable data protection regulations.”

In this regard, it is appropriate to once again bring to attention the constitutional doctrine regarding the requirements for the processing of special categories of personal data, which is summarized in the ruling of the Constitutional Court (STC) 76/2019, of May 22. This ruling contains the relevant doctrine on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and addresses both the characteristics and the content that the regulations intending to establish an interference in that fundamental right must have.

(…) By express mandate of the Constitution, any state interference in the realm of fundamental rights and public freedoms, whether it directly affects their development (Article 81.1 CE) or limits or conditions their exercise (Article 53.1 CE), requires a legal authorization (see, among others, STC 49/1999, of April 5, FJ 4). (…) This dual function of the legal reservation translates into a dual requirement: on the one hand, the necessary intervention of the law to authorize the interference; and, on the other hand, that legal norm "must meet all those indispensable characteristics as a guarantee of legal certainty," that is, "it must express each and every one of the assumptions and conditions of the intervention" (STC 49/1999, FJ 4). In other words, "it not only excludes delegations in favor of regulatory norms [...], but also implies other requirements regarding the content of the Law that establishes such limits" (STC 292/2000, FJ 15).

c) specify the essential public interest that underlies the restriction of the fundamental right (FJ 7 of STC 76/2019).

b) secondly, the law must regulate in detail the interferences with the fundamental right by establishing clear rules regarding the scope and content of the data processing it authorizes. That is to say, it must establish what the assumptions and conditions are for the processing of personal data related to special categories of personal data that will be subject to processing, through clear and precise rules (STC 76/2019, FJ 7 b).

Furthermore, this law must in all cases respect the principle of proportionality, as recalled by the Constitutional Court Judgment 14/2003, of January 28:

It follows from both the jurisprudence of the Constitutional Court and the EU Court that it is the law itself that establishes the processing of special categories of data (that is, the interference with the fundamental right) that must establish, itself, (i) the essential public interest purpose that justifies it, (ii) clear and precise rules regarding the scope and content of the data processing it authorizes, and (iii) minimum requirements so that individuals whose data have been transferred have sufficient guarantees to effectively protect their personal data against the risks of abuse.
In this regard, the text of the Draft Bill, in addition to the guarantees included in the Directive and transposed in section 3 of article 12, relating to the requirements of the persons who can access the data, adds in its section 2, as specific guarantees, that “In information requests, this necessity will be justified and access will only be granted in cases where such circumstance can be substantiated.” Although the principle of necessity, derived from the principle of proportionality, and the need for justification, is a general requirement applicable to all personal data processing regulated by the draft bill, this Agency positively values the emphasis on the need to comply with it in relation to the processing of special categories of data, requiring the appropriate substantiation. 
However, and as noted in our Report 29/2020, all guarantees deemed appropriate to safeguard the right to personal data protection of the affected individuals should be incorporated, taking into account the type of data being processed and the purpose of the processing, such as limiting access to data that is strictly necessary (principle of minimization), prohibiting or limiting its use for purposes other than the original ones (principle of purpose limitation), ordering its deletion when it is no longer necessary in relation to the pursued purpose (principle of storage limitation), or the need to adopt security measures appropriate to the level of risk derived from the type of data being processed. Likewise, section 3 could include the subjection of personnel processing such data to a specific duty of confidentiality. 
In order to assess the specific guarantees that may be appropriate to the level of risk of processing personal data for the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals, this Agency has been highlighting in its reports on regulatory provisions the advisability of conducting a risk analysis and, even, a Data Protection Impact Assessment, and that these be incorporated into the MAIN.
On the other hand, it should be noted that the Directive only provides for the processing of special categories of data in relation to cases of information exchange between competent authorities and FIUs, and between FIUs, which should also be specified in article 12.2 of the draft bill.
