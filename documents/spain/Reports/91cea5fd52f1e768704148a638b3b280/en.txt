The consultation reveals that since 2008 there has been a Collaboration Agreement signed between the TGSS and ASNEF that regulates the data verification process, with the fundamental objective of verifying the truthfulness of the information that entities collected from their clients when establishing business relationships, in order to understand the nature of their professional or business activity based on the requirements established by the regulations on the prevention of money laundering and the financing of terrorism. In this Agreement, the TGSS requires the collection and custody of the consent of the client or potential client for a period of two years. The text of the agreement is not provided with the consultation. Essentially, it asks whether the figure of blocking could be a sufficient legal basis for the financial entities that are part of ASNEF to safeguard, and not eliminate, the consent that the TGSS requires from these financial entities to request from their clients. It cites articles from the regulations on money laundering and from Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR).  

Secondly, it outlines the functioning of the Central Risk Information System of the Bank of Spain (CIRBE) and that the entities associated with ASNEF also consult this database to assess the credit risk of applicants for a financial operation or to verify data in relation to the obligations imposed by the regulations on money laundering. It cites the regulations that govern the CIRBE and concludes by asking, ultimately, whether the figure of blocking can serve for financial entities to use blocking as a legal basis to retain the signed application by the interested party in which they are informed of the financial entity's right to consult the CIRBE during the period established in the regulation.  

Alternatively, it asks whether it could be understood that the obligations imposed by the regulations on the prevention of money laundering and the financing of terrorism would justify the exclusion of the right to erasure (art. 17 GDPR) of the cited documents (consent and information) that support the aforementioned consultations, based on compliance with a legal obligation, in accordance with paragraph 3, letter b), of the aforementioned art. 17 GDPR.  

I  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines "processing" as any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, limitation, erasure, or destruction;  

As can be seen, it considers that the retention of personal data is a processing of data.  

Article 5.1, letter e) GDPR, within the principles relating to processing, determines that personal data shall be kept in a form which permits identification of the data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of the processing of personal data; personal data may be stored for longer periods as long as they are processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, without prejudice to the application of the appropriate technical and organizational measures imposed by this Regulation to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject ("limitation of the retention period");  

A question of retention for archiving purposes or for scientific purposes is not raised here, so the cited principle obliges the data controller to retain such data for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which they were collected.  

In accordance with this principle, Article 17 GDPR grants data subjects a right to erasure of their personal data, and a corresponding obligation for the controller to erase such data, among other circumstances when the personal data are no longer necessary in relation to the purposes for which they were collected or processed otherwise.

Article 17.3 GDPR establishes that paragraphs 1 and 2 [that is, there will be no grounds for deletion] shall not apply when the processing is necessary: b) for compliance with a legal obligation requiring the processing of data imposed by Union law or the laws of the Member States applicable to the data controller, or for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller.

In interpreting this paragraph, Recital 65 GDPR states: 
(…) In particular, data subjects should have the right to have their personal data erased and cease to be processed if they are no longer necessary for the purposes for which they were collected or otherwise processed, if the data subjects have withdrawn their consent for processing or object to the processing of personal data concerning them, or if the processing of their personal data otherwise infringes this Regulation. (…) However, the further retention of personal data must be lawful when it is necessary for the exercise of freedom of expression and information, for compliance with a legal obligation, for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller, for public interest reasons in the field of public health, for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes, or for the formulation, exercise, or defense of claims.

In these cases, the further retention of such data is lawful, says the GDPR. That is, there would be no right of the data subject to the deletion of personal data.

Article 25 of Law 10/2010, of April 28, on the prevention of money laundering and the financing of terrorism, as amended by Royal Decree-Law 11/2018, of August 31, establishes:
Article 25. Document retention.
1. Obliged subjects shall retain the documentation formalizing compliance with the obligations established in this law for a period of ten years, proceeding thereafter to its deletion. After five years from the termination of the business relationship or the execution of the occasional operation, the retained documentation shall only be accessible by the internal control bodies of the obliged subject, including the technical prevention units, and, where applicable, those responsible for its legal defense.

In particular, obliged subjects shall retain for use in any investigation or analysis regarding possible cases of money laundering or financing of terrorism, by the Executive Service of the Commission or any other legally competent authority:
a) A copy of the documents required under the due diligence measures, for a period of ten years from the termination of the business relationship or the execution of the operation.
b) The original or a copy with evidential value of the documents or records that adequately substantiate the operations, the parties involved in them, and the business relationships, for a period of ten years from the execution of the operation or the termination of the business relationship.
2. Obliged subjects, with the exceptions determined by regulation, shall store copies of the identification documents referred to in Article 3.2 on optical, magnetic, or electronic media that ensure their integrity, the correct reading of the data, the impossibility of manipulation, and their proper preservation and location.

In any case, the filing system of the obliged subjects must ensure the proper management and availability of the documentation, both for internal control purposes and for timely and proper response to the requirements of the authorities.

That is, it imposes on the obliged subjects an obligation to retain certain data and documents for specific periods of time. This legal obligation to retain documents and the purposes to which its use is subject after the five-year period referred to in the provision determine that the right to deletion of the data subject is displaced here by law. 

This is, on the other hand, what Article 32 of Law 10/2010, of April 28, on the prevention of money laundering and the financing of terrorism, establishes regarding the protection of personal data.

In short, Law 10/2010, of April 28, on the prevention of money laundering and the financing of terrorism, itself exempts the right of the interested party to the deletion of their personal data in that context. Therefore, it can be concluded that the legal obligation imposed on the obligated subjects by the regulations on the prevention of money laundering and the financing of terrorism, to retain the documentation referred to in Law 10/2010, constitutes a necessary processing for the fulfillment of a legal obligation that can be included within the exception to the right of deletion provided for in Article 17.3, letter b) of the GDPR. 
However, this exception to the right of the interested party to the deletion of their personal data does not imply, by any means, an exception that the obligated subject (the financial entity) must process personal data in accordance with data protection regulations (see Article 32.1 of Law 10/2010, of April 28, on the prevention of money laundering and the financing of terrorism), so the principles regarding processing provided for in Article 5 of the GDPR will continue to apply, including the principle of purpose limitation (letter b); and above all, the principle of integrity and confidentiality (letter f), which determines that such personal data must be processed in such a way as to ensure an adequate level of security of personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, through the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures. 
In conclusion, given that Article 32 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights establishes that blocking is an obligation imposed on data controllers when the deletion of data is appropriate, and since in this case, as stated, the deletion of data is not applicable, blocking cannot be considered a sufficient legal basis for the retention of data by the controller. And this, it should be added to conclude, without prejudice to the fact that the data controller may adopt those measures included in the definition or description of blocking contained in that Article 32 as technical and organizational measures for the protection of personal data, provided that, certainly, such measures do not hinder the processing imposed on obligated subjects by legal obligation under the legislation on the prevention of money laundering.
