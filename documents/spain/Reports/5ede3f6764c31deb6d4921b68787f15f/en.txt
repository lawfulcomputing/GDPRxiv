The inquiries raise a series of issues related to the legal position and functions of the data protection officer.  

I  
For the proper addressing of the issues raised, it is necessary to start from the different position that, in relation to the processing of personal data, corresponds to the data controller or processor and the data protection officer, based on the fact that these are autonomous concepts of European law that must be subject to uniform interpretation throughout the EU, as derived from Recital 10 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation - GDPR).  

To ensure a uniform and high level of protection for natural persons and to eliminate obstacles to the free movement of personal data within the Union, the level of protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons with regard to the processing of such data must be equivalent in all Member States. It must be ensured throughout the Union that the application of the rules for the protection of the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons in relation to the processing of personal data is consistent and homogeneous.

At present, the current regulations regarding the protection of personal data are contained in the aforementioned Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (hereinafter GDPR) and in Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD), so the provisions contained in these regulations regarding the categories of participants in the processing of personal data must be taken into account.  

As a starting point, we must refer to what is indicated in Recital 79 of the GDPR, which states that  
(…) The protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, as well as the responsibility of controllers and processors, also with respect to the supervision by the supervisory authorities and the measures adopted by them, requires a clear attribution of responsibilities under this Regulation, including cases where a controller determines the purposes and means of processing jointly with other controllers, or where processing is carried out on behalf of a controller. (…)  

The GDPR recognizes the need to clearly establish the map of participants in any data processing, in order to accurately determine the attribution of responsibilities in accordance with the aforementioned regulation.  

This regulation aims to ensure that there are no situations of action outside its scope of application, in order to provide supervisory authorities with the necessary elements to carry out their function and ultimately to provide European citizens with the protection that their personal data deserves. Therefore, any activity involving the processing of personal data will be attributable to some entity that meets the requirements of the different categories provided by the GDPR.  

In accordance with what has been indicated, the GDPR generally establishes (and without prejudice to the figures of the recipient and third party) three scenarios of participants in the processing of personal data: the data controller, the joint controllers, and the data processor.

As the Article 29 Working Party already pointed out in its Opinion 1/2010 on the concepts of "data controller" and "data processor," the concept of controller was a functional concept aimed at assigning responsibilities, indicating that “The concept of ‘data controller’ and its interaction with the concept of ‘data processor’ play a fundamental role in the application of Directive 95/46/EC, as they determine who should be responsible for compliance with data protection rules and how data subjects can exercise their rights in practice. The concept of data controller is also essential for determining the applicable national legislation and for the effective exercise of the supervisory tasks conferred on data protection authorities.” 

Furthermore, the aforementioned Opinion highlighted “the difficulties in implementing the definitions of the Directive in a complex environment where many hypothetical situations may arise involving the actions of controllers and processors, either alone or jointly, and with varying degrees of autonomy and responsibility” and that “The Group recognizes that the concrete application of the concepts of data controller and data processor is becoming increasingly complex. This is primarily due to the growing complexity of the environment in which these concepts are used and, in particular, to an increasing trend, both in the private and public sectors, towards organizational differentiation, combined with the development of ICT and globalization, which may give rise to new and difficult questions and, at times, may diminish the level of protection for data subjects.”

However, at the present time, it must be taken into account that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to personal data protection, which is now based on the principle of "accountability" or "proactive responsibility" or "demonstrable responsibility" as repeatedly pointed out by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and is included in the Preamble of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD): “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or the processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment,” taking into account the nature, scope or extent, context, and purposes of the processing in such a way that allows the controller to demonstrate, at all times, that the processing is in compliance with the GDPR. With respect to the advice provided by the DPO to the controller in impact assessments, such advice cannot be interpreted as a way for the DPO to receive instructions from the controller or to participate in decision-making regarding processing. - The DPO advises the controller, both in impact assessments and in any aspect of the processing activities carried out.

However, the assignment of other tasks must respect, in any case, the advisory and supervisory nature of the DPO, without implying direct intervention in decision-making regarding the purposes and means of processing, which would affect their independence and imply the existence of a conflict of interest. Thus, the necessary independence of the DPO and the need to avoid conflicts of interest prevent the assignment of direct responsibilities in an area that they will have to supervise and in which they would be subject to instructions from other bodies. 
Specifically, in relation to the possible conflict of interest, the guidelines on data protection officers adopted by the Article 29 Working Party, last reviewed and adopted on April 5, 2017, state the following: 
3.5. Conflict of Interest 
Article 38, paragraph 6, allows DPOs to "perform other functions and tasks." However, it requires that the organization ensure that "such functions and tasks do not give rise to a conflict of interest." 
The absence of a conflict of interest is closely linked to the requirement to act independently. Although DPOs may have other functions, they may only be entrusted with other tasks and responsibilities if these do not give rise to conflicts of interest. This means, in particular, that the DPO cannot hold a position in the organization that leads them to determine the purposes and means of personal data processing. Due to the specific organizational structure of each organization, this must be considered on a case-by-case basis. 
As a general rule, conflicting positions within an organization may include senior management positions (such as CEO, COO, CFO, Chief Medical Officer, Head of Marketing, Head of Human Resources, or Head of IT) but also other lower positions in the organizational structure if such positions lead to the determination of the purposes and means of processing. Additionally, a conflict of interest may also arise, for example, if a DPO is asked to represent the data controller or processor before the courts in cases related to data protection.

To conclude, and without prejudice to the specific infringements related to the DPO previously mentioned, it must be emphasized that, in practice, the data protection officer primarily assumes advisory and supervisory functions for the benefit of the controller or processor. III
Next, it is necessary to analyze a series of questions raised regarding the position and independence of the DPO, taking into account its framing within the organization of the controller, for which it is necessary to start from the provisions contained in Article 38 of the GDPR, particularly from what is stated in its paragraph 3:
3. The controller and the processor shall ensure that the data protection officer does not receive any instructions regarding the performance of those functions. He or she shall not be dismissed or sanctioned by the controller or the processor for performing his or her functions. The data protection officer shall report directly to the highest hierarchical level of the controller or processor.

2. In the legal norms regulating the figure of the data protection officer, the requirement of their independence is configured as inherent to the performance of their functions.

Consequently, the DPO must provide the data controllers and processors with all the information derived from the exercise of their functions that they require to fulfill their obligations, bearing in mind that the DPO's advice is not binding, although the data controller or processor must document the reasons for not following the DPO's advice, which inevitably requires knowledge of the actions taken by the DPO.
