The inquiry raises the question of whether it is compliant with data protection regulations for the consulting University to communicate, upon request, to parents data related to grades, enrollments, and scholarships concerning their children when the student is financially dependent on their parents or in other cases where the disappearance of the adult child has been reported or the child has a diagnosed health problem.

I

As a starting point, it should be noted that since May 25 of this year, the applicable regulation is constituted by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), a norm that displaces the application of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, so the analysis of the issues raised in the inquiry will be made from the perspective of such regulation, although it should be noted that regarding the present case, the conclusions of this report will not differ from those that would have been reached with the application of said Law.

II

The aforementioned Regulation extends its protection, as established in its Article 1.2, to the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, their right to the protection of personal data, defined in its Article 4.1 as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”

Thus, data relating to enrollments, grades, or scholarships of each student constitutes personal data whose processing is subject to the provisions of the aforementioned Regulation (EU) 2016/679.

The communication of personal data relating to the students of the consulting University to their parents constitutes data processing, understood as such, in accordance with Article 4.2 of the aforementioned Regulation, “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”

It should thus be taken into account that all processing of personal data must be based on one of the legitimizing grounds provided for in Article 6 of the aforementioned Regulation, according to which:

“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies:

a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes;

b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;

c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;

d) processing is necessary in order to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;

e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;

f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.

The provision in point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”

Various legitimizing grounds for processing are thus established, so that the consent of the student does not operate as the only possible one. In this case, the one established in letter f) of said article is of particular interest, formulated in terms very similar to those included in article 7.f of Directive 95/46/EC. Therefore, in examining the application of this provision, it is worth recalling what the Court of Justice of the European Union has stated in its rulings when interpreting the aforementioned article of Directive 95/46/EC.

In this regard, the Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union of November 24, 2011, which resolves the preliminary questions raised by the Supreme Court in the appeals filed against the Regulation implementing Organic Law 15/1999, and in which the direct effect of article 7 f) of Directive 95/46/EC was expressly declared, highlighted in paragraph 38 that article 7 f) of the Directive “establishes two cumulative requirements for the processing of personal data to be lawful, namely, on the one hand, that such processing of personal data is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data are communicated, and, on the other hand, that the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail.” In relation to this balancing, paragraph 40 pointed out that it “will depend, in principle, on the specific circumstances of the particular case in question and within which the person or institution carrying out the balancing must take into account the importance of the rights conferred on the data subject by articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union.”

In the same vein, more recently, the Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union of May 4, 2017, in case C-13/16, (Valsts policijas Rīgas reģiona pārvaldes Kārtības policijas pārvalde and Rīgas pašvaldības SIA «Rīgas satiksme»), recalled that article 7, letter f), of Directive 95/46 sets three cumulative requirements for the processing of personal data to be lawful: first, that the data controller or the third party or parties to whom the data are communicated pursue a legitimate interest; second, that the processing is necessary for the satisfaction of that legitimate interest; and third, that the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject in the protection of data do not prevail.

And lastly, it was noted that it was possible for such a presumption of prevalence to be broken by the circumstances of the specific case, recalling that Article 6.4 of Organic Law 15/1999 regulated the possibility of opposing treatment when it was not legitimized by the consent of the affected party. Thus, when it was claimed that the elements generating the presumption of a prevailing legitimate interest justifying such access were not present in the parent seeking access to the grades, it would be possible, by virtue of the right of opposition established in the aforementioned Article 6.4 of the Organic Law, to deny access. This would occur, for example, in cases where the minor bore their own educational expenses.

Such conclusions continue to apply following the commencement of the application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, in such a way that the consulting entity must, in each specific case presented to it, determine whether there exists a legitimate interest in the parents to access the data of their adult children held by the University, which must prevail as provided by said regulation “over the interests or the rights and fundamental freedoms of the data subject that require the protection of personal data.” This balancing requires determining, as stated by the Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union of May 4, 2017, previously cited, first, whether the person requesting the data pursues a legitimate interest, for which it will be necessary to know the purpose pursued with access to the data and that such legitimate interest is somehow substantiated; secondly, it must be examined whether knowledge of such data is necessary for the satisfaction of that legitimate interest; and, thirdly, whether there is a circumstance in the data subject or affected by the data processing that determines that, even though the person requesting the data may have a legitimate interest, this does not prevail over the interests or the rights and fundamental freedoms of the data subject. For this, the data subject must be aware that the data has been requested so that they can oppose such communication in accordance with the provisions of Article 21 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, according to which “The data subject shall have the right to object at any time, on grounds relating to his or her particular situation, to the processing of personal data concerning him or her based on the provisions of Article 6(1)(e) or (f), including profiling based on those provisions. The controller shall cease processing the personal data unless he or she demonstrates compelling legitimate grounds for the processing which override the interests, rights, and freedoms of the data subject, or for the establishment, exercise, or defense of legal claims.”

The enrollment and scholarships of adult students, if the student is economically dependent on them, can generally be presumed to have a legitimate interest in such access, provided that the exercise of the right of opposition by the interested party does not result in anything else. For these purposes, what is decisive is the economic dependency or the fact that a maintenance allowance is paid to them, so that if this circumstance does not exist for the person seeking access to the data, the presumption of legitimate interest disappears. The criterion of economic dependency must be examined on a case-by-case basis, although in principle it refers to the payment of a maintenance allowance or the financing of the living expenses of the interested party by both parents or by one of them; in such cases, the legitimate interest will only be held by the parent from whom the child is economically dependent.

When the purpose of the request for data is the modification of the maintenance allowance, as indicated in the report transcribed above, the elements that allow the communication of the data to be in accordance with the provisions of Article 6.1.f of the Regulation seem to be present, since there is a legitimate interest in the parent based on the right recognized by Article 152 of the Civil Code, and the knowledge of such data is necessary to satisfy that legitimate interest, given that such information must be provided as evidence in a judicial proceeding and such right seems to prevail over the interests and rights and freedoms of the interested party, without prejudice to the fact that they may exercise their right of opposition to such processing, and it must be examined whether the alleged circumstances undermine such presumption of the existence of a legitimate interest.

Regarding whether the circumstances related to the report of a child's disappearance or that the child has a diagnosed health problem can constitute a legitimate interest, given the diversity of situations that may arise, a general rule cannot be established. For example, it can be presumed that there is a legitimate interest in knowing the data of a person when there are indications that such disappearance is not voluntary, whether the missing person is dependent on their parents or not, and the data requested assists in the investigation of the circumstances of such disappearance. However, there will not be such a legitimate interest, in principle and without seeking a different legitimate purpose, in knowing the data held by the University of an adult who is economically independent and has voluntarily decided not to maintain relationships with their family.

Consequently, it must be examined, on a case-by-case basis, what legitimate interest is held by the person requesting the data, whether the knowledge of the data held by the University is necessary for the purpose pursued by them, and finally, whether the legitimate interest held must prevail over the rights and freedoms of the interested party, who must be informed so that they can exercise their right of opposition to the processing.
