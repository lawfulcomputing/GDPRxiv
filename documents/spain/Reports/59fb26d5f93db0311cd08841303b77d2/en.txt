N/REF: 0032/2023  

The inquiry submitted, presented by the Secretariat of the Town Hall of Villaviciosa de Odón (Madrid), raises certain questions related to the process of establishing a grouping of voters, some of which pertain to the application of regulations on the protection of personal data, which is the issue on which this report focuses. Furthermore, this report is issued with urgency, as requested by the Central Electoral Board, given the call for local and regional elections on April 3, 2023.  

I  
Organic Law 5/1985, of June 19, on the General Electoral Regime, recognizes in its article 44.1.c) the right to present candidates or lists of candidates to “The groupings of voters that meet the requirements established by the special provisions of this Law,” requiring in article 46.8 that “The candidacies presented by groupings of voters must be accompanied by the documents certifying the legally required number of signatures for their participation in the elections. No voter may give their signature for the presentation of multiple candidacies.”  

In the case of municipal elections to which the inquiry pertains, it is specifically regulated in article 187.3: “To present a candidacy, groupings of voters need a number of signatures from those registered in the electoral roll of the municipality, which must be notarized or authenticated by the Secretary of the corresponding municipal Corporation, determined according to the following scale:  
a) In municipalities with fewer than 5,000 inhabitants, no less than 1 per 100 of the registered voters, provided that the number of signatories is more than double that of the Councilors to be elected.  
b) In those with between 5,001 and 10,000 inhabitants, at least 100 signatures.  
c) In those with between 10,001 and 50,000 inhabitants, at least 500 signatures.  
d) In those with between 50,001 and 150,000 inhabitants, at least 1,500 signatures.  
e) In those with between 150,001 and 300,000 inhabitants, at least 3,000 signatures.  
f) In those with between 300,001 and 1,000,000 inhabitants, at least 5,000 signatures.  
g) In other cases, at least 8,000 signatures.”  

The official model for collecting signatures is contained in Royal Decree 605/1999, of April 16, on complementary regulation of electoral processes (model C.3), in which the name and surname, sex, ID number, date of birth, and the signature of the individuals supporting the candidacy must be recorded, which, in accordance with the aforementioned article 187.3, must be notarized or authenticated by the Secretary of the corresponding municipal Corporation in the terms authorized by the Central Electoral Board, which has accepted, starting from Instruction 1/2022 on the procedure for accreditation through electronic signature of the endorsements presented by groupings of voters in municipal elections (BOE of November 30), that the submission of endorsements may be carried out through an electronic signature procedure recognized as valid by the town hall before which its secretary is to authenticate the signatures.  

Otherwise, as stated in agreement 10/2023 of the Central Electoral Board, cited in the inquiry, “The Central Electoral Board has repeatedly declared that ‘voters who wish to endorse or present a candidacy through the channel established in article 187.3 LOREG, in relation to articles 44.1 and 46.8 of the aforementioned legal text, may carry out their proposal by stamping their signatures on a document for this purpose in which, next to each of them, the name and surname of the voter and the number, place of issuance, and date of the ID are indicated. In any case, the identity of the signatories must be certified by a notarial act or by the Secretary of the municipal Corporation, or whoever holds such position even if not in property, who certifies the authenticity of the signatures and identities. In light of the provisions of Constitutional Court Judgment 87/1999, of May 25, to comply with the requirement for signature authentication, the municipal notary must carry out ‘first the appropriate comparison of the signatures that endorse the candidacy, which must be contrasted with those appearing in the copies of the corresponding IDs provided, and then certify the inclusion or registration of the signatories in the electoral roll of the municipality,’ thus making the appearance of the endorsers of a candidacy before the public notary unnecessary (Agreement of April 14, 2014).”

It has also been indicated that "the photocopy of the ID card is an instrument that can allow the Secretary of the City Council or the Notary to verify the signatures that support the candidacy. However, if this verification of signatures raises doubts for the public notary, they may require the personal appearance of the endorser to attest to the authenticity of their signature and identity (Agreement of February 11, 2015)."
This interpretation aims to adhere to the anti-formalist criterion established by constitutional jurisprudence in rulings such as STC 81/1987, of May 27, or STC 87/1999, of May 25. The essential point is that the secretary of the corresponding municipal corporation can certify that the submitted signatures indeed correspond to the voters, for which they can use different methods, including the presentation of a photocopy of the national identity document. This means that if they have other means to verify that signature, they may use them to overcome that insufficiency. Without ruling out, on the other hand, that in case of doubts, they may also require the personal appearance of the signatories.
In this same vein, in the most recent agreement 69/2023, the JEC reiterates that “Although it is true that constitutional jurisprudence (SSTC 81/1987 and 87/1999) has emphasized the need to follow a flexible and anti-formalist criterion in verifying the authenticity of the support signatures for candidacies presented by groups of voters, it is up to each city council secretary to apply it considering the means available to them. It is not possible, therefore, as the interested party intends, to reject the possibility of requiring a copy of the national identity document to carry out the signature verification as an alternative to the personal appearance of the interested party before said Secretary.”
Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons concerning the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation) extends its protection, as established in its Article 1.2, to the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, their right to the protection of personal data, defined in its Article 4.1 as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”

And regarding the processing itself, Article 4.2 of the GDPR considers "processing": any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or modification, extraction, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, restriction, erasure or destruction;

Consequently, in the process of collecting signatures by the promoters of an electoral grouping, including, where applicable, the corresponding copy of the ID card, and its subsequent authentication by the Secretary or the corresponding Notary, it involves the processing of personal data of the individuals supporting the candidacy.

Regarding the application of data protection regulations to such processing, it must start from what is provided in Article 2.2.a) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), which states that it does not apply to the processing of personal data "in the exercise of an activity not covered by the scope of Union law," adding Article 2.3 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights, which states that "The processing to which Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is not directly applicable due to affecting activities not covered by the scope of Union law, shall be governed by the provisions of its specific legislation if any, and subsidiarily by the provisions established in the aforementioned regulation and in this organic law. This situation includes, among others, the processing carried out under the organic legislation of the general electoral regime, the processing carried out in the field of penitentiary institutions, and the processing derived from the Civil Registry, the Property Registries, and Commercial Registries."

Therefore, as this Agency has repeatedly pointed out (see Report 12/2023 regarding the draft Royal Decree amending Royal Decree 1799/2003, of December 26, which regulates the content of electoral lists and copies of the electoral roll and those cited therein), the processing of personal data carried out under electoral legislation is governed by its specific regulations and, subsidiarily, by the regulations on the protection of personal data.

II
Starting from the supplementary application of the regulations on the protection of personal data, the first issue that must be determined is the legal position corresponding to the subjects involved in the processing of personal data. 
As this Agency has repeatedly pointed out regarding the attribution of the status of data controller or data processor, there are different scenarios that may arise, depending on the specific circumstances of the case, the legal relationship established between the intervening subjects, and their specific obligations, as well as the obligations that may be imposed by the legal system, which will be decisive in assessing whether one acts as a data controller or as a data processor. 
To this end, it is necessary to start from the definitions established by the GDPR in its Article 4:
7) “data controller” or “controller”: the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the data controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States;
8) “data processor” or “processor”: the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller;
As the Article 29 Working Party already pointed out in its Opinion 1/2010 on the concepts of “data controller” and “data processor,” the concept of controller was a functional concept aimed at the assignment of responsibilities, indicating that “The concept of ‘data controller’ and its interaction with the concept of ‘data processor’ play a fundamental role in the application of Directive 95/46/EC, as they determine who must be responsible for compliance with data protection rules and how data subjects can exercise their rights in practice. The concept of data controller is also essential when determining the applicable national legislation and for the effective exercise of the supervisory tasks conferred on data protection authorities.”
However, at the present time, it must be taken into account that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to the protection of personal data, which is now based on the principle of “accountability” or “proactive responsibility” as has been

repeatedly indicated by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and is
included in the Explanatory Memorandum of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD): “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the data controller or the data processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.” Within this new system, it is the data controller who, through the instruments regulated in the GDPR itself, such as the record of processing activities, risk analysis, or data protection impact assessment, must guarantee the protection of this right by complying with all the principles set out in Article 5.1 of the GDPR, adequately documenting all decisions made to be able to demonstrate it.

Furthermore, based on this principle of proactive responsibility, directed essentially at the data controller, and in order to strengthen the protection of the affected parties, the GDPR has introduced new obligations that are enforceable not only against the controller but, in certain cases, also against the data processor, who may be sanctioned in the event of non-compliance with them.

In the present case, it is necessary to start from the legal regulation of personal data processing established in the LOREG and the competencies attributed by it, a criterion also reflected in the Guidelines 07/2020 of the EDPS, when referring to cases of control arising from legal provisions:
22. In some cases, control may be inferred from an explicit legal competence; e.g., when the designation of the data controller or the specific criteria for their appointment are established in national or EU law. In this sense, Article 4, point 7, establishes that “if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of processing, the data controller or the specific criteria for their appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States.” Although Article 4, point 7, only refers to the “data controller” in the singular, the EDPS also considers it possible for the law of the Union or of the Member States to designate more than one data controller, even as joint controllers of the processing.

It is time to establish who acts as such. It is therefore presumed that the legislator has designated the entity with the true capacity to exercise control as the data controller. In some countries, national law establishes that public authorities are responsible for the processing of personal data within the framework of their obligations. 
24. However, it is more common for legislation, rather than directly naming the data controller or setting the criteria for their appointment, to establish a task or impose on someone the duty to collect and process certain data. In such cases, the purpose of the processing is usually determined by law. The data controller will normally be the one designated by law to fulfill this purpose, this public task. This would be, for example, the case of an entity tasked with certain public duties (for example, social security) that could not be fulfilled without collecting at least some personal data, and that, therefore, creates a database or a registry to carry out such tasks. In this case, although indirectly, the legislation establishes who the data controller is. More frequently, the law may impose on public or private entities the obligation to retain or provide certain data. These entities would be considered, in principle, the data controllers necessary to fulfill this obligation.
Consequently, based on the legally attributed competencies, the promoters of the electoral groupings, referred to expressly in Article 186.5 of the LOREG, will be considered the data controllers, as it states that “The promoters of the groupings designate the representatives of their candidacies at the time of their presentation before the Electoral Boards of the Zone. This designation must be accepted at that moment.” As the JEC has pointed out, “those who promote or encourage the presentation of candidacies by unions of people and appear as such in the document designating the representatives of their candidacies before the corresponding Electoral Boards must be considered promoters” (Agreement 20/2006). Likewise, the Notary or the Secretary of the corresponding municipal Corporation will hold the status of data controller regarding the processing of personal data they carry out to comply with their legal obligation of signature authentication.
As data controllers, they must ensure compliance with the regulations on the protection of personal data, which apply subsidiarily in all matters not specifically regulated by electoral regulations.

III
Once the subsidiary application of the regulations on the protection of personal data and the status of data controllers of the promoter or promoters and the notary or secretary has been determined, it is appropriate to analyze the specific issue raised, related to compliance with the principle of transparency provided for in Article 5.1 a) of the GDPR and the corresponding duty to inform. It is appropriate to cite the provisions of Articles 12 and 13 of the GDPR.
Article 12 of the GDPR determines the following in its paragraph 1: 
1. The data controller shall take appropriate measures to provide the data subject with all information indicated in Articles 13 and 14, as well as any communication in accordance with Articles 15 to 22 and 34 related to the processing, in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language, in particular any information specifically addressed to a child. The information shall be provided in writing or by other means, including, where appropriate, by electronic means. When requested by the data subject, the information may be provided verbally, provided that the identity of the data subject is demonstrated by other means.
Article 12 of the GDPR regulates how the information should be provided, that is, what characteristics or qualities the information offered to the data subjects who will be processed must have.
When the data is obtained directly from the affected party, Article 13 of the GDPR determines in its paragraphs 1 and 2 the following: 
1. When personal data relating to a data subject is obtained from that data subject, the data controller shall provide all the information indicated below at the time the data is obtained:
a) the identity and contact details of the controller and, where applicable, of their representative;
b) the contact details of the data protection officer, where applicable;
c) the purposes of the processing for which the personal data are intended and the legal basis for the processing;
d) when the processing is based on Article 6, paragraph 1, letter f), the legitimate interests of the controller or a third party;
e) the recipients or categories of recipients of the personal data, where applicable;
f) where applicable, the intention of the controller to transfer personal data to a third country or international organization and the existence or

2. The basic information referred to in the previous section must contain, at a minimum:
a) The identity of the data controller and, if applicable, their representative.
b) The purpose of the processing.
c) The possibility of exercising the rights established in Articles 15 to 22 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
If the data obtained from the affected individual is to be processed for profiling purposes, the basic information must also include this circumstance. In this case, the affected individual must be informed of their right to object to the adoption of automated individual decisions that produce legal effects concerning them or significantly affect them in a similar manner, when this right applies in accordance with Article 22 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679. Thus, Article 11 of the LOPDGDD establishes the possibility of implementing a layered information system, differentiating a first layer of basic information and allowing referral to another informational space where the interested party can consult more extensively all matters related to the processing of their personal data.
From the analysis of the specific regulations contained in the LOREG and in Royal Decree 605/1999, of April 16, it is observed that it does not contain specific regulation regarding the information that must be provided to the individuals endorsing the establishment of the electoral group, nor can it be interpreted that the omission of such information in the signature collection model can be understood as a specific exclusion of the duty to inform contained in the regulations on personal data protection, which is a manifestation of one of its fundamental principles, the principle of transparency.
On the other hand, from the perspective of personal data protection regulations, what is important is that this obligation is fulfilled by providing information to the affected individuals, being indifferent, for these purposes, whether it is included in the model itself or provided in an independent document, without prejudice to the obligation of the controller, in accordance with the principle of proactive responsibility, to be able to demonstrate compliance with this obligation.
Therefore, the supplementary application of this regulation is appropriate, corresponding to the promoters of the electoral groups, as data controllers, to articulate the manner in which this obligation will be fulfilled, providing the signatories, at the moment when the personal data that must appear in the model is obtained, with the information required by Article 13 of the GDPR, which can be done in layers, in accordance with Article 11 of the LOPDGDD.

IV
Regarding the Notary or Secretary to whom the personal data will be communicated for the purpose of authenticating the signatures, and who also hold the status of data controllers for that specific purpose, the provisions of Article 14 of the GDPR will apply, which regulates how to fulfill the duty to inform when the data is not obtained directly from the affected individual:
1. When the personal data has not been obtained from the interested party, the data controller will provide the following information:
a) the identity and contact details of the controller and, if applicable, their representative;
b) the contact details of the data protection officer, if applicable;
c) the purposes of the processing for which the personal data are intended, as well as the legal basis for the processing;
d) the categories of personal data concerned;
e) the recipients or categories of recipients of the personal data, if applicable;
f) if applicable, the intention of the controller to transfer personal data to a recipient in a third country or international organization and the existence or absence of an adequacy decision by the Commission, or, in the case of transfers indicated in Articles 46 or 47 or Article 49, paragraph 1, second sentence, reference to the appropriate safeguards and the means to obtain a copy of them or the fact that they have been provided.
2. In addition to the information mentioned in paragraph 1, the data controller will provide the interested party with the following information necessary to ensure fair and transparent data processing regarding the interested party:
a) the period for which the personal data will be retained or, when that is not possible, the criteria used to determine this period;
b) when the processing is based on Article 6, paragraph 1, letter f), the legitimate interests of the data controller or a third party;
c) the existence of the right to request the data controller access to personal data concerning the interested party, and their rectification or deletion, or the limitation of their processing, and to object to the processing, as well as the right to data portability;

As can be seen, the obligation to inform will not be enforceable in those cases where the data subject already has the information. As indicated, such information should have been provided at the same time as the collection of the personal data, and it should include its subsequent communication to the Notary or the Secretary in accordance with Article 187.3 of the LOREG.
Notwithstanding the above, this Agency considers that the exception contained in letter c) of Article 14.5 is applicable to the present case, as such communication is expressly imposed in the LOREG itself, which contains adequate measures to protect the legitimate interests of the data subject, among which stand out, in addition to the minimization of the personal data that must be provided, the limitation of the purpose of the processing, directed exclusively at the authentication of signatures, and the intervention of a public official, subject to a specific duty of confidentiality.
Therefore, in order to ensure the proper compliance with their information obligations by the Secretary or the Notary, it is not necessary to prove that the affected parties already have the information, as the exception contained in Article 14.5.c) of the GDPR is applicable. All of this is without prejudice to the duty to inform that must be fulfilled, in any case, by the promoters of the electoral groupings in accordance with Article 13 of the GDPR.
