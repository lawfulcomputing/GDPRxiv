The inquiry raises a series of questions regarding the use of facial recognition techniques in the conduct of online assessment tests, based on the fact that the state of alarm declared by Royal Decree 463/2020, of March 14, which declares the state of alarm for the management of the health crisis situation caused by COVID-19, has determined the migration of all teaching activities to online environments.  

To this end, the inquiry starts from the premise that a determining element in the provision of public higher education services consists of the objective verification of students' knowledge, highlighting that "due to the conduct of an exam, various types of data are being processed in the physical world. One of the usual treatments during the conduct of an exam consists of verifying the identity of the person being examined, whether at the entrance to the classroom, during the test, or at the end of it when submitting the documentation. For this purpose, the presentation of an identification document is required, such as an ID card, residence card, passport, or university ID."  

As is evident, the physical presence of faculty in the classroom prevents, for example, a student from leaving their seat while being impersonated by another person, or a student from taking the assessment while pretending to be someone else. However, this possibility of control and supervision completely disappears when it comes to conducting an online exam from the student's own home. In this regard, identification must be carried out:  
▪ By assigning access identifiers to virtual classroom environments.  
▪ By remotely viewing the student using videoconferencing tools or webcams.  

Notwithstanding the above, the inquiry emphasizes that "traditional means have been supplemented by the existence of facial recognition tools. These tools, at their most precise level, should be able to ensure the unique identification of the person being examined and even detect facial expressions that identify anomalous behavior. In any case, at their simplest level, they are capable of establishing a facial pattern of the person starting the exam in front of a screen and ensuring that:  
▪ The person has not moved or left their seat in front of the terminal during the period assigned for taking the test.  
▪ They have not been replaced by a different person."

Furthermore, Article 4.1 defines “processing” as “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, collation or interconnection, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”

On the other hand, it is necessary to recall, as has already been indicated on numerous occasions by this Agency, that the reform brought about by the General Data Protection Regulation regarding the regime contained in Organic Law 15/1999 requires a change of perspective concerning the principles that underpin the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and, in particular, the one that made the “principle of consent” the central axis of the right to data protection.
Indeed, while the Organic Law and the Regulation do not differ excessively regarding the enumeration of the legitimizing causes for processing, a highly relevant modification occurs in the way these causes are presented in the applicable texts: thus, while from the wording of Organic Law 15/1999 it seemed to be deduced that the basic rule of legitimization was, in general terms, consent, with the remaining legitimizing causes being exceptional in relation to consent, which as a general rule should govern processing, in the text of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, consent is included as one of the six legitimizing causes for processing without holding greater or lesser importance than the others enumerated in the regulation.

Regarding proportionality, as the Instruction itself indicates, the Judgment of the Court 207/1996 determines that it is “a common and constant requirement for the constitutionality of any restrictive measure of fundamental rights, including those that entail an interference with the rights to physical integrity and privacy, and more particularly, the restrictive measures of fundamental rights adopted during a criminal process are determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality.
In this sense, we have highlighted that, to verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain if it meets the following three requirements or conditions: “whether such a measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, in addition, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and, finally, whether it is weighed or balanced, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test).”
Therefore, any control measure adopted must pass this proportionality test, determining whether the measure is appropriate, necessary, and balanced, as otherwise it is disproportionate and thus contrary to data protection regulations.

To clarify the interpretative doubts that arise regarding the consideration of biometric data as special categories of data, one can refer to the distinction between biometric identification and biometric verification/authentication established by the Article 29 Working Party in its Opinion 3/2012 on the evolution of biometric technologies:

Biometric identification: the identification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparing their biometric data (acquired at the time of identification) with a series of biometric templates stored in a database (i.e., a one-to-many matching process).

Biometric verification/authentication: the verification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparison between their biometric data (acquired at the time of verification) with a single biometric template stored on a device (i.e., a one-to-one matching process).

However, regarding special categories of data, the provision contemplated in paragraph (g) of Article 9.2 does not refer solely to the existence of a public interest, as many other provisions of the GDPR do, but is the only provision of the GDPR that requires that such interest be "essential," an adjective that qualifies said public interest, given the importance and necessity of greater protection of the processed data.

This provision finds its precedent in Article 8.4 of Directive 95/46/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of October 24, 1995, regarding the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data: “4. VII
To conclude, it is recalled that, as indicated at the beginning of this report, it focuses on the legal analysis of the issues raised in the inquiry regarding the use of facial recognition techniques in the conduct of online assessment tests. 

Consequently, although references have been made to them, the rest of the measures that universities may adopt for conducting online assessments are not the subject of study, considering that the inquirer itself, in its "Report on Non-Presential Assessment Procedures. Study of the Impact of Its Implementation in Spanish Universities and Recommendations" dated April 16, 2020 (http://www.crue.org/Documentos%20compartidos/Informes%20y%20Posicionamientos/Informe%20procedimientos%20evaluacion%20no%20presencial.pdf), identifies 11 different non-presential assessment procedures, in which different measures can be adopted to ensure the identity of students and the authorship of activities. 

Nevertheless, all measures that may be adopted must also respect the provisions established in the personal data protection regulations. 

On the other hand, it should be noted that in practice, different measures and systems can be applied together, which may result in an even greater intrusion into the right to personal data protection of students. In these cases, all measures that can be implemented must be evaluated together for their compliance with the GDPR, taking into account the different principles of data protection and, in particular, the principle of data minimization, so that only the data strictly necessary for the intended purpose is processed. This means that a measure that, when considered in isolation, may be compliant with the GDPR, in combination with others may lead to excessive data processing. Likewise, it should be noted that there are measures that, by themselves, would imply excessive processing for the purpose intended by the university, such as the biometric analysis of keystrokes, especially when combined with others such as facial recognition. 

Finally, it should be indicated that, as the Article 29 Working Party recalls in its Opinion 2/2013 on the applications of smart devices, in the event that the technology used involves the storage of information or the obtaining of access to information already stored on the students' device by the university or the data processor, Article 5.3 of Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of July 12, 2002, on the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on privacy and electronic communications), transposed into our legal system by Article 22.2 of Law 34/2002, of July 11, on information society services and electronic commerce, as amended by the final provision of Law 9/2014, of May 9, General Telecommunications Law, would apply, thus requiring such access, regardless of the legal basis that legitimizes the processing under the GDPR, the consent of the student:
