After examining your request for a report, submitted to this Legal Office, regarding the Draft Law on Democratic Memory, requested from this Spanish Agency for Data Protection (AEPD) in accordance with the provisions of Articles 57.1.c) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) and 5 b) of the Statute of the Agency, approved by Royal Decree 428/1993, of March 26, I am obliged to inform you of the following:  

The Draft Law on Democratic Memory is submitted for report. This draft has been presented to this AEPD for a report along with its regulatory impact analysis memorandum (MAIN). In this MAIN, in section 5.3, it is specified that the request for a report to this Spanish Agency for Data Protection aims to "ensure the coherence [of the legal text] with the personal data protection regulations." For this purpose, the following report is issued on the draft law regarding those articles or provisions that affect this matter.  

I  
First, it is necessary to refer to what is established in Article 9 of the draft, which proposes the creation of a National Census of Victims of the Civil War and the Dictatorship, regardless of their nationality, composed of data provided by various public administrations, victims, memorial organizations, university research groups, and any other national and international sources that have relevant information for it. The draft handles a concept of victim (art. 3) that includes both deceased or missing persons as well as persons who may still be alive. Therefore, the draft should clearly differentiate between the personal data that may correspond to deceased persons and to those still alive. Regarding the former, the GDPR does not apply (Recital 27 GDPR and art. 2.b) of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights, LOPDGDD) - although the GDPR does apply to the processing of personal data carried out for historical research purposes, which includes historical research and research for genealogical purposes - while taking into account - as already mentioned - that the GDPR does not apply to deceased persons (Recital 160 GDPR). Having mentioned the above, the function of the National Census, as established in the draft law, will be to collect the list of individuals who suffered any form of violence, coercion, or repression, including those who died in combat, noting the circumstances regarding the repression suffered for each of these cases (i.e., regarding individuals who are already deceased or still alive). The causes of such repression or violence may include, among other possible reasons—and in fact will be in most cases, as indicated in the explanatory memorandum of the draft law—ideological, religious, ethnic or racial reasons, or those related to the life or sexual orientation of such individuals. All these circumstances fall within the special categories of personal data, regulated in Article 9.1 of the GDPR.

The draft law is aware, in section 4 of Article 9, that such living victims, or the relatives of deceased or missing persons, may wish for such circumstances to be “confidential” (sic).

Thus, from this Article 9 of the draft law, the following issues arise. First (i) the processing of personal data of victims who may still be alive, regarding which the circumstances of the repression suffered will be noted in this Census. Second (ii) that such circumstances may constitute special categories of personal data, and finally (iii) the limitation that results from section 4 of Article 9 of the draft law on the fundamental right to data protection.

Regarding the first of these, the creation of the projected Census constitutes a processing of personal data according to the definition contained in Article 4.2 of the GDPR. Thus, “processing” will be any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or modification, extraction, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, restriction, deletion or destruction. The Census therefore involves data processing. Any data processing needs to be supported by a legal basis in accordance with Article 6 of the GDPR. The draft bill does not establish in Article 9 what the legal basis would be that would allow the processing of personal data necessary for the creation of the Census; however, as is evident from the content of the draft bill and its Explanatory Memorandum, the legislator of the draft bill considers that there is a public interest in said Census, which means that Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR could be applicable, understanding the legal basis for the processing of personal data related to the creation of the Census as being necessary for the fulfillment of a mission of public interest imposed by the law itself. However, the law does not explicitly state the purpose of the personal data processing that will be carried out, which is also necessary to understand the legality of the personal data processing (Article 5.1(b) and 6.4 of the GDPR), an absence that connects with the second of the issues mentioned above, which is the existence of special categories of personal data, also known as sensitive data. 

Consequently, with regard to data protection regulations, the draft bill is excessively generic. It is not enough to include a generic reference to compliance with data protection regulations, especially in a case of such significance and implications for the rights of the affected individuals, given the inclusion of special categories of data. A rigorous risk analysis must be conducted, and appropriate guarantees must be established in the Law. This is a requirement of both the GDPR and our constitutional doctrine (Judgments 292/2000 and 76/2019), as we will proceed to explain.

Well, the judgment of the Constitutional Court (STC) 76/2019, of May 22, contains the relevant doctrine regarding the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and addresses both the characteristics and the content that the regulations intending to establish an interference in that fundamental right must have.

(...) By express mandate of the Constitution, any state interference in the realm of fundamental rights and public freedoms, whether it directly affects their development (Article 81.1 CE) or limits or conditions their exercise (Article 53.1 CE), requires a legal authorization (see, among others, STC 49/1999, of April 5, FJ 4). (…) This dual function of the legal reservation translates into a double requirement: on the one hand, the necessary intervention of the law to authorize the interference; and, on the other hand, that legal norm "must meet all those indispensable characteristics as a guarantee of legal certainty," that is, "it must express each and every one of the assumptions and conditions of the intervention" (STC 49/1999, FJ 4). In other words, "it not only excludes delegations in favor of regulatory norms [...], but also implies other requirements regarding the content of the Law that establishes such limits" (STC 292/2000, FJ 15).

And this is because, in the realm of special categories of personal data, (…) the General Data Protection Regulation establishes the minimum, common, or general guarantees for the processing of personal data that are not special. In contrast, it does not itself establish the legal regime applicable to the processing of special personal data, neither in the context of the Member States nor for Union Law. Therefore, it also does not set the guarantees that must be observed for the various possible processing of sensitive data, appropriate to the risks of varying probability and severity that exist in each case; processing and special categories of data that are, or may be, very diverse from one another. The regulation merely contemplates the possibility that the legislator of the European Union or that of the Member States, each within their area of competence, foresee and regulate such processing, and indicates the guidelines that must be observed in their regulation. One of those guidelines is that the law of the Member State establishes "appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject" [Article 9.2.g) GDPR] and that "adequate safeguards are provided" (Recital 56 GDPR). It is evident that the establishment of appropriate and specific measures can only be explicit. If the internal regulation governing the processing of personal data related to political opinions does not provide for those adequate safeguards, but rather, at most, implicitly refers to the general safeguards contained in the General Data Protection Regulation, it cannot be considered that it has fulfilled the normative task that the regulation requires. Consequently, and as required by the Constitutional Court, the law that establishes certain interferences in the fundamental right to the protection of personal data of the data subjects, such as, in the present case in Article 9 of the draft bill, the possibility of processing personal data of the data subjects from particularly sensitive categories, such as their sexual orientation, their ideology, or others regulated in Article 9.1 GDPR, requires that it, first of all:
a) specify the essential public interest that underpins the restriction of the fundamental right (FJ 7 of the STC 76/2019). Article 9 of the draft bill does not expressly establish the essential public interest that underpins such interference. Just as in the ruling of the Constitutional Court 76/2019 cited, the norm seems to presume that such essential public interest required by Article 9.2.g) GDPR must exist but does not specify it. Therefore, the law must explicitly state what the essential public interest is that underpins the interference with the fundamental right through the processing of personal data proposed in Article 9 of the draft bill, and this, the TC, citing STC 292/2000, rejects that such identification of the legitimate purposes of the restriction can be made through generic concepts or vague formulas.

II
Regarding Article 24, relating to the National DNA Database of Civil War Victims, in its paragraph 5, the same reflections of the Constitutional Court previously expressed regarding the call for regulation for the “requirements and procedures to ensure compliance with data protection regulations” can be applied. It is not permissible to defer to regulation matters that must be determined in the law governing the processing of personal data. Compliance with data protection regulations either does not require any development, because the GDPR is, as such a European regulation, “binding in all its elements and directly applicable in each Member State,” or, regarding special categories of personal data, which undoubtedly includes the DNA contained in the biological samples to be provided by relatives, it is required that the law itself establishing the processing of personal data, as it constitutes an interference with the fundamental right to the protection of personal data recognized in Article 18.4 of the Spanish Constitution, must establish the purpose of the processing, a detailed regulation of the prerequisites and conditions for the processing of personal data, and finally the adequate guarantees.

In the opinion of this Agency, Article 24 adequately regulates the purpose of the processing as constitutive of a public interest that consists of the receipt and storage of DNA profiles “in order to compare such DNA profiles with a view to the genetic identification of the victims.” It is also considered appropriate that the provision of biological samples by relatives for obtaining DNA profiles is in any case voluntary, but the adequate guarantees required by the Constitutional Court as necessary are not established, and these guarantees must be regulated in the law itself.

Thus, Article 40.1 generally establishes a prohibition on granting subsidies. without any specification, - and without mention of a duration period in said prohibition – to those individuals who have been sanctioned by a firm administrative resolution for attacking, encouraging, or tolerating practices against democratic memory, in accordance with the provisions established in Title IV of the draft bill. However, Article 63.5 establishes as a specific sanction in this matter “the loss for a period of up to five years of the possibility of obtaining subsidies and public aid from public administrations in the area of democratic memory, (…) during the five previous years, in that same area.” While the first of these articles seems to establish a general prohibition on any type of subsidies, unlimited in time, the second establishes a period during which subsidies cannot be obtained exclusively in the area of democratic memory. Both regulations appear to be at least contradictory.

Well, Article 40.3 of the draft bill, for the purposes of complying with the provisions established in Article 40.1, a general prohibition on granting subsidies by any public administrations to those sanctioned under this draft law, establishes the obligation for the competent department in the area of democratic memory to establish a database that allows for computer cross-referencing for the monitoring and verification of those individuals who have been sanctioned by a firm administrative resolution in application of this law. That is to say, a database of administrative sanctions is established whose purpose is to allow other administrations access to said administrative sanctions to verify these circumstances for the purposes of denying subsidies in other areas.

Without prejudice to its incomplete connection with the general regulations on subsidies (Article 13.2.h) of Law 38/2003, of November 17, General Subsidies Law -LGS- and Article 26 of the LGS Regulation, approved by Royal Decree 887/2006, of July 21), and how that prohibition would play out, which is not the subject of this report, it is indeed relevant that Article 27 of the LOPDGDD establishes special provisions for the processing of data related to infringements and administrative sanctions. Paragraph 1 of said Article 27 establishes that the responsible parties for such processing must be the competent bodies in the sanctioning procedure, which is complied with; and that the processing will be limited to the strictly necessary data for the purpose pursued by it, which must necessarily be the establishment of a sanction for non-compliance with the law. This last aspect must also be complied with, as the personal data necessary in the processing of personal data whose purpose is the establishment of a sanction must comply with the principle of minimization in all cases. However, paragraph 2 of said Article 27 LOPDGDD establishes that when any of the conditions provided in the previous paragraph are not met, the processing of data related to infringements and administrative sanctions must have the consent of the interested party or be authorized by a norm with the rank of law, which will regulate, if applicable, additional guarantees for the rights and freedoms of the affected parties. These additional guarantees required by the provision must take into special account that within the types of infringement contained in the draft law there are some that, in the opinion of this Agency, could affect the ideology of individuals - a data particularly protected by art. 9.1 GDPR and art. 16 CE - and could also affect other fundamental rights such as the right to demonstration or expression, since, for example, art. 62.1.e) of the draft specifically regulates as an administrative infringement "the calling of events, awareness campaigns or publicity that by any means of public communication, written or verbal information, in their sound elements as their images are contrary to the regulations on democratic memory (sic) (...)". Such a broad wording could even encompass the exercise of the right to demonstration or expression that aimed at modifying the very law of democratic memory, as it could include as an infringement the calling of an event (for example, the calling of a demonstration), a publicity campaign (in the exercise of freedom of expression), or the issuance of an opinion "contrary to the [necessity or existence or content] of the regulations on democratic memory," which would entail other considerations regarding the constitutionality of such a measure. 

Focusing exclusively on the area of personal data protection, these additional guarantees - which article 27 LOPDGDD requires to be included in the very law that establishes the processing - must take into account the danger and risks that the processing of personal data established by this law in article 40 poses for the fundamental right to the protection of particularly protected data, such as the right to the ideology of individuals, without prejudice to the fact that, in case there are limitations to the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, the circumstances mentioned in art. 23.2 GDPR must be included again in the draft bill, at a minimum.
