The purpose of this consultation is to request advice from the AEPD regarding the application of the regulations on the protection of personal data within the framework of the actions of the Directorate of Competition (DC) of the National Commission on Markets and Competition (CNMC), focusing on the two major issues that have a direct impact on the actions of the DC as provided in the GDPR, relating both to the determination of the legal basis that legitimizes the processing of personal data and to the exercise of the rights of the data subjects.  

As indicated therein, these issues are of interest both from the perspective of the data being processed by the DC in its actions (inspections, processing of files) and also from the perspective of the companies or institutions that must carry out processing as a result of the actions of the DC (response to information requests, being subject to an inspection, filing complaints or requests) and which are developed within the framework of national and community legislation on competition that would constitute, in the opinion of the consultant, a special regime for the purposes of what is provided in Article 12.5 of Organic Law 3/2018 and in Article 23 of the GDPR.  

I  
Regarding the matter of personal data protection, the applicable regulation is Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR), fully applicable since May 25, 2018, and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights.  

The aforementioned Regulation extends its protection, as established in its Article 1.2, to the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons and, in particular, to their right to the protection of personal data, defined in its Article 4.1 as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  

Consequently, legal entities are excluded from its protection, but its protective scope extends to the natural persons who represent them, whose personal data must be processed in accordance with the provisions of that Regulation.  

The protection conferred by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 also includes sole traders, unlike the previous regulation, where Article 2.3 of the implementing regulation of Organic Law 15/1999, approved by Royal Decree 1720/2017, of December 21, excluded from its scope of application the data of sole traders when the processing of data concerning them was carried out in their capacity as merchants, industrialists, or shipowners. Regulation (EU) 2016/679 does not establish any exclusion in this regard, so the processing of personal data relating to sole traders must comply with the provisions contained in this regulation.  

II  
On the other hand, it is necessary to recall, as has been indicated on numerous occasions by this Agency, that the reform brought about by the General Data Protection Regulation regarding the regime contained in Organic Law 15/1999 requires a change of perspective concerning the principles that underpin the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, and, in particular, that which made the “principle of consent” the central axis of the right to data protection.  

Indeed, while the Organic Law and the Regulation do not differ excessively regarding the enumeration of the legitimizing grounds for processing, there is a highly relevant modification in the way these grounds are presented in the applicable texts: thus, while from the wording of Organic Law 15/1999 it seemed to be inferred that the basic rule of legitimization was, in general, consent, with the remaining legitimizing grounds being exceptional in relation to consent, which as a general rule should govern processing, in the text of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, consent is included as one of the six grounds for legitimization for processing, without holding greater or lesser importance than the others enumerated in the regulation.  

Furthermore, the General Data Protection Regulation itself makes it clear that the consent of the data subject should not constitute...

the legal basis for processing in certain cases. Thus, recital 42 states in its last sentence that “Consent should not be considered freely given when the data subject does not have genuine or free choice or cannot refuse or withdraw consent without suffering any detriment,” and recital 43 adds that “To ensure that consent has been given freely, it must not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, particularly when the controller is a public authority and it is therefore unlikely that consent has been given freely in all circumstances of that particular situation.”

In this way, it is not appropriate to obtain the consent of the affected party in cases where the processing is covered by any of the grounds included in letters b) to f) of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation; that is to say when:
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the implementation at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures; 
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject; 
d) the processing is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person; 
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller; 
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that such interests do not override the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require the protection of personal data, particularly when the data subject is a child.

In relation to these legal bases and regarding the processing of data by Public Administrations, it is a reiterated criterion of this Agency that the basis for such processing must be found in letters c) and e) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR. In this sense, in report 175/2018 it was already stated as follows:
“As a CONCLUSION on this point, it can be said that, in general, the legal basis for processing in relations with the Administration, in those cases where there is a relationship in which it cannot reasonably be said that there is a situation of balance between the data controller (the Administration) and the data subject (the administered) would not be consent (art. 6.1.a GDPR), but, depending on the cases, compliance with a legal obligation (art. 6.1.c GDPR) or the performance of a task of public interest or in the exercise of public powers (art. 6.1.e GDPR).

On the other hand, regarding the processing of personal data carried out under letters c) and e) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, the following clarifications are necessary:
First, as provided in paragraphs 2 and 3 of Article 6 of the GDPR, the legal norms that enable such processing may establish specific provisions in relation to them:

Secondly, and as indicated in the aforementioned report 175/2018: “Regarding the meaning of the expression ‘legal obligation’ contained in Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR, this expression is equivalent, in the Spanish data protection regulation, to ‘obligation established in a norm with the rank of law.’ Article 53.1 of the Constitution (CE) establishes that [t]he rights and freedoms recognized in Chapter Two of this Title bind all public authorities. Furthermore, regarding the basis of letter e) of Article 6 of the GDPR, the aforementioned report stated that “the Administration is bound by the principle of legality, so that, unlike individuals, it can only carry out what the legal system expressly permits. This is the meaning of the provisions in Articles 9.1 and 103 of the Constitution, so that when the law and the right have not attributed to the Administration the corresponding powers to act in a certain situation, that action cannot be carried out unless the legal system previously attributes such powers to it. Therefore, there is no empty space where, in the absence of law, the Administration can act. This is what has been termed the doctrine of the positive binding of the Administration to legality (García de Enterría). Consequently, for the Administration to act, it needs prior legal authorization (understood here as legal validity). This applies whether the Administration acts in the field of public law or in the field of private law.” Therefore, in this case, it will also be necessary for such legal authorization to be contained in a norm with the rank of law, as required by the aforementioned Article 53 of the Constitution.

Notwithstanding, in the opinion of this Agency, both concepts must be distinguished, as failing to do so would imply confusing, in almost all cases of administrative action, both legal bases. 

To this end, one must consider one of the essential characteristics of administrative powers, which is the obligation to exercise them, and the necessary differentiation of that generic duty from the specific obligations that the legal system may attribute to the Administration. This must start from the concept of obligation contained in Article 1088 of the Civil Code (“every obligation consists of giving, doing, or not doing something”), with the law being the primary source of obligations, as emphasized by Article 1089, adding that Article 1090 states, “Obligations derived from the law are not presumed. Only those expressly determined in this Code or in special laws are enforceable, and they shall be governed by the provisions of the law that established them; and, in what the law has not provided, by the provisions of this book.” Such legal obligations, in the field of administrative law, usually fall on the subjects of the law, although there may also be cases where the law assigns specific obligations to Public Administrations.

In this regard, it is appropriate to bring to attention the differences established by García de Enterría between the concepts of power, subjective right, duty, and obligation. Power, a species of the common genus of legal powers to which subjective rights also belong, is defined, in opposition to these, as a manifestation of personality consisting of an effective power, directly attributed by the legal system, prior, therefore, and independent of any specific legal relationship, and susceptible, for that reason, to unfold and act against generic circles of people, who, in relation to the holder of that power, find themselves in a situation of subjection. It is, therefore, a generic power, not referred to a particular subject, nor to a specific object, a power, ultimately, that can only be actualized and translated into a concrete power through its specific exercise, that is, into a true subjective right. By virtue of the principle of legality of the Administration, the legal system attributes powers to the Administration, so that every administrative action presents itself as the exercise of a power previously attributed by Law and, as a consequence of this legal and not contractual origin, powers are inalienable, non-transferable, and irrevocable. And while the figure of power is crucial to define the legal position of the Administration, it is not exclusive to it, since the subjects of the law can also be holders of this type of legal powers, both in the field of Private Law and in the sphere of Public Law. On the other hand, duty and obligation are two species of a common genus, duties in a broad sense, as behaviors, positive or negative, that are imposed on a subject in consideration of interests that are not their own, but those of another distinct subject or the general interests of the community.

In some cases, duties operate in generic directions, such that those burdened by them do not have a specific subject before them who holds a subjective right to demand the behavior that the duty consists of, but rather, at most, a power intended to act as a guarantee of the effective compliance with the duty. In these cases, we speak of duties in the strict sense, which, like powers, originate directly from the norm and not from any specific legal relationship or transaction. 

At other times, however, the situation of duty arises within the framework of a relationship that is strictly correlated with a subjective right of another subject who is part of that relationship and who, consequently, has the power to demand from the burdened subject, under penalty of liability, the effective compliance with the prescribed behavior, to the extent that such behavior is imposed within the context of the relationship considered, precisely in consideration of the specific interests of the holder of the right, reserving for this case of specific duties the designation of obligation. Hence, as García de Enterría concludes, there is usually a process of specification between the duty in the strict sense and the obligation, similar to that which frequently occurs between the power and the subjective right, a process that in this case is resolved in administrative acts of ascertainment or determination, which, in consideration of the circumstances present in each case, specify in relation to a determined subject the generic duty imposed by the norm and the concrete scope of the behavior that can be demanded from the burdened subject. 

In any case, the articulation of the content and scope of duties requires coverage at the level of formal Law, and based on that legal coverage, the imposition on the administered of a specific conduct constitutive of a duty in the broad sense is carried out through various legal instruments: laws and regulations, contracts, and administrative acts. 

However, nothing prevents, as indicated earlier, the legal system from imposing duties on Public Administrations, as occurs, for example, when the Administration, as an employer, must communicate the data of its labor personnel to the General Treasury of Social Security. 

Therefore, the legal basis provided in letter c) of article 6.1. of the GDPR will apply in those cases where a norm with the rank of law imposes a specific obligation on the Administration to give, do, or not do, which involves the processing of personal data, and different from the generic legal duty of the Administration to exercise the powers that the legal system attributes to it to serve the public interest objectively (article 103 of the Constitution). 

In this sense, we find different legal provisions in our legal system that impose legal obligations on the Administration.

 The processing of personal data for purposes other than those for which they were initially collected should only be permitted when it is compatible with the purposes of their initial collection. In such cases, a separate legal basis, distinct from that which allowed for the collection of personal data, is not required. In this case, the affected party must be informed of their right to object to the adoption of automated individual decisions that produce legal effects concerning them or significantly affect them in a similar manner, when this right applies in accordance with the provisions of Article 22 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
3. In these cases, the basic information will also include:
a) The categories of data subject to processing.
b) The sources from which the data originated.
