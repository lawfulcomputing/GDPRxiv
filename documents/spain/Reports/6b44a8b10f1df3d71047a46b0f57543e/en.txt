The inquiry raises the legality of posting on the company's bulletin board the productivity list of each employee not identified by name, but by registration number.  

According to the inquiry, the consulting party, engaged in the marketing of products derived from Iberian pork, has a room where hams are deboned and sliced, all of which are pure Iberian with a high economic value. Therefore, it is essential that the yield obtained from each ham/shoulder when deboning or slicing is maximized, as any waste in this regard incurs a high cost. Hence, it is intended to publish weekly on the existing bulletin board in that room the weekly productivity, obtaining this individual data from each person regarding the yield obtained from each ham/shoulder and the number of packages of sliced ham/shoulder produced.  

Based on this data, which is transferred at the end of the month to the Human Resources Department, a productivity bonus has long been paid above the minimums established in the National Collective Agreement for Meat Industries, with no publication of the amounts paid for this concept, except in each worker's own payroll.  

The objective of publishing the data is, in addition to transparency, to generate healthy competition among employees that helps improve the overall productivity of the room.  

The workers in the lists would be placed on the bulletin board identified by their registration number, a detail known only to the employee and the personnel in the Human Resources Department who must proceed with the payment of the aforementioned productivity bonus.  

I  

Although this Agency has had the opportunity to express its opinion on the publication of productivity data on other occasions, it is noteworthy that this report is issued at a time when the Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons concerning the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR), is fully applicable and has direct effect since May 25.  

The GDPR represents a profound modification of data protection norms that significantly affects, among other issues, the legal bases for data processing.  

Regarding the processing of personal data, it must be based on one of the legal bases for processing established in Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, according to which "Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child."  

In this regard, it is necessary to recall, as has been indicated repeatedly by this Agency, that the reform brought about by the General Data Protection Regulation regarding the regime contained in Organic Law 15/1999 requires a change of perspective concerning the principles underpinning the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and, in particular, the one that made the "principle of consent" the central axis of the right to data protection.

Indeed, although the Organic Law and the Regulation do not differ excessively regarding the enumeration of the legitimizing grounds for processing, there is a highly relevant modification in the way these grounds are presented in the applicable texts: thus, while the wording of Organic Law 15/1999 seemed to imply that the basic rule of legitimacy was, in general, consent, with the remaining legitimizing grounds being exceptional in relation to consent, which as a general rule should govern the processing, in the text of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, consent is listed as one of the six grounds for legitimacy for processing without holding greater or lesser importance than the others enumerated in the regulation.

Furthermore, the General Data Protection Regulation itself makes it clear that the consent of the data subject should not constitute the legal basis for processing in certain cases. Thus, Recital 42 states in its last sentence that “Consent should not be considered freely given when the data subject does not have genuine or free choice or cannot deny or withdraw consent without suffering detriment,” and Recital 43 adds that “To ensure that consent has been given freely, it should not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, particularly when the data controller is a public authority and it is therefore unlikely that consent has been given freely in all circumstances of that particular situation.”

Thus, it is not appropriate to seek the consent of the data subject in any case where the processing is covered by any of the grounds included in letters b) to f) of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

II
In the present case, for the exclusive purposes of data protection regulations, the publication of the productivity data of employees would find its legal basis in the provisions of Article 6.1.f) of the Regulation, according to which such processing will be possible if it “is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that such interests do not override the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require the protection of personal data.” 

Such publication would respond to a legitimate interest of the employer, which is to generate healthy competitiveness among employees that helps to improve overall productivity, as well as to a legitimate interest of the employees themselves.

Notwithstanding the existence of such legitimate interest, the appreciation of the same requires, as already pointed out by the Judgment of the CJEU of November 24, 2011, and the Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC prepared by the Article 29 Working Party on data protection, that a balancing or "weighing test" be carried out with the rights and interests of the affected parties, indicating for these purposes the aforementioned Opinion that “it is important to highlight the special role that guarantees can play in reducing undue impact on the data subjects and, therefore, in shifting the balance of rights and interests to the point that the legitimate interest of the data controller prevails,” citing among such possible guarantees data minimization, privacy-enhancing technologies, increased transparency, the general and unconditional right to voluntary exclusion, and data portability.

In the present case, each worker is identified by their registration number, a piece of information known only to the employee themselves and the Human Resources Department personnel, and the necessary organizational and technical measures must be adopted to prevent access to the rest of the personal data and the individual identification of each worker by unauthorized persons, ensuring that the confidentiality of such data is sufficiently guaranteed.

On the other hand, it must be ensured that the publication is carried out in a manner that is least harmful to the interested party. In this regard, this Agency, in relation to the publicity of the productivity of public officials, has deemed it necessary that access to the data does not lead to subsequent processing that may be contrary to the provisions of data protection legislation or generate situations that may jeopardize the rights of employees, with a general exposure of the data being preferable, avoiding its publication on a network, even if it is private, or its transmission by email. In any case, the exposure must take place in an environment where only the employees themselves have access, thus avoiding public dissemination to persons who are not part of the corresponding employment relationship, a requirement that seems to be met in the present case by limiting the publication to the notice board of the room where the work activity takes place.

“Now, regarding the breach of the duty of confidentiality, the Agency specifies as data disclosed to third parties the corresponding name, surname, job position, employee number, and the circumstances related to the objectives and productivity of each of the workers, within the framework of a productivity incentive system, which is based on the fulfillment of a series of objectives. To this end, a report is prepared approximately biweekly, which is sent to all employees in a zone for their knowledge, with information on the productivity of any employee in the branch being accessible to all employees of the same. It is specified in the report of prior actions that daily, at the start of the workday, a meeting of all personnel in each branch is held, in which the evolution of the fulfillment of objectives is analyzed and actions to be taken for their achievement are established.

In this regard, the Court considers that the information sent regarding the assessment of productivity, established in a system of incentives based on objectives and confined to the work area, cannot be considered a breach of the duty of confidentiality since the achievement of objectives and the incentive system derived from productivity fall within the sphere of labor relations.

In the ruling of the Constitutional Court 142/93, to which the appellant refers albeit with an incorrect number, it is stated: "The remuneration that the worker obtains from their work cannot, in principle, be detached from the sphere of the social and professional relations that the worker develops outside of their personal and intimate sphere, to be introduced into the latter, and it must be ruled out that knowledge of the remuneration received allows for the reconstruction of the intimate life of the workers." In the present case, it is not exactly the right to privacy that is at stake but rather the right to data protection, which exceeds the strict scope of personal intimacy; however, the ruling is expressive regarding the fact that data (in the case of the ruling, remuneration, and in this case, productivity) are linked to the sphere of labor relations in such a way that their knowledge in the context in which the aforementioned relations occur and for purposes that affect them cannot be considered a breach of the duty of confidentiality.

For all these reasons, the processing in question, as it is based on Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR and after weighing the interests and rights affected, having adopted adequate safeguards, is deemed compliant with the current regulations on the protection of personal data.
