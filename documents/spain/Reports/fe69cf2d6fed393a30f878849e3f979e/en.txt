Furthermore, the constitutional doctrine must be taken into account, primarily as reflected in the aforementioned rulings 292/2000 of November 30 and 76/2019 of May 22, according to which the limits to the fundamental right to the protection of personal data must be established by a norm with the rank of law, following the legislator's weighing of conflicting interests in accordance with the principle of proportionality, defining each and every one of the material prerequisites of the limiting measure through precise rules, which make the imposition of such limitation and its consequences foreseeable to the interested party, so that such a norm with the rank of law "must meet all those indispensable characteristics as a guarantee of legal security," that is, "must express each and every one of the prerequisites and conditions of the intervention" (STC 49/1999, FJ 4). In other words, "it not only excludes delegations in favor of regulatory norms [...], but also implies other requirements regarding the content of the Law that establishes such limits" (STC 292/2000, FJ 15).
Moreover, it must establish adequate guarantees, with the law itself containing the appropriate safeguards against the collection of personal data it authorizes. The Constitutional Court (TC) has been clear that the provision of adequate guarantees cannot be deferred to a moment after the legal regulation of the personal data processing in question. The adequate guarantees must be incorporated into the legal regulation of the processing itself, either directly or by express and perfectly delineated reference to external sources that possess the appropriate normative rank. Only that understanding is compatible with the dual requirement that stems from Article 53.1 CE (...). It is evident that if the norm included a reference for the integration of the law with the adequate guarantees established in norms of lower rank than the law, it would be considered a deregulation that sacrifices the legal reserve under Article 53.1 CE, and for this sole reason, it should be declared unconstitutional and null. (...). Ultimately, it is about "adequate guarantees of a technical, organizational, and procedural nature, which prevent risks of varying probability and severity and mitigate their effects, as only in this way can respect for the essential content of the fundamental right itself be ensured." Therefore, it is not sufficient for the establishment of such adequate and specific guarantees for the law to refer to the GDPR itself or to the LOPDGDD.

Additionally, such law must respect, in any case, the principle of proportionality, as recalled by the Judgment of the Constitutional Court 14/2003, of January 28:
"In other words, in accordance with a reiterated doctrine of this Court, the constitutionality of any restrictive measure of fundamental rights is determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality. For the purposes that matter here, it is sufficient to recall that, to verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions: whether the measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, furthermore, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and, finally, whether it is balanced or proportionate, deriving from it more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict proportionality test; SSTC 66/1995, of May 8 [RTC 1995, 66], F. 5; 55/1996, of March 28 [RTC 1996, 55], FF. 7, 8, and 9; 270/1996, of December 16 [RTC 1996, 270], F. 4.e; 37/1998, of February 17 [RTC 1998, 37], F. 8; 186/2000, of July 10 [RTC 2000, 186], F. 6).”

Consequently, two cumulative requirements are established: that the processing is carried out by someone who holds the status of a competent authority and for the specified purposes, so that if either of them is lacking, it would not be a transposition norm of the Directive and would be extending to cases not contemplated therein a more restrictive regulation of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data than that established in the GDPR, which would be the general norm applicable to processing where those requirements do not concur. In this regard, the following issues can be identified: legal position of the parties involved in the processing of personal data; subjects whose personal data may be subject to processing; legal basis; application of data protection principles and limitations on the rights of the affected individuals.

Next, it is appropriate to refer to the different subjects whose personal data may be subject to processing. 
In this regard, it should be noted that the primary purpose of the regulation is to protect informants, which the Directive defines as “a natural person who communicates or publicly reveals information about infringements obtained in the context of their work activities.” 
Directive (EU) 2019/1937 provides for the possibility that information may be provided anonymously, which is expressly referred to in paragraphs 2 and 3 of its Article 6:

The next issue raised is related to the lawfulness of processing, to which the draft bill dedicates Article 30:
Article 30. Lawfulness of personal data processing.

4. The processing of personal data resulting from a public disclosure shall be presumed to be covered by the provisions of Article 6.1.e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, and 11 of Organic Law 7/2021, of May 26.

As already mentioned, the Council of State in its Opinion 757/2017, on the Draft Organic Law on the Protection of Personal Data, considered that the processing of personal data in internal whistleblowing systems was legitimized by "the existence of a public interest legitimizing these treatments." Consequently, in general terms, these personal data processing activities are covered by letter e) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR: "the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller." 

However, following the entry into force of Directive (EU) 2019/1937, the obligation to establish internal whistleblowing channels for certain entities has been established in its Article 8, which the draft bill refers to in Article 10, concerning the private sector, and in Article 13, regarding the public sector. Additionally, Article 11 of Directive (EU) 2019/1937 introduces an obligation to establish external whistleblowing channels and to follow up on reports in its Article 11, which the draft bill refers to in its Title III, regulating the external communication channel of the Independent Whistleblower Protection Authority. Therefore, in these cases, the processing would find its legitimacy in letter c) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR: the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject.

In this regard, it should be noted the significance of whether the legitimacy is determined by one or the other assumption, to the extent that the right to object provided for in Article 21 of the GDPR, to which we will refer later, is recognized concerning processing based on letter e), but not concerning those covered by letter c).

On the other hand, the principle of legal reservation derived from Article 53 of the Constitution is also complied with, in the terms set forth by Article 8 of the LOPDGDD.

Therefore, without prejudice to the specific mentions contained in particular cases in the text and to which reference has been made, it should be expressly included in Article 31 of the draft bill the manner in which the duty of information regarding the person referred to in the information and those others mentioned therein who may have participation in the procedure, such as witnesses, will be fulfilled, with the fulfillment of said duty being limited to the moment when it does not harm the proper conclusion of the investigation, regarding the first, and to the moment when they are to intervene in the procedure, regarding the latter.
On the other hand, as a specific limitation that affects not only the right to information but also the right of access, it is provided that “The person to whom the facts reported refer shall in no case be informed of the identity of the informant or of the person who has made the public disclosure.”
Furthermore, Article 31, in its section 4, states that “In the event that the person to whom the facts reported in the communication refer or to whom the public disclosure refers exercises the right of opposition, it shall be presumed that, unless proven otherwise, there are legitimate compelling reasons that legitimize the processing of their personal data.”
In this regard, as indicated when analyzing the lawfulness of the processing, the right of opposition only applies to cases where the processing is legitimized under letter e) of Article 6.1. of the GDPR, but not to cases where it is based on letter c) of the same. Furthermore, it should be noted that there are cases in our legal system where, for reasons of public interest, the right of opposition is directly excluded, as occurs in Article 28.2. of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations: “Opposition shall not be admissible when the submission of the document is required in the context of the exercise of sanctioning or inspection powers.”
