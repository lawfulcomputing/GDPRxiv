I  
This Legal Office has repeatedly emphasized, in the various consultations submitted for its report, the paradigm shift that the full application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons in relation to the processing of their personal data and the free movement of such data, represents, as it is based on the principle of “accountability” or proactive responsibility, as stated in the Explanatory Memorandum of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights: “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the data controller or the processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.”  

Consequently, by virtue of this principle, the data controller must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the Regulation.  

Among the various instruments provided by the GDPR itself to ensure compliance with this principle are the codes of conduct, already foreseen in previous regulations as a self-regulation mechanism aimed at facilitating compliance with personal data protection regulations. In this regard, the Article 29 Working Party, in its working document on the examination procedure for community codes of conduct, approved on September 10, 1998 (WP13), had already defined the essential elements of such codes, emphasizing that the codes should have sufficient clarity and internal coherence and provide sufficient added value, in terms of being sufficiently focused on the specific data protection issues and problems in the organization or sector for which they are intended to be applied and offering sufficiently clear solutions for those questions and problems, facilitating, to the extent possible, examples of best practices, preferably prepared in consultation with the affected stakeholders or their representatives. Thus, the essential requirement of the codes is to provide a “genuine added value,” as the Article 29 Working Party recalled in its Opinion 02/2015 on the Code of Conduct for Cloud Computing of the CSIG.

These principles are explicitly stated in the GDPR, especially in its Recitals 77, 81, 98, and 99. Likewise, the recognition of codes of conduct as a tool to demonstrate compliance with the obligations of controllers and processors is included in Article 24.3, which states that “Adherence to codes of conduct approved pursuant to Article 40 or to a certification mechanism approved pursuant to Article 42 may be used as elements to demonstrate compliance with the obligations of the data controller” and in Article 35.8, which provides that “Compliance with the approved codes of conduct referred to in Article 40 by the respective controllers or processors shall be duly taken into account when assessing the impact of the processing operations carried out by those controllers or processors, particularly for the purposes of the data protection impact assessment.”

Given the significant role that codes of conduct play and their direct application, the GDPR regulates their nature, content, approval, and supervision in Articles 40 and 41 in a much more detailed manner than that contained in Article 27 of Directive 95/46/EC. This regulation must be complemented by the provisions of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights, which dedicates Article 38 to codes of conduct, also referring to them as an element for identifying higher risks of processing in Article 28.2.h). It also highlights the role of the supervisory bodies of codes of conduct as collaborating entities with data protection authorities in the preparation of audit plans in Article 54 and in the admission of complaints in Article 65.

Regarding procedural aspects, the aforementioned Article 38, in its section 6, provides that “the content of the register and the specificities of the procedure for the approval of codes of conduct shall be established by royal decree,” a regulatory development that has not yet occurred, so the general principles established in Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations, and the provisions of Royal Decree 1720/2007, of December 21, which approves the Regulation for the development of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the protection of personal data, shall be applied subsidiarily, to the extent that they do not contradict, oppose, or prove incompatible with the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and Organic Law 3/2018.

The role of codes of conduct and their connection with the principle of proactive responsibility, as a means to demonstrate compliance with the GDPR, from which the need to review all previously approved codes derives, is included in the Guidelines of the European Data Protection Board 1/2019 on codes of conduct and bodies of...

Furthermore, the aforementioned Guidelines detail the criteria that must be taken into account by the supervisory authorities for the approval of codes:
1) That it meets a particular need of that sector or processing activity, with the proposed solutions being beneficial not only for the controllers but also for the affected parties.
2) That it facilitates the application of the GDPR, identifying specific needs (for example, adapting sector terminology).
3) That it specifies the application of the GDPR, focusing on sector issues and providing added value, without merely reproducing the provisions of the GDPR.
4) That it provides effective mechanisms for monitoring compliance with the Code, both in terms of structures and procedures, with the existence of an accredited supervisory body being mandatory, except in the case of authorities and public bodies.
For the adaptation of the model codes currently registered under the previous regulations, the provisions of the second transitional provision of the LOPDGDD must be followed:
Second transitional provision. Model codes registered with the data protection authorities in accordance with Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data. The promoters of the model codes registered with the Spanish Data Protection Agency or with the autonomous data protection authorities must adapt their content to the provisions of Article 40 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 within one year from the entry into force of this organic law. If, after this period, the approval provided for in Article 38.4 of this organic law has not been requested, the registration will be canceled and communicated to its promoters.

Therefore, this Legal Office considers that, at the time of approving a new code of conduct or adapting an already registered model code, given the importance acquired by codes of conduct as a result of the introduction of the principle of proactive accountability, it is necessary to be particularly rigorous regarding its content, which must provide “genuine added value” in the terms previously indicated, as well as in the assessment of the sufficiency of the necessary adequate guarantees for its approval.
Finally, regarding the supervisory bodies of the codes of conduct referred to in Article 41 of the GDPR, the accreditation criteria approved by the Spanish Data Protection Agency in accordance with Opinion 1/2020 of the EDPB must be met.

IV
Regarding the content of the code of conduct, the GDPR has identified certain areas that it may cover in Article 40.2:
2. Associations and other representative bodies of categories of controllers or processors may develop codes of conduct or modify or expand such codes in order to specify the application of this Regulation, particularly regarding:
a) fair and transparent processing;
b) the legitimate interests pursued by the controllers in specific contexts;
c) the collection of personal data;
d) the pseudonymization of personal data;
e) the information provided to the public and to the interested parties;
f) the exercise of the rights of the interested parties;
g) the information provided to children and their protection, as well as the manner of obtaining consent from those holding parental responsibility or guardianship over the child;
h) the measures and procedures referred to in Articles 24 and 25 and the measures to ensure the security of processing referred to in Article 32;
i) the notification of personal data security breaches to the supervisory authorities and the communication of such breaches to the interested parties;
j) the transfer of personal data to third countries or international organizations, or
k) the extrajudicial procedures and other conflict resolution procedures that allow for the resolution of disputes between controllers and interested parties regarding processing, without prejudice to the rights of the interested parties under Articles 77 and 79.

At this point, it is important to highlight that the GDPR does not identify a minimum content for codes of conduct, but rather it is an enumerative list of subjects that they may address and does not exhaust all the scenarios that may arise to facilitate the application of the GDPR, unlike what was done in the Development Regulation of the LOPD of 1999, which required that model codes include the content referred to in Article 73 of the LOPD Regulation, which involved the entire lifecycle of personal data, a supervision procedure, and a list of adherents, in accordance with the provisions of Articles 75 and 76. Furthermore, there was the option to include additional commitments.

What must be included mandatorily are the mechanisms for supervising compliance as required by Article 40.4 of the GDPR in order to allow the supervisory body to carry out the mandatory control of compliance with its provisions by the controllers or processors who commit to applying it. Consequently, regardless of the material content of the codes of conduct, they cannot overlook including mechanisms for monitoring compliance, whether they pertain to codes applicable to private sector controllers or processors, or those promoted by public authorities and bodies.

Therefore, the analysis of the content of the code of conduct for the purpose of its approval will not be determined by the greater or lesser scope of the same, but by the extent to which it contributes to the better application of personal data protection regulations, providing "genuine added value" and sufficient guarantees, as indicated at the beginning of this report.

I.- MAINTENANCE OF CREDIT INFORMATION SYSTEMS WITH DATA RELATING TO THE FULFILLMENT OF MONETARY, FINANCIAL, OR CREDIT OBLIGATIONS (POSITIVE SOLVENCY FILES).  
The report issued by the General Subdirectorate of GDPR considers that its content does not conform to the provisions of the GDPR or the LOPDGDD, enumerating a series of reasons that can be summarized as follows: there is a confusion between the interest of the controller and the purpose of the processing, as the invoked interest appears to be merely to obtain an economic benefit while the need for third parties to know the data of economic compliance would constitute the purpose of the processing; the data was collected for another purpose, leading to the conclusion that the compatibility test would be contrary to the principle of purpose; Article 5 of the GDPR requires that data be processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject, and the legitimate interest requires that the processing be necessary; the processed data can lead to the creation of a profile of the data subjects that shows their personal and family life and intimacy; there are countries that do not consider legitimate interest as a basis for carrying out these treatments; sufficient guarantees are not provided; the legislator has regulated the processing of data from negative files based on legitimate interest, remaining silent on this type of files; the prevalence of the legitimate interest of the controller or third parties over the rights and freedoms of the affected parties is not evident.  
1.- Historical and legislative background. Differentiated treatment of credit information systems related to the fulfillment of obligations and those related to non-compliance in Spanish legislation and administrative doctrine.

This Legal Office shares the opinion expressed by the General Subdirectorate RGPD, which also corresponds to the traditional treatment that positive files have had in our legal system, whose legitimacy was based on obtaining the consent of the affected party, as this Agency has repeatedly pointed out. 

In this regard, in report 180/2010, it is already highlighted how the processing of data related to the credit situation of a specific individual was one of the most complained about issues before the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, which led to the issuance of its first general Instruction (Instruction 1/1995 of March 1, from the Data Protection Agency, regarding the provision of information services on asset solvency and credit) in reference to this type of processing, in order to resolve the problems it posed from the perspective of applying the principles of data quality, enshrined in Article 6 of the Directive, and security, provided for in Article 17.

Subsequently, Article 29 of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, proceeded to regulate it, differentiating two cases:

“1. Those engaged in the provision of information services on asset solvency and credit may only process personal data obtained from the registers and sources accessible to the public established for this purpose or from information provided by the interested party or with their consent. 
2. Personal data relating to the compliance or non-compliance with monetary obligations provided by the creditor or by those acting on their behalf or interest may also be processed. In these cases, the interested parties regarding whom personal data has been registered in files will be notified, within thirty days from such registration, of a reference to those that have been included and will be informed of their right to request information on all of them, under the terms established by this Law.

3. In the cases referred to in the two previous paragraphs, when the interested party requests it, the data controller will communicate the data to them, as well as the evaluations and assessments that have been communicated about them during the last six months and the name and address of the person or entity to whom the data has been revealed. 
4. Only personal data that is decisive for assessing the economic solvency of the interested parties may be registered and transferred, and that does not refer, when adverse, to more than six years, provided that they truthfully reflect the current situation of those.”

Analyzing this differentiation, the aforementioned report 180/2010 stated the following: 
Thus, on the one hand, the files of asset solvency and credit in the strict sense would collect information obtained from the interested party themselves or from publicly accessible sources, and these could incorporate both positive and negative information, remaining, in general, subject to the common regime of data protection norms. 

In contrast to such files, Article 29.2 of the Organic Law refers to files related to the compliance or non-compliance with monetary obligations, in which the data may be provided directly to the credit information system by creditors or those acting on their behalf or interest, understanding that the legislator believes that the legitimization for the processing of such data may find its cause in what is provided in Article 7 f) of Directive 95/46/EC, given that the legislator appreciates the existence of a legitimate interest for the processing of data that prevails over the right to data protection of the interested party, who simply must be informed about this processing. 

The exclusion of the intervention of the interested party in the processing of data in these files (regarding whose permanence they may not oppose, unless the principles of data protection are not complied with) has as compensation, to ensure the appropriate balance of interests, a reinforcement of the guarantees of compliance with the principles of data quality, which will require...

to take extreme precautions aimed at ensuring that the data is adequate, relevant, and not excessive, that reflects the actual situation of the debtor, and that is not retained for a period that exceeds the purpose justifying the processing.  
Consequently, the Spanish legislator has analyzed the application of the balance rule enshrined in Article 7(f) of the Directive in relation to credit information systems, taking into account the purpose or "legitimate interest" pursued by the processing, which is the sustainability of the financial and credit system and the guarantee of access to credit by individuals and companies, as derived from the provisions of Articles 8 and 9 of Directive 2008/48/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 23, 2008, on consumer credit agreements.  
And considering such analysis, the legislator has determined that the purpose pursued with these treatments will prevail over the debtor's right to data protection in those cases where the debtor has not fulfilled their obligations, with the consent of the interested party not being necessary in such cases, and the direct communication of the data to the file by the creditor or someone acting on their behalf being sufficient. Conversely, when the data does not refer to defaults but to the mere existence of credit or its compliance (i.e., to so-called positive files), the legislator has considered that the legitimate interest of the data controller only prevails over the right to data protection of the interested party in cases where such data is manifestly public, with the remaining cases requiring the consent of the interested party.  
Once this consideration has been made, and taking into account that the legislator considers the legitimate interest of the data controller and potential recipients of the data to be prevalent in cases of credit information systems with negative data, it has established a system of reinforced guarantees to ensure that the data contained in the system complies with the requirements established in data protection legislation and in particular in Directive 95/46/EC, from which the Spanish Law derives, as well as a series of mechanisms aimed at ensuring that the interested party, who cannot decide in any way about the processing in these cases, due to the applicability of the aforementioned Article 7(f) of the Directive and who cannot exercise their right of opposition, as the processing is expressly recognized by the Law, has full knowledge of the processing of their data and can adequately exercise the remaining rights that the Law and the Directive recognize.  
The system is finally closed with the express authorization granted by the legislator to the regulator, that is, to the Bank of Spain, for the creation of a specific file related to the credit risk incurred by credit financial entities (Central Credit Risk Information of the Bank of Spain, regulated by Law 44/2002, on measures for the reform of the financial system), to which all operations, positive or negative, that imply the existence of a credit risk will be communicated by the financial entities. In this case, given that the purpose pursued by the file is the adequate supervision by the regulator of the credit risk incurred by the entities, the legislator has also considered the legitimate interest or purpose pursued by the file to be prevalent over the right to data protection of the interested parties, so it will not be necessary to obtain their consent, with the processing being legitimized by Article 7(f) of the Directive.  
In summary, Spanish legislation recognizes the existence of two distinct types of credit information systems, positive and negative, establishing a differentiated regime for both, which, originally, is based on the existence of a different legitimization for the processing of data in each case, which will also affect the guarantees to be adopted for compliance with the different principles enshrined by the Directive.

The code of conduct aims to support such treatments based on the existence of a legitimate interest, in accordance with the provisions of Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR: the treatment is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that the interests or the rights and fundamental freedoms of the data subject requiring the protection of personal data do not prevail over those interests, particularly when the data subject is a child.

Recitals 47 to 49 of the GDPR include the identification of some cases of legitimate interest:

(47) The legitimate interest of a data controller, including that of a controller to whom personal data may be communicated, or of a third party, may constitute a legal basis for processing, provided that the interests or the rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail, taking into account the reasonable expectations of the data subjects based on their relationship with the controller. Such legitimate interest could arise, for example, when there is a relevant and appropriate relationship between the data subject and the controller, such as in situations where the data subject is a customer or is in the service of the controller. In any case, the existence of a legitimate interest would require a meticulous assessment, including whether a data subject can reasonably foresee, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that processing for such purpose may occur. In particular, the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject could prevail over the interests of the data controller when personal data is processed in circumstances where the data subject does not reasonably expect that further processing will take place. Since it is up to the legislator to establish by law the legal basis for the processing of personal data by public authorities, this legal basis should not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions. The processing of personal data strictly necessary for the prevention of fraud also constitutes a legitimate interest of the data controller concerned. The processing of personal data for direct marketing purposes may be considered as carried out for legitimate interest.

In this regard, it should be noted that in that balancing between the possible legitimate interest of the controller and the rights of the data subject, the latter are considered a fundamental right in accordance with Article 18.4 of the Constitution and Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union. While, like all fundamental rights, the right to personal data protection is subject to limitations, these must be in favor of other constitutionally protected rights or goods. Recital 4 of the GDPR itself states that [the] processing of personal data must be designed to serve humanity. The right to the protection of personal data is not an absolute right but must be considered in relation to its function in society and maintain a balance with other fundamental rights, according to the principle of proportionality. And the circumstance that a fundamental right is subjected to scrutiny in the balancing against other types of interests, legitimate but not enjoying the same level of protection, should not be indifferent.

Nevertheless, the concept of public interest, or that of general interest, which is more frequently used by our constitutional text, is a legal concept that is indeterminate with a dual function: to provide legitimizing coverage for the actions of the Administration and, on the other hand, to serve as a means of limiting administrative powers. In this regard, for the public interest to be invoked as a legal basis legitimizing the processing, Article 6.3 of the GDPR requires that it be established in Union Law or in the Law of the Member States applicable to the data controller, and Article 8 of the LOPDGDD requires, in the case of a national norm, that it have the rank of law.

Therefore, the public interest requires, first of all, its specification by the legislator, taking into account all affected interests, in order to determine the restrictions that may be imposed on particular interests as a consequence of the presence of such general interests.

2.2. Interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of the data subjects.
Regarding the interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of the data subjects, and unlike the extensive justification contained regarding the legitimate interest of the controller or third parties, the code contains a brief analysis in which it does not even assess, unlike negative files, the possibility that the data may be erroneous or not up to date, limiting itself to stating the following:

2.3. Result of the weighting.
Regarding the result of the weighting, the code considers that it should be resolved in favor of the interests of the data controllers and the third-party credit grantors, based on premises that, as analyzed in detail in the previous sections, are not shared by this Agency, since the appreciation of the existence of a “national general interest” requires the legislator to expressly state so, and until this is done, the main legitimate interest pursued with the processing of personal data must be considered to be commercial benefit, both for the data controllers and for third parties, as the assessment of creditworthiness is a legal obligation that can be fulfilled through the different scenarios contemplated by the regulations, without the need to consult the files, so that such consultation would only allow for greater knowledge of their clients, which would reduce the risks taken and benefit their profit and loss account by decreasing bad debts and the corresponding provisions.
On the other hand, the impact on the interests, rights, and freedoms of the affected parties can be very intense, and the existence of a reasonable expectation cannot be accepted merely due to the existence of the CIRBE, since the reality is that, as of today, the only existing private positive files only deal with data from those who have consented, and such files are not widespread, far from it, as evidenced by the continuous claims from the sector and part of the doctrine in this regard. Furthermore, in the CIRBE there is a legally established lower limit on the amount of credits that can be reflected in it (currently 1,000 euros), a circumstance that is non-existent, precisely due to the lack of any regulation, in private positive files, so the impact of these on the interests, rights, and freedoms of the interested parties is potentially much greater than that arising from the CIRBE.
The different situation in which the entities providing information to the file and the affected parties find themselves has also not been taken into account, which is also referenced in Opinion 6/2014:
A multinational company may, for example, have more resources and bargaining power than the individual interested party and, therefore, may find itself in a better position to impose on the interested party what it believes corresponds to its “legitimate interest.” This may occur even more so if the company has a dominant position in the market. If these situations are not corrected, they will be to the detriment of individual interested parties. Just as competition and consumer protection legislation helps ensure that this power is not misused, data protection legislation could also play an important role in ensuring that the rights and interests of the affected parties are not unduly harmed.
And without there being, as detailed, a homogeneous criterion in this regard in the countries around us. This lack of a homogeneous criterion is reflected in the code of conduct itself, as in the examples it cites, it can be seen that each country does it in a different way. Thus, according to what is stated in it, most countries have legal norms, either in personal data protection regulations or in specific laws, that regulate credit information agencies, establishing specialities regarding the types of data they can process (which in the cases of Sweden and Norway would be limited to data from public sources and government databases) and even establishing the obligation for credit institutions to participate in credit information systems (as in the case of the Netherlands).
2.4. Measures to be adopted to minimize the risk to the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the interested parties.
Legal Office
When the legal basis for legitimizing the processing of personal data is intended to be founded on legitimate interest, the specific guarantees to be adopted acquire special relevance in order to assess the prevalence of the same with respect to the interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of the affected individuals, as noted in Opinion 6/2014:
The legitimate interest of the data controller, when it is lesser and not very pressing, generally only overrides the interests and rights of the data subjects in cases where the impact on these rights and interests is even more trivial. On the other hand, a significant and pressing legitimate interest may, in some cases and subject to guarantees and measures, even justify a significant intrusion into privacy or any other important repercussion on the interests or rights of the data subjects.
Here it is important to highlight the special role that guarantees can play in reducing undue impact on the data subjects and, therefore, in shifting the balance of rights and interests to the point that the legitimate interest of the data controller prevails. Of course, the use of guarantees alone is not sufficient to justify any type of processing in any context. Furthermore, the guarantees in question must be adequate and sufficient, and must, unquestionably and significantly, reduce the impact on the data subjects.
However, as will be pointed out later, these are guarantees that cannot be assessed in the abstract, as the measures to be adopted must be adjusted to a rigorous analysis of the risks that the processing entails for the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals, through the instruments provided in the GDPR. Moreover, the guarantees to be adopted under the principle of proactive responsibility do not exempt compliance with the other principles contained in Article 5 of the GDPR.
In this case, since the impact of the processing on the interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of the affected individuals has not been adequately assessed, it has also not been possible to precisely identify the appropriate guarantees aimed at minimizing it. At this point, although Opinion 6/2014 notes that the impact assessment does not refer to either the "risk analysis" or the "Data Protection Impact Assessment" in the sense of the GDPR, "it should be taken into account that, in one way or another, the impact analysis under Article 7, letter f), can be an important part of any 'risk assessment' or 'Data Protection Impact Assessment' and can also help to detect situations in which it is necessary to consult the data protection authority," and even the same may be useful for conducting the balancing:
When assessing the impact, the terminology and methodology of traditional risk assessment can be useful to some extent, and therefore, some elements of this methodology are highlighted below. However, a comprehensive methodology for impact assessment, in the context of Article 7, letter f), or more generally, would exceed the scope of this Opinion.
In the broader sense of impact assessment, one can consider whether the data has been disclosed to the public or otherwise made available to a large number of people, or whether a large amount of personal data is processed or combined with other data (for example, in the case of profiling, for commercial purposes, for law enforcement purposes, or others).
In addition to potentially leading to the processing of more sensitive data, such analysis can also lead to strange, unexpected, and sometimes inaccurate predictions, for example, regarding the behavior or personality of the affected individuals. Depending on the nature and impact of such predictions, this can be highly intrusive to the individual's privacy.
In general, the more negative and uncertain the impact of the processing may be, the less likely it is that the processing will be considered, overall, legitimate. Thus, in the opinion of this Agency, any profiling of clients carried out as a result of enriching the data available to the entity by virtue of its relationship with the client with information from other sources would require either the request for the product or service by the interested party, as already indicated, or that the interested party provides their consent for the treatment. This is based on the fact that the obligation of the entities to obtain available information about the solvency and risk level of a client or potential client can be based on the request for the service by the client, but not on the unilateral decision of the entity to carry out that profiling.

If the data were derived solely from the information available to the entity regarding the products or services contracted by the client, without being supplemented with information originating from other distinct sources, certainly the conduct of the entity, consisting of profiling for the purpose of sending offers of products or services to its clients, would be less invasive of the rights and interests of the clients, and in this case, the applicability of the provisions of Article 6.1 f) of the General Data Protection Regulation could be considered.

However, in order to reach this conclusion, it will be essential for the entities to clearly differentiate the treatment being referred to from that which has been analyzed in the previous section of this report. This means that a treatment of the characteristics now studied cannot be considered covered by Article 6.1 f) if the information provided to the interested party only indicates that the data will be processed for the purpose of sending advertising for other products and services of the entity, since, prior to that sending, another treatment would be taking place, consisting of the elaboration of profiles based on the information available to the entity.

In the same way, it should be possible for the interested party to oppose this treatment even if they have not opposed receiving other types of offers from the entity.

For all these reasons, it would be possible to justify under Article 6.1 f) of the General Data Protection Regulation the treatment by the entity of the information it holds regarding the products and services contracted by its clients to assess their solvency for the purpose of offering them new products that involve financing, provided that the client has been duly informed about this treatment and has, in any case, the possibility to specifically exercise their right to oppose it.

3. Conclusion.
For all the above, this Legal Office considers, as has the General Subdirectorate of GDPR, that the processing of personal data by the controllers in positive files cannot be justified by the legitimate interest of the controller or the third party, as the interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of the affected individuals prevail.
As already indicated, the national legislator has neither regulated positive files as a case of public interest (as it has done, for example, regarding processing for surveillance purposes, advertising exclusion systems, or internal whistleblowing information systems in Articles 22, 23, and 24 of the LOPDGDD), nor has it established a presumption, iuris tantum, of the prevalence of legitimate interest, identifying the appropriate requirements and guarantees.
And in the absence of legal regulation, what is intended in the code of conduct is to achieve a similar effect to Article 20 of the LOPDGDD, establishing in general a presumption of legality that is supported by this Agency, overlooking, as has been analyzed in detail, the significant differences that exist between different files, taking into account the current regulations and the greater complexity and interference in the interests, rights, and freedoms of the affected individuals that positive files imply.
On the other hand, as has been pointed out in the report, the need to specify the contribution of these files, in general, to the stability of the financial system, as well as the guarantee of participation by the largest number of entities and, where appropriate, the limitation of the right to object, would require that this be established by a norm with the rank of law, and that this also specifies all the necessary guarantees after a rigorous analysis of the risks they entail. Furthermore, it should be clearly specified whether the possible existence of such files is limited to those owned by the public, such as the CIRBE, or whether the existence of privately owned files with a general scope, as proposed in the code of conduct, is also accepted.
At this point, in the event that the competent authorities consider it essential to consult such privately owned files as a means of ensuring the stability of the financial system, and after weighing all the interests involved, it should be regulated precisely and in detail, in addition to the obligation to communicate credits by the granting entities, the information that must be incorporated into the files, the guarantees for its accuracy, the conditions under which it would be accessible to other entities, the entities that may access it, the retention periods, the exercise of the rights of the affected individuals, and any other guarantees deemed appropriate, in order to adequately protect the fundamental right to data protection, with the desirable outcome being that the corresponding processing is legitimized on the legal basis of compliance with a legal obligation, under Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR.
And, until the law is approved clarifying these issues, the legitimate interest of the controller or third parties cannot be considered prevailing over the interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of the affected individuals to legitimize the processing of personal data by the controllers of credit information systems with data related to compliance with monetary, financial, or credit obligations (positive solvency files).

Background: “publicly accessible sources”.
As is known, Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, incorporated the concept of publicly accessible sources that was already contained in Article 1.3 of Royal Decree 1332/1994, of June 20, which develops certain aspects of Organic Law 5/1992, of October 29, regulating the automated processing of personal data, with the aim of limiting the cases in which the legitimate interest referred to in Article 7.f) of Directive 95/46/EC would apply, as an exception to the general principle of consent, stating in Article 6.2 that “Consent shall not be necessary […] when the data is found in publicly accessible sources and its processing is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party to whom the data is communicated, provided that the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject are not violated […]”.
88

                                                           Legal Office
For these purposes, publicly accessible sources were defined in Article 3.j) as “those files whose consultation can be carried out by any person, not hindered by a restrictive norm or without further requirements than, where appropriate, the payment of a fee. The following are considered public access sources: the promotional census, telephone directories as provided for by their specific regulations, and lists of individuals belonging to professional groups that contain only the data of name, title, profession, activity, academic degree, address, and indication of their membership in the group. Likewise, official journals and bulletins and media outlets are considered public access sources,” with this Agency considering that such enumeration was exhaustive (Report 2/2011). 
However, this situation changed following the Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union (Third Chamber) of November 24, 2011 (Case National Association of Credit Financial Establishments (ASNEF), already cited, which considered the requirement that the data be found in publicly accessible sources in order to assess the legitimate interest contrary to the directive, declaring that “Article 7, letter f), of Directive 95/46/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of October 24, 1995, on the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, must be interpreted as opposing a national regulation which, in order to allow the processing of personal data necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data is communicated, requires, in the absence of consent from the data subject, not only that the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject are respected, but also that such data be found in publicly accessible sources, thus categorically and generally excluding any processing of data not found in such sources” and recognizing that Article 7.f) has direct effect. By virtue of this judgment, the Supreme Court, in its ruling of February 8, 2012, annulled Article 10.2.b) of Royal Decree 1720/2007, of December 21, which approves the Regulation for the development of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the protection of personal data (“The data subject to processing or transfer must be found in publicly accessible sources and the data controller, or the third party to whom the data is communicated, must have a legitimate interest for its processing or knowledge, provided that the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject are not violated. However, public administrations may only communicate under this section the data collected from publicly accessible sources to private file holders when authorized to do so by a law”).

Notwithstanding this annulment, this Agency understood that the concept of publicly accessible sources persisted, as reasoned in report 178/2012:
On the other hand, it is undeniable that the Supreme Court ruling has annulled the provisions of Article 10.2 b) of the Regulation implementing the Organic Law, but this does not necessarily imply that the legal concept of publicly accessible sources has been completely displaced from the framework established by data protection norms.
Firstly, because this concept, both in the wording established by Organic Law 15/1999 and in the clarification made by Article 7 of the implementing Regulation, remains in force, without these provisions having been subject to any assessment by the Supreme Court. Moreover, and secondly, it cannot be doubted that the concept of publicly accessible sources as a criterion that will allow for the balancing required by Article 7 f) of Directive 95/46/EC has been expressly recognized by the frequently cited ruling of November 24, 2011, whose paragraph 44 states that “with regard to the balancing required by Article 7, letter f), of Directive 95/46, it is appropriate to take into consideration the fact that the severity of the infringement of the fundamental rights of the person affected by such processing may vary depending on whether the data is already, or is not, included in publicly accessible sources,” adding in paragraph 45 that “Indeed, unlike data processing that appears in publicly accessible sources, data processing that appears in non-publicly accessible sources necessarily implies that the data controller and, where applicable, the third party or parties to whom the data is communicated will subsequently have certain information about the private life of the data subject. This more serious infringement of the rights of the data subject enshrined in Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter must be assessed at its true value, weighing it against the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data is communicated.”

In the same vein, the ruling of the National Court of May 31, 2012, when referring to the balancing provided for in Article 7 f) of the Directive states the following:
“Balancing of conflicting interests that will depend on the specific circumstances of each case and in which, however, it may be taken into consideration, for the purposes of determining the possible infringement of the fundamental rights of the affected party, the fact that the data is already, or is not, included in publicly accessible sources. However, this is simply one more element of consideration.
It is possible, ultimately, and in accordance with this Community Jurisprudence, that there are personal data processing activities that do not appear in one of the sources that our internal legislation refers to as 'public access sources' (Article 3.f) LOPD and Article 7 RLOPD) but that, nevertheless, do not require the consent of the data subjects because their processing is necessary to satisfy a legitimate interest of the data controller or the assignee, provided that the rights and freedoms of the data subject are respected.”
Thus, the effect produced by the ruling on the applicable data protection norms and on the interpretation of the same that this Agency must carry out implies that, firstly, it will not be possible to consider that a specific processing that does not fall within one of the other legitimization cases established by Article 6 of Organic Law 15/1999 is contrary to said provision solely on the basis that the data is not collected from publicly accessible sources; but, secondly, the legitimization of such processing will not proceed in all cases, given that it will always be essential to carry out an adequate balancing between the legitimate interest pursued by the controller or the assignee and the rights of the affected parties, and in particular their right to data protection.
Therefore, according to the doctrine of this Agency, the fact that the data appeared in publicly accessible sources persisted, albeit as one more element of the necessary balancing that must be carried out by the data controller, taking into account the nature of the data and the source of the information, and always in accordance with the circumstances of the specific case.

The Judgment of the Court of Justice has expressly declared the direct effect of article 7 f) of Directive 95/46/EC, according to which “Member States shall provide that the processing of personal data may only be carried out if (...) it is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data are communicated, provided that the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject requiring protection under paragraph 1 of article 1 of this Directive do not prevail.” Therefore, this provision must be taken directly into account in the application of personal data protection regulations by Member States, and consequently by this Spanish Agency for Data Protection, since as the Supreme Court points out in its judgment of February 8, 2012, “it produces immediate legal effects without the need for national rules for its application, and therefore can be invoked before administrative and judicial authorities when its transgression is observed.”
As the Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union recalls in paragraph 38, article 7 f) of the Directive “establishes two cumulative requirements for the processing of personal data to be lawful, namely, on the one hand, that such processing of personal data is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data are communicated, and, on the other hand, that the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail” and, in relation to the aforementioned balancing, paragraph 40 recalls that it “will depend, in principle, on the specific circumstances of the particular case in question and in which the person or institution carrying out the balancing must take into account the importance of the rights that

For this reason, the judgment states in paragraph 46 that Member States, when adapting their legal systems to Directive 95/46, must “seek to base themselves on an interpretation of this that allows them to guarantee a fair balance between the different rights and fundamental freedoms protected by the legal system of the Union,” and, according to paragraph 47, “nothing prevents Member States from establishing the principles that should govern such balancing, in exercising the margin of appreciation conferred upon them by Article 5 of Directive 95/46.”
Therefore, to determine whether the application of the aforementioned provision is appropriate, the balancing rule provided for therein must be applied; that is to say, it will be necessary to assess whether in the specific case under analysis there exists a legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data is communicated that prevails over the interest or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject that require protection in accordance with the provisions of Article 1 of the LOPD, which states that “this Organic Law aims to guarantee and protect, concerning the processing of personal data, the public freedoms and fundamental rights of individuals, and especially their honor and personal and family privacy,” or whether, on the contrary, such fundamental rights or interests of the data subjects referred to in the data processing must prevail over the legitimate interest on which the data controller intends to base the processing of personal data.
Thus, for the purpose of carrying out the necessary balancing required by the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice, it must be considered whether, taking into account the specific circumstances that arise in the present case, the interest alleged by the consultant to process certain personal data should prevail over the right to data protection of the affected individuals whose data is subject to such processing.
Article 6.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR), is in force but not yet applicable—although its provisions may be considered and evaluated for the scope of the issues raised, STS July 21, 2016, among others—establishes a similar regulation of legitimate interest to that established by Directive 95/46/EC. Thus, two conditions are required: a) that the processing is “necessary” for the satisfaction of the legitimate interests of the data controller, and b) that such interests prevail over the “interests” or the “fundamental rights and freedoms” of the data subject that require the protection of personal data.
The AEPD considers that the personal data whose processing the consultant intends is not “necessary.” One must not confuse necessity with convenience. The assessment of economic solvency could be considered, according to the terms of the consultation, convenient, but in no case necessary for the contracting of insurance. According to this doctrine, the Supreme Court requires that in cases where the controller uses “legitimate interest” as a basis, the data subject must have had the opportunity to “expressly object,” as stated, among others, in Judgment 158.

This Chamber has repeatedly stated that it is necessary to ensure that the demand is made in such a way that there is proof of its receipt by the recipient, as the presentation of a letter, regarding which there is no record of its receipt, does not fulfill the aforementioned requirement, considering that the purpose of knowing the existence of the debt is to proceed with payment (or at least, for the debtor to be aware of the existence of such debt and to be able to react to it).

Regarding the demand, the Chamber has reiterated (SSAN, Section 1, of May 9, 2003, Rec. 1067/1999; March 8, 2006, Rec. 319/2004; July 18, 2007, Rec. 17/2006; May 28, 2008, Rec. 107/2007; February 17, 2011, Rec. 177/2010; September 20, 2012, Rec. 127/2011 and many others), that when the affected party denies the existence of such demand, the burden of proving compliance with this obligation falls on the data controller or processor, and the computer records of the entity itself are insufficient for this purpose, as they do not provide evidence of the effective execution or fulfillment of the aforementioned obligation.

Thus, we have pointed out that the regulations do not indeed require that the demand be carried out in a specific manner or that it be sent by certified mail with acknowledgment of receipt, but what is required is that whoever reports the data to a delinquency file must ensure that all legally and regulatory required conditions are met for this purpose, which implies that they must be in a position, if necessary, to prove the fulfillment of these requirements (SAN of March 35, 2010, Rec. 407/2009, among others).
