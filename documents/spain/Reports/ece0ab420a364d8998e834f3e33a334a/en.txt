Having examined your request for a report, submitted to this Legal Office, regarding the Draft Royal Decree creating the Register of Beneficial Ownership and approving its Regulations, requested from this Spanish Agency for Data Protection (AEPD) in accordance with the provisions of Article 47 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the protection of personal data and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), in relation to Article 57.1, letter c), of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data, and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), and 5 b) of the Statute of the Agency, approved by Royal Decree 389/2021, of June 1, I am obliged to inform you of the following:  

The cited Draft Royal Decree originates from Articles 30 and 31 of Directive (EU) 2015/849 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of May 20, 2015, on the prevention of the use of the financial system for money laundering or terrorist financing, and amending Regulation (EU) No 648/2012 of the European Parliament and of the Council, and repealing Directive 2005/60/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council and Directive 2006/70/EC of the Commission (IV Directive), as amended by Directive (EU) 2018/843 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of May 30, 2018, which amends Directive (EU) 2015/849 on the prevention of the use of the financial system for money laundering or terrorist financing, and amending Directives 2009/138/EC and 2013/36/EU (V Directive). These provisions regulate the necessity to establish in each Member State a "central register" that allows access to certain adequate, accurate, and up-to-date information regarding the beneficial ownership of companies and other legal entities, including details of the beneficial interests held, as well as certain information relating to the beneficial ownership of trusts (of the "trust" type) and other similar legal instruments.  

These provisions were transposed in Spain by Royal Decree-Law 7/2021, of April 27, which amended Article 4 and added Articles 4 bis and 4 ter, and Additional Provisions Three and Four, of Law 10/2010, of April 28, on the prevention of money laundering and terrorist financing. Article 4 is titled Identification of the Beneficial Owner; Article 4 bis is titled Information on the Beneficial Ownership of Legal Entities; and Article 4 ter is titled Information on the Beneficial Ownership of Trusts and Other Analogous Legal Instruments. Additionally, Additional Provision Three is called Register of Beneficial Ownership, and Additional Provision Four is titled Access to the Register of Beneficial Ownership.  

This Agency, in its Report 83/2020, had the opportunity to express its opinion on the Draft Law that amended Law 10/2010, of April 28, on the prevention of money laundering and terrorist financing (hereinafter "Law 10/2010" or LPBC) to adapt it to these provisions. However, the LPBC was not amended by that Draft Law; rather, given the extraordinary and urgent need to proceed with the transposition of certain Directives before formal complaints were filed by the European Commission before the Court of Justice of the EU for having exceeded the deadline for their transposition, this transposition was carried out by means of a Decree Law (Royal Decree-Law 7/2021, of April 27, transposing European Union directives in the areas of competition, prevention of money laundering, credit institutions, telecommunications, tax measures, prevention and remediation of environmental damage, posting of workers in the provision of transnational services, and consumer protection). The wording of the transposed regulations does not coincide in some aspects with that contained in the Draft Law presented for Report. This AEPD refers to the cited Report 83/2020, and when necessary for the purposes of the Draft Royal Decree now submitted for Report, will refer, as appropriate, to its content.

Both Directive V and the LPBC, in transposing this aspect, establish that the purpose of this Central Register is to disclose the data on the beneficial ownership of companies and other legal entities or trusts. From the perspective of personal data protection regulations, its purpose must be exclusively that, which would justify the interference of the treatments provided for in these regulations in the fundamental right to the protection of personal data of the interested parties. In our Report 83/2020, we already extensively recalled the doctrine of the Constitutional Court and the CJEU regarding the fact that interferences in the aforementioned fundamental right must not only be established by law, but that this law must establish the necessary guarantees. The Constitutional Court has had the opportunity to examine the requirements for laws that establish personal data treatments, as restrictions on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data of the interested party, to be considered in accordance with the Constitution. Thus, this constitutional doctrine can be summarized in the ruling of the Constitutional Court (STC) 76/2019, of May 22. This ruling contains the relevant doctrine regarding the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and addresses both the characteristics and the content that the regulations intending to establish an interference in that fundamental right must have. (…) By express mandate of the Constitution, any state interference in the area of fundamental rights and public freedoms, whether it directly affects their development (Article 81.1 CE) or limits or conditions their exercise (Article 53.1 CE), requires a legal authorization (see, among others, STC 49/1999, of April 5, FJ 4). (…) This dual function of the legal reserve translates into a double requirement: on the one hand, the necessary intervention of the law to authorize the interference; and, on the other hand, that legal norm "must meet all those indispensable characteristics as a guarantee of legal certainty," that is, "it must express each and every one of the assumptions and conditions of the intervention" (STC 49/1999, FJ 4). In other words, "it not only excludes delegations in favor of regulatory norms [...], but also implies other requirements regarding the content of the Law that establishes such limits" (STC 292/2000, FJ 15). Furthermore, such law must respect, in any case, the principle of proportionality, as recalled by the Judgment of the Constitutional Court 14/2003, of January 28:

II
In paragraph 2 of Article 2 of the Regulation, it is established that the person in charge of the General Directorate of Legal Security and Public Faith will be in charge of the Register. In turn, Article 8, letters f) and g) of the Regulation
Legal Office
establishes that f) the responsible party for the Register of Beneficial Owners is the Ministry of Justice, and g) the entity in charge of processing the data of the Register of Beneficial Owners is the General Directorate of Legal Security and Public Faith. Furthermore, the Fourth Additional Provision, section 5, of the LPBC, when mentioning the person in charge of the General Directorate of Legal Security and Public Faith, refers to them as “the person in charge of the Register,” not as “the person in charge of processing.”

In the opinion of this Agency, there is a certain confusion between terms that have a specific and technical meaning in personal data protection regulations, as the expression “data processor” is specifically defined in the GDPR, whereas “register keeper” is not. This AEPD, in its Report 83/2020, already cited, highlighted this issue:

Firstly, the 3rd Additional Provision assigns the “management” of the central unique Register to the Ministry of Justice, thus considering it responsible for the processing of personal data, and therefore subject to the obligations established by the GDPR, including maintaining the corresponding record of processing activities, providing information to data subjects, access, rectification, etc. The provision does not mention this circumstance, so it should do so for the complete regulation of personal data processing.

Thus, there is no doubt that the LPBC designates the “Ministry of Justice” as the responsible party for processing. Nothing prevents the law from doing so (see Recital 45 and art. 6.3 GDPR) when the basis for processing is compliance with a legal obligation or a task carried out in the public interest (art. 6.1.c) and d) GDPR), as is the case.

However, this AEPD considers that the LPBC, by establishing that “the person in charge of the General Directorate of Legal Security and Public Faith - DGSJyFP - will be the person in charge of the Register” (Fourth Additional Provision), is not referring to them as “the data processor,” in the sense of the technical-legal definition of art. 4.8 GDPR (natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller), but rather as an organ of the Ministry of Justice to whom, precisely because they are an integral part of that Ministry, the “management” of the Register is entrusted.

If the DGSJFP were a true “data processor,” in the technical-legal sense of the GDPR, the regulation being reported should comply with the requirements of art. 28 GDPR, and more specifically with art. 28.3 GDPR (and 33.2 LOPDGDD), so that this “data processing assignment” should be reflected in a contract “or other legal act” (for example, this same regulation being reported) with the stipulations a) to h) of art. 28.3 GDPR.

In summary, this AEPD considers that in the present case the DGSJyFP is not a “data processor” in the sense of the GDPR, so that reference in art. 8.g) of the Regulation should be removed. The responsible party for processing is the Ministry of Justice, through its own organs, such as the DGSJyFP. The expression “register keeper” does not have a meaning in data protection regulations, but rather pertains to mere administrative organization, and as such, it will be included in the corresponding modification of the basic organic structure of the Ministry of Justice to reflect these competencies.

A different matter, and already anticipating future events, is that, in the future, and as described in the MAIN (p. 31 et seq.), there will be true data processors, such as those who, through service contracts, carry out maintenance, development, and CAU tasks for the RETIR application, or (see p. 29 MAIN), those who, by virtue of a possible “assignment to a public entity,” carry out functions of digitization, archiving, consolidation of files, management of resolutions, recording of powers of attorney, and preparation of documentation for its definitive archiving in the CADA, without belonging to the Ministry of Justice.

III
In art. 2.4 of the Regulation, the possibility of an appeal for resolutions made by the person in charge of the Register of Beneficial Owners regarding “denial of access” is established. 

This AEPD merely wishes to note that this reference to “denial of access” and its appeals regime pertains to the possibility of third parties accessing the data of the Register to know its content, but does not refer to the “right of access” specific to personal data protection regulations, regulated in art. 15 GDPR and 13 LOPDGDD, by which data subjects have the right to obtain confirmation from the data controller as to whether or not personal data concerning them is being processed, the appeals regime of which is specifically regulated in the LOPDGDD in arts. 64 and following (see art. 64.1 LOPDGDD).

VI
 In Article 6.1 of the draft, it is required that the applicant for access to information not only identify themselves but also provide certain information about the legal entity whose information is requested, such as the Tax Identification Number (NIF) of the legal entity, and the registration details in the Competent Registry. This information may not be accessible to the general public, or they may simply be unaware of it. Furthermore, it is not clarified what it means for the applicant to provide “the registration details in the Competent Registry,” nor which Competent Registry is being referred to. It seems as if to request information from the RETIR, one would need to know in advance the registration details of that entity in the RETIR itself, which would not make sense. It is considered that these requirements constitute a restriction on the scope of information provided by the RETIR.
 Section 4 of Article 6 of the draft requires the manifestation of a legitimate interest when the information requested from the RETIR is related to a property. Directive 2015/849 does not seem to make such a distinction, requiring legitimate interest to access information about a property. As already mentioned in our Report 83/2020, legitimate interest is required by Directive 2015/849 when it comes to trust-type fiduciaries (Article 31.4.c), but not in other cases.
 Section 5 of Article 6 regulates that public information from registries with competencies regarding beneficial owners can be requested, but limiting its content to what is provided in this Regulation. This Agency considers that this section should be reconsidered, as firstly its regulation could incur a cause for nullity, since it cannot be ruled out that some of these registries (as the provision does not mention any) are regulated by a law, and secondly, the legislator has already considered in the regulation of each of those registries (which, it is reiterated, are not specified) what circumstances can be accessed. Therefore, this section, due to its ambiguity, should be removed, without prejudice to the fact that the legislator, in whatever regulation governs the registries referred to, may establish, in each case (and not through a general norm) what circumstances must be made public.
