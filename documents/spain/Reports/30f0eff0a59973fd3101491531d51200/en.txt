The inquiry raises whether the communication—by the General Directorate of Traffic to vehicle sharing companies—of the verification data in a traffic light format (red/green) is compliant with the provisions of data protection regulations, in order to allow these companies to verify the validity and currency of the driving license or permit of those requesting to drive one of their vehicles, without the need to obtain the express consent of the interested party.  

For these purposes, the consulting body points out the possibility that the legitimizing basis of Article 6.1, letters b) and/or c), of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation - GDPR), may apply to the aforementioned data communication.  

In summary, the question arises as to what the legal basis for legitimization for such processing would be, which would enable the corresponding transfer of personal data, invoking the need to implement a verification mechanism that ensures that the user, at the moment they intend to drive the vehicle offered by a shared mobility company, holds a fully valid driving permit or license, avoiding inaccuracies and omissions aimed at evading the control of the same and compliance with its validity and currency requirements.  

According to the consulting body, the aforementioned verification raises the possibility of establishing a system of interconnection between rental or vehicle sharing companies and the DGT, whereby the latter would automatically give the "green light" to the company to allow the customer to drive the vehicle, as there would be no impediment, whatever its legal nature, to granting validity to the driving authorization.

Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation - GDPR), and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights — LOPDGDD — constitute the legal framework of reference in Spain that affects the protection of personal data.  

These regulations govern the principles and foundations to which the collection and processing of personal data by any public or private entity that carries out personal data processing in the exercise of its activity must adhere, without prejudice to existing special rules for certain activities or processing, such as personal data processing subject to Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offenses or enforcement of criminal sanctions, and the free movement of such data, which repeals Framework Decision 2008/977/JAI of the Council, transposed into Spanish law by Organic Law 7/2021, of May 26.  

First of all, it should be noted that Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  

In turn, according to the content of the “Definition” in point 2 of Article 4 of the GDPR, for there to be processing of personal data, it is required to carry out “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.” For its part, the definition of “file” is contained in section 6 of Article 4, which refers to “any structured set of personal data, accessible according to specific criteria, whether centralized, decentralized, or distributed in a functional or geographical manner.”

The Consolidated Text of the Law on Traffic, Vehicle Circulation, and Road Safety, approved by Royal Legislative Decree 6/2015, of October 30, hereinafter LSV, establishes in its article 61:
“1.- The driving of motor vehicles and mopeds will require prior acquisition of the necessary driving permit or license, aimed at verifying that the driver has the capacity, knowledge, and skills required for driving the vehicle in the terms determined by regulation.
2.- The permit and the driving license may have a limited validity period, the terms of which may be reviewed as determined by regulation.
3.- Its validity will also be conditioned on the fact that its holder has not lost the assigned points credit.”
In light of the aforementioned provision, it is obvious that in the case subject to consultation, there will be processing of personal data in the terms of article 4, paragraphs 1 and 2, of the GDPR.
Regarding the legally attributed competence for the issuance and review of driving permits and licenses, article 5 —Competencies of the Ministry of the Interior— of the aforementioned LSV, provides in its letter a) that “(...) it is the responsibility of the Ministry of the Interior: The issuance and review of permits and licenses to drive motor vehicles and mopeds and the special authorization to drive vehicles that transport dangerous goods, with the requirements regarding knowledge, technical and psychophysical aptitudes, and periodicity determined by regulation, as well as the declaration of nullity, detriment, or loss of validity of the same,” indicating in paragraph 1 of its article 6 —Autonomous Agency Central Traffic Headquarters— that “The Ministry of the Interior exercises the competencies related in the previous article through the autonomous agency Central Traffic Headquarters.”
In this regard, article 2 of Royal Decree 818/2009, of May 8, which approves the General Regulation of Drivers —RGC—, related to the competence to issue driving permits and licenses, develops the previous provisions, stating that “The driving permits and licenses, as well as the administrative authorizations that temporarily replace them, will be issued by the Provincial Traffic Headquarters, except for those that authorize the driving of vehicles of the Armed Forces or the General Directorate of Police and the Civil Guard.”

II
At this point, it should be noted that data protection regulations contemplate different legal bases for legitimization that may lead to the processing of personal data. According to Article 6 – “Lawfulness of processing” – of the General Data Protection Regulation, such processing is lawful, and therefore legitimate when:
“1.   Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:
a)  the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes; 
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or for the implementation at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures; 
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject; (the bold is ours)
d) the processing is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person; 
e)  the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller; (the bold is ours)
f)  the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, provided that such interests do not override the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child. 
The provision in letter f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions.”
This Spanish Agency for Data Protection has had the opportunity to analyze the legal basis for data processing by public administrations in its Legal Reports 108/2018, 155/2018, and 175/2018. In these reports, it is stated that:

"It should be noted that the Administration is bound by the principle of legality, such that, unlike individuals, it can only carry out what the legal system expressly allows. This is the meaning of the provisions in Articles 9.1 and 103 of the Constitution, so that when the law and the rights have not attributed to the Administration the corresponding powers to act in a given situation, that action cannot be carried out unless the legal system previously grants it those powers. Therefore, there is no empty space where the Administration can act in the absence of law. This is what has been termed the doctrine of the positive binding of the Administration to legality (García de Enterría). Consequently, for the Administration to act, it requires prior legal authorization (understood here as normative authorization). This applies whether the Administration acts in the realm of public law or in the realm of private law. Thus, we can see that even in the latter realm, where it would act on the same level as individuals, the Administration needs a norm that enables it to act.
(…)
The data processing that the Administration needs to carry out for this purpose would, in principle, have as its legitimizing legal basis the very basis established in Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR, that is, that the processing must be necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest, since the Administration is not only positively bound to the law and rights in such a way that it cannot act without it or behind its back, but for that same reason, all its actions are guided, in broad terms, by the public interest, even if its actions take place in the realm of private law.
(…)
In any case, the GDPR excludes the possibility that the processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions (without distinguishing whether such functions are subject to public or private law) can have as its legal basis for processing the letter f) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, that is, legitimate interest. This is undoubtedly because it is based on the premise, as demonstrated by Recital 45 of the GDPR, that when data processing is carried out in compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller or if it is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers, such processing must have a basis in the law of the Union or of the Member States. In short, such data processing will be covered by letter e) of Article 6.1 if the applicable law has attributed a competence to the Administration, and it will not be covered otherwise.
(…)”
Thus, the Agency in its Report 0175/2018 states that “In general, the legal basis for processing in relations with the Administration, in those cases where there is a relationship in which it cannot reasonably be said that there is a situation of balance between the data controller (the Administration) and the data subject (the administered) would not be consent (Article 6.1.a) of the GDPR), but, depending on the cases, the fulfillment of a legal obligation (Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR) or the fulfillment of a task of public interest or in the exercise of public powers (Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR).” 
In the present case, the concurrence of the basis of Article 6.1.c) is ruled out, as there is no legal obligation that supports the processing.

In seeking the configuration of the service and the transfer of personal information exclusively on a contractual basis —namely, the hiring of shared vehicle use (sharing)— it is necessary to analyze the possible concurrence of the basis set forth in Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR. 
In this regard, Article 8 of the LOPDGDD, titled “Processing of data by legal obligation, public interest or exercise of public powers,” states in its section 2 that:
“2. The processing of personal data may only be considered based on the fulfillment of a mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred to the controller, under the terms provided in Article 6.1(e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when it derives from a competence attributed by a norm with the rank of law.”
Therefore, for the processing to be covered by Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR, it is required that a norm with the rank of law attributes the competence, which, in accordance with the principle of positive binding of the Administration to the law, may only be exercised in the terms and within the limits in which it has been attributed by such legal norm and, where applicable, by the regulatory provisions that develop it. 
Well, in relation to the requirement for a specific legal provision in the traffic safety regulations —specifically in the LSV— regarding the registration of drivers and offenders, and the competencies of the Central Traffic Headquarters concerning the issuance, review, and control of driving permits and licenses, we can conclude that such provision is not explicitly contained in that formal law, nor is it incorporated into its regulatory development —Royal Decree 818/2009, of May 8, which approves the RGC— that regulates the aforementioned permits and licenses, thus failing to meet the necessary requirement for the application of what is provided in Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR.
Furthermore, it should be noted that the voluntary nature of the use of a vehicle offered by a company in the shared mobility sector, and the hypothetical performance of the corresponding data processing within the framework of its collaboration with the Central Traffic Headquarters, excludes the effective application of the legal basis of Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR, since the protection of the public interest or the exercise of public powers cannot depend on voluntariness or the autonomy of the will of the parties. Moreover, such voluntariness —stemming from an agreement of wills regarding the verification of the requirements demanded by the applicable regulations for driving vehicles— is not provided for in the regulations governing the actions of the JCT, so there would be a lack, as seen, of the norm with the rank of law that legitimizes such processing, given that the

Regarding consent —the legal basis for data processing under article 6.1 a) of the GDPR—, as this Agency has generally indicated, the legal basis for processing in relations with the Administration, in those cases where there is a relationship in which it cannot reasonably be argued that there is a situation of balance between the data controller (the Administration) and the data subject (the administered), would not be consent (art. 6.1.a) GDPR), but, depending on the cases, the fulfillment of a legal obligation (art. 6.1.c) GDPR) or the fulfillment of a mission of public interest or in the exercise of public powers (art. 6.1.e) GDPR). 
Therefore, and although in data processing by Public Administrations consent from the affected parties cannot be excluded outright, it is exceptional, as it does not generally meet the requirement of being free. Thus, even hypothetically, it is appropriate to analyze the lack of concurrence of the requirements of said legitimizing basis in the case submitted for report. 
Therefore, it cannot be considered that, in the verification and checking activities raised in the consultation, based on an automated communication system between the JCT and the affected companies, there exists a relationship of balance between the Central Traffic Headquarters and the interested parties, and consequently, the legitimizing basis for the processing of personal data cannot be categorized under what is provided in letter a) of article 6.1 of the GDPR, since consent would not be free. Likewise, it would not be demonstrated that the other necessary requirements for valid consent are met, such as that the consent is informed, specific, and unequivocal. 
Furthermore, taking into account the definition of “consent” in article 4.11 of the GDPR, and the requirements pertaining to the freedom of its granting and the balance of the parties —as provided in Recitals 42 and 43 of the GDPR— in the case subject to report, the due conditions would not be met.

IV
Finally, from a technological perspective, it is worth mentioning that, recently, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) has proceeded to review and publish a new version of its Guidelines 1/2020 on the processing of personal data in the context of connected vehicles and related mobility applications, which were adopted on January 28, 2020, and whose latest version dates from March 9, 2021. As noted in these Guidelines, this new type of data processing is characterized by its special complexity, as it is not limited to the involvement of traditional actors in the automotive industry, but is complemented by the emergence of new agents belonging to the digital economy.

In section 1.5 of these Guidelines, emphasis is placed on the risks to privacy and data protection arising from the new systems and interconnection functionalities. The evolution of the so-called "Internet of Things" particularly impacts privacy in the context of connected vehicles. Thus, issues related to the security and control of personal data are even more delicate in this context, as they entail concerns regarding road safety and can affect the physical integrity of the driver, in an environment traditionally perceived as isolated and protected from external interference.

In these Guidelines, it is also noted that drivers and passengers of vehicles may not always be adequately informed about the processing of data that takes place in or through a connected vehicle. Generally, the information is provided to the vehicle owner, who may not be the driver, and, in general, such information may not be provided in real-time and in a convenient manner. Therefore, there is a risk that there are not enough functionalities or options offered to exercise the necessary control for affected individuals to take advantage of their data protection and privacy rights. This point is important as, during their useful life, vehicles may belong to more than one owner, either because they are sold or because they are leased. Vehicles are shared or rented, not only by companies but also by individuals, and the

“1. A single assessment may address a series of similar processing operations that entail similar high risks.”
However, as has been explained, the processing of personal data referred to in this consultation could be contrary to the provisions of personal data protection regulations, as it does not currently comply with the legal bases for legitimacy under Article 6 of the GDPR. 
Consequently, to carry out the aforementioned processing, it would be necessary — prima facie — to proceed with the appropriate modification of the Consolidated Text of the Law on Traffic, Motor Vehicle Circulation, and Road Safety, approved by Royal Legislative Decree 6/2015, of October 30, in order to accommodate the communication of information containing personal data included in driving permits and/or licenses for the purpose described in the consultation.
In turn, in the documentation attached to said legal modification, and, in particular, in the Analysis Report of the corresponding regulatory project, a complete examination of the various functionalities pursued by the consulting body should be carried out — with special mention of the technical and organizational measures to be adopted to prevent access to other data contained in the Register of Drivers and Offenders by unauthorized third parties, which, if applicable, would serve to evaluate the impact of the processing — ensuring due compliance with the information duty established in Article 13 of the GDPR, and guaranteeing full respect for the data protection principles of Article 5 of the GDPR, with special mention of the principle of “purpose limitation” of processing in its paragraph 1.b).
