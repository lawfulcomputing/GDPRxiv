At the time of formulating the inquiry, it must be based on the new regime established by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) and in Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights.  

Indeed, as indicated in the Explanatory Memorandum of Law 3/2018, “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the data controller or the processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.”  

Therefore, it is the data controller who must comply with the principles set forth in Article 5 of the GDPR, among which is, as noted, the principle of proactive responsibility, included in its paragraph 2, “the data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and able to demonstrate it (‘proactive responsibility’).” And among the principles of paragraph 1 is that of “lawfulness,” included in its letter a), so that personal data shall be processed lawfully, with Article 6 regulating the legal bases that determine the lawfulness of the processing. Therefore, it is the responsibility of the data controller to determine the legal basis that may support the corresponding processing.  

A fundamental role, finally, within this new model of active responsibility established in the General Data Protection Regulation will be played by the data protection officer, which the General Regulation governs in Articles 37 to 39. In particular, Article 37.1 a) mandates the designation of a data protection officer in cases where “the processing is carried out by a public authority or body, except for courts acting in their judicial capacity.”

Finally, Article 39.1 lists the functions of the data protection officer, which include “informing and advising the controller or the processor and the employees involved in the processing of the obligations that they have under this Regulation and other Union or Member State data protection provisions” (paragraph a), “monitoring compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, other Union or Member State data protection provisions, and the policies of the controller or processor regarding personal data protection, including the allocation of responsibilities, awareness and training of staff involved in processing operations, and the corresponding audits” (paragraph b), and “providing the requested advice regarding the data protection impact assessment and supervising its implementation in accordance with Article 35” (paragraph c).

It is also the responsibility of the data protection officer “to act as a contact point for the supervisory authority on issues related to processing, including the prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and to consult, where appropriate, on any other matter” (paragraph e).

Therefore, if the controller has doubts about the legal basis that may determine the lawfulness of a particular processing operation, they must consult their data protection officer in cases where, as in this instance, their appointment is mandatory, who must provide the necessary advice. 

Only in the event that the data protection officer has legal doubts regarding the matter submitted for their consideration that cannot be resolved with the criteria already provided by the AEPD or due to new issues arising from the application of the new legal framework for the protection of personal data that have a general scope in which a report that contributes to legal certainty is deemed appropriate, may the data protection officer elevate such consultation to this Legal Office, accompanying the consultation with their own report in which the issues subject to consultation are analyzed in detail and with justification.

In this case, the consultation is formulated by the DPO but is limited to a description of the facts that, regarding the facial recognition system through the comparison of the photograph of the ID card or other identifying document, and the image taken by the user themselves through a selfie video or photograph of the user, should be detailed more precisely, accompanying the risk analysis and, where appropriate, the data protection impact assessment.

Regarding the possible legal bases for the processing, the consultation is limited solely to raising the question, so it must be completed with the corresponding motivated report in which the DPO includes their own conclusions and, especially, regarding the legitimate interest, conducts the corresponding balancing.
