II
The second issue to be analyzed is related to the regulation of the right to conscientious objection in Article 19 bis and the registry of conscientious objectors in Article 19 ter. 
The right to conscientious objection for healthcare professionals directly involved in voluntary termination of pregnancy was already provided for in the second paragraph of section 2 of Article 19 of Organic Law 2/2010, of March 3, on sexual and reproductive health and voluntary termination of pregnancy:
Healthcare professionals directly involved in voluntary termination of pregnancy shall have the right to exercise conscientious objection without the access and quality of care of the service being compromised by the exercise of conscientious objection. The refusal or denial to perform the termination of pregnancy for reasons of conscience is always an individual decision of the healthcare personnel directly involved in the voluntary termination of pregnancy, which must be expressed in advance and in writing. In any case, healthcare professionals shall provide appropriate treatment and medical attention to women who require it before and after undergoing a termination of pregnancy.
The submitted text proceeds to eliminate this regulation from Article 19 and introduces a new regulation in a more detailed manner in Article 19 bis, highlighting the need for the exercise of this right to be compatible with the rights and freedoms of women who decide to terminate their pregnancy and the necessity for public centers to be organized in such a way as to guarantee the necessary healthcare personnel for effective and timely access to voluntary termination of pregnancy:
“Article 19 bis. Conscientious objection.
Healthcare professionals directly involved in the practice of voluntary termination of pregnancy may exercise conscientious objection, without the exercise of this individual right undermining the human right to life, health, and freedom of women who decide to terminate their pregnancy.”

The rejection or refusal to perform the intervention for the interruption of pregnancy on grounds of conscience is always an individual decision of the healthcare personnel directly involved in carrying out the voluntary interruption of pregnancy, which must be expressed in advance and in writing. Access to or the quality of care provided will not be affected by the individual exercise of the right to conscientious objection. For these purposes, public services will always be organized in such a way as to ensure the necessary healthcare personnel for effective and timely access to the voluntary interruption of pregnancy. Likewise, all healthcare personnel will always provide appropriate treatment and medical attention to women who require it before and after undergoing an interruption of pregnancy.

However, the main novelty relates to the creation of registers of healthcare professionals who have exercised their right to conscientious objection, which is included in Article 19 ter:

“Article 19 ter. Register of conscientious objectors.
For organizational purposes and for adequate management of the service, a register of healthcare professionals who decide to object on grounds of conscience regarding direct intervention in the practice of voluntary interruption of pregnancy will be created in each autonomous community. Those who declare themselves conscientious objectors will be so for the purposes of the direct practice of the service of voluntary interruption of pregnancy both in the public and private healthcare sectors.”

And this is because the right to the protection of personal data, derived from art. 18.4 CE, guarantees individuals a power of disposition over their personal data "which imposes on public authorities the prohibition of becoming sources of that information without the necessary guarantees; and also the duty to prevent risks that may arise from the improper access or disclosure of such information" (STC 292/2000, FJ 6). Characteristic elements of the constitutional definition of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data include the rights of the affected individual to consent to the collection and use of their personal data and to be informed about them, "and to make that content effective, it is essential to recognize the right to be informed of who holds their personal data and for what purpose, and the right to oppose that possession and use by requiring the relevant party to cease the possession and use of the data. That is, demanding from the data controller that they inform them of what data they hold about them, accessing their relevant records and entries, and what has been done with that data, which also extends to potential assignees" (STC 292/2000, FJ 6).

Thus, the unconstitutionality of the indicated paragraph arises from the fact that the interested party must be informed, both of the possibility of the assignment of their personal data and of the specific destination of such data, "since only in this way will their right to consent be effective, as an essential power of their right to control and dispose of their personal data. For this, it is not enough to know that such assignment is possible according to the provision that has created or modified the file, but also the circumstances of each specific assignment. Otherwise, it would be easy for the data controller to circumvent the consent of the interested party through generic information that their data may be assigned" (STC 292/2000, FJ 13), thus frustrating the right of the interested party to control and dispose of their personal data, which derives from art. 18.4 CE and is specified in art. 11.1 LOPDCP when it states that personal data subject to processing "may only be communicated to a third party for the fulfillment of purposes directly related to the legitimate functions of the assignor and the assignee with the prior consent of the interested party," consent that will not be necessary when the assignment is authorized by law [art. 11.2 a) LOPDCP].

III
Based on the previous analysis, the main novelty of the text submitted regarding the exercise of the right to conscientious objection lies in the express provision, contained in article 19 ter, that Autonomous Communities create a registry of healthcare professionals who decide to object on grounds of conscience regarding direct intervention in the practice of voluntary termination of pregnancy, the purpose of which is to allow public health administrations, within the scope of their respective competencies, to comply with the obligations imposed by law to guarantee the provision in hospital centers, according to criteria of gratuity, accessibility, and proximity, establishing the necessary devices and human resources to guarantee the right throughout the territory under equitable conditions.

The constitutionality of the creation of these registries has been expressly recognized by the Constitutional Court in its Judgment 151/2014 of September 25, previously cited. However, from the perspective of the protection of personal data, some considerations must be made, starting from the fact that the existence of such registries constitutes a limitation on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and considering that the data related to conscientious objection would be regarded as special categories of personal data, in accordance with said regulations.
Legal Office
In this regard, Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) includes in its Article 9, among what it refers to as special categories of data, “political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs,” establishing as a general rule the prohibition of their processing. 

Therefore, the data related to the objection of conscience, insofar as it reflects the aforementioned opinions or beliefs, would be prohibited unless one of the circumstances provided for in paragraph 2 of Article 9 occurs that lifts this prohibition. 

In this respect, although previous reports from this Agency as well as the Constitutional Court itself referred to the existence of consent derived from the circumstance of exercising the right to objection of conscience, at this moment, the new regulation of consent as a legitimization of the processing of personal data contained in the GDPR must be taken into account, along with the way it is being interpreted by the European Data Protection Board, particularly regarding this report, concerning the inadmissibility of tacit or presumed consent, which was generally accepted under the validity of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, and the need to ensure freedom in its provision. 

To this end, one must start from the definition of the consent of the data subject contained in Article 4.11 of the GDPR: any manifestation of free, specific, informed, and unequivocal will by which the data subject accepts, either by means of a statement or a clear affirmative action, the processing of personal data concerning them, which is subject to detailed analysis by the European Data Protection Board in its Guidelines 5/2020 on consent in the sense of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 adopted on May 4, 2020. 

Thus, the necessity for consent to derive from a clear affirmative action, a requirement that is further reinforced by the express reference to it being explicit contained in Article 9.2 of the GDPR (“the data subject has given explicit consent to the processing of such personal data for one or more specified purposes, except where Union or Member State law provides that the prohibition referred to in paragraph 1 may not be lifted by the data subject”), excludes, in any case, the existence of implicit or presumed consent.

This constitutional doctrine is fundamentally captured in rulings 292/2000 of November 30 and 76/2019 of May 22, according to which the limits on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data must be established by a norm with the rank of law, following the legislator's weighing of conflicting interests in accordance with the principle of proportionality, defining each and every one of the material premises of the limiting measure through precise rules that make the imposition of such limitation and its consequences foreseeable to the interested party, so that such a norm with the rank of law "must meet all those indispensable characteristics as a guarantee of legal certainty," that is, "must express each and every one of the premises and conditions of the intervention" (STC 49/1999, FJ 4). In other words, "it not only excludes delegations in favor of regulatory norms [...], but also implies other requirements regarding the content of the Law that establishes such limits" (STC 292/2000, FJ 15).

Likewise, it must establish adequate guarantees, with the law itself containing the appropriate guarantees against the collection of personal data that it authorizes. The Constitutional Court (TC) has been clear that the provision of adequate guarantees cannot be deferred to a moment subsequent to the legal regulation of the processing of personal data in question. The adequate guarantees must be incorporated into the very legal regulation of the processing, either directly or by express and perfectly delineated reference to external sources that possess the appropriate normative rank. Only this understanding is compatible with the dual requirement that stems from Article 53.1 CE (...). It is evident that if the norm included a reference for the integration of the law with the adequate guarantees established in norms of lower rank than the law, it would be considered a delegalization that sacrifices the legal reservation under Article 53.1 CE, and for this reason alone, it should be declared unconstitutional and null. (...). Ultimately, it is about "adequate guarantees of a technical, organizational, and procedural nature, which prevent risks of varying probability and severity and mitigate their effects, as only in this way can respect for the essential content of the fundamental right itself be sought." Therefore, it is also insufficient for the establishment of such adequate and specific guarantees for the law to refer to the GDPR itself or to the LOPDGDD.
