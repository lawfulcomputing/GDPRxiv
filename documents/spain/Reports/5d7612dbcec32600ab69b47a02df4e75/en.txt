The inquiry raises the question of whether, in accordance with the provisions of Article 33.2 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDDD), the administrative concessionary entities of public services, which have been acting as "data processors" in the field of municipal management in tasks such as the utilization and exploitation of public domain or water concessions, should continue to be considered as such processors, with no possibility of them ever holding the status of data controllers.  
According to the inquirer, the Agency referred in its report 541/2008 to the possible "dual nature" of such entities in relation to their actions concerning data protection, being able to operate in certain cases as data controllers. Consequently, in light of the recent entry into force of the LOPDDD, the local entity inquiring requests a report on the correct interpretation of the provisions of Article 33.2 of said Organic Law, determining whether the entities acting as administrative concessionaries in the services mentioned have the status of processors or controllers of the data processing.  
I  
Article 4.7 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation -GDPR-), defines the data controller as "the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; where the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be provided for by the law of the Union or of the Member States."  
Furthermore, the aforementioned Regulation refers -in paragraph 8 of its Article 4- to the data processor as "the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller." In this regard, it is worth recalling that the figure of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, so that in those cases where the data controller entrusts a third party with the provision of a service that requires access to personal data by that third party, such access cannot be considered as a transfer of data by the controller to the service provider, but rather that the processing of the data is carried out by the processor on behalf of and for the controller as if the controller were carrying it out themselves.  
Article 28.3 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regulates this figure and requires in its third paragraph the existence of a contract or other legal act under Union or Member State law that binds the processor to the controller. Such contract or legal act must be in writing, including in electronic format, as stated in paragraph 9 of that article. Among the stipulations that must be included in such a contract is the provision that the processor "shall process personal data only on documented instructions from the controller, including with regard to the transfers of personal data to a third country or an international organization, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law applicable to the processor; in such a case, the processor shall inform the controller of that legal requirement before the processing, unless that law prohibits such information on important grounds of public interest." Furthermore, paragraph 10 of Article 28 states that "Without prejudice to the provisions of Articles 82, 83, and 84, if a processor infringes this Regulation by determining the purposes and means of processing, it shall be considered a controller in relation to that processing."  
For its part, and in the same sense, Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights, refers in its Article 33 to the data processor, providing that:  
“Article 33. Data Processor.

3. The data controller shall determine whether, upon the conclusion of the services provided by the processor, the personal data must be destroyed, returned to the controller, or delivered, if applicable, to a new processor. Data destruction shall not proceed when there is a legal provision that requires their retention, in which case they must be returned to the controller, who will ensure their retention as long as such obligation persists.
4. The data processor may retain, duly blocked, the data as long as responsibilities may arise from their relationship with the data controller.
5. In the public sector, the competencies typical of a data processor may be attributed to a specific body of the General Administration of the State, the Administration of the autonomous communities, the Entities that comprise the Local Administration, or to the Agencies linked or dependent on them by adopting a regulatory norm of such competencies, which must incorporate the content required by Article 28.3 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.” 
Thus, taking into account the definitions of data controller and data processor contained in both the General Data Protection Regulation and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, it must be considered that the defining criterion of the status of data controller is given by the power to determine the purposes and means of processing, while the processor must limit their actions to following the instructions of the controller, being deemed a controller if they determine purposes and means, that is, if they use for their own purposes the personal data that the controller has communicated to them for the processing object of the assignment, without prejudice to the possibility of incurring a breach of the GDPR with such action.
Consequently, the existence of a data processor will be delimited by the concurrence of two characteristics derived from the aforementioned regulations. On the one hand, the impossibility of decision regarding the purpose, content, and use of the processing, and on the other hand, the absence of a direct relationship between the data subject and the processor, who must in any case act on behalf of and for the account of the controller as if the relationship were between the controller and the data subject.
To delimit the cases in which the contractor will have the status of data controller or data processor, it must therefore start from the very nature of the main contract between the parties and the consequences of its celebration, in the sense of determining whether the award gives rise to the birth of a direct relationship between the provider of the public service and the administered party that makes use of such service.
The doctrine emanating from the National Court has clarified the scope of the concept of data processor. Thus, the Judgment of September 28, 2005, recalls that
“The difference between a data processor and a transfer in some cases is somewhat complex, but as this Section pointed out in the recent judgment of April 12, 2005 (appeal 258/2003), the typical aspect of the data processing assignment is that an external subject or one unrelated to the data controller will process personal data belonging to the processing carried out by the latter in order to provide a service in a specific area. II
At the time of issuing this report and regarding the inquiry about personal data processing carried out by contractors of Public Administrations, it is necessary to start from the regulations contained in Law 9/2017, of November 8, on Public Sector Contracts, which transposes into the Spanish legal system the Directives of the European Parliament and of the Council 2014/23/EU and 2014/24/EU, of February 26, 2014, which repealed Law 30/2007, of October 30, on Public Sector Contracts. 
Under the title “Protection of Personal Data,” the twenty-fifth additional provision of Law 9/2017, of November 8, on Public Sector Contracts, which transposes into the Spanish legal system the Directives of the European Parliament and of the Council 2014/23/EU and 2014/24/EU, of February 26, 2014, states that:
“1. The contracts regulated by this Law that involve the processing of personal data must fully comply with Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, and its implementing regulations.
2. In the event that the contract involves the contractor's access to personal data for which the contracting entity is responsible, the contractor will be considered a processor of the data. (the bold text is ours)
In this case, access to such data will not be considered a communication of data, provided that the provisions of Article 12.2 and 3 of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, are met. In any case, the provisions of Article 12.2 of that Law must be documented in writing.
When the contractual service ends, the personal data must be destroyed or returned to the responsible contracting entity, or to the processor designated by it.

The third party acting as the processor shall duly block the data as long as responsibilities may arise from its relationship with the data controller.
3. In the event that a third party processes personal data on behalf of the contractor, acting as the processor, the following requirements must be met:
a) That such processing has been specified in the contract signed by the contracting entity and the contractor.
b) That the processing of personal data complies with the instructions of the data controller.
c) That the contractor acting as the processor and the third party formalize the contract under the terms provided in Article 12.2 of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13.
In these cases, the third party will also be considered a processor of the data.”
Well, precisely the transcribed provision must be connected with what is established in section 2 of Article 33 of the LOPDDD -to which the inquiry refers-, when the consideration of the figure of the “data controller” is ruled out in those cases where the assignments are made within the framework of public sector contracting legislation. 
That is, as can be inferred from that provision, despite the eventual action of a certain entity in its own name and without it being established that it acts on behalf of another, and even the potential establishment of direct relationships with the affected parties (even when there is a contract or legal act with the content set forth in Article 28.3 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679-), the consideration of “processor” in relation to the assignments made within the framework of public sector contracting legislation does not diminish. 

Therefore, it has been the national legislator itself who has considered public administration contractors as processors of data. This provision aligns with what is established in Recital 45 and in Article 6, paragraphs two and three of the GDPR, which provides that in cases where the processing finds its legal basis in letters c) and e) of the same provision, national law may specify the general conditions of this Regulation governing the lawfulness of personal data processing, establishing specifications for determining the data controller.

In conclusion:
In general terms, it should be understood that public-administrative contracting governed by the legal framework of Law 9/2017, of November 8, on Public Sector Contracts, grants the contracting public entity - that is, the public administration, administrative body, or acting public entity - the status of "data controller" for the treatments derived from its contractual activity in the management of public services of local competence.
Consequently, also in general terms, the entities contracted by the public administration or by the administrative bodies for the provision of public services will be considered as "data processors."
Therefore, in the case submitted for consultation - related to the activity of concessionary companies in the field of municipal management, in tasks such as the use and exploitation of public domain or water concessions - we would find ourselves in a situation of access to data on behalf of third parties, which characterizes the figure of the "data processor," corresponding to the data controller the determination of the purposes and means to be used in relation to the processing of personal data.
Thus, the enabling legal title - regarding the data processing carried out by the concessionary entity by virtue of the entrusted mandate - will derive from the administrative contract signed, which confers upon it the status of data processor.
In this context, characterized by the concurrence of the activity of a controller and a processor, lies the very nature of the service concession contract, defined by Law 9/2017, of November 8, on Public Sector Contracts, in whose Article 284 it is provided:
“Article 284. Scope of the service concession contract.
1. The Administration may indirectly manage, through a service concession contract, the services under its ownership or competence as long as they are susceptible to economic exploitation by private individuals. In no case may services involving the exercise of authority inherent to public powers be provided through a service concession.
2. Before proceeding with the contracting of a service concession, in cases where it concerns public services, its legal regime must have been established, which expressly states that the activity in question is assumed by the respective Administration as its own, determines the scope of the benefits in favor of the administered, and regulates the legal, economic, and administrative aspects related to the provision of the service.
3. The contract shall clearly express, in any case, the scope of the concession, both in functional and territorial terms.”
