In the consultation regarding the activity of the consultant as a service provider in the construction area, such as those related—among others—to technical control, certificates of conformity, prevention for companies, coordination in matters of safety and health, assistance in the management of operational risks, and quality control, clarification is requested regarding their legal position within the framework of data protection regulations, determining whether they are a data controller or a data processor.  

As stated, currently, only the promoter of the works and leased services is considered the data controller, while the consultant identifies as the data processor concerning access and communications of the flow of personal data generated at the works. However, "given their impartiality and freedom of professional judgment, doubts arise regarding their possible fit within the figure of the data controller."  

In this regard, it is mentioned that part of the functions assigned to the consultant are carried out by virtue of legally established obligations, as well as that the communications of personal data—both received and sent—are necessary for the fulfillment of the contractual relationship signed with the promoter.  

I  
Article 4.7 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation - GDPR), defines the data controller as "the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the data controller or the specific criteria for their appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States."  

Furthermore, the aforementioned Regulation refers—in paragraph 8 of its article 4—to the data processor as "the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller." In this sense, it is worth recalling that the figure of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, so that in those cases where the data controller entrusts a third party with the provision of a service that requires access to personal data by this party, such access cannot be considered as a transfer of data by the controller to the party providing such service, but rather that the processing of the data is carried out by the processor on behalf of and for the account of the controller as if it were the controller itself carrying it out.  

Article 28 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regulates this figure and requires in its paragraph 3 the existence of a contract or other legal act under Union law or the law of the Member States that binds the processor to the controller. Such contract or legal act must be in writing, including in electronic format, as indicated in paragraph 9 of that article. Among the stipulations that must be included in such contract, it is stated first that the processor "shall process personal data only on documented instructions from the controller, including with regard to the transfers of personal data to a third country or an international organization, unless required to do so by Union law or the law of the Member States applicable to the processor; in such a case, the processor shall inform the controller of that legal requirement before processing, unless that law prohibits such information on important grounds of public interest." Furthermore, paragraph 10 of Article 28 establishes that "Without prejudice to the provisions of Articles 82, 83, and 84, if a processor infringes this Regulation by determining the purposes and means of processing, it shall be considered a data controller with respect to that processing."  

Expanding on the above, Recital (81) of the GDPR states that:  
“(…) Processing by a processor shall be governed by a contract or other legal act under Union law or the law of the Member States that binds the processor to the controller, which shall set out the subject matter and duration of the processing, the nature and purposes of the processing, the type of personal data and categories of data subjects, taking into account the specific functions and responsibilities of the processor in the context of the processing to be carried out and the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subject. (…)

II
The GDPR highlights the need to clearly establish the map of participants in all data processing, in order to accurately determine the attribution of responsibilities in accordance with the aforementioned regulation. 
As a starting point, we must refer to what is indicated in Recital 79 of the GDPR, which states that (…) The protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, as well as the responsibility of controllers and processors, also concerning the supervision by the supervisory authorities and the measures adopted by them, requires a clear attribution of responsibilities under this Regulation, including cases where a controller determines the purposes and means of processing jointly with other controllers, or where the processing is carried out on behalf of a controller (…).
This regulation aims to ensure that there are no cases of action outside its scope of application, in order to provide supervisory authorities with the necessary elements to carry out their function and, ultimately, to provide European citizens with the protection that their personal data deserves. Therefore, any activity involving the processing of personal data will be attributable to some entity that meets the requirements of the different categories provided by the GDPR.

Well, the legal description of the various figures involved in the project and execution of the work, as well as the enumeration of their functions and the level and degree of their responsibility, must be connected with the legal concepts of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD —data controller and data processor— following the interpretation provided by the European Data Protection Board in its Guidelines 7/2020, definitively adopted on July 7, 2021.
Based on the indicated criteria, and being the data controller the one who determines the purposes and means of processing, it is necessary to analyze how such decisions are articulated. To do this, one must start from the definition of the promoter as (i) “a natural or legal person on whose behalf a work is carried out” (Article 2.1.c. Royal Decree 1627/1997), (ii) being said promoter the one who designates a coordinator in matters of safety and health during the preparation of the construction project (Article 3.1), and (iii) also proceeding to their designation during the execution of the same (Article 3.2), (iv) without the designation of the coordinators exempting the promoter from their responsibilities (Article 3.4).
Likewise, the obligations related to the preparation of the safety and health study or the basic safety and health study in construction works also correspond to the promoter (Article 2 Royal Decree 1627/1997), without prejudice to the fact that they may designate another person or entity to manage said work, its implementation, and its monitoring.
In this scenario, the figure of the safety and health coordinator —CSSFE— designated by the promoter (Article 2.1 e. and f.) fits, who, both during the preparation of the construction project and during its execution (Article 3), will act by delegation, as a data processor.
Consequently, the functions and tasks assigned to the CSSFE contracted by the promoter under Articles 2 and 3 of Royal Decree 1627/1997, of October 24, will always be carried out on behalf of said promoter, who, within their organizational scope and in accordance with the purposes intended for the processing of personal data, will choose to hire one or another natural or legal person for the performance of the corresponding work, being these at the expense and risk of the promoter of the work, who is not exempt from their obligations.
Thus, we find ourselves in a clear example of a data processor, with the CSSFE accessing the data on behalf of the data controller. So much so that, in their case, in the event that —within their own organization— the promoter had the adequate personal and material means required by the applicable regulations, nothing would oppose the possibility of them proceeding to designate a CSSFE integrated within their own organization.
The correct designation of the data processor constitutes another manifestation of the principle of proactive responsibility, since the GDPR imposes on the data controller an obligation of diligence when choosing a data processor, indicating Recital 81 the following: (…) To ensure compliance with the provisions of this Regulation regarding the processing carried out by the processor on behalf of the controller, the controller, when entrusting processing activities to a processor, must only use processors that provide sufficient guarantees, in particular, with regard to specialized knowledge, reliability, and resources, for the application of technical and organizational measures that meet the requirements of this Regulation, including the security of processing.(…). Thus, in most cases, the promoter will have to resort to hiring another natural and/or legal person for the coordination of the safety and health project and for its effective execution.
In summary, from the legal description of the figure of the CSSFE, the nature of their appointment —which corresponds to the promoter—, and the nature of their functions, it is clearly derived that said coordinator will access personal data and proceed to its processing in their capacity as a data processor.

The processing of personal data of the affected individuals occurs in compliance with a legal obligation of the promoter, arising from the provisions of Articles 2, 3, and 4 of Royal Decree 1627/1997, of October 24, which establishes minimum safety and health provisions in construction works, issued in development of Article 6 of Law 31/1995, of November 8, on the Prevention of Occupational Risks.
As previously stated, the role of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, so that, in cases like the one under consultation, the data controller entrusts a third party —the consulting entity— with the provision of a service that involves the processing of personal data, thus configuring the assignment and allowing access to information containing personal data on behalf of the controller. 
The status of CSSFE, as the data processor for the promoter, is not undermined by the fact that the professionals holding this position perform their duties with impartiality and professional freedom of judgment. Furthermore, in accessing data on behalf of third parties, the attribution of processing operations to the controller means that the data processing is carried out by the processor on behalf of the controller as if it were the controller itself conducting it.
Thus, as interpreted by Guidelines 7/2020, on the concepts of "data controller" and "data processor" in the GDPR, in its final version of July 7, 2021:

As previously stated, for the relationship between the data controller and
the processor to be qualified as such, it is necessary to meet the requirements
of Article 28.3 of the GDPR and Article 33 of the LOPDGDD.
According to these premises, in the case submitted for report,
indeed, the activity carried out by the CSSFE does not extend to a
decision-making power over the data, nor does it allocate them to other
different purposes, thus being a true processor of the data.
Thus, its activity of accessing the data on behalf of the controller is
carried out solely for the purpose of providing a service to that data
controller, with this service relationship being contractually established; that
is to say, in any case, it will be necessary to sign the corresponding
contract, which must be entered into between the data controller —the
promoter of the work— and the data processor —the consulting entity—.
Thus, the consulting entity, even in this case —in which it performs
its functions with total impartiality and independence, following the provisions
of the regulations that specifically apply—, (i) processes personal data for the
benefit of the controller, (ii) and carries out the processing on behalf of that
controller, even though it is not under its authority or direct control, but
(iii) serving the interests of the promoter (controller).

assignment, it must be reiterated that Article 28.3 of the GDPR requires the
existence of a contract or other legal act under the law of the Union or of the
Member States that binds the processor to the controller. This contract or
legal act must be in writing, even in electronic format, as indicated in
number 9 of that article.
Among the provisions that this contract must contain, the first
stipulation is that the processor "will process personal data only in
accordance with documented instructions from the controller, including with

The fact that the relationship arising from the contract is that between
a controller and a processor implies that upon termination of the
relationship, the provisions of Article 33 of the LOPDGDD will apply, so that when the provision of services by the processor ends, the
controller will determine whether the personal data must be
destroyed, returned to the controller, or delivered, if applicable, to a new
processor. In any case, the destruction of the data will not proceed when
there is a legal provision that requires its retention, in which case it must
be returned to the controller, who will ensure its retention as long as such
obligation persists.
In turn, all applicable security measures must be included in the signed
contracts, as well as the obligation of the processor to comply with the
provisions of data protection regulations. Very specifically, the processor
must ensure compliance with the obligations regarding security set forth in
Article 28 of the LOPDGDD, relating to the proactive responsibility measures
that apply to controllers and processors, which are specified in the adoption
of appropriate technical and organizational measures that must be applied
to ensure and demonstrate that the processing is in accordance with the
GDPR, the LOPDGDD itself, and the applicable sectoral legislation.
Regarding the required security measures, Article 32 of the GDPR does
not establish a closed list of security measures that the controller and the
processor must adopt, such that by adopting them, they would fulfill their
obligations regarding security, but rather, under the principle of proactive
responsibility, it establishes that “taking into account the state of the
art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and
purposes of the processing, as well as the varying probabilities and
severity of risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the
controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and
organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the
risk,” which may include, among others:
“a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability,
and resilience of processing systems and services;
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data
quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;
d) a process for regular verification, evaluation, and assessment of the
effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the
security of the processing.”
In short, in their capacity as processors, the CSSFE are also fully subject
to the applicable security regulations regarding data protection.
In summary, according to the analysis carried out in this report, from the
perspective of data protection —in accordance with the GDPR and the
previous criteria of this Agency—, the consulting entity and, where
applicable, the CSSFE dependent on it, hold the status of “processors,” as
they process personal data on behalf of the promoter, who determines the
purposes and means of the processing. The promoter, in their capacity as
the controller, carries out the work on their own account, without the
designation of CSSFE exempting them from their responsibilities, and
without the impartial and independent nature of the functions performed by
the latter turning them into a controller, a contract as provided in Article
28.3 of the GDPR must be signed between the promoter and the
consultant.
According to Articles 4.7 and 24 of the GDPR, the promoter will be the
controller, corresponding to the CSSFE, in light of the concurrent
circumstances, the legal status of the processor —ex 28 GDPR and 33
LOPD—, acting on behalf of the controller in the exercise of its functions.
