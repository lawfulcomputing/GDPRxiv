The figure is defined in Article 3.g) of the LOPD as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or any other body that, alone or jointly with others, processes personal data on behalf of the data controller.” In turn, Article 5.1.i) of the RDLOPD indicates similarly regarding the data processor: “The natural or legal person, public or private, or administrative body that, alone or jointly with others, processes personal data on behalf of the data controller or the file controller, as a consequence of the existence of a legal relationship that links them to the same and delineates the scope of their action for the provision of a service.”

In our opinion, the General Directorate, as long as it processes personal data on behalf of the file or data controller by virtue of a legal relationship that links them – including, as we will see later, an attribution of specific competencies or functions – and provided that certain legal requirements are met, will be considered as the data processor. This does not depend, therefore, on its organic or functional dependency, or on its specific framing within the corresponding Department or body. 

In this regard, as we will see later, functional dependency or the lack thereof does not determine, in our opinion, the status of data processor, since what is essential is the material content of the legal relationship and the manner in which it is formalized (in principle, through a contract), and not the existence of organic or functional dependency between bodies or organizations. 

As we stated in the report of July 20, 2006, “what is important to delineate the concepts of data controller and data processor is not the reason that motivates their processing, but the sphere of direction, control, or management that the controller may exercise over the processing of personal data that they hold by virtue of that reason and which would be entirely prohibited to the data processor.”

The figure of the data processor is essential in data protection legislation, so that the processor, together with the data controller, must adopt the necessary measures to ensure the security of the data (Article 9 LOPD) and will consequently be liable for sanctions (Article 43 LOPD). Furthermore, Article 26 of the RDLOPD refers to the exercise of the rights of access, rectification, cancellation, and opposition before a data processor – which must communicate this to the controller – and in accordance with Article 79 of the RDLOPD, must implement, together with the data controller, the corresponding security measures. This is why Title VIII of the Regulation dedicates its Article 82 to the data processor.

As is well known, the LOPD, when regulating the figure of the data processor, does not require the consent of the affected party for the transfer of data in its favor; so that in those cases where the data controller entrusts a third party with the provision of a service that requires access to personal data by this third party, such access cannot be considered a transfer of data. This is why Article 12 of the LOPD establishes: “Access to data by a third party shall not be considered a communication of data when such access is necessary for the provision of a service to the data controller.”
In this regard, as noted in the report of this Agency dated July 27, 2006, “To determine whether in a specific case we are dealing with a transfer of data or with the performance of activities regulated by Article 12 of the Organic Law, it will be necessary to consider the circumstances of each case, so that there will be a transfer in those cases where the recipient of the data can apply the same to its own purposes, deciding on the object and purpose of the processing, which will in turn make it a data controller, while the figure regulated by Article 12 of the Organic Law will apply in those other cases where the receiving entity of the data is limited to carrying out certain operations on the same, without deciding on its purpose.”

First of all, the provision of services by the DGTNT to the data controllers is documented in writing, allowing for the verification of its existence and content. It is true that this is not a contract, in the sense of an agreement of wills, but this provision of services has an even greater extent and proof as it is attributed through a Decree, a legal norm issued by the Government Council of an Autonomous Community and published in the corresponding Official Gazette. Only in this way will it be considered a true norm with legal effects erga omnes, which implies the presumption of knowledge of it and its binding nature. 

Well, a legal norm with binding character is endowed with a written form and a possibility of proof that is far superior to the agreement of a contract between two parties. However, Article 12.2 of the LOPD not only requires written evidence but also refers to content. As stated in the Supreme Court ruling of April 17, 2007, “this necessarily requires a form that reflects and records not only its celebration but also its content, which is even specified in its essential clauses in the very precept. For SANTAMARÍA PASTOR, simultaneously with the determination of the structure of an organization, it is necessary to distribute the tasks assigned to it through the allocation of competencies. This author defines competence as “the ownership of a series of public powers exercisable regarding certain matters, services, or public purposes.” Therefore, it is the interests (purposes and matters) and powers assigned to each entity and, within it, to each body that composes it. Competencies, according to unanimous Spanish doctrine, are distributed materially, territorially, and hierarchically. Within this hierarchical distribution of competencies, the bodies always act subject to the principle of hierarchy (art. 3 of the LOFAGE).

Linking thus with the concept of competence in administrative law, this implies that the DGTNT will always process data in accordance with the directive or hierarchical power exercised by other bodies; that is, it will comply with the instructions of the data file manager or those derived from the applicable regulations. Furthermore, regardless of the hierarchical or functional relationship with other bodies, or the fact of having or not having legal personality, the DGTNT must exercise its competencies in accordance with the limits imposed by the legal system (principle of legality, art. 103 of the Constitution) and subject to the corresponding directive power. 

Competence, according to Article 12 of Law 30/1992, “will be exercised precisely by the administrative bodies that have it attributed as their own.” And if such competence, attributed in a regulation, consists of providing services to other bodies or organizations, and the provision of services involves the processing of personal data, the attribution of competence in the regulation implicitly carries the condition of data processor, the subjection to the hierarchical power of the superior bodies, whose instructions must be complied with, and the exercise of competencies for the intended purposes – under penalty of abuse of power – without the data being allocated to purposes other than those provided for the exercise of the competence. The regulations also do not allow the communication of data to other persons or entities; nor the retention of data beyond the fulfillment of the services. 

It would be different if the regulatory attribution of competence allowed the use of data for purposes other than the mere provision of IT services to other bodies, in which case we would have to reconsider whether the figure of data processor would be applicable. But to the extent that a legal norm, when attributing competencies to bodies, specifies that the functions of one of them are to provide data processing services for others, we may be facing a data processing assignment without the need to resort to a contractual document.

In this regard, a positive response must be given. Article 28.3 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation - GDPR) allows the status of data processor to be governed either by a contract or by a “legal act.” While the notion of a contract generally raises no doubts as to what it entails, without needing to delve deeper at this moment, the concept of a legal act appears as indeterminate in the GDPR. 

However, as indicated in Recital 87 in relation to Recital 19, both of the GDPR, it can be concluded that at the very least, this concept includes “legal norms,” whether from Union Law or from the Member States themselves. Recital 19 of the GDPR states: 
The protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purposes of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses or executing criminal sanctions, including the protection against threats to public security and the free movement of such data and its prevention, is subject to a specific legal act at the Union level. This Regulation should therefore not apply to processing activities carried out for such purposes. Nevertheless, personal data processed by public authorities under this Regulation must, if intended for such purposes, be governed by a more specific legal act of the Union, specifically Directive (EU) 2016/680 of the European Parliament and of the Council. 

In short, the GDPR itself considers that a Directive, that is, a legal norm, falls within the concept of “legal act.” Therefore, a norm such as a law, a royal decree, or another regulatory norm would have the character of a “legal act” for the purposes of the GDPR, so that the status of data processor may arise from a legal norm, as is the case in the inquiry raised. 

The same conclusions were reached in the Report cited in the inquiry (120294/2016) under the regime of the then-current Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data (LOPD), which cites other reports, such as the Report of February 19, 2013, or that of June 18, 2014. Thus, regarding the processing of personal data solely following documented instructions from the data controller, the principle of competence establishes that under no circumstances, as a data processor, may the consultant process personal data for purposes other than those attributed to the data controller by the regulatory norm of competence. The regulatory norm itself of the data controller determines the purposes of the processing and constitutes the instructions, public and published by the norm itself, that the data processor must follow. Of course, the data controller may also provide specific instructions as necessary regarding specific cases, but the norm does not require that the instructions always be specific to particular cases; rather, instructions valid for a generality of cases, documented in the norm that attributes competence to the data controller, are acceptable and constitute the instructions that the processor must follow regarding the processing of personal data.

Within this new system, it is the data controller who, through the instruments regulated in the GDPR itself, such as the record of processing activities, risk analysis, or data protection impact assessment, must ensure the protection of this right by complying with all the principles set forth in Article 5.1 of the GDPR, adequately documenting all decisions made in order to be able to demonstrate compliance.
