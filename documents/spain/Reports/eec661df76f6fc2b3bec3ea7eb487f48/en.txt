After examining your request for a report, submitted to this Legal Advisory Office, regarding the Draft General Telecommunications Law, requested from this Spanish Agency for Data Protection (AEPD) in accordance with the provisions of Article 47 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on personal data protection and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), in relation to Article 57.1, letter c), of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons in relation to the processing of personal data, and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), and 5 b) of the Agency's Statute, approved by Royal Decree 389/2021, of June 1, I am obliged to inform you of the following:  

Before analyzing the text submitted for the report, it is necessary to point out that, given the legal basis of the report that will be provided immediately and its mandatory nature, in accordance with the provisions of the aforementioned regulations, it should be indicated in the Explanatory Memorandum of the regulation that it has been submitted to the prior report of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection.  

The draft transposes into the Spanish legal system Directive (EU) 2018/1972 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of December 11, 2018, which establishes the European Code of Electronic Communications, regulating the general regime of telecommunications, compiling the current regulations, updating those aspects that have undergone significant modifications since the approval of the previous General Law of 2014, and laying the foundations for the deployment of very high-capacity networks. In this way, it introduces structural reforms in the legal regime of telecommunications, in order to promote investment by operators in very high-capacity networks, improve the protection of users' rights, and update, among others, the regulations on Universal Service, spectrum management, and network security, contributing to the structuring of the territory and the fight against depopulation and climate change.  

I  
Directive (EU) 2018/1972 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of December 11, 2018, which establishes the European Code of Electronic Communications, consolidates into a single text the package of Community Directives approved in 2002 and amended in 2009, which are part of the regulatory framework for electronic communications networks and services (access, authorization, framework, and universal service directives), with the exception of Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of July 12, 2002, on the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on privacy and electronic communications), which remains in force until the approval of what is known as the Eprivacy Regulation.  

Consequently, as stated in Recital 15 of Directive (EU) 2018/1972, "The processing of personal data through electronic communications services, for remuneration or otherwise, must comply with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council," without prejudice to the specifications contained in Directive 2002/58/EC as a special rule applicable to electronic communications.  

In this regard, the GDPR itself in Recital 173 provides that "This Regulation should apply to all matters relating to the protection of fundamental rights and freedoms in relation to the processing of personal data that are not subject to specific obligations with the same objective established in Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, including the obligations of the data controller and the rights of natural persons. To clarify the relationship between this Regulation and Directive 2002/58/EC, the latter should be amended accordingly. Once this Regulation is adopted, Directive 2002/58/EC should be reviewed, particularly to ensure consistency with this Regulation."  

And in Article 95, it refers to the relationship of the GDPR with Directive 2002/58/EC, stating the following:  
This Regulation shall not impose additional obligations on natural or legal persons regarding processing within the framework of the provision of public electronic communications services in public communication networks of the Union in areas where they are subject to specific obligations with the same objective established in Directive 2002/58/EC.  

Therefore, Directive 2002/58/EC maintains, in relation to the GDPR, the character of a special rule that it had with Directive 95/46/EC, as recognized in its Recital 10 ("In the electronic communications sector, Directive 95/46/EC applies, in particular to all matters relating to the protection of fundamental rights and freedoms that are not specifically covered by the provisions of this Directive, including the obligations of the...").

Directive 95/46/EC applies to electronic communication services that are not of a public nature.), and in its Article 1, relating to the scope and objective, which after stating in its paragraph 1 that “This Directive establishes the harmonization of national provisions necessary to ensure an equivalent level of protection of freedoms and fundamental rights and, in particular, the right to privacy and confidentiality, with regard to the processing of personal data in the electronic communications sector, as well as the free movement of such data and of electronic communication equipment and services within the Community” adds in its paragraph 2 that “The provisions of this Directive specify and complement Directive 95/46/EC for the purposes mentioned in paragraph 1.”
Therefore, Directive 2002/58/EC and, consequently, the provisions of this draft bill that transpose said Directive, establish a special regime, to which only the processing regulated by it would be subject, as opposed to the general data protection regime set forth in the GDPR. For this reason, the provisions of the same will apply to all processing carried out within the scope of Union law that is not specifically regulated by the Directive, as can be inferred from the scope established in Article 2 of the Regulation and in the terms specified in its Article 95. Likewise, this special norm character is equally applicable regarding Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD), whose provisions must be taken into account when establishing the “lex generalis” applicable to guarantee the fundamental right to the protection of personal data.
Based on the above, this report will focus, on one hand, on the analysis of those provisions that are a transposition of Directive 2002/58/EU and, on the other hand, on those that, being a transposition of Directive (EU) 2018/1972, affect the processing of personal data. Additionally, the sanctioning regime established by said regulations will be analyzed as it affects the competencies of this Agency.
For the issuance of this report, the criteria maintained by this Agency in previous reports have been taken into account, such as report 49/2011, regarding the Draft Law amending Law 32/2003, of November 3, General Telecommunications Law, and report 21/2013 regarding the Draft General Telecommunications Law.

Also, as an important novelty, to ensure that the scope of the regulatory framework keeps pace with technological and market developments and that providers of similar services are subject to a similar regulatory burden (level playing field), it is established that the definition of electronic communication service under the Code covers:
- internet access services;
- interpersonal communication services (ICS); and

The inclusion of interpersonal communication services is a key change. These are services that allow interactive and interpersonal exchanges of information through electronic communication networks between a finite number of people where the sender determines the recipients. This could include voice calls, emails, and messaging services. This change will allow services provided over the Internet, commonly referred to as "over the top" or OTT services, to fall within the scope of the regulatory regime, with the exception of those services where the communication element constitutes a secondary and ancillary feature.
Under the Code, the obligations of ICS based on numbering are equalized to those of traditional operators in multiple areas (privacy protection, ILC, consumer protection, restrictions on the transmission of harmful or illegal content, rates). ICS independent of numbering are subject to fewer obligations, especially when it comes to microenterprises.
On the other hand, any limitation of the fundamental right to personal data protection would require not only a norm with the rank of law that provides for it, in accordance with Article 53 of our Constitution, but also, according to reiterated constitutional doctrine to which reference will be made later, that such limitation be adopted after weighing by the legislator the conflicting interests in accordance with the principle of proportionality, defining all and each of the material prerequisites of the limiting measure through precise rules, which make the imposition of such limitation and its consequences foreseeable to the interested party, and establishing adequate guarantees, precautions that, as can be seen, correspond to those provided for in Article 100 of Directive 2018/1972.
For all these reasons, the general exclusion of microenterprises regarding the rights of end users and consumers in no case can affect the fundamental right to personal data protection. For the same reason, such a general exclusion regarding the companies, public authorities, or end users referred to in the second paragraph of section 2 of Article 64 is also not admissible:
   
Nor are companies, public authorities, or end users that provide access to a public electronic communications network through WLAN obliged to respect the rights recognized in this chapter, when such provision does not form part of an economic activity or is ancillary to another economic activity or a public service that does not depend on the transport of signals over those networks.
Therefore, this exclusion cannot be understood as meaning that the fundamental right to personal data protection should not be respected, but rather that it should not be in the specific terms regulated in the draft bill, which, given the special law nature of this regulation and as previously indicated, will imply that they remain subject to the general regime of GDPR and LOPDGDD to the extent that it affects the processing of personal data.

On the other hand, it is noteworthy the reference made to the concept of consent in relation to the GDPR in section 5 of Article 66, as a general applicable norm, to which letter f) of Article 2 of Directive 2002/58/EC already referred. 
Article 4.11 of the GDPR defines “the consent of the data subject” as “any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes by which they signify their agreement, either by a statement or by a clear affirmative action, to the processing of personal data relating to them.” 
This provision merely reflects the requirements for valid consent that had been outlined by the Article 29 Working Party, especially in its Opinion 15/2011 on the definition of consent, ensuring that the data subject has control over their data. As the Article 29 Working Party pointed out in its Guidelines on Consent in the sense of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 adopted on November 28, 2017, and which has been included in the recent Guidelines of the European Data Protection Board 05/2020 on consent under Regulation 2016/679, of May 4, 2020, if the GDPR is not fully complied with, “the control of the data subject will be merely illusory and consent will not be a valid legal basis for processing, which will render such processing activity unlawful,” taking into account, moreover, that “the requirements stipulated in the GDPR for obtaining valid consent apply to situations that fall within the scope of the Directive on privacy and electronic communications.”
