Legal Advisory Office  
N/REF: 0057/2020  
The inquiry raises whether the content of the right to be forgotten as provided in Article 17 of Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (GDPR) and in Article 93 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), encompasses search results on the internet when these are derived from information other than necessarily the name.  

I  
As a starting point, reference must be made to the provisions that are to be interpreted; thus, Article 17 of the GDPR, under the heading "Right to erasure ('the right to be forgotten')" states the following:  
1. The data subject shall have the right to obtain from the controller the erasure of personal data concerning him or her without undue delay, and the controller shall be obliged to erase personal data without undue delay when one of the following circumstances applies:  
   a) the personal data are no longer necessary in relation to the purposes for which they were collected or otherwise processed;  
   b) the data subject withdraws consent on which the processing is based according to Article 6(1)(a) or Article 9(2)(a), and where there is no other legal ground for the processing;  
   c) the data subject objects to the processing pursuant to Article 21(1), and there are no overriding legitimate grounds for the processing, or the data subject objects to the processing pursuant to Article 21(2);  
   d) the personal data have been unlawfully processed;  
   e) the personal data must be erased for compliance with a legal obligation in Union or Member State law to which the controller is subject;  
   f) the personal data have been collected in relation to the offer of information society services referred to in Article 8(1).  
2. When the controller has made the personal data public and is obliged, under the provisions of paragraph 1, to erase such data, the controller, taking into account the available technology and the cost of implementation, shall take reasonable steps, including technical measures, to inform controllers that are processing the personal data of the data subject's request for erasure of any link to, or copy or replication of, those personal data.  
3. Paragraphs 1 and 2 shall not apply to the extent that the processing is necessary:  
   a) for exercising the right of freedom of expression and information;  
   b) for compliance with a legal obligation which requires processing by Union or Member State law to which the controller is subject, or for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
   c) for reasons of public interest in the area of public health in accordance with Article 9(2)(h) and (i), and paragraph 3;  
   d) for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes or statistical purposes in accordance with Article 89(1), insofar as the right referred to in paragraph 1 is likely to render impossible or seriously impair the achievement of the objectives of that processing, or  
   e) for the establishment, exercise or defense of legal claims.  

For its part, the LOPDGDD indicates in its Article 93 under the heading "Right to be forgotten in Internet searches" the following:  
1. Everyone has the right for search engines on the Internet to remove from the lists of results obtained from a search carried out based on their name the published links containing information related to that person when they are inadequate, inaccurate, irrelevant, outdated, or excessive, or have become such due to the passage of time, taking into account the purposes for which they were collected or processed, the time elapsed, and the nature and public interest of the information.

In accordance with the above, it is observed that while Article 17 of the GDPR contains a "general" regulation, applicable not only to the scope of the right to be forgotten against search engines but to any other, on the contrary, Article 93 of the LOPDGDD expressly regulates these aspects, indicating that we are dealing with a search engine and that the search is conducted through the name. 

From the reading of both provisions, it can be affirmed that a differentiation is established between the right of deletion, in general terms, and the right to be forgotten against search engines. 

That is to say, there is no doubt that Article 17 of the GDPR is applicable to the right to be forgotten against search engines, but it is also applicable to the right of deletion in any other area; however, it is Article 93 of the LOPDGDD that expressly regulates the right to be forgotten in internet searches. 

Therefore, one could adhere to the literal interpretation of the norm (Article 3.1 of the Civil Code) and understand that the right to be forgotten is confined solely to internet searches conducted based on the name, without such application implying emptying the direct effect of the Regulations or even going against what is stated in the GDPR, as its regulation also encompasses other areas. 

However, there are other interpretative criteria, such as the social context in which the norms must be interpreted, and above all, the purpose they seek, which must be observed and, therefore, regarding the query raised, other aspects must be taken into account as outlined below and in accordance with the jurisprudence and works of the EDPB.

II
First of all, one must refer to the Judgment of the CJEU in the case "Google Spain SL and Google Inc./Spanish Data Protection Agency (AEPD) and Mario Costeja González" (C-131/12) (hereinafter the CJEU Judgment) which recognizes a right to be forgotten against search engines that is distinct from the right to deletion of personal data on a webpage, that is, from the right of deletion in the strict sense of Article 17 GDPR. Thus, in paragraphs 82 and 85 it states that:

(...) the supervisory authority or the judicial body may order the operator to remove from the list of results obtained after a search conducted based on a person's name links to web pages published by third parties that contain information relating to that person, without an order in this regard presupposing that that name or that information is, with the full consent of the publisher or by order of one of these authorities, deleted beforehand or simultaneously from the webpage on which they have been published (...)

(...) it cannot be excluded that the data subject may exercise, under certain circumstances, the rights provided for in point b) of Article 12 and in point a) of the first paragraph of Article 14 of Directive 95/46 against that operator, but not against the publisher of that webpage (...)

And in paragraph 3 of the operative part, it indicates that:

(...) the operator of a search engine is obliged to remove from the list of results obtained after a search conducted based on a person's name links to web pages published by third parties that contain information relating to that person, even in the event that this name or this information is not deleted beforehand or simultaneously from these web pages, and, where applicable, even if the publication on those pages is lawful in itself. (...)

Furthermore, this differentiation is also expressed by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) in its "Guidelines 5/2019 on the criteria for the right to be forgotten in cases of search engines under the GDPR," dated July 7, 2020, that is, in accordance with Article 17 of the GDPR which recognizes the Right of Deletion, indicating in paragraphs 7 and 9 that: 

(...) There are some considerations when applying Article 17 of the GDPR in relation to the processing of data by a search engine provider. In this regard, it is necessary to point out that the processing of personal data carried out in the context of the activity of the search engine provider must be distinguished from the processing carried out by the publishers of third-party websites, such as media outlets that provide journalistic content online (...)

(...) Requests for deletion of names from the List do not result in the complete deletion of personal data. In fact, the data...

The differentiation between the right to erasure against the web page controllers, and the right to be forgotten against search engines is particularly relevant from the perspective of the concept of personal data and the purpose served by each.
Regarding the concept of personal data as provided in Article 4.1 of the GDPR, which considers any information about an identified or identifiable natural person ("the data subject") as such; an identifiable natural person is considered to be any person whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by means of an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or several elements specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that person, as it has different implications and intensity in one case than in the other.
While in the first case the concept of personal data in its broadest sense is relevant to address the information that must be subject to erasure, in the second case, the concept of personal data is more restricted or limited since not every time personal data is used in the search will we be facing situations that allow the exercise of the right to be forgotten, but it will depend, first of all, on the level of identification of the affected person, that is, whether it is comparable to the name and surnames (depending on the specific case) and secondly, derived from the quantum of that comparability, from the interference in their rights.
Indeed, both rights (erasure and forgetting) arise from Article 17 of the GDPR, but the right to be forgotten against search engines as stated in Article 93 of the LOPDGDD, expressly serves the purpose of protecting the affected individual from the interference that a data controller (the search engine) different from the one processing data at source (the website publisher) poses by offering a complete and structured view of a person's profile.
Hence, its content is the possibility for a data subject to request the provider of an online search engine to remove one or more links to web pages from the list of results displayed after a search conducted by their name (section 1 of the Guidelines 5/2019 EDPB).
It is not the same for personal data to be found on a webpage processed by a controller than for, on the contrary, multiple links to web pages to be related to a person that allow for a complete and structured profile of that person. The interference in one case is not the same as in the other. In this sense, section 87 of the CJEU ruling indicates that:
Indeed, to the extent that the inclusion, in the list of results obtained after a search carried out based on a person's name, of a webpage and information contained therein relating to this person significantly facilitates the accessibility of that information to any internet user conducting a search about the data subject and can play a decisive role in the dissemination of this information, it may constitute a greater interference with the fundamental right to respect for the private life of the data subject than the publication by the publisher of this webpage.
Therefore, the right to erasure and the right to be forgotten against search engines pursue different purposes and levels of protection.
That is to say, the concept of personal data can resolve what information must be deleted in the right to erasure against the publishers of web pages, but it is insufficient by itself to resolve the object of the inquiry, in the sense of whether the name and surnames (personal data) are the elements that the search engine will take into account, or whether, on the contrary, it may be other information that is introduced as a search element.
In other words, not every time personal data is used in the search will the right to be forgotten be granted (since the concept of personal data encompasses multiple aspects, for example, a license plate, an ID number, a dynamic IP address (CJEU ruling of October 19, 2016, case Breyer), the answers to an exam (CJEU ruling of December 20, 2017, case Nowack), or even the logs of a home alarm system (SAN No. 146/2018 of July 23, 2019), but it will depend on the level of identification regarding the affected person, that is, whether it is comparable to the name and the interference in their rights in relation to the specific case.
III
Having said the above, to recognize the existence of the right to be forgotten, the CJEU enumerates several circumstances to take into account, but the fundamental aspect to resolve the inquiry posed is the interference that arises when the search engine offers a complete and structured view of a person based on a search conducted from their name. In this sense, section 80 of the ruling indicates the following:
(…) In this regard, it should be noted, first of all, that, as stated in sections 36 to 38 of this ruling, a processing of personal data such as that contested in the main proceedings, carried out by the
Legal Office
The manager of a search engine can significantly affect the fundamental rights to respect for private life and the protection of personal data when the search conducted using that search engine is carried out based on the name of a natural person, since such processing allows any internet user to obtain, through the list of results, a structured view of the information related to this person that may be found on the Internet, which potentially affects a multitude of aspects of their private life, which, without that engine, would not have been interconnected or would have been very difficult to do so, and thus allows the establishment of a more or less detailed profile of the person in question. Furthermore, the effect of the interference with the rights of the data subject is multiplied due to the significant role that the Internet and search engines play in modern society, which confer a ubiquitous character to the information contained in such a list of results (see, in this regard, the judgment in eDate Advertising and others, C-509/09 and C-161/10, EU:C:2011:685, paragraph 45).
(…)
That is to say, what is relevant is the interference with the rights of the affected individual regarding the information that the search engine "returns" in a search conducted based on their name, or based on terms that are comparable in terms of the ability to identify or individualize that the name has compared to all others, that is, that it is a unique identifier of the person.
Taking the above into account, it can be generally stated that when the search terms are specified as the name of the affected individual (first and last name), the search engine returns a complete digital view of that individual (in the words of the CJEU, a structured view of the information related to this person that may be found on the Internet), and it is at this point that the greatest interference with their rights occurs. That is to say, the more complete the profile of the person returned by the search engine, the greater the interference.
Conversely, it could happen that if the search is conducted using other information different from the full name of the affected individual, for example, only the first name and one last name, the interference could be less, since the individualization of a person would be lower as it may coincide with the first name and last name of another. Even if another identifier less comparable to the level of identification of the name is used, such as, for example, a license plate, the results would be more partial and therefore, with less interference.
The greater the individualization, the more complete the profile will be, and therefore, greater interference occurs in the privacy of the affected individual.

Secondly, the various circumstances to be considered as outlined by the CJEU can be summarized as follows:
- An initially lawful processing of accurate data may become, over time, incompatible with the Directive, when such data is no longer necessary in relation to the purposes for which it was collected or processed, particularly when it is inadequate, irrelevant, no longer relevant, or excessive in relation to these purposes and the time elapsed (paragraph 93).
- A fair balance must be sought between the legitimate interest of internet users in accessing information in a search that concerns the name of a person and the fundamental rights of that person, and it may result that, for specific reasons, such as the role played by the aforementioned individual in public life, the interference with their fundamental rights is justified by the overriding public interest in having, as a result of this inclusion, access to the information in question (paragraphs 81, 93, and 97).
- The balance may depend, in specific cases, on the nature of the information, the sensitivity of the information to the private life of the affected person, and the public interest in having access to the information, which may vary depending on the role that person plays in public life; in this case, the overriding public interest must be based on specific reasons that the judicial body must verify, if applicable (paragraphs 81 and 98).
- The outcome of the balancing may differ depending on whether we are dealing with processing carried out by a search engine manager or by the publisher of the website, as the legitimate interests that justify that processing may be different and the consequences for the affected individual may not be the same: inclusion in the list of results may constitute a greater interference with the fundamental right to respect for the private life of the affected individual than the publication by the publisher of this website (paragraphs 86 and 87).
- The right of the affected individual to have information related to them no longer linked to their name by a list of results obtained after a search based on their name does not presuppose that the inclusion of that information causes them harm (paragraph 96).
As can be seen, the CJEU indicates that it is necessary to seek a fair balance between the legitimate interest of internet users in having access to the information.

For its part, the Article 29 Working Group (currently replaced by the European Data Protection Committee, EDPB) in Opinion WP225/2014 of December 26, on “Guidelines for the application of the Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union C-131/2012” states that while the CJEU expressly mentions “searches carried out based on the name of a person,” the following must be taken into account: (…)21(…) that “It should be noted that the judgment uses the term ‘name’ without further specification. Thus, it can be concluded that the right applies to possible different versions of the name, including surnames or different spellings.” And in the section referring to the questions for the application of the criteria, it is indicated that “DPAs will also consider pseudonyms and nicknames as relevant search terms when the individual can establish that they are linked to their real identity.” 
Here it is important to pause on the last phrase, “when the individual can establish that they are linked to their real identity,” meaning that the possibility of using other terms is recognized, but it falls on the affected party to prove that level of equivalence in terms of identifiability and uniqueness, between the term used (restricted solely to the pseudonym and the nickname) and the name.
Likewise, in the EDPB Guidelines 5/2019, of July 7, 2020, it is indicated in its section 18 that: In accordance with Article 17.1.a of the GDPR, a data subject may request a search engine provider, after a search generally conducted based on their name, to delete the content of their search results when the personal data returned by the data subject in those search results are no longer necessary in relation to the purposes of processing by the search engine. 
That is to say, the aforementioned Guidelines establish a general rule, “that the search is conducted based on the name,” therefore the existence of exceptions can be admitted, but always depending on the specific case since, as has been seen, the elements to be considered are varied and dynamic. 
As previously indicated, there must be an equivalence in terms of singularization and unequivocal identifiability against third parties, between the terms used in the search and the name of the affected party, without the use of any personal data serving these purposes.

In these cases, it has been taken into account that there was an equivalence regarding identifiability and individualization between the indicated terms and the name of the affected party, all in relation to the interference in the rights of the affected party derived from the complete and structured profile that the search engine "returned."
On the contrary, other claims referring to searches based on the ID number, the name of a town, the location in an online mapping service, the name of the medical center where the affected party provided their services, etc., have been dismissed, as the criterion indicated in section 21 of the aforementioned "WP225/2014 Guidelines of December 26" was not met, and it could not be understood that the level of individualization and the interference produced was comparable to if the search were conducted using the name of the affected party.
Therefore, the admission of variants other than the name must be considered exceptional, and it will all depend on the degree of identification against the generality that arises from the use of those terms, as a nickname by which an affected party is known in their most immediate social environment (their family, friends, etc.) is not the same as a nickname or artistic name that identifies a person against the entirety or a large part of the population; that is, what is relevant is how the person is known at a general level and not by just a few.
From the joint interpretation of the involved provisions (articles 17 GDPR and 93 LOPDGDD), the Opinion and Guidelines of the CEPD, the cited jurisprudence, and the Resolutions of the AEPD, and taking into account the purpose and object of protection of the right to be forgotten against search engines, the general rule must be that the Right to be Forgotten against search engines only encompasses searches conducted based on the name (first name and surnames), and exceptionally, it may be admitted when the search is conducted using other terms that produce the same effects as the name regarding identification and individualization, derived from the specific case of the person in question (surnames, pseudonym, nickname, artistic name), and the granting of the right will depend on the concurrent circumstances in each case, taking into account especially the profile of the person returned by the search engine.
VI
In accordance with the above, the consultation must be resolved based on the general rule, that is, the search must be conducted using the name as it is the element that identifies or makes the affected party identifiable, which individualizes and singularizes them, and exceptionally, other terms may be included.

Thus, the use of other search terms such as surnames, the abbreviation of the compound name, the pseudonym, the nickname, or the artistic name just mentioned, are not considered a numerus clausus, as they respond to the case law analyzed to date. However, it should also be noted that their mere invocation does not imply, per se, the granting of the right, but must be understood as exceptional. Therefore, it is necessary for the affected party to invoke and prove the quantum of identification derived from them and, above all, the impact on their rights. The fundamental aspect will be the degree of identification of the affected party using terms other than the name, in relation to the impact on their rights (the interference that occurs when the search engine returns a complete and structured profile of a person). 
That is to say, it will not only be necessary for the term to allow for unambiguous identification of the affected party, but also for that identification to make them commonly known. Derived from the intensity or degree of identification and from the "structured profile returned by the search engine," the interference that is intended to be avoided occurs, as this is the core of the right to be forgotten in internet searches. 
In light of the above, the right to be forgotten must be recognized, not automatically, and solely based on whether the full name or other information is used, but rather in consideration of the concurrent circumstances in each case. For this, a prior assessment will be necessary, the result of which offers "the fair balance" cited in the Judgment of the CJEU (C-131/12).
