The consultation raises the question of whether it is compliant with Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR) and with Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), to communicate to the Prevention Delegates at Correos (the company hereinafter) the document relating to the list of workplace accidents and occupational diseases that have caused the worker a work incapacity of more than one working day, along with the name and surname of the injured persons, or, on the contrary, whether it is sufficient to communicate only the professional identification number (NIP) without providing the name and surname of the injured party.  

Attached to the consultation is an extract from a Record of Infraction from the Labor Inspection, which, in summary, considers it necessary that in workplaces with a large workforce, the identifying data of the injured party (name and surname) be communicated to the Prevention Delegates, as the provision of the NIP is deemed insufficient for these delegates to be able to understand what has happened in the workplaces and exercise the powers attributed to them by Law 31/1995, of November 8, on the Prevention of Occupational Risks (LPRL). It is added in this Record that we are not dealing with health data, and that the legal basis for such processing is provided for in Article 6.1 c) of the GDPR, in relation to Article 8.1 of the LOPDGDD, all in accordance with the LPRL.  

I  
First, it is necessary to analyze the type of information that will be subject to processing in the sense of whether we are dealing with health data and therefore special categories of data as provided in Article 9 of the GDPR. Article 4.15 of the GDPR establishes that “health data” is defined as: personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status;  

For its part, Recital 35 of the GDPR indicates that: Among the personal data related to health, all data related to the health status of the data subject that provide information about their past, present, or future physical or mental health should be included. This includes information about the natural person collected in connection with their registration for health care purposes, or in connection with the provision of such care, in accordance with Directive 2011/24/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council (9); any number, symbol, or data assigned to a natural person that uniquely identifies them for health purposes; information obtained from tests or examinations of a part of the body or from a bodily substance, including that derived from genetic data and biological samples, and any information relating, by way of example, to a disease, a disability, the risk of suffering from diseases, medical history, clinical treatment, or the physiological or biomedical status of the data subject, regardless of its source, for example, a doctor or other health professional, a hospital, a medical device, or an in vitro diagnostic test.  

According to the Dictionary of the Spanish Language of the Royal Spanish Academy, the word “health” refers to: 2. f. Set of physical conditions in which an organism finds itself at a given moment.”  

For its part, the Judgment of the National Court of November 24, 2020, which resolves Appeal No. 791/2018, interprets the concept of health data in reference to information about doping, ultimately understanding it to be included in this category, and it is worth mentioning as it establishes the need to carry out an extensive interpretation of Article 4.15 and Recital 35, in the sense that it is irrelevant that the definition does not contain a specific exclusion of any concept and that it is also of no consequence whether the processing of the data occurs in a hospital environment or not, as it was alleged by the appellant that in other resolutions of the AEPD and of the National Court itself, this hospital element was always present and that, as in the case analyzed (infraction regarding doping), it was not such that we were not dealing with health data.

In the present case, it is unquestionable that the processing of personal data originates from a medical-hospital environment, since the worker who has taken sick leave for more than one day had to be treated and diagnosed by a medical professional regarding events that have influenced their state of health.
Finally, other reports must also be indicated (including those numbered 355/2010, 110/2014, 273/2014, and 4/2017), which resolve similar inquiries to the one raised and which will be analyzed below, where "specially protected data" has been considered, in the terminology of the now-repealed Organic Law on Data Protection of 1999 (LOPD), the information to which the Prevention Delegates will have access in the exercise of the functions referred to in the inquiry.
Taking the above into account, the information related to the accidents that a worker has suffered in the course of their work, and which has consequently caused sick leave exceeding one day, is undoubtedly a result of an examination of their state of health following that accident and therefore, by transferring that information to the Prevention Delegate, one is informing about aspects related to the worker's health.
Indeed, there has been a provision of healthcare services that reveals health status, and that places such information under the protection of the definition in Article 4.15 of the GDPR.
Furthermore, without prejudice to the analysis regarding the provision of the NIP or the name and surname that will be addressed later, it is undeniable that this information about health, associated with an identifier that allows for the direct or indirect identification of a person (according to the definition of personal data contained in Article 4.1 GDPR), means that we are dealing with the processing of personal data related to health and therefore the regime provided for in Article 9 of the aforementioned GDPR applies.
II
Section 2 of Article 9 GDPR establishes the circumstances that allow for exceptions to the general prohibition on the processing of special categories of data, which must be addressed in relation to the inquiry raised in subsection b) concerning when:
(…) the processing is necessary for compliance with obligations and the exercise of specific rights of the data controller or the data subject in the field of labor law and social security and protection, to the extent that such is authorized by the law of the Union of the Member States or a collective agreement under the law of the Member States that establishes adequate guarantees for the respect of fundamental rights and the interests of the data subject; (…)
In this sense, from the LPRL regarding the processing of workers' data by the Prevention Delegates as a consequence of the communication that the employer must make, the following provisions must be cited:
Article 23 under the heading "Documentation" establishes the following: 1. The employer must prepare and keep available to the labor authority the following documentation related to the obligations established in the previous articles:

It is also noteworthy that the provision does not indicate anything about the identification of the "injured parties," neither in a disassociated or anonymized manner, pseudonymized, nor in any other way, but rather refers to the list of accidents.
Indeed, Article 36 of the LPRL under the heading "Competencies and powers of the Prevention Delegates" establishes the following:

III
Therefore, once the general prohibition on processing has been lifted in accordance with section 2.b) of article 9 of the GDPR, it is necessary to find a legal basis as provided for in article 6 that legitimizes the processing.
Indeed, as indicated, the LPRL itself (and indirectly in the TRLISOS) establishes the obligation regarding the data controller, in this case, the employer, to carry out the processing in question, so that in accordance with section 1. c) of article 6 of the GDPR, the processing is considered necessary for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller.
And regarding what is indicated in article 8.1 of the LOPDGDD, the norm with the rank of law that must concur is the LPRL and the TRLISOS.
Therefore, the legal basis that legitimizes such processing is that provided for in article 6.1 c) of the GDPR.

VII
Currently, the applicable GDPR includes in its Article 5 the principles for data processing, and in relation to the subject matter of the consultation, special attention should be paid to those provided in paragraphs 1 c) and f), according to which personal data shall be:
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”);
f) processed in such a way as to ensure an adequate level of security for personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, through the application of appropriate technical or organizational measures (“integrity and confidentiality”).
Also, the provisions in paragraph 2 of the aforementioned Article 5 of the GDPR should be taken into account, which establishes the principle of proactive responsibility, stating that the data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and capable of demonstrating it (“proactive responsibility”).
That is to say, it will be the responsibility of the data controller to previously assess the circumstances of the specific case in order to effectively apply the indicated principles, on one hand, legality, fairness, and transparency (paragraph 1 a) of Article 5 GDPR) and on the other, minimization and confidentiality (paragraph 1 c) and f) of Article 5 GDPR).
It should also be noted what is indicated in Recital 4 of the GDPR, which recognizes that the right to data protection is not an absolute right but must be considered in relation to its function in society and maintain a balance with other fundamental rights, according to the principle of proportionality.
Regarding the principle of minimization, it should be indicated that it is closely related to the principle of proportionality established in the doctrine of the Constitutional Court and that, among other issues, serves to determine whether the interference with a fundamental right is lawful in relation to the preservation of other rights or legal goods or in application of other norms, when we are faced with a conflict between two or more options offered by the legal system in its application to a specific case.
The Constitutional Judgment No. 186/2000, Rec. Amparo Appeal 2,662/1997, of July 10, 2000, has had the opportunity to rule on the extent of the principle of proportionality. Indeed, according to the doctrine of the high court: “the constitutionality of any restrictive measure of fundamental rights is determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality. For the purposes that matter here, it is sufficient to recall that (as summarized by SSTC 66/1995, of May 8, FJ 5; 55/1996, of March 28, FFJJ 6, 7, 8, and 9; 207/1996, of December 16, FJ 4 e), and 37/1998, of February 17, FJ 8) to verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions:
if such a measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test);
if, in addition, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test);
and, finally, if it is balanced or proportionate, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict sense proportionality test).”
Indeed, suitability consists of the causal relationship, from means to end, between the adopted means and the proposed end. That is to say, it is an analysis of a means-end relationship.
For the case mentioned in the consultation, and without prejudice to the analysis that the data controller must carry out, by way of example, it could be stated that the communication to the Prevention Delegates of the names and surnames of the workers who have suffered an accident or occupational illness and who have been on leave for more than one day could be suitable for the Prevention Delegates to exercise the powers conferred upon them by the LPRL in Article 36.1 d) LPRL.

In relation to the necessity, this will consist of examining whether there are other alternative means to the chosen one that are not burdensome or at least are burdensome to a lesser extent. This involves the analysis of a means-means relationship, that is, a comparison between means; the chosen one and the hypothetical one or ones that could have been adopted to achieve the same end.

In the case presented, considering that it involves large workforces, it should be assessed whether knowing the department or division of the workplace to which the personal identification number corresponds would be sufficient to know where the accident occurred.
And finally, strict proportionality or weighting involves comparing the degree of realization or optimization of the end and the intensity of the intervention in the right.
In this case, it should be evaluated whether there are more advantages or benefits for the general interest than detriments for the worker, taking into account whether there are other means to know where, when, and how the accident in question occurred, without the name and surnames of the worker related to their health data having to be communicated. 
The detriment or interference in the rights of the affected party due to the mere processing of health data is proportional to the scope of the protection that the GDPR itself and other sectoral regulations provide to health-related information.

Regarding the principle of confidentiality, Article 5 of the

1. The data controllers and processors, as well as all persons involved
in any phase of this, shall be subject to the duty of confidentiality referred to in
Article 5.1.f) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
2. The general obligation indicated in the previous section shall be

This principle, along with those described in Article 5 of the GDPR, serves
the purpose of ensuring that the processing of personal data respects the privacy
of the affected individuals and is effective through the use of instruments such as
anonymization or pseudonymization.
The main difference between the two is the existence or not of
reversibility of the identification of the data being processed; therefore, whether
they are subject to the protection scope of the GDPR or, on the contrary, whether
they are excluded.
Article 4.5) of the GDPR considers "pseudonymization" as:
the processing of personal data in such a way that the data can no longer be
attributed to a data subject without the use of additional information, provided
that such additional information is kept separately and is subject to technical and
organizational measures intended to ensure that the personal data cannot be
attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person;
Regarding the application of the GDPR to anonymous or pseudonymized
data, Recital 26 states the following:
“The principles of data protection should apply to all information
concerning an identified or identifiable natural person. Pseudonymized personal
data, which could be attributed to a natural person by the use of additional
information, should be considered information about an identifiable natural
person. To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means,
including singling out, that could reasonably be used by the data controller or
any other person to identify the natural person directly or indirectly should be
taken into account. To determine whether there is a reasonable likelihood that
means will be used to identify a natural person, all objective factors should be
considered, such as the costs and time required for identification, taking into
account both the technology available at the time of processing and technological
advancements. Therefore, the principles of data protection should not apply to
anonymous information, that is, information that does not relate to an identified
or identifiable natural person, nor to data that has been rendered anonymous in
such a way that the data subject is not identifiable, or ceases to be so. As a
result, this Regulation does not affect the processing of such anonymous
information, including for statistical or research purposes.

provision does not refer to reversibility or the possibility of identification using
additional information.
