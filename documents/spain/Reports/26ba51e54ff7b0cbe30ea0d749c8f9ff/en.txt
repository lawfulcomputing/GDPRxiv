The inquiry raises the question of what the legal basis would be that determines the lawfulness of processing the location data of the caller by mobile phone to emergency services through the use of AML (Advance Mobile Location).  

As described in the inquiry, AML is a mechanism, already deployed in some countries, by which emergency call centers (112 services and similar, known as PSAPs by their acronym in English) can automatically receive information about the location of the caller (when calling from a mobile phone) with a precision far superior to what can currently be obtained through the information provided by mobile network operators, based on the location of the base station from which the call originates.  

AML is independent of the operator, as it operates on the mobile phone (directly from the operating system, so it does not require the user to download an app or make prior configuration). When it detects that a call is being made to an emergency number, AML activates the mobile's high-precision location (typically obtained from nearby WIFI or Bluetooth networks, or from a GNSS service, such as GPS or Galileo) and generates a message with the location coordinates. The message (which can be an SMS, a data message, or both) is sent to a predefined number and/or URL for each country. The service can be configured so that the message is resent at regular intervals while the voice call remains active.  

Furthermore, the inquiry states that PSAPs in countries where AML has already been deployed unanimously acknowledge the improvement it brings to the response of emergency services, as it allows them to have much more precise information about the location they need to go to, and that in Spain, the 112 services, managed by the Autonomous Communities, are aware of these improvements and have repeatedly requested that the deployment of AML be activated in our country. For its part, the European Emergency Number Association (EENA) promotes and supports the deployment of this system and disseminates information about the technology that can be consulted on its website https://eena.org/aml/.

II  

Firstly, this inquiry refers to the processing of location data obtained directly by the mobile terminal used, understanding location data, in accordance with Article 2 of Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of July 12, 2002, on the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on privacy and electronic communications), as amended by Directive 2009/136/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of November 25, 2009, as "any data processed in an electronic communications network or by an electronic communications service that indicates the geographical position of the terminal equipment of a user of a publicly available electronic communications service."
Legal Office
As stated in Opinion 5/2005 of the Article 29 Working Party regarding the use of geolocation data for the provision of value-added services, there has been a spectacular increase in the use of location data in recent years, due to the “exponential increase in the use of satellite location data, which can currently be very precise and valuable, especially with regard to assisting people in distress” and to “the unprecedented spread of mobile telephony, thanks to which every user always carries a device through which they can be located.”

Thus, the aforementioned Opinion highlighted that “given that the processing of these data is a particularly sensitive issue as it relates to the essential question of the free movement of persons in an anonymous manner, the European legislator, taking into account the considerations of the European data protection authorities, has adopted specific rules that establish the obligation to obtain the consent of users or subscribers before proceeding with the processing of location data necessary for providing a value-added service and to inform users or subscribers of the conditions of such processing (Article 9 of Directive 2002/58/EC of July 12, 2002).”

On the other hand, attention must be paid to the definition of value-added services provided in paragraph g) of that Directive, which qualifies as such “Any service that requires the processing of traffic data or location data other than traffic data that goes beyond what is necessary for the transmission of a communication or its billing.”

Article 9 of Directive 2002/58/EC regarding location data other than traffic data states the following:
“1. In the event that location data, other than traffic data, relating to users or subscribers of public communications networks or publicly available electronic communications services may be processed, such data may only be processed if they are made anonymous, or with the prior consent of the users or subscribers, to the extent and for the time necessary for the provision of a value-added service. The service provider must inform users or subscribers, before obtaining their consent, of the type of location data other than traffic data that will be processed, the purpose and duration of the processing, and whether the data will be transmitted to a third party for the purpose of providing the value-added service. Users and subscribers must be offered the possibility to withdraw their consent for the processing of location data other than traffic data at any time.”

However, in the present case, we find ourselves in a different situation, considering that it would not properly be a value-added service and, especially, regarding the purpose pursued by the processing, which consists of facilitating the location of the caller by mobile phone to emergency services through emergency call centers (services 112 and similar) and which is subject to specific regulation in our legal system.

Then, the phone sends an automatic SMS to the emergency services with the location of the caller, before turning off the GNSS again. The service may also use Wi-Fi, depending on which is better at a given moment.

Therefore, this system is much more accurate than the one currently employed, in which determining the location from which the call is made requires a search of the telephone network servicing the caller, establishing only an approximate location based on the antennas handling the call. However, with the AML mechanism, according to EENA, the system is 4,000 times more accurate than traditional GSM location, with a total of 85% of calls located within a radius of less than 50 meters, while with mobile network location, the radius can extend several kilometers.

In this regard, Opinion 13/2011 of the Article 29 Working Party on geolocation services in smart mobile devices already indicated that the positioning method using data from base stations “provides a quick and general idea of the location, but is not very precise compared to GPS and Wifi data. The accuracy is approximately 50 meters in densely populated urban areas and up to several kilometers in rural areas,” while GPS technology “offers exact positioning, between 4 and 15 meters.”

Hence, several countries have already implemented this technology (according to the EENA website, as of June 2019, Austria, Belgium, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, Ireland, Lithuania, Moldova, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Slovenia, the United Arab Emirates, the United Kingdom, and the United States would have implemented it) and European authorities promote its use, as stated in Article 109.6 of Directive 2018/1972 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of December 11, establishing the European Electronic Communications Code (already transcribed) and based on its Recital 290:

Nevertheless, terminal-based location technologies have proven to be significantly more accurate and cost-effective due to the availability of data provided by the European Geostationary Navigation Overlay Service, the Galileo satellite system, and other global satellite navigation systems and Wi-Fi data. Therefore, the information regarding the caller's location obtained from the terminal must complement the network-based location information, even if it is only available after the emergency communication has been established. Member States must ensure that, whenever possible, the caller's location obtained from the terminal is made available to the most appropriate PSAP. This may not be possible in some cases, for example, when the location is not available on the terminal or through the interpersonal communications service used, or when it is not technically feasible to obtain this information. Additionally, Member States must ensure that PSAPs are capable of retrieving and managing the available information regarding the caller's location, as long as it is feasible. The establishment and transmission of such information must be free of charge for both the end user and the authority responsible for processing the emergency communication, regardless of the means of establishment (for example, via the terminal or the network) or transmission (for example, through a voice channel, SMS, or IP).

Therefore, the processing of location data would be covered under letter d) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR: the processing is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or another natural person, regardless of who carries out such processing, since in this case the determining factor for the lawfulness of the processing is the protection of an essential interest for life, as highlighted in Recital 46:
“The processing of personal data should also be considered lawful when it is necessary to protect an essential interest for the life of the data subject or that of another natural person. In principle, personal data should only be processed on the basis of the vital interest of another natural person when the processing cannot be manifestly based on another legal basis. Certain types of processing may respond to both important public interest reasons and the vital interests of the data subject, such as when the processing is necessary for humanitarian purposes, including the control of epidemics and their spread, or in situations of humanitarian emergency, especially in the event of natural or human-made disasters.”

It must be understood that the processing of data generated from telephone calls made to the Emergency number 112 is significant and falls within the scope of application of Organic Law 15/1999 from the moment personal data of individuals contacting this service must or may be collected. This determines the full application of the provisions of this regulation concerning the processing of such personal data, considering for these purposes what is established in Article 3 c) of Organic Law 15/1999, which defines data processing as "automated or non-automated operations and technical procedures that allow for the collection, recording, storage, processing, modification, blocking, and cancellation, as well as the transfers of data resulting from communications, inquiries, interconnections, and transfers." However, paragraph 2 exempts this requirement in cases where the processing of data aims to protect a vital interest of the affected party, thus referring to situations where the processing is necessary to safeguard the vital interest of the affected party or another person.

II
        Having established the above, it is necessary to examine whether there is legitimacy for the intended data transfer within the studied legal framework. 
        The transfer or communication of personal data, defined in Article 3.i) of the LOPD as “any disclosure of data made to a person other than the data subject,” is regulated in Article 11 of the same Organic Law, which in principle requires the consent of the affected party, although it provides for a series of exceptions. However, when it comes to sensitive or specially protected data, the provisions of Article 7 of the LOPD must also be adhered to. 
        Moreover, the legal analysis of the issue must also consider the provisions of Directive 1995/46/EC of October 24, 1995, regarding the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data. 
                
The Directive defines the processing of personal data in its Article 3 b) as “any operation or set of operations, whether or not performed by automated means, applied to personal data, such as collection, recording, organization, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form that facilitates access to them, comparison or interconnection, as well as their blocking, erasure, or destruction.”
Consequently, the transfer of data is included in the general concept of processing provided by the Directive, which, in turn, reproduces Article 3 c) of the Organic Law, which defines processing as “Automated or non-automated operations and technical procedures that allow for the collection, recording, storage, processing, modification, blocking, and cancellation, as well as the transfers of data resulting from communications, consultations, interconnections, and transfers.”

Articles 7 and 8 of the Directive establish, respectively, the legitimizing grounds for the processing of personal data, differentiating between the general grounds in the first of the provisions and those related to sensitive or specially protected data in the second.

In Article 7 of the Directive, we highlight paragraph d), which states that “Member States shall provide that the processing of personal data may only be carried out if: (…) d) it is necessary to protect the vital interest of the data subject.”

Article 11.2 f) adds in its first clause that the transfer of data may occur without the consent of the data subject “when the transfer of personal data related to health is necessary to address an emergency that requires access to a file.”

The aforementioned provisions must, as has been indicated, be interpreted in accordance with community law, given that they are a transposition into domestic law of Directive 95/46/EC. Therefore, the Organic Law establishes an authorization for the processing without consent of the specially protected data of the data subject, based on the necessary assistance to them in emergency situations, enabling in Article 7.6 access to specially protected data in cases where the affected person cannot provide that consent and in Article 11.2 f) access to the medical history of the patient when the urgency of the situation so requires.
