The inquiry raises whether it is in accordance with the provisions established in the data protection regulations for entities providing health surveillance services to third parties to communicate to the employer those extra services that employees have the right to request (for example, certain vaccines or extended medical examinations) that employers are obligated to pay to the entities providing them. It indicates that for companies to pay for such services, they require that the invoice specifies the extra services that the specific employee has requested, in order to verify that such service is within the catalog of services that employees have the right to request.  

In order to respond to this inquiry, it must be taken into account that as of May 25, 2018, the Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), applies, thus displacing the application of Organic Law 15/1999. Therefore, the issue raised must be examined in light of the provisions in the same.  

Said Regulation defines in its Article 4.15 health data as “personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status.”  

Recital 35 of the Regulation clarifies this definition by stating that “Among the personal data related to health, all data concerning the health status of the data subject that provide information about their physical or mental health past, present, or future must be included. This includes information about the natural person collected in connection with their registration for health care purposes, or in connection with the provision of such care, in accordance with Directive 2011/24/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council; any number, symbol, or data assigned to a natural person that uniquely identifies them for health purposes; information obtained from tests or examinations of a part of the body or of a bodily substance, including that derived from genetic data and biological samples, and any information relating, by way of example, to a disease, a disability, the risk of suffering from diseases, medical history, clinical treatment, or the physiological or biomedical status of the data subject, regardless of their…

Consequently, the data referred to in the consultation will have the status of health-related data, the communication of which to the employer by the entities providing health surveillance services will constitute data processing, as defined in Article 4.2 of the aforementioned Regulation, according to which it will be considered “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or modification, extraction, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, limitation, erasure or destruction.”
An essential condition for data processing to be lawful is that the entity carrying it out is authorized to do so in the manner provided for in the aforementioned Regulation (EU) 2016/679. In this regard, it should be noted that Article 9 of said Regulation, concerning the processing of special categories of personal data, establishes that “1. The processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation is prohibited.”
However, paragraph 2 of said article provides for certain exceptions to this prohibition, of which it is relevant to examine the one contained in its letter b). This provision states that “Paragraph 1 shall not apply when one of the following circumstances applies: (…) b) the processing is necessary for compliance with obligations and the exercise of specific rights of the data controller or the data subject in the field of employment law and social security and protection, to the extent that it is authorized by Union law or the law of the Member States or a collective agreement under the law of the Member States that establishes adequate safeguards for the respect of fundamental rights and the interests of the data subject.”
It must therefore be examined whether the data processing referred to in the consultation is necessary for compliance with the obligations imposed by Spanish legislation on employers in relation to workers.
The legal framework on the subject of the consultation is constituted by Law 31/1995, of November 8, on the Prevention of Occupational Risks and the implementing provisions of said regulation.

In this regard, Article 2 of the aforementioned Law, in establishing its purpose, declares that “The purpose of this Law is to promote the safety and health of workers through the application of measures and the development of the activities necessary for the prevention of risks arising from work,” providing that for such purposes the Law establishes “the general principles related to the prevention of occupational risks for the protection of safety and health, the elimination or reduction of risks arising from work” and that to fulfill these objectives “it will regulate the actions to be developed by public administrations, as well as by employers, workers, and their respective representative organizations.”
Finally, said article in its number 2 determines that “the labor provisions contained in this Law and its regulatory norms shall in any case have the character of minimum mandatory law that cannot be waived, and may be improved and developed in collective agreements.”
For its part, Article 6 of the aforementioned Law provides for the regulatory development of the law in aspects such as the modalities of organization, operation, and control of prevention services (letter e); working conditions or specific preventive measures in particularly dangerous jobs, especially if special medical controls are provided for them, or when risks arise from certain characteristics or special situations of workers (letter f); or the procedure for the qualification of occupational diseases, as well as requirements and procedures for communication and information to the competent authority regarding damages arising from work (letter g).
In its Article 14, it recognizes the right of workers to effective protection in matters of safety and health at work. To comply with this, the Law establishes as an obligation of the company to constitute a prevention service responsible for the activities of prevention and protection against occupational risks. For the performance of this activity, it must either have its own prevention service or contract with an adequately accredited external prevention service.
Regarding health surveillance, Article 22.1 imposes on the employer the obligation to guarantee workers in their service “the periodic surveillance of their state of health based on the inherent risks of the work,” stating in its second paragraph the voluntary nature of such surveillance except for the cases it exempts, providing that “This surveillance may only be carried out when the worker gives their consent. This voluntary nature shall only be excepted, prior to a report from the representatives of the workers, in cases where the performance of the examinations is essential to assess the effects of working conditions on the health of workers or to verify whether the state of health of the worker may pose a danger to themselves, to other workers, or to other persons related to the company, or when so required.”

Notwithstanding the above, the employer and persons or bodies with responsibilities in the area of prevention shall be informed of the conclusions derived from the assessments carried out regarding the worker's fitness for the performance of their job or the need to introduce or improve protective and preventive measures, so that they can properly carry out their functions in preventive matters.”
From all of this, it follows that the employer is obliged to provide, through their own health service or with the external service with which they have contracted health surveillance, the surveillance actions that are established either in the regulations developing the Occupational Risk Prevention Law or other regulations, or those agreed upon in collective agreements, to the extent that through these, such health surveillance activities can be improved. This obviously determines their obligation to pay the invoices received from the companies with which they have contracted such surveillance services.
The issue centers on whether the employer can know which of their employees have requested what the consultation refers to as extra services (extension of assessments, vaccinations) in order to verify if they are obliged to pay for them in each case. Article 22 of Law 31/1995, previously examined, establishes the principle of “confidentiality of all information related to health,” limiting access to medical information to medical personnel and health authorities conducting health surveillance. However, in its second number, it states that the employer shall be informed of the conclusions derived from the assessments carried out in relation to the fitness of the worker for their job.

It follows that the knowledge of which workers have undergone medical examinations is not contrary to this principle of confidentiality, provided that the limits established in the provision are absolutely respected, and the employer does not have access to the resulting conclusions except in the cases permitted in Article 22.4 and within the limits established in that provision. 
On the other hand, the employer is obliged, as stipulated in Article 23.1 d) of Law 31/1995, to “prepare and keep available to the labor authority” the documentation related to the “Practice of health checks of workers provided for in Article 22 of this Law and the conclusions obtained from them in the terms set out in the last paragraph of section 4 of the aforementioned article,” from which it also seems to follow that they must be aware of which employees have participated in the health surveillance actions that they are obliged to provide. 
Consequently, the fact that the employer is informed of which employees have undergone the medical examinations that the employer must provide (or an extension thereof or have received a vaccine, in the terms set by the regulations governing such obligation in each case or the collective agreements in which they have been agreed) for the purpose of reimbursement by the employer of the invoices to the entity providing the prevention services that has actually carried out the medical activity could be legitimized under the provisions of Article 9.b of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, as long as it is framed within the limits established by the regulations on occupational risk prevention. 
However, it must be taken into account that any processing of personal data must respect the principles set out in Article 5 of the aforementioned Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the first paragraph letter a) of which requires that personal data be “processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (‘lawfulness, fairness, and transparency’)” 
Recital 39 states in this regard that “All processing of personal data must be lawful and fair. For natural persons, it must be completely clear that personal data concerning them is being collected, used, consulted, or otherwise processed, as well as the extent to which such data is or will be processed. The principle of transparency requires that all information and communication relating to the processing of such data be easily accessible and easy to understand, and that clear and plain language be used. This principle particularly refers to the information of the data subjects about the identity of the data controller and the purposes of the processing, as well as the additional information to ensure fair and transparent processing with respect to the affected natural persons and their right to obtain.

Article 13 of the Regulation governs the information that must be provided when personal data is obtained from the data subject, stating the following: 
“1. When personal data relating to a data subject is obtained from that data subject, the data controller, at the time of obtaining this data, shall provide all the information indicated below: 
a) the identity and contact details of the controller and, where applicable, of their representative; 
b) the contact details of the data protection officer, where applicable; 
c) the purposes of the processing for which the personal data are intended and the legal basis for the processing; 
d) when the processing is based on Article 6(1)(f), the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or a third party; 
e) the recipients or categories of recipients of the personal data, where applicable; 
f) where applicable, the intention of the controller to transfer personal data to a third country or international organization and the existence or absence of an adequacy decision by the Commission, or, in the case of transfers referred to in Articles 46 or 47 or Article 49(1), second paragraph, reference to the appropriate or suitable safeguards and the means to obtain a copy of these or the fact that they have been provided.

2. In addition to the information mentioned in paragraph 1, the data controller shall provide the data subject, at the time the personal data is obtained, with the following information necessary to ensure fair and transparent data processing: 
a) the period for which the personal data will be stored or, when that is not possible, the criteria used to determine that period; 
b) the existence of the right to request from the data controller access to personal data relating to the data subject, and their rectification or erasure, or the restriction of processing, or to object to the processing, as well as the right to data portability; 
c) when the processing is based on Article 6(1)(a) or Article 9(2)(a), the existence of the right to withdraw consent at any time, without affecting the lawfulness of the processing based on consent before its withdrawal; 
d) the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority; 
e) whether the communication of personal data is a legal or contractual requirement, or a requirement necessary to enter into a contract, and whether the data subject is obliged to provide the personal data and has been informed of the possible consequences of not providing such data; 
f) the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling, referred to in Article 22(1) and (4), and, at least in such cases, meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the anticipated consequences of such processing for the data subject.

3. When the data controller intends to process personal data for a purpose other than that for which it was collected, they shall provide the data subject, prior to such further processing, information about that other purpose and any relevant additional information in accordance with paragraph 2.

4. The provisions of paragraphs 1, 2, and 3 shall not apply when and to the extent that the data subject already has the information.”

Consequently, the fair processing of data by the service provider determines that, when collecting data from employees participating in health surveillance activities, it must inform them in accordance with the provisions of this regulation, which implies that they are aware of the communication of data that will be made to the employer (recipient of the data) and the purpose for which it is communicated, which must be expressed in clear and easily understandable language.
