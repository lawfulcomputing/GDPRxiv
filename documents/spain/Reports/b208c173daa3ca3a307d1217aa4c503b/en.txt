The consultation raises a series of questions related to the legitimacy for the processing of personal data through the exchange of information from the medical history of workers between the doctors of the public health service and the doctors of the Mutual Societies.  

As noted in the consultation, the Mutual Societies and the Public Health Services (SPS) of the Autonomous Communities are analyzing how to share the clinical information for which they are responsible for processing independently, specifically:  
- On one hand, it is intended that the doctors of the Mutual Societies have telematic access to the clinical information that the SPS have about the workers protected by these Entities who are in a situation of medical leave due to Common or Professional Contingency affected by a Temporary Disability process due to Common or Professional Contingency who have received healthcare in the Organizations dependent on the SPS, with the purpose of verifying their health circumstances, so that the doctor of the Mutual Societies can adequately carry out any of the activities that are legally assigned to them.  
- On the other hand, it is advisable that the doctors of the SPS have telematic access to the medical histories of the workers protected by the Mutual Societies.  

I  
To respond to this consultation, it must start from the regulation contained in Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), which displaces the application of Organic Law 15/1999, so the question raised must be examined in light of what is provided therein.  

This Regulation defines in its Article 4.15 health data as “personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status.”  

Recital 35 of the Regulation clarifies this definition by stating that “Among the personal data related to health, all data related to the health status of the data subject that provide information about their physical or mental health past, present, or future must be included. This includes information about the natural person collected in connection with their registration for health care purposes, or in connection with the provision of such care, in accordance with Directive 2011/24/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council; any number, symbol, or data assigned to a natural person that uniquely identifies them for health purposes; information obtained from tests or examinations of a part of the body or a bodily substance, including that derived from genetic data and biological samples, and any information relating, by way of example, to a disease, a disability, the risk of suffering from diseases, medical history, clinical treatment, or the physiological or biomedical status of the data subject, regardless of its source, for example, a doctor or another health professional, a hospital, a medical device, or an in vitro diagnostic test.”  

The Court of Justice (CJEU) interpreted this concept broadly, considering as health data the information related to all aspects, both physical and mental, of a person's health, both regarding their health status (that they have injured a foot) and that they are in a situation of partial leave (CJEU ruling of November 6, 2003, Lindqvist, C-101/01).  

For its part, number 2 of Article 4 defines the processing of data as “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”

II
Therefore, health data processing must be carried out, in any case, in accordance with the provisions established by European law and by national regulations with the force of law that govern them.
Regarding legal limitations, it should be noted that the right to the protection of personal data is a fundamental right, the content of which consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that empowers the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which data this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who holds that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose that possession or use” (STC 76/2019, of May 22, and STC 292/2000, of November 30). Furthermore, these rulings also highlighted the need for any interference to be provided for by law or a regulation of the European Union, while respecting, in any case, the principle of proportionality.
Specifically, the Constitutional Court, in the aforementioned STC 76/2019, of May 22, after citing, among others, its previous STC 292/2000, of November 30, states:
- Secondly, by express mandate of the Constitution, any state interference in the field of fundamental rights and public freedoms, whether it directly affects their development (art. 81.1 CE) or limits or conditions their exercise (art. 53.1 CE), requires a legal authorization (see, among others, STC 49/1999, of April 5, FJ 4). In STC 49/1999, FJ 4, we defined the constitutional function of this legal reservation in the following terms:
This legal reservation to which the Spanish Constitution generally subjects the regulation of fundamental rights and public freedoms recognized in its Title I serves a dual function, namely: on the one hand, it ensures that the rights attributed to citizens by the Constitution are not affected by any unauthorized state interference; and, on the other hand, in a legal system like ours where Judges and Magistrates are subject "only to the rule of Law" and there is, in essence, no binding precedent (SSTC 8/1981, 34/1995, 47/1995, and 96/1996), it ultimately constitutes the only effective way to guarantee the requirements of legal certainty in the field of fundamental rights and public freedoms. Therefore, regarding our legal system, we have characterized legal certainty as a sum of legality and certainty of the Law (STC 27/1981, legal foundation 10).”
This dual function of the legal reservation translates into a dual requirement: on the one hand, the necessary intervention of the law to authorize the interference; and, on the other hand, that legal norm “must meet all those indispensable characteristics as a guarantee of legal certainty,” that is, “it must express each and every one of the assumptions and conditions of the intervention” (STC 49/1999, FJ 4). In other words, “it not only excludes delegations in favor of regulatory norms [...], but also implies other

Regarding the characteristics of the laws that intend to regulate the processing of special categories of data, the Constitutional Court has already extensively ruled in the aforementioned Judgment No. 76/2019 of May 22. 
The ruling first analyzes the legal regime to which the processing of special categories of data is subject under the GDPR:
 "According to paragraph 1 of Article 9 GDPR, the processing of personal data revealing political opinions is prohibited, just as the processing of personal data revealing ethnic or racial origin, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union affiliation, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, data related to health, or data related to the sexual life or sexual orientation of a natural person is prohibited. However, paragraph 2 of the same provision authorizes the processing of all those data when any of the ten circumstances provided there [letters a) to j)] occur. Some of those circumstances have a limited scope of application (labor, social, associative, health, judicial, etc.) or respond to a specific purpose, thus, in themselves, delimit the specific treatments they authorize as exceptions to the general rule. Furthermore, the enabling effectiveness of several of the cases provided there is conditioned on the Union Law or that of the Member States expressly providing for and regulating them within their scope of competencies: this is the case for the circumstances listed in letters a), b), g), h), i), and j).
The processing of special categories of personal data is one of the areas in which the General Data Protection Regulation has expressly recognized that Member States have "room for maneuver" when it comes to "specifying their rules," as stated in its recital 10. This legislative configuration margin extends both to the determination of the enabling causes for the processing of specially protected personal data - that is, to the identification of the essential public interest purposes and the assessment of the proportionality of the processing to the pursued end, essentially respecting the right to data protection - as well as to the establishment of "appropriate and specific measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject" [art. 9.2 g) GDPR]. The Regulation therefore contains a specific obligation for Member States to establish such guarantees, in the event that they enable the processing of specially protected personal data."
On the other hand, regarding the guarantees that the legislator must adopt, the aforementioned Judgment No. 76/2019 of May 22 reminds us that 
"In light of the potential intrusive effects on the affected fundamental right resulting from the processing of personal data, the jurisprudence of this Court requires the legislator to not only comply with the aforementioned requirements but also to establish adequate guarantees of a technical, organizational, and procedural nature, which prevent risks of varying probability and severity and mitigate their effects, as only in this way can respect for the essential content of the fundamental right itself be ensured."

Notwithstanding the above, it should be noted that access to the medical history in the terms proposed by the inquiry implies that doctors who do not participate in the patient's care process will know certain confidential information, so such processing must be carried out in compliance with certain guarantees that respond to preserving the right to privacy and the protection of data of the affected individuals. 
The LAP itself in its article 7 recognizes the right to privacy indicating the following: 
“1. Every person has the right to have the confidentiality of data concerning their health respected, and that no one may access it without prior authorization protected by law.
2. Health centers will adopt the necessary measures to guarantee the rights referred to in the previous section, and will develop, when appropriate, the rules and standardized procedures that ensure legal access to patient data.”
And in the same vein, the aforementioned article 16 LAP section 6 states that “The personnel who access the data of the medical history in the exercise of their functions are subject to the duty of confidentiality.”
For its part, the GDPR incorporates the principles of minimization, purpose limitation, and confidentiality, as provided in article 5 sections b), c), and f) of the GDPR which indicate that personal data will be:
b) collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and not further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; according to article 89, section 1, the further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (“purpose limitation”);
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”);
f) processed in a manner that ensures an adequate level of security for personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, through the application of appropriate technical or organizational measures (“integrity and confidentiality”).
For its part, the LOPDGDD establishes in article 5 under the heading “Duty of Confidentiality” the following:
1. The data controllers and processors, as well as all persons involved in any phase of this, shall be subject to the duty of confidentiality referred to in article 5.1.f) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
2. The general obligation indicated in the previous section shall be complementary to the professional secrecy duties in accordance with the applicable regulations.
3. The obligations established in the previous sections shall remain in effect even when the relationship of the obligated party with the data controller or processor has ended.
Compliance with these principles implies that the access carried out will serve solely and exclusively for the purpose of evaluation, control, and monitoring of temporary incapacity processes with the qualitative and quantitative limitations inherent to the purpose for which access is granted.

Regarding their legal position in relation to the processing of personal data, they hold the status of data controller, as we indicated in Report 100/2018: 
Thus, the Mutuals will act as data controllers regarding those personal data processing activities they carry out in the exercise of the functions attributed to them by the consolidated text of the General Social Security Law, approved by Royal Legislative Decree 8/2015, of October 30, when companies and self-employed workers contract with them for professional contingencies, the economic benefit for temporary incapacity arising from common contingencies, and the protection for cessation of activity, since, in such cases, access to the data by the Mutuals does not constitute processing carried out on behalf of the companies that contract with them and subject to their instructions, but such processing is determined by the Social Security Law itself.
