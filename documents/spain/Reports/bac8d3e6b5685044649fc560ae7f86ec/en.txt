The inquiry raises several questions regarding the compliance with the GDPR and LOPDGDD of certain personal data processing in the hiring and delivery of products by telecommunications operators and financial entities through messaging and/or parcel delivery entities, as well as the considerations these entities must have concerning the data processing that arises from the provision of such services.  

It is requested, based on factual assumptions analyzed in various sanctioning procedures resolved by this Agency, whether the capture of the image of the ID card by messaging entities in different scenarios is compliant with data protection regulations: in the delivery of goods acquired through financing to comply with anti-money laundering regulations; in the delivery of duplicate SIM cards; and in the delivery of mobile devices.  

I  
As a preliminary matter, it should be noted, as mentioned, that the inquirer is based on factual assumptions that have been analyzed in sanctioning procedures processed by the General Subdirectorate of Data Inspection of this Agency—some expressly cited and others indirectly referenced—and that respond to a specific case law, so this report will not examine what has been resolved in those cases. There are neither sufficient elements of judgment nor is it one of the functions of this Legal Cabinet.  

The object of this report will focus on the general issues that can be extracted from the inquiry raised and not on the adequacy of specific measures for specific treatments, as the establishment of the latter is, precisely, the responsibility of the data controllers, and, where applicable, data processors, in accordance with the new model of proactive responsibility established in the legal framework of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.  

Indeed, proactive responsibility implies the implementation of a compliance and management model of the GDPR that determines the generalized compliance with obligations regarding data protection. It includes the analysis, planning, establishment, maintenance, updating, and control of data protection policies in an organization—understood as the set of guidelines that govern the actions of an organization, practices, procedures, and tools, among others—from privacy by design and by default, which ensure compliance with the GDPR, prevent the materialization of risks, and allow the controller to demonstrate compliance.  

Determining what measures are appropriate for the treatments cited by the inquirer or validating those proposed, with the information provided and from the perspective of a sanctioned entity, with various procedures open in the administrative pathway and others challenged in the judicial pathway, would, on one hand, be contrary to the new model that the GDPR implements, in which the system has shifted from being reactive to becoming proactive. “[A]t the present time, it must be taken into account that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to personal data protection, which is now based on the principle of accountability or proactive responsibility, as repeatedly pointed out by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and which is reflected in the Explanatory Memorandum of the LOPDGDD: the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or the processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.”  

That is to say, only the data controller knows well the context in which the GDPR must be applied, and therefore, it is they who must determine the most appropriate measures based on the risk analysis for each processing activity.  

On the other hand, issuing a ruling as requested by the inquirer, far from contributing to legal certainty, would have the opposite effect, as it could be interpreted as a general rule regarding issues that stem from and are based on specific factual assumptions with a marked case-by-case component and evident vested interest.

To this end, it is necessary to start from the definitions established by the GDPR in its Article 4:
7) “data controller” or “controller”: the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the data controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States;
8) “data processor” or “processor”: the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller;

Without prejudice to the attribution of direct obligations to the processor, the aforementioned Guidelines, based on the fact that the concepts of controller and processor under the GDPR have not changed compared to Directive 95/46/EC and that, in general, the criteria for attributing the different roles remain the same (paragraph 11), reiterate that these are functional concepts, aimed at assigning responsibilities according to the actual roles of the parties (paragraph 12), which implies that in most cases, the specific circumstances of the case must be taken into account (case by case) based on their actual activities rather than the formal designation of an actor as "controller" or "processor" (for example, in a contract), as well as autonomous concepts, whose interpretation must be carried out under the European regulations on personal data protection (paragraph 13), and taking into account (paragraph 24) that the need for a factual assessment also means that the role of a data controller does not derive from the nature of an entity processing data but from its specific activities in a specific context, so that the same entity may act simultaneously as a data controller for certain processing operations and as a processor for others, and the qualification as controller or processor must be evaluated with respect to each specific data processing activity.

In any case, the legal relationship established between the parties must be analyzed carefully and in depth in order to identify who determines the purposes and means, for which the repeatedly cited Guidelines of the EDPB provide different criteria that can serve to establish such positions, starting from the fact that the word "determine" implies actually exercising influence over the purposes and means, for which it is not an obstacle that the service is defined in a specific way by the processor, as long as a detailed description is presented to the controller and it can make the final decision on how the processing is carried out and request changes if necessary, without the processor being able to subsequently introduce modifications to the essential elements of the processing without the controller's approval (paragraph 28) or that a certain margin of maneuver is recognized to the processor to make some decisions regarding the processing (paragraph 35), allowing the processor to make decisions about non-essential means (paragraph 39), so that the processor must not process the data in any way other than in accordance with the instructions of the controller, without prejudice to the fact that such instructions may leave a certain degree of discretion on how to best serve the interests of the controller, allowing the processor to choose the most appropriate technical and organizational measures (paragraph 78). 

Moreover, another criterion to consider is whether the entity involved in the processing does not pursue any own purpose in relation to the processing, but is simply paid for the services rendered, since in this case, it would act, in principle, more as a processor than as a controller (paragraph 60).

Therefore, although as noted in our Report 11/2020, the figure of the data processor responds to the need to address phenomena such as the outsourcing of services by companies and other entities, the EDPB reminds us that not all service providers that process personal data in the course of providing a service are a "processor" in the sense of the GDPR, as it does not depend on the nature of the entity processing the data, but rather on its specific activities in a particular context. Thus, if the processing does not constitute a key element of the service, the service provider may be in a position to independently determine the purposes and means of that processing required to provide the service, in which case it may be considered a controller and not a processor. Conversely, reiterating that the EDPB emphasizes the necessity of a case-by-case analysis to determine the degree of influence that each entity effectively has in determining the purposes and means of processing (paragraph 80), it may still act as a processor even if the processing of personal data is not the main object of the service, provided that the service client determines the purposes and means of processing in practice (paragraph 81).
