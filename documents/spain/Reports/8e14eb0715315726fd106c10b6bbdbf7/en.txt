The inquiry raises a series of doubts regarding the interpretative criteria to be applied to the exception provided in Article 21.2 LSSI for the sending of commercial communications by electronic means.  

I  
The sending of commercial communications by electronic means is regulated by Article 21 of Law 34/2002, of July 11, on Information Society Services and Electronic Commerce, whose paragraph 1 clearly states that “The sending of advertising or promotional communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means that have not been previously requested or expressly authorized by the recipients is prohibited.” This rule would only be exempted in the case of a prior contractual relationship and “provided that the provider has lawfully obtained the contact details of the recipient and uses them to send commercial communications related to products or services of its own company that are similar to those that were initially the subject of the contract with the client,” as established in the first paragraph of paragraph 2 of the provision.  

This provision was amended by Law 32/2003, of November 3, General Telecommunications Law, as a consequence of the transposition of Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of July 12, 2002, concerning the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on Privacy and Electronic Communications). This Directive introduced the principle of “opt-in” throughout the European Union, that is, the need to obtain the prior consent of the recipient for sending emails for commercial purposes. Thus, any sending of advertising or promotional communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means is subject to the prior provision of consent, unless there is a prior contractual relationship and the subject does not express their will to the contrary.  

And it cannot be accepted, as the inquirer suggests, that Article 21 LSSI allows for tacit consent, as it is sufficiently clear in requiring express consent, as was pointed out in Recital 40 of Directive 2002/58/EC and has been reiterated by this Agency.  

The only exception that allows for the rule of express consent is the existence of a prior contractual relationship and that it concerns similar products or services in the terms indicated in Article 21.2 LSSI, noting that, in this regard, Recital 41 of Directive 2002/58/EC states the following:  

(41) In the context of a pre-existing relationship with the customer, it is reasonable to allow the use of the customer's electronic identifiers for the purpose of offering similar products or services, but exclusively by the same company that obtained the electronic identifiers in accordance with Directive 95/46/EC. At the time of collecting the electronic identifiers, the customer must be clearly and unequivocally informed about their subsequent use for direct sales purposes, and must be given the opportunity to refuse such use. This opportunity must continue to be offered to the customer each time they receive a subsequent direct sales message, at no charge other than the possible costs of transmitting this refusal.  

This means that in the matter at hand, the principle of specialty applies, so that the legitimizing causes for the processing of data referred to in Article 6 of the General Data Protection Regulation, which generally regulates the processing of personal data, must yield to the special rule contained in Law 34/2002 for these specific treatments, since otherwise this latter norm would be rendered devoid of content.  

In this sense, it is appropriate to bring to attention what was stated in the report of this Agency number 195/2017 cited by the inquirer:  

This requires, before resorting to the applicability of Article 6.1 f) of the General Data Protection Regulation, that consideration be given to the application to the case, when communications are carried out by electronic means, of what is established in Law 34/2002, of July 11, on Information Society Services and Electronic Commerce, whose Article 21 states the following:  

“1. The sending of advertising or promotional communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means that have not been previously requested or expressly authorized by the recipients is prohibited.  

2. The provisions of the previous paragraph shall not apply when there is a prior contractual relationship, provided that the provider has lawfully obtained the contact details of the recipient and uses them to send commercial communications related to products or services of its own company that are similar to those that were initially the subject of the contract with the client.”

In any case, the provider must offer the recipient the possibility to oppose the processing of their data for promotional purposes through a simple and free procedure, both at the time of data collection and in each of the commercial communications directed to them.

When the communications have been sent by email, this means must necessarily consist of the inclusion of an email address or another valid electronic address where this right can be exercised, with the sending of communications that do not include such address being prohibited.

Law 34/2002 constitutes a special regulation in relation to these activities, so the provisions of the General Data Protection Regulation cannot be invoked to resolve the issue raised at this point; rather, the provisions of this special regulation must be taken into account when the communications are carried out through electronic means.

This Agency is certainly aware of the existence of a proposed Regulation on the respect for private life and the protection of personal data in the electronic communications sector, which repeals Directive 2002/58/EC (Regulation on Privacy and Electronic Communications), whose Article 16 would refer to unsolicited communications. However, aside from the fact that this provision also starts from the principle of consent with a similar exception to that contained in the reproduced Article 21.2, it should be noted that, even if it is intended for this Regulation to apply on the same date as the full applicability of the General Data Protection Regulation, it is currently simply a proposal for a provision whose final content cannot yet be known, so the currently applicable regulation must be adhered to.

Thus, the aforementioned Article 21 operates as a limit that must be adhered to in all cases when marketing or advertising actions are carried out through electronic means, as it establishes the general rule of the express consent of the interested party for their execution unless such actions refer to “products or services of their own company that are similar to those initially contracted with the customer.”

This means that the new regime of the General Data Protection Regulation will be irrelevant regarding these actions, which would also not affect the factual assumptions analyzed by the consultation, since, as required by Law 34/2002, the express consent of the customers is necessary, with the only exception mentioned, and it has not been possible, under the currently applicable regime, to carry out this type of actions based on consent obtained in accordance with the procedure provided in Article.

II

In this regard, the issue raised has been analyzed and resolved by the National Court in Judgment of January 9, 2009, in case 97/2007, in which the fourth legal basis states the following:

"The plaintiff also argues that the email addresses of the aforementioned University are obtainable from its website and ultimately from publicly accessible sources, so according to the LOPD, which is considered to have preferential application, the consent of the recipients of the emails is not necessary. It should be noted that in the Inspection report conducted on the plaintiff, it was found that this entity possesses and uses software applications that allow the capture of email addresses collected from websites.

The interpretation made by the plaintiff cannot be accepted for the reasons that will be outlined below.

The LSSI, of July 11, aims to incorporate into the Spanish legal system Directive 2000/31/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of June 8, concerning certain aspects of information society services. Its Explanatory Memorandum states that regarding commercial communications, it prohibits their sending by email or other equivalent electronic communication means, unless the recipient has given their consent.

Subsequently, Directive 2002/58/EC of the Parliament and of the Council, of July 12, 2002, concerning the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on privacy and electronic communications), was issued, applicable to this matter, which was transposed into Law 32/2003, General Telecommunications, whose first additional provision, section number one, gave the current wording to article 21 LSSI.

In its Recital 40, the Directive states that "Guarantees must be provided to subscribers against intrusion into their privacy through unsolicited communications for direct marketing purposes, especially through automatic dialers, faxes, and email messages, including SMS. On the one hand, sending these forms of unsolicited commercial communications can be relatively easy and inexpensive, and on the other hand, it can cause annoyance and even a cost for the recipient. Furthermore, in some cases, their volume can lead to difficulties in electronic communication networks and terminal equipment. It is justified, for this type of unsolicited communications for direct marketing purposes, to require obtaining the prior express consent of the recipients before they can be sent communications of this nature."

Furthermore, in its Recital 10, it notes that in the electronic communications sector, Directive 95/46/EC applies, particularly for all issues related to the protection of fundamental rights and freedoms "that are not specifically covered by the provisions of this Directive."

That is to say, one should not first refer to the LOPD that transposes Directive 95/46/EC, but rather to the specific regulations on the matter, in this case, article 21 of the LSSI, which is a transposition of article 13 of Directive 2002/58/EC, a provision that has been infringed by the plaintiff for not having the consent of its recipients for the sending of the aforementioned emails and not having a prior contractual relationship, with the requirements established in article 21.2 LSSI that justifies the sending of the message, the only case that exempts the obtaining of consent in these cases."

This criterion has been reiterated in other subsequent judgments, such as the Judgments of April 29 and September 17, 2010, or the Judgment of September 16, 2016.

III

This criterion has not been modified as a result of the entry into force and full application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), which respects Directive 2002/58/EC as a special norm.

In this regard, Recital 173 states the following:

This Regulation shall apply to all issues relating to the protection of fundamental rights and freedoms in relation to the processing of personal data that are not subject to specific obligations with the same objective established in Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council (2), including the obligations of the data controller and the rights of natural persons.
