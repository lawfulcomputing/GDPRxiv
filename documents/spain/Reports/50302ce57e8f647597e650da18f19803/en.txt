The draft royal decree complies with the mandate of Article 41.3 of Organic Law 10/2022, of September 6, regulating those aspects that guarantee the right to access the economic aid contained in said provision for victims of sexual violence, the procedure for which must be regulated by the competent administrations of the Autonomous Communities.  

This Agency previously informed about the draft law that subsequently became the aforementioned Organic Law (LO) 10/2022, of September 6 (Report 053/2020), which considered that the accreditation of the existence of sexual violence could be carried out either (i) through a document that determines the existence of a criminal process in which there is a victim of sexual violence crimes (whether it be a conviction, a judicial resolution ordering a precautionary measure in favor of the victim, or a report from the public prosecutor establishing that there are indications that the applicant for aid is a victim of sexual violence), or (ii) through other types of resolutions not connected to criminal proceedings (report from social services, from reception services, from labor inspection, or any other title as long as it is provided for in the sectoral regulatory provisions). As a consequence of the provisions of Articles 10 of the GDPR and 10 of the LOPDGDD regarding criminal data, there were no adequate guarantees for the rights and freedoms of those interested in the draft law to carry out such treatments (STC 76/2019, of May 22). It was suggested, therefore, that an additional provision be included in the law that establishes therein, from the perspective of the regulation protecting the fundamental right to the processing of personal data, the adequate guarantees. This Additional Provision establishing such guarantees was not introduced in the legislative procedure.  

In the Project now submitted for report, this law is developed to establish the procedure for granting the aids provided for in Article 41 of LO 10/2022 to victims of sexual violence who do not have monthly income exceeding the interprofessional minimum wage calculated in accordance with the manner provided for in said law. The Project contains an Additional Provision that comprehensively regulates, and which this Agency values very positively, the necessary personal data treatments for the aforementioned purpose.  

The Regulatory Analysis Report (MAIN) dedicates, within the section on impact analysis of the project, alongside others (gender impact, economic impact, market competition impact, etc.), the impact on the fundamental right to the protection of personal data. It states that, with the collaboration of the Data Protection Delegate (DPD) of the Ministry of Equality, a risk analysis regarding data protection of the provisions contained in the draft royal decree has been carried out, and that the EVALÚA_RIESGO RGPD v2 tool from the Spanish Agency for Data Protection has been used, which aims to identify risk factors for the rights and freedoms of the interested parties whose data are present in the treatment.  

This Agency has repeatedly recommended in its reports that the pre-legislator, in those cases, such as the present one, where the treatments are legally based on Article 6.1.c) or e) of the GDPR (that is, treatments whose basis is a legal obligation or a mission of public interest), and is established by Union Law or the Law of the Member State applicable to the data controller and such Law regulates the specific operation of the treatment or set of operations in question, as is the case with the treatment operations that derive from the project being reported, make use of the possibility established in Article 35.10 of the GDPR so that the proposing body of the general provision, in the course of the procedure for creating the provision of the norm (law, royal decree, etc.) carries out a risk analysis and, where appropriate, a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) as part of a general impact assessment in the context of the adoption of said legal basis (that is, that it analyzes in the MAIN the impacts regarding data protection along with the other impacts that the MAIN usually refers to: gender impact, family impact, etc.).  

On this occasion, and very appropriately, the pre-legislator has carried out said risk analysis, has summarized its results in the MAIN, and has reflected these results in a specific provision (in this case in an Additional Provision, DA) the necessary issues related to the personal data treatments necessary to achieve the objective of the Royal Decree, which is the development of Article 41 of LO 10/2022.  

Entering into the specific content of the DA, this Agency suggests that, without prejudice to the very favorable opinion it holds, which has already been expressed, it should align, in terms of content, even more closely with the framework that appears in the MAIN on pages 22 and 23, titled “basic information on the processing of data,” which contains all the characteristics of the treatments.  

Thus, for example, the table determines who will be responsible for the treatments (Autonomous Communities and the cities of Ceuta and Melilla), which the text of the DA in the Project does not include.

The project only mentions Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR. In contrast, the Additional Provision (DA) wisely mentions as a reason that legitimizes the processing under Article 9.1 of the GDPR the concurrence of the reason in Article 9.2.h) of the GDPR, that is, when the processing is necessary for preventive or occupational medicine, assessment of the worker's capacity, medical diagnosis, provision of health or social care, or management of health and social care systems and services, provided that it is based on Union law or the law of the Member States or under a contract with a healthcare professional, and without prejudice to the conditions and guarantees set forth in paragraph 3, which refers to the fact that personal data may be processed for the purposes mentioned in paragraph 2, letter h), when such processing is carried out by a professional subject to the obligation of professional secrecy, or under their responsibility, in accordance with Union law or the law of the Member States.

In this regard, it should be added that, considering the regulation that personal data of special categories are processed (thus, from the MAIN it results that data proving the status of victim, or health or disability data will be requested for these purposes), it would have been advisable to carry out a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) within the normative procedure, which would allow the data controllers or processors not to have the obligation to carry out such an impact assessment, prescribed in Article 35.1 of the GDPR precisely because it had already been carried out during the process of drafting the general regulation.

It would be advisable, in paragraph 4 of the DA, to include by name the bodies that are expected to be recipients of the data, which are contained in the table but not in the wording of the DA, paragraph 4. Similarly, it is suggested to include in the DA the categories of personal data that will be subject to processing, so that the interested parties have appropriate information regarding the interference with their fundamental right to data protection arising from the regulation.

Furthermore, it could also be included that no international transfers of such data are foreseen, and the rights of the interested parties regarding the processing (access, rectification, etc.), as well as the appropriate technical and organizational measures that have been established.

In this last aspect, it is noteworthy that the MAIN, although it does not seem to have carried out a DPIA from which such measures result, as already mentioned, has foreseen some technical, organizational, and procedural measures that can anticipate risks of different probabilities and severities and mitigate their effects. However, these measures have not been incorporated into the text of the regulation (into the DA). Thus, for example, the MAIN states that [r]egarding the data collected, they must be those essential for addressing the purposes of the processing and must be kept in a pseudonymized manner, avoiding the use of direct identifiers, and assigning an identifier to the accredited victim that will be provided when requesting assistance and will serve for their future authentication. This measure of pseudonymization and assigning an identifier to the victim that will serve for their subsequent accreditation has not been reflected in the regulation, and it would be a good measure, without prejudice, of course, to the fact that the controllers may adopt additional measures.

Finally, and in a more general aspect, it would be advisable to update the text of the MAIN to reflect the existence of this Additional Provision, since although, as stated, in the section relating to the impacts of the regulation it is included, the same does not occur, for example, in section II, Content, which does not mention the existence of such an additional provision. This may simply be due, and perhaps understandably, to the fact that the DA and its reflection in the MAIN, as a result of the aforementioned collaboration with the DPD of the Ministry, may have occurred after the initial text of the Draft Royal Decree and its reflection in the MAIN.

In any case, and without prejudice to the suggestions and comments made in this Report, this AEPD reiterates its favorable opinion regarding the analysis carried out by the pre-legislator, in collaboration with the Data Protection Officer, on the matter of data protection in the project.
