I  
This Legal Office has repeatedly highlighted, in the various consultations submitted for its report, the paradigm shift that the full application of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of their personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR), represents, as it is based on the principle of "accountability" or proactive responsibility, as stated in the Explanatory Memorandum of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD): “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the data controller or the processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.”  

Consequently, by virtue of this principle, the data controller must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the Regulation.  

Among the various instruments provided by the GDPR itself to ensure compliance with this principle are the codes of conduct, already foreseen in previous regulations as a self-regulation mechanism aimed at facilitating compliance with personal data protection regulations. In this regard, the Article 29 Working Party, in its working document on the procedure for examining community codes of conduct, approved on September 10, 1998 (WP13), had already defined the essential elements of such codes, emphasizing that the codes should have sufficient clarity and internal coherence and provide a sufficient added value, in terms of being sufficiently focused on the specific issues and problems of data protection in the organization or sector for which they are intended to be applied, and offer sufficiently clear solutions for those questions and problems, providing, to the extent possible, examples of good practices, preferably prepared in consultation with the affected stakeholders or their representatives.

The role of codes of conduct and their link to the principle of proactive responsibility, as a means to demonstrate compliance with the GDPR, from which the need to review all previously approved codes derives, is outlined in the Guidelines of the European Data Protection Board 1/2019 on codes of conduct and monitoring bodies under Regulation 2016/679, highlighting, furthermore, the benefit they may provide to supervisory authorities by allowing them to gain a better understanding and knowledge of the data processing activities of a specific industry, profession, or other specific sector.
These guidelines identify some of the areas where codes of conduct may be useful, such as fair and transparent processing, legitimate interests, security and data protection by design and by default, and obligations of the data controller, based on the premise that codes may have a broader or narrower scope, depending on the particular sector, without the need to encompass compliance with all legislation. They also identify elements of their content that can help give them added value, such as the establishment of detailed guidelines for specific processing activities, best practices, practical solutions to problems identified in a specific sector, consideration of public concerns in general or perceived within the sector itself, etc.
Furthermore, the aforementioned Guidelines detail the criteria that must be taken into account by the supervisory authorities for the approval of codes:
1) That it meets a particular need of that sector or processing activity, with the proposed solutions being beneficial not only for the controllers but also for the affected parties.
2) That it facilitates the application of the GDPR, identifying specific needs (for example, adapting the terminology of the sector).
3) That it specifies the application of the GDPR, focusing on the issues of the sector and providing added value, without merely reproducing the provisions of the GDPR.
4) That it provides effective mechanisms for monitoring compliance with the Code, both in terms of structures and procedures, with the existence of an accredited supervisory body being mandatory, except in the case of authorities and public bodies.

All of this means that the impact of applying data protection rules must inspire the entirety of the regulation on clinical trials with medicines and the various actions and procedures developed under it, given the special impact that it has on the various participants and particularly on the subjects of the trial regarding the rights guaranteed by data protection regulations.
In this regard, it should not be forgotten that in the case we are dealing with, the data of the trial subjects being processed are specially protected data related to their health, so the specific rule contained in Article 7.3 of Organic Law 15/1999 applies directly. Thus, it will not be possible to justify the processing and transfer of data on other grounds than those provided for in Articles 6 and 11 of the Organic Law, requiring the consent of the interested party or a specific legal authorization.
Certainly, the Draft, like Royal Decree 223/2004, starts from the principle that processing will, in any case, be subject to the informed consent of the trial subject. However, it must be taken into account that this mere consent is not the only principle to be considered in the processing of data carried out within the framework of clinical trials.
In particular, it is relevant at this point to consider the principles established in Article 4 of the Organic Law, being essential, first of all, to guarantee proportionality in processing, so that according to Article 4.1 the data are adequate, relevant, and not excessive in relation to the purpose that justifies their processing, which entails two fundamental consequences: on the one hand, the volume of data subject to processing must be reduced to what is actually indispensable for fulfilling the purposes of the trial, and, in any case, the preservation of the health of the trial subjects; on the other hand, the number of persons or entities with access to such data must also be minimized.
This second conclusion requires taking into account that in the preparation of the protocol, approval, development, and monitoring of the trial, a plurality of subjects are involved, both from the perspective of its execution and its control: thus, reference must be made in any case to the figures of the sponsor, the investigator, and the monitor, as well as to the persons contracted by the sponsor (commonly referred to as contract research organizations). Furthermore, reference must be made to the role of the Ethics Committees for Research with Medicines, the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products, the competent authorities of the Autonomous Communities, and the European Medicines Agency. Data protection regulations, and especially those that guarantee the aforementioned principle of proportionality, must be applied in all information flows between all the actors involved, in order to ensure that access to the data is carried out only when it is effectively necessary for the proper fulfillment of the purposes pursued by the processing.
Precisely in this sense, the reference made by the Proposal for Regulation that the Union database will not include information about the trial subjects must be interpreted.
In relation to minimization and proportionality in the processing of data, this Agency wants to reiterate the conclusions that were already reached when analyzing the pharmacovigilance regime submitted to its report, regarding the necessity that data be processed only when it is effectively necessary. Regarding the content of the code of conduct, the GDPR has identified certain areas that it may cover in Article 40.2:
2. Associations and other representative bodies of categories of controllers or processors may develop codes of conduct or modify or expand such codes in order to specify the application of this Regulation, as regards:
a) the fair and transparent processing;
b) the legitimate interests pursued by the controllers in specific contexts;
c) the collection of personal data;
d) the pseudonymization of personal data;
e) the information provided to the public and to the data subjects;
f) the exercise of the rights of the data subjects;
g) the information provided to children and their protection, as well as the manner of obtaining the consent of the holders of parental responsibility or guardianship over the child;
h) the measures and procedures referred to in Articles 24 and 25 and the measures to ensure the security of processing referred to in Article 32;
i) the notification of personal data breaches to the supervisory authorities and the communication of such breaches to the data subjects;
j) the transfer of personal data to third countries or international organizations, or
k) the extrajudicial procedures and other dispute resolution procedures that allow resolving disputes between the controllers and the data subjects regarding processing, without prejudice to the rights of the data subjects under Articles 77 and 79.
At this point, it should be noted that the GDPR does not identify a minimum content for codes of conduct, but rather it is an enumerative list of subjects that they may cover and does not exhaust all the cases that may arise to facilitate the application of the GDPR, unlike what was done in the implementing regulation of the LOPD of 1999, which required that model codes include the content referred to in Article 73 of the LOPD Regulation, which involved the entire life cycle of personal data, a supervision procedure, and a list of adherents, in accordance with what was provided in Articles 75 and 76.
Moreover, there was the option to include additional commitments.

What must be included mandatorily are the mechanisms for supervising compliance as required by Article 40.4 of the GDPR in order to allow the supervisory body to carry out the mandatory control of compliance with its provisions by the controllers or processors who commit to applying it.
Consequently, regardless of the material content of the codes of conduct, they cannot overlook including the mechanisms for controlling their compliance, whether they are codes applicable to controllers or processors.

Regarding the innovations introduced by the Code, they are summarized in sections VII, VIII, and IX of its Introduction, facilitating the understanding of its scope:
VII
The most relevant aspects introduced by the Code of Conduct concerning data processing within the framework of scientific research can be summarized as follows:
• It regulates only the processing by promoters of coded data, given that the practice of the sector shows that they do not carry out, under any circumstances, data processing with uncoded personal data.
• It establishes compliance with legal obligations as the legal basis for data processing in this area, without the need for the consent of the research subject for the processing of their data, without prejudice to the informed consent that must be provided for their participation in a clinical trial.
• It establishes the need to provide information on data protection separately from that contained in the patient information document that must be provided to them in accordance with clinical trial regulations.
• It clarifies the roles of the different parties involved in the processing, specifying that the research promoter and the healthcare center or principal investigator will be responsible for their respective processing activities, with each of them bearing the obligations arising from their activity, without the possibility of assessing the existence of joint liability between them for the breaches committed by the other party.
• It regulates the cases of secondary use of data obtained within the framework of a research study for future research, generally not requiring the consent of the participants in the research.
• It includes the figure of a trusted third party, to whom it will be possible to turn to carry out the procedure for coding the personal data of the participants in the research, so that the promoter cannot re-identify them either alone or with the mere assistance of the researcher.
• It regulates the obligations of active responsibility that must be fulfilled concerning data processing within the framework of the research. In particular, it resolves certain issues related to the notification of breaches or security incidents that may have been incurred by third parties whose services were contracted by the promoter.
• It establishes clarifications regarding international transfers of personal data, indicating that, as long as it is completely impossible for the recipient of a data transfer located in a third country or international organization to re-identify the participants in the research, due to prior anonymization of the data by the Promoter sending the data, the regulatory provisions in this area will not apply. However, if such re-identification were possible, the adoption of adequate guarantees would be necessary if the destination country did not offer a level of protection comparable to that of the GDPR.
• It incorporates different models of clauses that will govern the legal relationships between the various parties involved. These models, however, may be modulated in terms of their content as long as the final text offers equivalent guarantees to those included in them.

This means that the processing of the data is carried out by the processor on behalf of the controller as if it were the controller itself carrying it out.

e) Check that the storage, distribution, return, and documentation of the investigational medicinal products are safe and appropriate.
f) Send the sponsor reports of the monitoring visits and all relevant contacts with the investigator.”
Therefore, the sponsor, as the entity “responsible for initiating, managing, and organizing the funding of a clinical trial” (Article 2.14 of Regulation (EU) 536/2014), determines the purposes and means of the trial, holding, in accordance with the GDPR, the status of data controller. Among the obligations specifically imposed on the sponsor by the regulations is the monitoring of the clinical trial (Article 48 of Regulation (EU) 536/2014), a function that is not performed directly but is entrusted to a third party, being obliged to “Designate the monitor who will oversee the progress of the trial” (Article 39.2.h of Royal Decree 1090/2015) without “Under any circumstances should the monitor be part of the research team” (Article 2.2.i of the Royal Decree). 
Consequently, the monitor, as expressed in the aforementioned Article 2.2.i) of the Royal Decree, is “chosen by the sponsor” to fulfill an obligation that solely corresponds to the sponsor, so the only relationship the monitor maintains is the one that legally binds them to the sponsor, to whom they provide monitoring services, having to “Work in accordance with the sponsor's standardized operating procedures” and having among their functions the “ensuring that the data is recorded correctly and completely” (Article 40.1.a) of the Royal Decree). For these purposes, Regulation (EU) 536/2014 requires, among the documentation to be provided by the sponsor for the authorization of the clinical trial, “a declaration from the sponsor (in the protocol or in a separate document) confirming that the investigators and the institutions participating in the clinical trial will allow monitoring, audits, and regulatory inspections related to the clinical trial, including direct access to the data and source documents;” (Annex 1, D, ah).
Therefore, from the perspective of the protection of personal data, in accordance with the GDPR and the prior criteria of this Agency, the monitor holds the status of “data processor,” as they process personal data on behalf of the sponsor, who has the obligation to carry out the monitoring, being the only one responsible for the choice of the monitor, for which they “will only choose a processor that offers sufficient guarantees” (Article 28.1 of the GDPR). In the exercise of their monitoring function, the monitor must follow only the instructions of the sponsor, “in accordance with the sponsor's standardized procedures” (Article 40.1.a) of the Royal Decree), and a contract as provided in Article 28.3. of the GDPR must be signed between the sponsor and the monitor.

Therefore, as the data processor for the sponsor, the applicable legal basis for the processing of personal data carried out by the monitor will be the same as that of the sponsor. In this case, since monitoring is an obligation imposed on the sponsor by Regulation (EU) 536/2014, it will be as contemplated in Article 6.1(c) of the GDPR: “the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject.” 
IV
The status of the monitor as the data processor for the sponsor is not undermined by the circumstance that the sponsor only accesses pseudonymized data and the monitor accesses the identifying data from the patient's medical history. As we have seen, this access occurs in compliance with a legal obligation of monitoring imposed on the sponsor, without prejudice to the fact that the sponsor does not have such data due to measures adopted to comply with other principles of the GDPR, such as purpose limitation (Article 5.1(b)) and data minimization (Article 5.1(c)), with the consequent impact on the adoption of security measures based on the different personal data processed. As we have seen, the GDPR does not consider access to data by the controller as a determining criterion for assessing the existence of a data processing agreement; rather, it requires that the processing operations, which include, for example, collection, be attributed to the controller.
The monitor's access to the personal data of the medical history is done on behalf of the sponsor and not on behalf of the center responsible for that medical history. Likewise, the center does not participate in the selection of the monitor nor can it give instructions regarding the exercise of its function, and there is no legal relationship between the center and the monitor. The center's inability to give instructions to the monitor regarding the performance of its function is emphasized by the AEMPS, which in its “Instructions Document from the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products for conducting clinical trials in Spain,” expressly states that “The necessary separation of functions provided for by legislation on clinical research, clearly distinguishing the research activities carried out in a specific center by the researchers of the trial from those of monitoring the same, cannot be conditioned by instructions from that center to the monitor regarding the monitoring of the trial.” (Section 63, which will be referenced later).

Indeed, while the Organic Law and the Regulation do not differ excessively regarding the enumeration of the legitimizing grounds for processing, there is a highly relevant modification in the way these grounds are presented in the applicable texts: thus, while from the wording of Organic Law 15/1999 it seemed to be inferred that the basic rule of legitimization was, in general, consent, with the remaining legitimizing grounds being exceptional in relation to consent, which as a general rule should govern processing, in the text of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, consent is listed as one of the six legitimizing grounds for processing without holding greater or lesser importance than the others enumerated in the regulation.

Thus, it is not appropriate to obtain the consent of the data subject in any case where the processing is covered by any of the grounds included in letters b) to f) of Article 6.1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

As indicated, the code of conduct should not be considered as a code aimed at incorporating adequate guarantees for the execution of international data transfers, as provided in article 46.2.e) of the GDPR, therefore, it refers, regarding the international transfers that may be carried out by both controllers and processors, to the provisions of Chapter V of the GDPR.

Furthermore, it will be necessary to interpret what should be understood by "specific and unequivocal" consent for the relevant purposes of this report; that is to say, what the meaning of such consent requirements is, taking into account the provisions of the current regulations on data protection as well as their applicability to the field of data processing for biomedical research purposes. 
To this end, it is essential to consider the recitals of the General Data Protection Regulation itself, which clarify and interpret the content of its articles. 
As a starting point, the Regulation provides a very broad interpretation of the concept of scientific research, since according to its recital 159, "The processing of personal data for scientific research purposes should be interpreted, for the purposes of this Regulation, in a broad manner, which includes, for example, technological development and demonstration, fundamental research, applied research, and research funded by the private sector. Furthermore, it should take into account the Union's objective established in Article 179, paragraph 1, of the TFEU to create a European research area. Among the purposes of scientific research, studies conducted in the public interest in the field of public health should also be included."
In this same vein, Article 157 broadens the scope of research, taking into account the possible collection of data from records. It thus recalls that "by combining information from records, researchers can obtain new, highly valuable knowledge about widespread medical conditions, such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and depression. Based on records, the results of research can be more robust, as they are based on a larger population. Within the social sciences, record-based research allows researchers to gain essential insights into the long-term correlation, with other living conditions, of various social conditions, such as unemployment and education. The results of research obtained from records provide solid and high-quality knowledge that can serve as a basis for the design and implementation of knowledge-based policies, improve the quality of life for numerous individuals, and enhance the efficiency of social services. To facilitate scientific research, personal data may be processed for scientific purposes, subject to appropriate conditions and guarantees established in Union or Member State law."

And in relation to the processing of special categories of data, it expressly points out Recital 52 which states that “exceptions to the prohibition on processing special categories of personal data must be authorized when established by Union or Member State law and provided that appropriate safeguards are in place to protect personal data and other fundamental rights, when it is in the public interest, in particular (…) for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific and historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes” and adds the first paragraph of Recital 53 that “the special categories of personal data that deserve greater protection may only be processed for health-related purposes when necessary to achieve those purposes for the benefit of individuals and society as a whole,” again referring to the processing of data for scientific research purposes.

Finally, and in a completely explicit manner, Recital 33 of the regulation refers specifically to the consent given for the processing of data for scientific research purposes, taking into account the intrinsically dynamic nature of such research. It states that “It is often not possible to fully determine the purpose of the processing of personal data for scientific research at the time of its collection. Therefore, data subjects should be allowed to give their consent for specific areas of scientific research that respect the recognized ethical standards for scientific research. Data subjects should have the opportunity to give their consent only for specific areas of research or parts of research projects, to the extent permitted by the pursued purpose.”

From all of this, it can be inferred that the requirements of specificity and unequivocal nature for the provision of consent should not be interpreted restrictively in the context of scientific research, limited to a specific research project for which all available information is provided, but rather it should be considered that they apply in cases where consent is given in relation to a specific field of research, which may be extended in the future without this affecting its validity in any way, even to “purposes” or areas of research that could not even have been determined at the time consent was given, without the need to obtain new consent from the data subject, taking into account the benefits for individuals and society as a whole that may arise from such unforeseen research.
