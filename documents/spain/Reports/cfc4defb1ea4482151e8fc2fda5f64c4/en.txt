The inquiry focuses on the legality of data processing in a credit information system that will contain both information related to compliance with monetary obligations (positive information) and information related to the non-compliance of such obligations (negative information), under the legal basis provided for in Article 6.1(f) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR).  

This issue has been studied by this Legal Office from the broadest perspective allowed by the analysis of the Code of Conduct of the infomediary sector for the protection of personal data presented by the Multisectoral Association of Information (ASEDIE), which has been unfavorably reported in our recent report 89/2020, dated March 16, 2021. The arguments regarding the processing of personal data by so-called "positive files" based on the legal basis of Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR are fully transferable to the present inquiry, and thus we proceed to transcribe them:  

I.- MAINTENANCE OF CREDIT INFORMATION SYSTEMS WITH DATA RELATING TO COMPLIANCE WITH MONETARY, FINANCIAL, OR CREDIT OBLIGATIONS (POSITIVE SOLVENCY FILES).  

The report issued by the General Subdirectorate of GDPR considers that its content does not comply with the provisions of the GDPR or the LOPDGDD, listing a series of reasons that can be summarized as follows: there is a confusion between the interest of the data controller and the purpose of the processing, as the invoked interest appears to be merely to obtain an economic benefit, while the need for third parties to know the data of economic compliance would constitute the purpose of the processing; the data was collected for another purpose, leading to the conclusion that the compatibility test would be contrary to the principle of purpose; Article 5 of the GDPR requires that data be processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject, and the legitimate interest requires that the processing be necessary; the data processed could lead to the creation of a profile of the data subjects that reveals their personal and family life and intimacy;  

there are countries that do not consider legitimate interest as a basis for carrying out these types of processing; there are insufficient guarantees provided; the legislator has regulated the processing of data from negative files based on legitimate interest, remaining silent on this type of files; the legitimate interest of the controller or third parties is not seen as prevailing over the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals.  

1.- Historical and legislative background. Differentiated treatment of credit information systems relating to compliance with obligations and those related to non-compliance in Spanish legislation and administrative doctrine.  

This Legal Office shares the opinion expressed by the General Subdirectorate of GDPR, which also corresponds to the traditional treatment that positive files have had in our legal system, whose legitimacy was based on obtaining the consent of the affected party, as this Agency has repeatedly pointed out.  

In this regard, in report 180/2010, it was already highlighted how the processing of data relating to the credit situation of a specific individual generated one of the highest numbers of complaints before the Spanish Data Protection Agency, which led to the issuance of its first General Instruction (Instruction 1/1995 of March 1, from the Data Protection Agency, regarding the provision of information services on asset solvency and credit) in reference to this type of processing, in order to resolve the problems it posed from the perspective of applying the principles of data quality, enshrined in Article 6 of the Directive, and security, as provided in Article 17.  

Subsequently, Article 29 of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, proceeded to regulate it, differentiating two cases:

Analyzing this differentiation, the aforementioned report 180/2010 stated the following:
Thus, on one hand, the files of patrimonial solvency and credit in the strict sense would collect information obtained from the interested party themselves or from publicly accessible sources, and they could incorporate both positive and negative information, remaining, in general, subject to the common regime of data protection regulations. 
In contrast to such files, Article 29.2 of the Organic Law refers to files related to the compliance or non-compliance with monetary obligations, in which the data may be provided directly to the credit information system by creditors or those acting on their behalf or interest, understanding that the legislator finds the legitimacy for the processing of such data in what is provided in Article 7 f) of Directive 95/46/EC, given that the legislator appreciates the existence of a legitimate interest for the processing of the data that prevails over the right to data protection of the interested party, who simply must be informed about this processing.
The exclusion of the intervention of the interested party in the processing of data in these files (regarding which they cannot oppose its permanence, unless the principles of data protection are not met) has as compensation to guarantee the appropriate balance of interests, a reinforcement of the guarantees of compliance with the principles of data quality, which will require extreme caution aimed at ensuring that the data is adequate, relevant, and not excessive, that reflects the real situation of the debtor and that is not retained for a period of time that exceeds the purpose justifying the processing.
Consequently, the Spanish legislator has analyzed the application of the balance rule enshrined in Article 7 f) of the Directive in relation to credit information systems, taking into account the purpose or "legitimate interest" pursued by the processing, which is the sustainability of the financial and credit system and the guarantee of access to credit by individuals and companies, as derived from what is established in Articles 8 and 9 of Directive 2008/48/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 23, 2008, on consumer credit agreements.
And taking into account such analysis, the legislator has considered that the purpose pursued with these treatments will prevail over the right to data protection of the debtor in those cases where they have not fulfilled their obligations, not requiring the consent of the interested party in such cases, and being sufficient the direct communication of the data to the file by the creditor or someone acting on their behalf or interest. On the contrary, when the data does not refer to non-compliance, but to the simple existence of credit or its compliance (that is, to the so-called positive files), the legislator has considered that the legitimate interest of the controller only prevails over the right to data protection of the interested party in cases where such data is...

Once this consideration has been made, and taking into account that the legislator considers the legitimate interest of the data controller and possible recipients of the data to be predominant in cases of credit information systems with negative data, it has established a system of reinforced guarantees to ensure that the data contained in the system complies with the requirements established in data protection legislation and, in particular, in Directive 95/46/EC, from which the Spanish Law derives, as well as a series of mechanisms aimed at ensuring that the interested party, who cannot decide in any way on the processing in these cases, as the aforementioned Article 7 f) of the Directive applies, and who cannot exercise their right of opposition, since the processing is expressly recognized by the Law, has full knowledge of the processing of their data and can adequately exercise the remaining rights that the Law and the Directive recognize to them.
The system is finally closed with the express authorization granted by the legislator to the regulator, that is, to the Bank of Spain, for the creation of a specific file related to the credit risk incurred by credit financial entities (Central Credit Risk Information of the Bank of Spain, regulated by Law 44/2002, on measures for the reform of the financial system), to which all operations, positive or negative, that imply the existence of a credit risk will be communicated by the financial entities. In this case, given that the purpose pursued by the file is the adequate supervision by the regulator of the credit risk incurred by the entities, the legislator has also considered the legitimate interest or purpose pursued by the file to be predominant over the right to data protection of the interested parties, so it will not be necessary to resort to their consent, with the processing being legitimized by Article 7 f) of the Directive.
In short, Spanish legislation recognizes the existence of two distinct types of credit information systems, positive and negative, establishing a differentiated regime for both that, originally, is based on the existence of a different legitimization for the processing of data in each case, which will also affect the guarantees to be adopted for compliance with the different principles enshrined by the Directive.

Debtor files and information services on financial solvency and credit
These files are perhaps the blacklists that have the most influence on a large number of citizens and whose existence can be verified in all Member States. It is also true that it is the processing of personal data that generally raises the highest number of complaints to the European data protection authorities. The first aspect we must mention regarding these files is the existence of various types of regulation across all Member States. In some cases, it is contained in the transposition regulations of Directive 95/46/EC, while in others it appears in sector-specific commercial or financial regulations. Therefore, it is not a matter of assessing or judging the legitimacy of the existence of such files, which, as noted, have a legal basis in the respective Member States, but rather of analyzing their application and implementation. These activities involve the agreement between different businesses to share information about customers, generally through a centralizing entity, which significantly and directly affects commercial or service conditions. The legal regulation of these lists is based on the need for companies to have information that allows them to assess risks when they agree to provide a service or deliver goods on credit, thus also fulfilling a function of stability and sanitization of commercial transactions.

On the other hand, a clear distinction should be made between the files referred to as information files on financial solvency and credit and those intended to facilitate information regarding the non-fulfillment of monetary obligations. 

The former are intended for the assessment of the economic and financial capabilities of a person to meet a future credit obligation. The latter store data related to the fulfillment or non-fulfillment of monetary obligations, with the aim of knowing the existence of previous unpaid obligations by a specific person, which, if they exist, logically leads to a negative assessment when granting new credit.

Subsequently, after the approval of the GDPR, this Agency has been emphasizing that the reform brought about by it regarding the regime contained in Organic Law 15/1999 requires a change of perspective concerning the principles that underpin the fundamental right to the protection of personal data and, in particular, regarding the one that made the "principle of consent" the central axis of the right to data protection, since in the text of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, consent is listed as one of the six grounds for legitimization for processing, without holding greater or lesser importance than the others enumerated in the regulation, so it is not appropriate to obtain the consent of the affected party in cases where the processing is covered by any of the grounds included in letters b) to f) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR. 
However, in relation to positive files, the Agency's criterion has not changed, considering it necessary, given the greater impact that such files have on the rights and interests of the affected parties, the need for their consent, a circumstance that had even been anticipated by the Draft Organic Law on the Protection of Personal Data, as indicated in report 194/2017: 
Regarding credit information systems, Article 14 incorporates into the text of the new legal framework the rules already contained in Article 29 of Organic Law 15/1999 and its implementing regulations, contained in Chapter I of Title IV of its Regulation. 
As a novelty, however, paragraph 2 of the article refers to files related to the fulfillment of monetary obligations. 
Thus, a structure is established in which paragraph 1 refers to the traditionally called "negative files," for which Article 6.1 f) of the General Data Protection Regulation would apply as a legitimizing basis for processing, in accordance with what is provided in Article 9.3 of the Draft Law, dedicating paragraph 2 to the so-called "positive files," in which the legislator considers that the aforementioned rule of weighting does not apply as a legal basis for processing, requiring the consent of the affected party. 
Along with them, paragraph 3 establishes the common provisions for both information systems or for systems containing data on both compliance and non-compliance, incorporating the currently applicable provisions for solvency and credit files. Finally, paragraph 4 establishes a specialty regarding compliance with the principle of purpose for this type of information systems, which does not allow for the creation of profiles based on other different information.

All of the above must be understood without prejudice to the already mentioned possibility of drafting these provisions in the form of a presumption iuris tantum, as indicated in the observations to Article 9.3 of the Draft Bill.

In light of this observation, the national legislator chose to establish a presumption, iuris tantum, of prevailing legitimate interest in the case of negative files, increasing the guarantees compared to those that had been required up to that point for such files. However, it intentionally chose not to establish the same presumption regarding positive files, which thus remained without legal regulation, requiring the corresponding balancing to be carried out by the data controllers, “and, ultimately, by the data protection authorities and by the courts.”

However, this Agency has not changed its position regarding positive files, given, as previously noted, the greater impact they have on the rights and interests of the affected parties, which prevents the appreciation of the prevalence of the legitimate interests of the controller or third parties over the fundamental interests, rights, and freedoms of the affected parties, as will be analyzed in detail in this report. Based on the above, it is appropriate to analyze whether the processing proposed in the code of conduct could be supported, as intended, by legitimate interest, considering that the legislator has not established a presumption of the same, although, as stated in the Explanatory Memorandum of the LOPDGDD, "In Title IV, 'Applicable provisions for specific treatments' are included, incorporating a series of cases that should in no case be considered exhaustive of all lawful treatments. The conditions set forth in the text, although in this case the responsible party must carry out the legally required balancing, as the prevalence of their legitimate interest is not presumed. 
The code of conduct aims to support such processing based on the existence of a legitimate interest, in accordance with the provisions of Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR: the processing is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that the interests or the rights and fundamental freedoms of the data subject requiring the protection of personal data do not prevail, particularly when the data subject is a child.
Recitals 47 to 49 of the GDPR include the identification of some cases of legitimate interest:
(47) The legitimate interest of a data controller, including that of a controller to whom personal data may be communicated, or of a third party, may constitute a legal basis for processing, provided that the interests or the rights and freedoms of the data subject do not prevail, taking into account the reasonable expectations of the data subjects based on their relationship with the controller. Such legitimate interest could arise, for example, when there is a relevant and appropriate relationship between the data subject and the controller, as in situations where the data subject is a customer or is in the service of the controller. In any case, the existence of a legitimate interest would require a meticulous assessment, including whether a data subject can reasonably foresee, at the time and in the context of the collection of personal data, that processing for such purpose may occur. In particular, the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject could prevail over the interests of the data controller when the processing of personal data occurs in circumstances where the data subject does not reasonably expect further processing to take place. Since it is up to the legislator to establish by law the legal basis for the processing of personal data by public authorities, this legal basis should not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions. The processing of personal data strictly necessary for the prevention of fraud also constitutes a legitimate interest of the data controller in question. The processing of personal data for direct marketing purposes may be considered to be carried out for legitimate interest.
(48) Controllers that are part of a corporate group or entities affiliated with a central body may have a legitimate interest in transmitting personal data within the corporate group for internal administrative purposes, including the processing of personal data of customers or employees. The general principles applicable to the transmission of personal data within a corporate group to a company located in a third country are not affected. 
(49) It constitutes a legitimate interest of the data controller to process personal data to the extent strictly necessary and proportionate to ensure the security of the network and information, that is, the ability of a network or information system to withstand, at a certain level of trust, accidental events or unlawful or malicious actions that compromise the availability, authenticity, integrity, and confidentiality of the personal data stored or transmitted, and the security of the related services offered by, or accessible through, these systems and networks, by public authorities, computer emergency response teams (CERT), computer security incident response teams (CSIRT), providers of electronic communications networks and services, and providers of security technologies and services. This could include, for example, preventing unauthorized access to electronic communications networks and the malicious distribution of codes, and thwarting denial-of-service attacks and damage to computer and electronic communications systems.
The Judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union of November 24, 2011, which expressly declared the direct effect of the provisions of Article 7(f) of Directive 95/46/EC, relating to legitimate interest as a basis for processing, recalls in paragraph 38 that Article 7(f) of the Directive “establishes two cumulative requirements for the processing of personal data to be lawful, namely, on the one hand, that such processing of personal data is necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party or parties to whom the data are communicated, and, on the other hand, that the rights and fundamental freedoms of the data subject do not prevail” and, in relation to the aforementioned balancing, paragraph 40 recalls that this “will depend, in principle, on the specific circumstances of the particular case at hand and in which the person or institution carrying out the balancing must take into account the importance of the rights conferred on the data subject by Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union.”
Legal Office
In this regard, it is important to highlight that in the balancing act between the possible legitimate interest of the data controller and the rights of the data subject, the latter are considered a fundamental right in accordance with Article 18.4 of the Constitution and Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union. While, like all fundamental rights, the right to the protection of personal data allows for limitations, these must favor other constitutionally protected rights or goods. Recital 4 of the GDPR itself states that [the] processing of personal data must be designed to serve humanity. The right to the protection of personal data is not an absolute right but must be considered in relation to its function in society and maintain a balance with other fundamental rights, in accordance with the principle of proportionality. The fact that a fundamental right is subjected to scrutiny in the balancing act against other types of legitimate interests, which do not enjoy the same level of protection, should not be overlooked.

The Article 29 Working Party, in its extensive Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC, carefully analyzes this legal basis and provides interesting examples in this regard, in order to assess when, by virtue of the "balancing test," the legitimate interest of the controller may be considered predominant.

From its conclusions, the following points are of interest:
a) Firstly, the legitimate interest of the data controller should not be considered a legal basis that can only be used sparingly to cover gaps in rare or unforeseen situations as "a last resort," or as a final possibility if other grounds for legitimacy cannot be applied. It should not be perceived as a preferred option nor should its use be improperly extended because it is considered less restrictive than other legal bases. On the contrary, it is a valid means just like any other to legitimize the processing of personal data. 

An appropriate use of Article 7, letter f), in the correct circumstances and subject to the relevant safeguards, can help prevent misuse and avoid excessive reliance on other legal bases. An adequate assessment of the balance under Article 7, letter f), sometimes with a possibility of voluntary exclusion from processing, can in some cases be a valid alternative to inappropriate use, for example, of the legal basis based on "consent" or "necessity for the performance of a contract." Considered in this way, Article 7, letter f), presents complementary guarantees compared to other previously determined grounds for legitimacy. Therefore, it should not be considered as "the weakest link" or an open door to legitimize all data processing activities that are not covered by any of the other grounds for legitimacy.

b) Secondly, the interest of the data controller, understood in the broadest possible sense, in order to be considered "legitimate," must be lawful, that is, in accordance with national and EU legislation. It must also be articulated with sufficient clarity and be specific enough to allow the "balancing test" to be carried out in contrast to the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject. It must also represent a real and current interest, that is, it should not be speculative.

c) The processing must also be "necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest" pursued by the data controller or, in the case of disclosure of the data, by the third party. Therefore, less invasive means to serve the same purpose should always be preferred.

d) Considering the difficulties posed by the application of the "balancing test," key factors are identified that must be considered when carrying it out:
• the nature and source of the legitimate interest, including the fact of:
  - whether the processing of data is necessary for the exercise of a fundamental right, or
  - whether it is otherwise a matter of public interest or benefits from legal or normative, social, or cultural recognition by the affected community.
• the impact on the data subjects, including:
  - the nature of the data, for example, whether the processing affects data that may be considered sensitive or that is contained in public sources;
  - the manner in which the data is processed, for example, whether the data has been disclosed to the public or otherwise made available to a large number of people, or whether large amounts of personal data are processed or combined with other data (for example, in the case of profiling for commercial purposes, law enforcement, or others);
  - the reasonable expectations of the data subject, especially in relation to the use and disclosure of the data in the relevant context;
Legal Office
- the position of the data controller and the data subject, including the power balance between both, or whether the data subject is a child or otherwise belongs to a vulnerable segment of the population.
• the additional safeguards to prevent undue impact on data subjects, including:
- data minimization (for example, strict limitations on data collection or immediate deletion after use);
- technical and organizational measures to ensure that data cannot be used to take measures or undertake other actions concerning individuals ("functional separation");
- extensive use of anonymization techniques, data aggregation, privacy protection technologies, privacy by design, data protection impact assessments, and privacy assessments;
- increased transparency, general and unconditional right to voluntary exclusion ("opt-out"), data portability, and related measures to empower data subjects.
In relation to these safeguards, and with the overall assessment of the balance, three issues often play a crucial role in the context of Article 7, letter f), and therefore require special attention:
- the existence or possible need for additional measures to increase transparency and accountability;
- the right of the data subject to object to processing, and beyond the objection, the possibility of voluntary exclusion without the need for justification; and
- the empowerment of data subjects: data portability and the availability of viable mechanisms for the data subject to access, modify, delete, transfer, or otherwise reuse (or allow third parties to reuse) their own data.
The conclusions drawn in the aforementioned Opinion of the Article 29 Working Party, although referring to Directive 95/46, are fully applicable following the entry into force of the GDPR, which has not introduced significant variations in the characterization of the legitimate interest of the controller or third parties as a legal basis for the processing of personal data.

Notwithstanding the above, it is necessary to take into account the difference in the legal regime between the provisions of the LOPD and the GDPR regarding the processing of legitimate interest in relation to the affected parties, as noted in Report 136/2018: 
Article 6.1(f) of the GDPR establishes that processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met, among which it cites legitimate interest, regarding which the application to the present case is the subject of the inquiry. In this regard, it should be noted that the regime of legitimate interest as a basis for legitimizing the processing of personal data is regulated differently in the Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data (LOPD) and in the GDPR. In the LOPD, the essential basis for legitimizing processing was the unequivocal consent of the affected party (art. 6.1). However, such consent was not necessary when (among other circumstances) the data was included in "publicly accessible sources" and its processing was necessary for the satisfaction of the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller or by the third party to whom the data is communicated, provided that "the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject are not violated." In the GDPR, for the existence of legitimate interest as a legal basis for processing, it is not required (i) that the data is included in publicly accessible sources, (ii) nor is it established for the legitimacy of this cause that "the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject are not violated," but rather a balancing must be carried out to determine the prevalence between the claimed legitimate interest and the "interests, or the rights and fundamental freedoms" of the data subject that require the protection of personal data (art. 6.1(f) GDPR). 
This highlights the standard that must be followed in determining such prevalence. First, the protection regime of the data subject in the GDPR against the data controller who intends to use their personal data based on a legitimate interest is more intense in the GDPR than in the LOPD, since in the latter, to allow legitimate interest, it was sufficient that their rights or freedoms were not "violated," while in the GDPR regime, it is sufficient that their interests, rights, or freedoms prevail over that legitimate interest. Note that the word interests is added, as opposed to the violation of the necessary rights or freedoms previously in the LOPD. And that no violation is necessary; it is enough that their interests, rights, or freedoms are affected, even if only slightly, as long as that interest prevails over the claimed legitimate interest.

Likewise, the information contained in the files regarding the same person would increase. Thus, the more data that is shared, the greater the impact on the interests, rights, and freedoms of the affected parties arising from the mere existence of the processing, and the greater the risks to privacy that may arise from the misuse of such data or from the existence of a security breach. 2.4. Measures to be adopted to minimize the risk to the interests or rights and fundamental freedoms of the interested parties. 
When the legal basis for legitimizing the processing of personal data is intended to be founded on legitimate interest, the specific guarantees to be adopted acquire special relevance in order to assess the prevalence of the same concerning the interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of the affected parties, as recalled by Opinion 6/2014: 
The legitimate interest of the data controller, when it is lesser and not very pressing, generally only overrides the interests and rights of the interested parties in cases where the impact on these rights and interests is even more trivial. On the other hand, an important and pressing legitimate interest may, in some cases and subject to guarantees and measures, even justify a significant intrusion into privacy or any other significant impact on the interests or rights of the interested parties. 

Here it is important to highlight the special role that guarantees can play in reducing undue impact on the interested parties and, therefore, in shifting the balance of rights and interests to the point that the legitimate interest of the data controller prevails. Of course, the use of guarantees alone is not sufficient to justify any type of processing in any context. Furthermore, the guarantees in question must be adequate and sufficient, and must, unquestionably and significantly, reduce the impact on the interested parties.

The first consequence of the above is that the case in which the entity resorts to sources other than those derived from the relationship of the client with the entity for profiling purposes should be excluded from the application of Article 6.1 f) of the General Data Protection Regulation. For all these reasons, it would be possible to cover under Article 6.1 f) of the General Data Protection Regulation the processing by the entity of the information it has regarding the products and services contracted by its clients to assess their solvency for the purpose of offering new products that involve financing, provided that the client has been informed with due clarity about this processing and has, in any case, the possibility to specifically exercise their right to object to it.

3. Conclusion.
For all the above, this Legal Office considers, as has the General Subdirectorate of GDPR, that the processing of personal data by the controllers in positive files cannot be justified by the legitimate interest of the controller or the third party, as the interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of the affected parties prevail.

As already indicated, the national legislator has neither regulated positive files as a case of public interest (as it has done, for example, regarding processing for the purposes of video surveillance, advertising exclusion systems, or internal whistleblowing information systems in Articles 22, 23, and 24 of the LOPDGDD), nor has it established a presumption, iuris tantum, of the prevalence of legitimate interest, identifying the appropriate requirements and guarantees.

And in the absence of legal regulation, what is intended in the code of conduct is to achieve a similar effect to Article 20 of the LOPDGDD, establishing in general a presumption of lawfulness that is supported by this Agency, overlooking, as has been analyzed in detail, the significant differences that exist between different files, taking into account.

On the other hand, as indicated in the report, the need to specify the contribution of these files, in general, to the stability of the financial system, as well as the guarantee of participation by the largest number of entities and, where applicable, the limitation of the right to object, would require that this be established by a norm with the rank of law, and that this also specifies all the necessary guarantees after a rigorous analysis of the risks they entail. Furthermore, it should be clearly specified whether the possible existence of such files is limited to those owned by public entities, such as the CIRBE, or whether the existence of privately owned files with a general scope, such as those proposed in the code of conduct, is also accepted.

At this point, in the event that the competent authorities consider it essential to consult such privately owned files as a means of ensuring the stability of the financial system, and after weighing all the interests involved, it should be regulated precisely and in detail, in addition to the obligation to communicate credits by the granting entities, the information that must be incorporated into the files, the guarantees for their accuracy, the conditions under which it would be accessible to other entities, the entities that may access it, the retention periods, the exercise of the rights of those affected, and any other guarantees deemed appropriate, in order to adequately protect the fundamental right to data protection, with the desirable outcome being that the corresponding treatments are legitimized on the legal basis of compliance with a legal obligation, under Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR.

And, until the law is approved clarifying these issues, the legitimate interest of the data controller or third parties cannot be considered prevailing over the interests, rights, and fundamental freedoms of those affected to legitimize the processing of personal data by the controllers of credit information systems with data related to the fulfillment of monetary, financial, or credit obligations (positive solvency files).
