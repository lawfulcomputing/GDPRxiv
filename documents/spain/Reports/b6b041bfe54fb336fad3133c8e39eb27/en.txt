The inquiry refers to compliance with Regulation 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR) and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), when Local Entities intend to apply the payment of taxes by third parties not obligated, and specifically in the communication of personal data of the obligated party to the third party arising from such payment.  

The inquirer states that “it is common for third parties other than the obligated party to appear in our offices, requesting copies of third-party receipts, in order to proceed with the payment of tax debts, which occurs in collaborating financial entities, outside our facilities” and that “it is a common practice of the Tax and Cadastre Management Body of the Provincial Council of Pontevedra to deliver payment documents of tax debts to third parties other than the tax obligated person, prior to partial dissociation of identifying data of the obligated party and those related to their tax domicile, delivering a receipt with the corresponding tax information related to the tax or public law income that is going to be made effective.”  

Furthermore, it cites Report 436/2004 of this Legal Advisory Office in which, under the repealed Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data (LOPD), and under its article 11.2 a), the communication of the data of the tax obligated party to the third party was permitted by virtue of what is established in article 1158 of the Civil Code, in relation to article 18.1 of the General Collection Regulation, considering that today it would not be applicable since the current GDPR and LOPDGDD have modified the assumptions that allow the processing of data, in the sense that the legitimacy of the aforementioned communication of data raises interpretative and application doubts either from paragraph c) or from paragraph e) of article 6.1 of the GDPR.

As a starting point, the provisions of article 4 of the GDPR in its paragraphs 1 and 2 must be taken into account, which states the following:  
1) “personal data”: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (“the data subject”); an identifiable natural person is considered to be any person whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by means of an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that person;  
2) “processing”: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction;  

In the present case, the communication made by the tax administration - directly or through collaborating entities - of the debtor's data to the person who effectively makes the payment must be considered personal data processing and, therefore, must comply with the provisions of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.  

Article 5 of the GDPR establishes the “Principles relating to processing” and in its paragraph 1. a) includes the principle of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency, which states:  
1. Personal data shall be:  
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject (“lawfulness, fairness, and transparency”)  

Regarding the legitimacy for data processing, article 6.1 of the GDPR enumerates the assumptions that legitimize processing by stating the following:  
1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;

The consultation raises the application of the cases provided for in paragraphs c) and e) of the aforementioned provision.
Recital 41 of the GDPR states that "When this Regulation refers to a legal basis or to a legislative measure, this does not necessarily require a legislative act adopted by a parliament, without prejudice to the requirements of conformity with the constitutional order of the Member State in question. However, such legal basis or legislative measure must be clear and precise and its application foreseeable for its addressees, in accordance with the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter, 'Court of Justice') and the European Court of Human Rights.
Recital 45 of the GDPR states that "When carried out in compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller, or if necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority, the processing must have a basis in Union or Member State law."
Now, the LOPDGDD establishes in its article 8 under the heading "Processing of data by legal obligation, public interest or exercise of public powers" the following:
1. The processing of personal data may only be considered based on compliance with a legal obligation enforceable against the data controller, in the terms provided for in article 6.1.c) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when such is provided for by a rule of European Union law or a rule with the rank of law, which may determine the general conditions of processing and the types of data subject to it as well as the transfers that may occur as a result of compliance with the legal obligation. Such a rule may also impose special conditions on the processing, such as the adoption of additional security measures or others established in Chapter IV of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.
2. The processing of personal data may only be considered based on the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred on the data controller, in the terms provided for in article 6.1 e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when it derives from a competence attributed by a rule with the rank of law."
That is to say, the Spanish legislator has determined that it must be by a rule with the rank of law, the instrument that either imposes an obligation on the data controller that involves the processing of personal data, or that attributes to the data controller public competences that imply processing of data.
The consulting party argues that there is no rule with legal rank that obliges payment by a third party (6.1 c) GDPR) and that the payment made by the third party cannot be considered a competence or exercise of public powers, as they are obviously a private individual and do not have any public function regarding tax authority. (6.1 e) GDPR).
II
In Report 436/2004, which the consulting party cites and which is issued under the LOPD, the following was indicated:
In matters of personal data protection, article 11.1 of Organic Law 15/1999 states that "Personal data subject to processing may only be communicated to a third party for the fulfillment of purposes directly related to the legitimate functions of the transferor and the transferee with the prior consent of the data subject," although communication will be possible if there is a rule with the rank of law that provides legal coverage for it.
In light of all that has been indicated so far, it is necessary to analyze whether there is a rule with the rank of law that provides coverage for the transfer in question.
Legal Office
considering that such regulation is not included in Article 95 of the General Tax Law, as well as the fact that neither the current Law nor the Law of 1963 regulates in its articles the payment of the tax debt made by a third party. 
This figure is included in Article 18.1 of the General Collection Regulation, already cited, which states, “In addition to those obligated under Article 10 of this Regulation, any person may make the payment, whether or not they have an interest in fulfilling the obligation, whether they know and approve it, or whether the obligated party is unaware of it.” Article 18.3 adds that “In no case shall the third party who pays the debt be entitled to exercise before the Administration the rights that correspond to the obligated party. However, they may exercise the rights that derive in their favor exclusively from the act of payment.” 
The aforementioned provision implies the application to the payment of the tax debt of the provisions established for payment by a third party in Article 1158 of the Civil Code, whose first paragraph states that “Any person may make the payment, whether or not they have an interest in fulfilling the obligation, whether they know and approve it, or whether the debtor is unaware of it.” Furthermore, the second paragraph of the mentioned provision provides that “The one who pays on behalf of another may claim from the debtor what they have paid, unless they have done so against the express will of the debtor,” adding in the third paragraph that “In this case, they may only claim from the debtor what would have been useful to them from the payment.” 
Consequently, from the provisions of civil and tax legislation, it follows that the payment of the tax debt made by a third party legitimizes them, on the one hand, to exercise before the Public Treasury the actions derived from such payment and, on the other, to claim against the debtor, in this case the tax debtor, the payment of the tax debt. 
To do this, it will be essential that they can prove the payment of the tax debt, which must be verified through the procedure established in Chapter III of Title I of Book I of the General Collection Regulation, expressly stating that Article 31 requires the payment receipt to include the identifying data of the tax debtor and the debt subject to payment, without which, logically, the paying third party could not exercise the actions granted to them by the Regulation itself and the Civil Code.

Consequently, the assignment of the tax debtor's data is essential to comply with the actions established in the Civil Code, a norm with the rank of Law, as well as those that may correspond to the payer against the Public Treasury, in the cases provided for in the General Tax Law. 
Therefore, it must be considered that the assignment referred to in the inquiry is covered by the provisions of Article 11.2 a) of Organic Law 15/1999. 
Well, it should be noted that what is stated in the report, under the current general regime of personal data protection RGPD and LOPDGDD, must be nuanced in that mere legal authorization is no longer sufficient, but its application must be redirected to the existence of a specific obligation established by law. That is, Article 6.1 c) of the RGPD requires a “legal obligation that is applicable to the data controller,” therefore, it is not enough for it to be covered by a law as deduced from the aforementioned report, given that what is provided in Article 1158 of the Civil Code is a possibility, not an obligation, and moreover, it is not applicable to the data controller, which would be the corresponding tax administration body. 
III
Regarding the application of Article 6.1 e) of the RGPD, as the legal basis for the processing derived from the inquiry, it should be indicated that we are dealing with the processing of personal data whose controller is the local administration and which consists of the communication of data by this to a third party who makes the payment of the tax, as a consequence of the provision of the corresponding payment receipt. 
That is to say, the processing is not the act of payment by the third party, but the communication of data that occurs as a result of it, which, as we will see, has important consequences when establishing the appropriate legal basis for such processing. 
Therefore, the first aspect to consider is the nature of the data controller, which, having the status of local administration, requires referring to Law 7/1985, of April 2, Regulating the Bases of the Local Regime (LRBRL), which in its Article 4.1 b) provides the following: 
1. As public administrations of a territorial nature, and within the scope of their competencies, municipalities, provinces, and islands correspond in all cases:

b) Tax and financial powers.
For its part, Royal Legislative Decree 2/2004, of March 5, which approves the consolidated text of the Law Regulating Local Finances (TRLRHL), in its Article 2.2 under the heading "Enumeration of the resources of local entities" provides as follows:
2. For the collection of taxes and amounts that, as public law revenues, such as non-tax public patrimonial benefits, public prices, and monetary fines and sanctions, must be collected by the treasury of local entities in accordance with the provisions of the previous section, said treasury shall hold the prerogatives legally established for the treasury of the State, and shall act, where applicable, in accordance with the corresponding administrative procedures.
Finally, Law 58/2003, of December 17, General Tax Law (LGT) in its Article 4 under the heading "Tax Power" indicates in its section 2 the following:
2. The autonomous communities and local entities may establish and demand taxes, in accordance with the Constitution and the laws.
Taking into account the above, the first conclusion reached is that Local Entities are legally attributed the tax power, and consequently, any processing of personal data that arises from its exercise will find in these laws the legitimization to carry it out.  
IV
Regarding the specific processing that occurs as a result of payment by a third party, Royal Decree 939/2005, of July 29, which approves the General Collection Regulation (RGR) provides in its Article 33 under the heading "Legitimization, place of payment and method of payment" the following:
1. Any person, whether or not they have an interest in fulfilling the obligation, may make the payment, either during the voluntary period or the executive period, whether they know and approve it, or whether the debtor is unaware of it.
The third party who pays the debt shall not be entitled to exercise before the Administration the rights that correspond to the debtor.
For its part, Article 41 of the RGR under the heading "Proofs and certifications of payment" provides the following:
1. Anyone who makes a payment of a debt in accordance with the provisions of this regulation shall have the right to receive proof of payment.
2. Proofs of payment in cash shall be, as applicable:
a) Receipts.
b) Payment letters signed or validated by competent bodies or by entities authorized to receive payment.
c) Certifications evidencing the payment made.
d) Any other document expressly granted the status of proof of payment by the Ministry of Economy and Finance and, in particular, those determined by the regulations governing income by telematic means.
3. Proofs of payment in cash must indicate, at a minimum, the following circumstances:
a) Name and surname or corporate name or full designation, tax identification number, and address of the debtor.
b) Concept, amount of the debt, and period to which it refers.
c) Date of payment.
d) Body, person, or entity that issues it.

Having said the above, it is necessary to refer to Articles 34 and 95 of the LGT to analyze the existence of possible limits that may be encountered in the processing of data that arises from the inquiry.
Article 34.1 section j) of the LGT under the heading "Rights and guarantees of taxpayers" recognizes the right of taxpayers to the confidentiality of tax data concerning them; specifically, it states the following:
1. The following are rights of taxpayers, among others: (...)
i) Right, under the legally established terms, to the confidentiality of the data, reports, or background obtained by the tax administration, which may only be used for the application of the taxes or resources whose management is entrusted to it and for the imposition of sanctions, without being able to be transferred or communicated to third parties, except in the cases provided for in the laws.
And Article 95 of the LGT, in similar terms, confines the use of reserved data to the effective application of the taxes, and establishes a catalog of exceptions when we are faced with a different purpose. Specifically, it indicates the following:

1. The data, reports, or background information obtained by the tax administration in the performance of its functions are confidential and may only be used for the effective application of the taxes or resources for which it is responsible and for the imposition of the applicable sanctions, without being able to be transferred or communicated to third parties, unless the transfer is intended for:

From the reading of articles 34.1 j) and 95.1 of the LGT, it is concluded that a general regime of limitation on the use of tax data is established - which gives meaning to its character of "confidential" - and a list of exceptions when the processing is carried out by a third party for purposes other than the effective application of the taxes. 
In the present case, we are dealing with the processing of personal data carried out by the data controller, which is the local administration, in the exercise of its competencies and which is included in the effective application of the taxes or resources for which it is responsible, being made effective through articles 33 and 41 of the aforementioned RGR. This must be connected with what is indicated in article 1158 of the Civil Code (a supplementary rule applicable to the legal regime of public administrations), that is, it serves the purpose of allowing the new creditor to prove such condition for the purpose of executing the corresponding credit.
V
Therefore, the legal basis that legitimizes such processing is found in article 6.1 e) of the GDPR, which allows the processing of data when the processing is necessary for the fulfillment of a mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred to the data controller; and in article 8.2 of the LOPDGDD, which requires that such public powers be attributed by a norm with the rank of law.
In the present case, we are obviously not dealing with the exercise of competencies or powers of the third party making the payment (since, in addition to being a private individual, they are not the data controller), but rather the processing that occurs is the issuance and delivery by the data controller (or their appointee) of information about the payment obligor to that third party.
Therefore, the processing that occurs is the materialization of one of the different faculties that comprise the tax power, which is held by the local administration by legal attribution.
In this case, and in relation to the requirement of a norm with the rank of law that is required by article 8.2 of the LOPDGDD, it is appropriate to cite the LRBRL, the Local Finance Law, and the LGT itself, which expressly attribute the tax power to local entities and frame within that power, specifically in the management of taxes, the possibility for payment to be made by a third party (and, consequently, the data processing that derives from it), all in accordance with what is provided in article 1158 of the Civil Code regarding the action of the new creditor against the payment obligor.
VI
Furthermore, there is another aspect that cannot be overlooked and that is closely related to the impact or interference in the privacy of the tax obligor.
The third party not obligated to pay who proceeds to do so - and to whom, with the application of articles 33.1 and 41.3 RGR, data of the obligor will be communicated - logically and necessarily must know some data or information about the tax in question and the obligor, since otherwise, not only would they not be able to carry out such payment, but it would not make sense for them to be willing to pay something about which they have no information or knowledge and relation.
This means that the issuance of the corresponding receipt in accordance with article 41.3 RGR and the specific communication of data contained therein will reveal to the third party information that they presumably already know, since the aforementioned provision states in its paragraphs a) and b) that the receipt will contain the name and surname, tax identification number, address of the debtor, and the concept and amount of the debt.
Consequently, the interference in the privacy of the debtor by the issuance and communication of the receipt to the third party who has made the payment cannot be considered of notable intensity or severity, all without prejudice to the application of the principles of purpose limitation and data minimization that will be analyzed next.

In addition to the principle of legality, it should be noted that Article 5.1 b) and c) encompasses the principles of purpose limitation and data minimization, according to which personal data shall be:
b) collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and shall not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes (“purpose limitation”);
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”);
Furthermore, Article 5.2 of the GDPR establishes the principle of proactive accountability, according to which:
The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and shall be able to demonstrate it (“proactive accountability”).
In accordance with the aforementioned principles, two consequences arise. The first is that the data controller must conduct an analysis regarding the application of these principles when issuing receipts to third parties unrelated to the debtor.
Indeed, and without prejudice to the fact that Article 41.3 of the RGR establishes a minimum content, nothing prevents the data controller, directly or through collaborating entities, from establishing formulas or applying techniques that prevent the communication of more data than is strictly necessary or that are intended for incompatible purposes.
Article 4.5 of the GDPR defines “pseudonymization” as the processing of personal data in such a way that the data can no longer be attributed to a data subject without the use of additional information, provided that such additional information is kept separately and is subject to technical and organizational measures to ensure that personal data is not attributed to an identified or identifiable natural person;
In this regard, the consultant proposes the partial anonymization of the receipt, omitting the ID number of the taxpayer and their tax address, techniques that are considered adequate and serve the principle of minimization. 
Thus, the third party who has made the payment must have proof that they have indeed proceeded with it, whereby a certificate that records its effective execution by the third party, along with the file or tax number, date, and period to which it refers, would be sufficient. This would allow them, if deemed necessary, to exercise the actions derived from Article 1158.2 and 3 of the Civil Code.

In conclusion, nothing prevents the data controller from, in application of the principles of minimization and in the exercise of the obligations derived from the principle of proactive accountability, specifying the content of Article 41.3 of the RGR for these special or extraordinary cases, where a third party makes the payment, through pseudonymization and, where appropriate, modification - which does not imply suppression - of the minimum content provided for in that provision.
The second consequence is that, when issuing the receipt, the data controller should inform about the purpose served by the data communication and the warnings regarding the limitations on the use of the information contained in the receipt. 
A data protection clause in the corresponding receipts could have a preventive effect, contributing to increasing the awareness of the third party regarding the obligations and commitments arising from access to the data of the taxpayer.
Consequently, the third party to whom the data has been provided may only process the data of the payer for that purpose, make the payment, and, where applicable, exercise the action derived from Article 1158 of the Civil Code.
