The inquiry raises the question of whether it is in accordance with the provisions of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, for one of the parties in a judicial process in which the inquirer is not a party, to submit as evidence a complaint against her and a judicial ruling related to her and her minor descendants.  

As a starting point, it is worth recalling that Judgment 292/2000 of the Constitutional Court, while enshrining the right to the protection of personal data derived from Article 18.4 of the Constitution as an autonomous right informing the constitutional text, also states that “this Court has declared that the right to the protection of data is not unlimited, and although the Constitution does not expressly impose specific limits on it, nor refers to the Public Authorities for its determination as it has done with other fundamental rights, there is no doubt that they must be found in the remaining fundamental rights and constitutionally protected legal goods, as required by the principle of unity of the Constitution.”  

The legislator has created a system in which the protection of the right to the protection of personal data yields in those cases where the legislator (constitutional or ordinary) has considered the existence of reasoned and founded motives that justify the necessity of data processing, incorporating such cases into norms of at least the same rank as the one regulating the protected matter.  

Following this criterion, it must be interpreted that the use of the documents referred to in the inquiry as evidence in court could be based on the Spanish Constitution itself, where the fundamental right of the parties to obtain effective judicial protection in the exercise of their rights and interests is regulated (Article 24.1 of the Constitution), and specifically the fundamental right of the parties “to use the relevant means of evidence for their defense” (Article 24.2 of the Constitution).  

In this same sense, the National Court declared in its ruling of October 22, 2010, the following:  
“On one hand, it must be taken into account that one of the reasons that excludes the need for consent for the transfer of personal data is that the communication to be made has judges or courts as its recipients (Art. 11.2.d) LOPD).

An exception in which it is not unreasonable to include those cases where the evidence, although initially not requested by the Judge or Court, but submitted by the parties, has subsequently not been rejected, but incorporated by the Judge into the proceedings, as seems to be the case, and as is inferred from the trial record, occurred in the present case.  

On the other hand, and although it is true that judicial proceedings are also subject to data protection regulations, as we indicated in SAN 9-10-2009 (Rec. 37/2009), since the right to data protection, as a fundamental and autonomous right provided for in Article 18.4 CE, binds all public authorities (Art. 53 CE), including the Judiciary, as also indicated by STS 18-9-2006 Rec. 274/2002. However, this LOPD must be applied with great caution, and to the extent that it is compatible with the own functions (jurisdictional and non-jurisdictional) of the aforementioned judicial bodies, since the uniqueness of jurisdictional activity and the interests underlying it sometimes require a limitation or modulation of the rights and guarantees of citizens.  

In addition to the fact that the subjection of judicial files to the LOPD must be understood (according to the same SAN 9-10-2009 Rec. 37/2009) without detriment to the jurisdictional function and, therefore, pertaining to what should be considered as an "accessory" or administrative aspect of the jurisdictional function, focusing specifically on the judicial procedure, there are also in it a series of interests and guarantees that hold significant value in that process, such as the true clarification of the facts or the legitimate exercise of the right of defense of the parties, which must be weighed in those cases where such interests and guarantees converge with the right contemplated in Article 18.4 CE, to the extent that they may lead to a significant limitation of such right to the protection of personal data.”  

Therefore, it is up to the Judge or Court, within the framework of the process, to determine whether the proposed evidence is admitted or not, so it does not fall to this Agency to assess whether the evidence complies with the principles governing the processing and transfer of personal data, as such assessment could collide with the right to effective judicial protection of the interested party.  

Consequently, it will be the Court that is aware of the process that determines, in the Order of admission of the evidence, whether to uphold the right to effective judicial protection of judges and courts contained in Article 24 of the Constitution, in its aspect of the right to relevant evidence.
