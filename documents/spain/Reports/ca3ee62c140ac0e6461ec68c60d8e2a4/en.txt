Article 55. Regulatory Powers. Circulars of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection.

2. Its preparation shall be subject to the procedure established in the Statute of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, which must provide for the necessary technical and legal reports and the hearing of interested parties.

I.
The right to the protection of personal data and privacy in relation to unsolicited communications has been regulated, until the approval of the current Law 11/2022, of June 28, General Telecommunications Law (hereinafter, “LGTel”), in Article 48.1 of the now repealed Law 9/2014, of May 9.

Said article established an “opt-in” (consent) regime for unsolicited commercial communications made through automatic calls without human intervention or fax messages (section 1.a)) in contrast to the “opt-out” (right of opposition) regime provided for those made through different systems (section 1.b)).

The aforementioned “opt-out” regime, which recognized end users a right of opposition to receiving calls for commercial communication purposes, was maintained identically in the draft LGTel that was sent by the Government to the Cortes Generales.

However, following the processing of the bill in the Congress of Deputies, the approval of an amendment resulted in the replacement of the aforementioned “opt-out” regime (right of opposition) with an “opt-in” regime based on consent.

Subsequently, during the continuation of the parliamentary processing of the LGTel, the legitimizing basis of consent was supplemented with a reference to the legitimizing bases contained in Article 6.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 on the processing of personal data (GDPR), as an additional formula in coherence with the aforementioned regulation.

Thus, the wording of Article 66.1.b) of the current LGTel enshrines the right of end users of publicly available interpersonal communication services based on numbering to:
“(…)
b) not to receive unwanted calls for commercial communication purposes, unless there is prior consent from the user themselves to receive such commercial communications or unless the communication can be supported by another legitimizing basis provided for in Article 6.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 on the processing of personal data.”

It is worth mentioning that the referred “opt-in” regime, which has long been in effect for commercial communications by email or equivalent electronic means (see Article 21 of Law 34/2002, of July 11, on information society services and electronic commerce (LSSI)), is in accordance with the provisions of Article 13 of the current Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of July 12, 2002, on the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on privacy and electronic communications).

II.
Indeed, with the wording of article 66.1.b), the legislator has opted for a regulatory change that involves transitioning from the “opt-out” regime (right of opposition) provided for in article 48.1.b) of the repealed Law 9/2014 to an “opt-in” regime (based on consent or another legitimizing basis, e.g., article 6.1 GDPR). This regulatory change can only materialize, in practice, in a new way of applying and interpreting the rights of end users regarding the receipt of calls for commercial communication purposes, as a legal basis is now required for this purpose.

As described, this “opt-in” regime primarily rests on the prior consent of the user to receive calls for commercial communication purposes, and it may also be supported—by express provision of the aforementioned paragraph b) of article 66.1 of the LGTel—by other legitimizing bases provided for in article 6.1 of the GDPR, such as legitimate interest.

In this regard, it is considered that the concurrence of this legal basis of legitimate interest must be verified in each case, taking into account the specific rights and interests affected, and therefore, generic invocation is not permissible. It should be noted that allowing an unrestricted application of the legitimate interest of third parties as a legitimizing basis for sending commercial communications could ultimately distort the regime change established by the legislator, stripping it of its practical effects.

III.
Therefore, this Directorate Center observes the need to define the terms and circumstances under which the legitimate interest of the data controller can be invoked, although a clear case is already identified by including—according to the provisions of article 3 of the draft—the existence of a prior contractual relationship with the data controller, in line with what is provided in paragraph 2 of article 21 of the LSSI. However, it is especially necessary regarding the invocation of legitimate interest by third parties, given that a broad and flexible interpretation of this legitimizing basis for the processing of personal data by third parties for the purpose of making unsolicited commercial communications could be contrary to the purpose of the regulatory change that has been made in the conduct of such calls under article 66.1.b) of the LGTel or would leave it practically without practical application as a regulatory innovation, since in reality, the legal regime prior to the current LGTel would continue to apply.

One of the main issues that has arisen during the public consultation process, and regarding which the highest number of observations has been received, both for and against, is related to the possibility that making telephone calls for commercial purposes to random phone numbers, a widespread practice in Spain, could still be understood as permissible under Article 66.1.b) of the LGTel, as it is legitimized in accordance with Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR, provided that once the interests of the parties involved are assessed, the outcome is favorable for the legitimate interest pursued by the data controller.

As we have previously pointed out, the direct recognition of the right not to receive unwanted calls entails the obligation of the data controllers not to make such calls unless they demonstrate the existence of one of the two exceptions contemplated in the aforementioned provision, which, given their exceptional nature, must be subject to restrictive interpretation. This reinforces the need for this circular to establish the criteria that will allow for the delimitation of the cases in which the AEPD will consider the balancing carried out by the data controller as adequate to deem their legitimate interest prevailing, avoiding that the corresponding balancing could be conducted in such broad terms that it evades the change in legal regime that has occurred with the new regulation.

For these purposes, it cannot be argued that the randomly generated phone number is not a personal data and therefore, the call could be made since other personal data of the person being called is unknown.

This Agency is aware of the existence of various rulings from the National Court, well before the GDPR, in which it was considered that the randomly generated phone number could not be regarded as personal data. In this regard, in the ruling of November 16, 2005, the Court considered that the “automatic call made to the complainant's home, without any human intervention, randomly, without knowing the owner of that phone and without any mention of personal data of the recipient, can be defined as processing of personal data in the terms established in the aforementioned Article 3.c) of the LOPD, since at no time has there been such collection, recording, storage, or processing of the same. There has simply been a telephone call with the characteristics described, but in it, there has been no processing of the personal data of the appellant, since the author did not even know the owner or address of that phone number, nor did that recording contain any reference to personal data of the recipient, but was merely advertising. Therefore, [...] that inclusion in its file of the complainant's phone number in the terms in which the plaintiff did it, [...] is not personal data according to the concept defined in Article 3.a) of the LOPD – any information concerning identified or identifiable natural persons, since the names of their owners are not contained in their automated files, but simply those phone numbers taken from publicly accessible telephone directories, without other associated data.” In similar terms, it was stated in the ruling of September 17, 2008, considering that in a similar case it had not been proven “that through the mobile phone number the owner had been identified or that such identification was possible from the said number, so that the mentioned phone number, devoid of other circumstances that identify or could allow the identification of its owner, prevents it from fitting into the legal definition of personal data.”

The GDPR starts from a broad concept of personal data as any information concerning an identified or identifiable natural person (“the data subject”); an identifiable natural person is considered to be any person whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by means of an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or several elements specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that person (Article 4.1).
In relation to the requirement of identifiability, Recital 26 states that “To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means, such as singling out, that may reasonably be used by the data controller or by any other person to identify the natural person, directly or indirectly, must be taken into account. Therefore, since it involves the communication of personal data between different controllers, consent must be the legal basis that enables the transfer of data between companies in the group, of which the data subject may only be a client of one of them, as it allows for the proper guarantee of control over their personal data by the data subject, being aware of to whom the data is transferred and for what purpose, prior to the provision of consent. 
Legitimate interest as a legal basis enabling such processing, within the framework of a generic balancing of the interests, rights, and freedoms of the data subjects, would reduce the guarantees that the GDPR provides to data subjects regarding their rights and freedoms, diminishing the ability to decide and control over personal data, in addition to the impact on the rights of the data subjects and their reasonable expectations regarding what will happen to their data.
In this regard, Recital 48 of the GDPR states that “Controllers that are part of a corporate group or entities affiliated with a central body may have a legitimate interest in transmitting personal data within the corporate group for internal administrative purposes, including the processing of personal data of customers or employees. Therefore, the specific consent of the affected party is required to transfer their personal data, specifically the phone number, between different companies in the group for commercial purposes, as stated in the draft circular.

VII
To conclude this report, reference must be made to other specific guarantees that have been included in the new Article 6 of the draft circular, related to the duty to inform and the manner of withdrawing consent or exercising the right to object, including the obligation to record calls. Without prejudice to the fact that some of them are already provided for in the general legislation on consumers and users, such as the obligation to identify the entity making the call and, where applicable, on whose behalf it acts (Article 96 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2007, of November 16, which approves the consolidated text of the General Law for the Defense of Consumers and Users and other complementary laws), their inclusion in the circular, from the perspective of protecting the fundamental right to personal data protection, contributes to reinforcing the principles of fairness and transparency of processing (Article 5.1.a) of the GDPR) and as a specific guarantee to identify the legal basis on which the call is based, while facilitating the exercise of the rights of the affected parties such as the right of access, the right to object, the right to erasure, or the right to withdraw consent.

Similarly, considering that the withdrawal of consent can be made at any time (Article 7.3 of the GDPR) and that the exercise of the right to object in the case of direct marketing (Article 21.2 of the GDPR) can be exercised at any time (Article 21.2 of the GDPR), the obligation to inform in each call about the possibility of revoking consent or exercising the right to object to receiving unwanted commercial calls is deemed appropriate, as well as that any unequivocal expression of the user against receiving such calls is understood as a revocation of consent or, where applicable, the exercise of the right to object, taking into account the duty of the data controller to provide means to facilitate the interested party's exercise of their rights (Article 12.2 and Recital 59 of the GDPR) and the intended purpose of the reform.

Finally, the recording of calls is essential in accordance with the principle of proactive responsibility as a way to demonstrate compliance with the requirements established in this circular, as well as to facilitate the end user’s exercise of their right of access to their personal data, which includes "the right to obtain an authentic reproduction of their personal data, understood in a broad sense, that are subject to operations that must be classified as processing by the data controller," as stated in the judgment of the CJEU of May 4, 2023 (case C-487/21, paragraph 28).
