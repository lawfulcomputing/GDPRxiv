The inquiry raises whether it is in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (hereinafter GDPR), the Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD), and Law 2/2023 of February 20, regulating the protection of persons who report regulatory infringements and the fight against corruption (hereinafter Law 2/2023), the use by the inquirer of the information received under said law that does not fall within its scope of application to fulfill other purposes such as efficiency, transparency, and good governance.  

Specifically, the viability of processing that information (which contains personal data) communicated through the Internal Information System and that is not related to the behaviors referred to in Article 2 of Law 2/2023 is raised in order to efficiently address all communications received through this System.  

I  
The inquiry presented involves the processing of personal data, so the GDPR and the LOPDGDD will apply.  

For processing to be in accordance with the current legal framework on data protection, it must comply, among other issues, with the data protection principles set forth in Article 5 of the GDPR, which states:  
1. Personal data shall be:  
   a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (“lawfulness, fairness, and transparency”);  
   b) collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (“purpose limitation”);  
   c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”);  
   d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable steps must be taken to ensure that personal data that are inaccurate with respect to the purposes for which they are processed are erased or rectified without delay (“accuracy”);  
   e) kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of the processing of personal data; personal data may be stored for longer periods as long as they are processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, without prejudice to the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures imposed by this Regulation to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject (“storage limitation”);  
   f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, by applying appropriate technical or organizational measures (“integrity and confidentiality”).  
2. The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and be able to demonstrate it (“accountability”).

In the present case, from the analysis of the legal provision on which the consulting party intends to base the processing of data that it becomes aware of as a result of Law 2/2023 (i.e., Article 112 of Law 40/2015), it must be considered that the requirements that have just been indicated to justify the interference with the right to data protection that would occur are not met.

That said, the Opinion 3/2013 of the Article 29 Working Party, currently the European Data Protection Committee, on the “Limitation of Purpose” is perfectly applicable today and is particularly clarifying given the elements affected by this consultation. 
As a starting point, regarding the principle of limitation of purpose, it indicates the following: 
(...) the principle of limitation of purpose inhibits the "diversion of mission," which could otherwise lead to the use of personal data available beyond the purposes for which they were initially collected. 
(...) The prohibition of "incompatibility" set forth in Article 6, paragraph 1, letter b), does not completely exclude new uses of the data, as long as this is carried out within the parameters of compatibility. 
(...) The principle of limitation of purpose is designed to provide a balanced approach: an approach aimed at reconciling the need for predictability and legal certainty regarding the purposes of processing, on one hand, and the pragmatic need for a certain degree of flexibility on the other. 
(...) The limitation must, for example, prevent the use of personal data of individuals in a manner (or for other purposes) that they may find unexpected, inappropriate, or otherwise unacceptable. At the same time, the concept of compatible use also offers a certain degree of flexibility for data controllers. 
(...) The fact that further processing has a different purpose does not necessarily mean that it is automatically incompatible: this must be assessed on a case-by-case basis, as will be demonstrated below. 
(...)

Importance. It is hardly conceivable that the hypothetical informant could expect subsequent processing of that kind. It must be assumed that the Informant Channel is approached with trust in the guarantees and safeguards provided by the regulation, and for a specific purpose, which makes it coherent to consider that the subsequent processing intended by the consultant is not expected and, consequently, is surprising. 
In this regard, the aforementioned Opinion indicates the following:
In general, the more unexpected or surprising the continued use is, the more likely it is to be considered incompatible.
It also offers the following considerations regarding this predictability, taking into account the context of data collection; it should be noted that we would be in the context of an internal channel for reporting cases of corruption:
In all these cases, it is also important to consider whether the status of the data controller, the nature of the relationship or the service provided, or the applicable legal or contractual obligations (or other promises made at the time of collection) could lead to reasonable expectations of stricter confidentiality and stricter limitations on use. In general, the more specific and restrictive the context in which the data is collected, the greater the limitations that may exist for subsequent processing. Once again, it is necessary to take into account the factual context rather than simply relying on fine print.
Thirdly, regarding the nature of the data and the impact of subsequent processing on the data subjects; we cannot overlook that the hypothetical informant could provide data of very diverse nature that, in their opinion, would fall within the scope of the law, and that therefore could involve possible breaches of the legal system. Data could be provided as foreseen in Article 10 of the GDPR, or in Articles 10 and 27 of the LOPDGDD; and special categories of data as per Article 9.1 of the GDPR could also be involved.
The Opinion refers to these possibilities:
The nature of the processed data plays a fundamental role in all its provisions. Therefore, it would be important to assess whether the subsequent processing involves sensitive data, either because they belong to the special categories of data contemplated in Article 8 of the Directive, or for other reasons, such as in the case of biometric data, genetic information, communication data, location data, and other types of personal information that require special protection. In general, the more delicate the information, the more restricted the scope of application for compatible use would be.   
And finally, regarding the safeguards applied by the data controller to ensure fair processing and prevent any undue impact on the data subjects, we must take into account that the regulation itself establishes very strict guarantees of confidentiality, anonymity, unauthorized access, deletion, and, where applicable, retention. All of these could be affected depending on how the new processing is carried out. In fact, since it is presumed that they do not fall within the scope of application of Law 2/2023, all the guarantees that it establishes would be circumvented.
In this regard, the Opinion proposes that:
In some cases, requesting specific authorization for the new processing may, in particular, help to compensate for the change of purpose. That is, a new legal basis under Article 7, letter a), may contribute, in some situations, to compensating for the incompatibility. However, it is important to reiterate that the compatibility requirements under Article 6, paragraph 1, letter b), and the requirement for an adequate legal basis under Article 7 are cumulative. That is, a new legal basis alone cannot legitimize a new incompatible use.
Furthermore, the application of additional technical and organizational measures may be particularly important. The identification of relevant measures is facilitated if certain basic objectives of data protection and data security are taken into account. The classic objectives of data security are availability, integrity, and confidentiality. To effectively meet the requirements of data protection, the objectives of transparency and isolation must also consider the possibility of intervention.
When it comes to determining technical and organizational measures that can be considered adequate safeguards to compensate for the change of purpose, attention often focuses on the concept of isolation. Among the relevant measures, total or partial anonymization, pseudonymization, or data aggregation, privacy-enhancing technologies, as well as other measures to ensure that data cannot be used to make decisions or take other actions regarding individuals ("functional separation") can be mentioned, among others.
