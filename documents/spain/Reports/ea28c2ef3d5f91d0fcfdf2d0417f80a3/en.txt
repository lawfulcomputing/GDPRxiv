The inquiry raises the question of “whether there would be a breach of the principles of the GDPR due to the fact that the Office for Prevention and Fight Against Corruption is communicating the personal data of all vaccinated citizens without these being anonymized, or whether, in accordance with the previously mentioned regulations, it would be coherent and sufficient to provide anonymized data or limit it exclusively to what is strictly necessary for the purpose of the investigation initiated by the aforementioned Office, which in no case has been detailed in the request received.”  

For these purposes, the information request sent by the Office for Prevention and Fight Against Corruption of the Balearic Islands to the Health Service of the Balearic Islands is attached, with the aim of obtaining information regarding the vaccination protocols of the Balearic Islands against COVID-19 and the specific actions of vaccine administration, including, among other requested information, the following:  

2.1. Regarding each of the vaccination processes and teams: establishment, office, or location where vaccination has been carried out (residences, PAC, hospitals, clinics, and others), lists of individuals initially planned for vaccination, individuals actually vaccinated, vials supplied, and vials rejected (if applicable).  
2.2. Name, surname, and ID number of the vaccinated person.  
2.3. Identification of the group to which they correspond in the vaccination protocol.  
2.4. Date and time of the first and second doses (in the case of the latter, if it has been administered).  
2.5. Identification of the supplied vial (numerical code).

As this Agency has repeatedly pointed out, at the time of formulating the inquiry, it must be based on the new regime established by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR) and in Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights.  

Indeed, as indicated in the Explanatory Memorandum of Law 3/2018, “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to another...”

which rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the data controller or the processor of the risk that may arise from the processing of personal data in order to, based on that assessment, adopt the appropriate measures."
Consequently, it is the data controller who must comply with the principles set forth in Article 5 of the GDPR, among which, as seen, is the principle of proactive responsibility, included in its paragraph 2, "the data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and able to demonstrate it ('proactive responsibility')." And among the principles of paragraph 1 is that of "lawfulness," included in its letter a), so that personal data shall be processed lawfully, with Article 6 regulating the legal bases that determine the lawfulness of the processing. Therefore, it is the responsibility of the data controller to determine the legal basis that may support the corresponding processing.
A fundamental role, finally, within this new model of active responsibility established in the General Data Protection Regulation will be played by the data protection officer, which the General Regulation governs in Articles 37 to 39. In particular, Article 37.1 a) mandatorily imposes the designation of a delegate in cases where "the processing is carried out by a public authority or body, except for courts acting in their judicial capacity."
In turn, Article 38.1 clearly establishes that "The data controller and the processor shall ensure that the data protection officer is involved appropriately and in a timely manner in all issues related to the processing of personal data," and Article 39.2 provides that "The data protection officer shall perform their duties with due regard to the risks associated with processing operations, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing."
Finally, Article 39.1 enumerates the functions of the data protection officer, among which are "to inform and advise the controller or the processor and the employees involved in the processing of their obligations under this Regulation and other data protection provisions of the Union or the Member States" (paragraph a), "to monitor compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, other data protection provisions of the Union or the Member States, and the policies of the controller or the processor regarding personal data protection, including the allocation of responsibilities, awareness-raising and training of staff involved in processing operations, and the corresponding audits" (paragraph b), and "to provide the advice requested."

For its part, Recital (35) states that personal data related to health must include all data related to the health status of the data subject that provides information about their physical or mental health, past, present, or future. (…) any number, symbol, or data assigned to a natural person that uniquely identifies them for health purposes; information obtained from tests or examinations of a part of the body or a bodily substance, including that derived from genetic data and biological samples, and any information related, for example, to a disease, a disability, the risk of suffering from diseases, medical history, clinical treatment, or the physiological or biomedical status of the data subject, regardless of its source, for example, a doctor or another health professional, a hospital, a medical device, or an in vitro diagnostic test. 
Furthermore, the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European Union has also advocated for a broad interpretation of the concept, as already noted in the Judgment of November 6, 2003 (Case C-101/01, Lindqvist) interpreting Directive 95/46/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of October 24, 1995, on the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, in its paragraph 50: 
Considering the object of this Directive, it is necessary to give a broad interpretation to the expression "health data," used in its Article 8, paragraph 1, so that it encompasses information related to all aspects, both physical and mental, of a person's health. 
From the above, it must be concluded that the information regarding the condition of having received the vaccine, as it reveals information about the health status of the individuals who have received it, is a health data, and therefore must be included within the "special categories of data" in accordance with Article 9 of the GDPR.

The processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health, or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation is prohibited.

In the same sense, the Court of Justice of the European Union has ruled, highlighting the existing limits on the mass communication of personal data even in cases where they are requested by the competent authorities for the prevention, investigation, inquiry, and prosecution of crimes.

Subsequently, in its judgment of December 21, 2016 (Joined Cases C-2013/15 and C-698/15, Tele2 Sverige AB and others), the Court analyzed whether the national transposition rules of the aforementioned Directive 2006/24/EC could be considered compliant with Union Law, noting that there was no such compliance in a rule that provided for the generalized and indiscriminate collection of data and did not subject access to the same to prior administrative and judicial control.

Regarding the first of the mentioned issues, paragraph 94 of the judgment recalled that “in accordance with Article 52(1) of the Charter, any limitation on the exercise of the rights and freedoms recognized by it must be established by law and respect its essential content,” adding that paragraph 96 stated that “the respect for the principle of proportionality is also derived from the repeated case law of the Court of Justice according to which the protection of the fundamental right to respect for private life at the Union level requires that exceptions to the protection of personal data and limitations of that protection do not exceed what is strictly necessary (judgments of December 16, 2008, Satakunnan Markkinapörssi and Satamedia, C-73/07, EU:C:2008:727, paragraph 56; of November 9, 2010, Volker und Markus Schecke and Eifert, C-92/09 and C-93/09, EU:C:2010:662, paragraph 77; Digital Rights, paragraph 52, and of October 6, 2015, Schrems, C-362/14, EU:C:2015:650, paragraph 92).”

Having stated the above, according to paragraph 100, “the interference that a regulation of this type entails in the fundamental rights recognized in Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter is of great magnitude and must be considered particularly serious.” And paragraph 103 adds that “while it is true that the effectiveness of the fight against serious crime, especially organized crime and terrorism, may largely depend on the use of modern investigative techniques, this objective of general interest, however fundamental it may be, cannot alone justify that a national regulation establishing the generalized and indiscriminate retention of all traffic and location data should be considered necessary for the purposes of that fight (see, by analogy, regarding Directive 2006/24, the judgment Digital Rights, paragraph 51).”

It is thus concluded that “a national regulation such as that contested in the main proceedings exceeds, therefore, the limits of what is strictly necessary and cannot be considered justified in a democratic society, as required.”

Once the applicable regulations and jurisprudence for the resolution of this inquiry have been presented, in order to assess the compliance with personal data protection regulations of the information request made by the Office of Prevention and Fight against Corruption in the Balearic Islands, the following should be highlighted:
First, that the aforementioned Office has been created, as stated in its Explanatory Memorandum, as a measure to improve democratic quality and as a tool to combat fraud and corruption, and “its objective is to prevent and investigate possible cases of fraudulent use or destination of public funds or any illicit exploitation, arising from behaviors that involve a conflict of interest or the personal use of information derived from the functions of public sector personnel.” It also adds that “The creation of this office complies with the provisions of Article 6 of the United Nations Convention against Corruption, adopted in New York on October 31, 2003, by ensuring the existence of a specialized and independent body responsible for preventing corruption.” 
The aforementioned United Nations Convention against Corruption, ratified on June 9, 2006, has been in force for Spain since July 19.

2006, highlights in its Preamble the concern for “the seriousness of the problems and the threats posed by corruption to the stability and security of societies by undermining institutions and the values of democracy, ethics, and justice, and by compromising sustainable development and the rule of law.” 
Likewise, the fight against corruption is included in other European regulations, such as the Convention on the fight against corruption involving officials of the European Communities or of the Member States of the European Union, signed in Brussels on May 26, 1997, or in more recent regulations such as the latest Directives on public procurement, incorporated into our legal system by Law 9/2017, of November 8, on Public Sector Contracts, which transposes into Spanish law the Directives of the European Parliament and of the Council 2014/23/EU and 2014/24/EU, of February 26, 2014.  
Therefore, the fight against corruption can be considered, for the purposes of Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, as an essential public interest.
On the other hand, based on the duty to provide information imposed by Article 10 of Law 16/2016, of December 9, creating the Office for the Prevention and Fight against Corruption in the Balearic Islands, it provides a series of guarantees, highlighting the duty of confidentiality established in Article 7.3: “Persons exercising their activity in the Office for the Prevention and Fight against Corruption are obliged to keep secret all that they know by reason of their function in the legally established terms, a duty that persists after their cessation in the exercise of the position. The breach of this duty of secrecy gives rise to the responsibility that, in each case, corresponds.” Likewise, another guarantee would be those provided in Article 10.1 itself, when it states that “access to information must be justified, the relationship with the investigated activity must be motivated, and this must be recorded in the file.”
However, while the previous circumstances could lead to considering admissible, in a specific and duly justified case, access to certain health data, in the present case, such a request is deemed excessive and contrary to the regulations on personal data protection, for the following reasons:
First, nothing is indicated in the information request regarding the specific purpose for which the information is being requested, although based on it, it seems to refer to verifying compliance with vaccination protocols concerning individuals who have received the vaccine. And Article 9.2.g) of the GDPR requires that the processing be necessary, and the request has not justified the necessity of accessing the personal data of vaccinated individuals in relation to the investigation being conducted, which would require the performance of the triple test of suitability, necessity, and proportionality, assessing whether the communication of personal data related to the health of certain individuals is necessary for the fulfillment of the purpose of controlling the administrative action pursued, and whether this purpose cannot be achieved by other means that do not require the communication of such data, such as, for example, the communication of aggregated information (i.e., anonymized, in such a way that it does not allow the identification of individuals) related to the different vaccination groups provided in the vaccination protocols or non-belonging to any of them, indicating the total number of individuals that would be in each of those situations.
On the other hand, because such a request implies a massive processing of personal data that, as noted in the consultation, would affect at least 108,500 individuals, which is contrary to the principle of data minimization and to the doctrine of the Constitutional Court and the Court of Justice of the European Union previously cited.
