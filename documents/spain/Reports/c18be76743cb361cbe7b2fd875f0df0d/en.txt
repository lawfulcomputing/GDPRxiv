Legal Advisory Office  
N/REF: 0017/2020  

Having examined your request for a report regarding the processing of data resulting from the current situation arising from the spread of the COVID-19 virus, it must first be clarified, in general terms, that the regulation on the protection of personal data, as it is aimed at safeguarding a fundamental right, applies in its entirety to the current situation, given that there is no reason to suspend fundamental rights, nor has such a measure been adopted.  

Without prejudice to the above, the regulation on the protection of personal data itself (Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation, GDPR)) contains the safeguards and rules necessary to legitimately allow the processing of personal data in situations, such as the present, where there is a public health emergency. Therefore, when applying the provisions provided for these cases in the GDPR, in accordance with the applicable sectoral regulations in the field of public health, considerations related to data protection—within the limits set by law—should not be used to hinder or limit the effectiveness of the measures adopted by the authorities, especially health authorities, in the fight against the epidemic, since the regulation on the protection of personal data already contains provisions for such cases that balance and weigh the interests and rights at stake for the common good.  

I  
Recital (46) of the GDPR already recognizes that in exceptional situations, such as an epidemic, the legal basis for processing may be multiple, based both on public interest and on the vital interest of the data subject or another natural person.  
(46) The processing of personal data must also be considered lawful when it is necessary to protect an essential interest for the life of the data subject or that of another natural person. In principle, personal data should only be processed on the basis of the vital interest of another natural person when the processing cannot be manifestly based on a different legal basis. Certain types of processing may respond to both significant public interest reasons and the vital interests of the data subject, such as when the processing is necessary for humanitarian purposes, including the control of epidemics and their spread, or in situations of humanitarian emergency, especially in cases of natural or man-made disasters.  

Therefore, as a legal basis for lawful processing of personal data, without prejudice to the existence of other bases—such as compliance with a legal obligation, art. 6.1.c) GDPR (for the employer in the prevention of occupational risks for its employees)—the GDPR explicitly recognizes the two mentioned: mission carried out in the public interest (art. 6.1.e) or vital interests of the data subject or other natural persons (art. 6.1.d).  

Article 6.1, letter d) GDPR considers not only that the vital interest is a sufficient legal basis for processing to protect the "data subject" (as this is a term defined in art. 4.1 GDPR as an identified or identifiable natural person), but that this legal basis can be used to protect the vital interests "of another natural person," which by extension implies that such natural persons may even be unidentified or unidentifiable; that is, this legal basis for processing (the vital interest) may be sufficient for the processing of personal data aimed at protecting all those persons susceptible to being infected in the spread of an epidemic, which would justify, from the perspective of personal data processing, in the broadest possible way, the measures adopted for that purpose, even if they are aimed at protecting unnamed or, in principle, unidentifiable or identifiable persons, since the vital interests of such natural persons must be safeguarded, and this is recognized by the regulation on the protection of personal data.  

Paragraph 3 of Article 6 GDPR does not establish the necessity for the basis of processing due to vital interest to be established by Union Law or the Law of the Member States applicable to the data controller, as this paragraph refers exclusively to processing established for the fulfillment of a legal obligation, or for the fulfillment of a mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers, both referred to in letters c) and e) of that Article 6 GDPR, but not for the processing included in letter d).

However, for the processing of health data, it is not enough that there is a legal basis under Article 6 of the GDPR; rather, according to Articles 9.1 and 9.2 of the GDPR, there must be a circumstance that lifts the prohibition on processing such special categories of data (including health data). 
This AEPD understands that such circumstances can be found, in this case, in several of the headings of Article 9.2 of the GDPR. Thus:
(I) In letter b), in the relationships between employer and employee, since the processing is necessary for the fulfillment of obligations and the exercise of specific rights of the data controller (the employer) or the data subject in the field of labor law and social security and protection, as the employer is subject to the regulations on occupational risk prevention (Law 31/1995, of November 8, on the Prevention of Occupational Risks), from which arises, Article 14 and concordant articles of said law, a duty of the employer to protect workers against occupational risks, for which the employer must guarantee the safety and health of all workers in their service in aspects related to work. For that same reason, Article 29 of Law 31/1995, of November 8, on the Prevention of Occupational Risks, which transposes Article 13 of Council Directive (89/391/EEC), of June 12, 1989, on the application of measures to promote the improvement of the safety and health of workers at work, also establishes obligations for workers regarding risk prevention. Thus, it is the responsibility of each worker to ensure, according to their capabilities and by complying with the preventive measures adopted in each case, their own safety and health at work and that of other persons who may be affected by their professional activity, due to their acts and omissions at work, in accordance with their training and the instructions of the employer. This is specified in that they must immediately inform their direct hierarchical superior, and the workers designated to carry out protection and prevention activities or, where appropriate, the prevention service, about any situation that, in their opinion, poses a reasonable risk to the safety and health of workers; contribute to the fulfillment of the obligations established by the competent authority in order to protect the safety and health of workers at work and cooperate with the employer so that they can guarantee working conditions that are safe and do not pose risks to the safety and health of workers. In the context of the current situation arising from COVID-19, this means that the worker must inform their employer in case of suspicion of contact with the virus, in order to safeguard, in addition to their own health, that of other workers at the workplace, so that appropriate measures can be adopted. The employer must process such data in accordance with the GDPR, adopting the appropriate security measures and proactive responsibility required by the processing (Article 32 GDPR).
(II) In letter g), and in letter i), which can be examined together, as both refer to a public interest, the first of which is qualified as "essential" and the second which refers to a public interest qualified "in the field of public health, such as protection against serious cross-border health threats," all based on Union Law or that of the Member States which establishes adequate and specific measures to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject, particularly professional secrecy.
(III) In letter h), when the processing is necessary to carry out a medical diagnosis, or assessment of the worker's work capacity or any other type of health assistance or for the management of health and social care systems and services.
(IV) A final closing circumstance that would allow the processing of health data could even be that established in letter c), in the case that the circumstances provided for in this section are met, which would apply when the processing is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or another natural person, in the event that the data subject is not physically or legally able to give their consent. 
Within these criteria, therefore, the GDPR has sought to give the greatest possible freedom to data controllers in cases of necessity to safeguard vital interests of data subjects or other natural persons, essential public interests in the field of public health, or compliance with legal obligations, within the measures established in the corresponding legal regulations of the Member State or the European Union in each applicable case.
II
Consequently, in a public health emergency situation such as that referred to in the request for this report, it is necessary to take into account that, in the exclusive field of personal data protection regulations,

The application of personal data protection regulations would allow the data controller to make those decisions necessary to safeguard the vital interests of individuals, compliance with legal obligations, or the safeguarding of essential interests in the field of public health, within the framework established by the applicable material regulations. The nature of such decisions (from the perspective of personal data protection regulations, it is reiterated) will be those that data controllers must adopt according to the situation they find themselves in, always aimed at safeguarding the essential interests already reiterated. However, data controllers, while acting to safeguard these interests, must act in accordance with what the authorities established in the regulations of the corresponding Member State, in this case, Spain, stipulate.

Thus, the Spanish legislator has equipped itself with the necessary legal measures to confront health risk situations, such as Organic Law 3/1986, of April 14, on Special Measures in Public Health (amended by Royal Decree-Law 6/2020, of March 10, which adopts certain urgent measures in the economic sphere and for the protection of public health, published in the Official State Gazette on March 11, 2020) or Law 33/2011, of October 4, General Public Health. Article 3 of the first of these regulations states that: 
"[t]o control communicable diseases, the health authority, in addition to carrying out general preventive actions, may adopt the necessary measures for the control of patients, of persons who are or have been in contact with them, and of the immediate environment, as well as those deemed necessary in case of risk of a transmissible nature."

Similarly, Articles 5 and 84 of Law 33/2011, of October 4, General Public Health refer to the previous Organic Law 3/1986, and to the possibility of adopting additional measures in case of risk of disease transmission. 

Therefore, in matters of risk of disease transmission, epidemics, health crises, etc., the applicable regulations have granted "the health authorities of the different public administrations" (art. 1 Organic Law 3/1986, of April 14) the powers to adopt the necessary measures provided for in these laws when urgent or necessary health reasons require it.

Consequently, from the perspective of personal data processing, the safeguarding of essential interests in the field of public health corresponds to the various health authorities of the different public administrations, who may adopt the necessary measures to safeguard these essential public interests in situations of public health emergency. 

It will be these competent health authorities of the different public administrations who must adopt the necessary decisions, and the various data controllers must follow these instructions, even when this involves processing the health data of individuals. The above expressly refers to the possibility of processing the health data of certain individuals by data controllers when, at the direction of the competent health authorities, it is necessary to communicate to other individuals with whom that individual has been in contact the circumstance of their contagion, to safeguard both those individuals from the possibility of contagion (their vital interests) and to prevent those individuals, due to ignorance of their contact with an infected person, from spreading the disease to other third parties (the vital interests of third parties and essential and/or qualified public interest in the field of public health).

Likewise, and in application of what is established in the regulations on occupational risk prevention and occupational medicine, employers may process, in accordance with these regulations and with the guarantees that these norms establish, the data of their employees necessary to ensure the health of all their employees, which also includes other employees different from the interested party, to ensure their right to health protection and to prevent contagions within the company and/or workplaces.

III
However, the processing of personal data in these health emergency situations, as mentioned at the beginning of this report, continues to be governed by personal data protection regulations (GDPR and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights, LOPDGDD), so that...

All its principles contained in Article 5 of the GDPR apply, including the principle of processing personal data lawfully, fairly, and transparently, the principle of purpose limitation (in this case, safeguarding the vital/essential interests of individuals), the principle of accuracy, and, of course, it is important to emphasize the principle of data minimization. Regarding this last aspect, it must be expressly stated that the data processed must be exclusively limited to what is necessary for the intended purpose, and such processing cannot extend to any other personal data that is not strictly necessary for that purpose, without confusing convenience with necessity, because the fundamental right to data protection continues to apply normally, without prejudice to the fact that, as mentioned, the data protection regulations themselves establish that in emergency situations, for the protection of essential public health interests and/or vital interests of individuals, necessary health data may be processed to prevent the spread of the disease that has caused the health emergency. Regarding the principle of purpose limitation in relation to cases of health data processing for public interest reasons, Recital (54) of the GDPR is clear when it states that:
The processing of special categories of personal data, without the consent of the data subject, may be necessary for reasons of public interest in the area of public health. Such processing must be subject to appropriate and specific measures to protect the rights and freedoms of individuals. […] This processing of health-related data for reasons of public interest must not allow third parties, such as employers, insurance companies, or banking entities, to process personal data for other purposes.
