I

The first of these relates to Article 28.2 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations. This article reads as follows, in its current wording (the bold is ours):

2. In the absence of opposition from the interested party, Public Administrations must obtain the documents electronically through their corporate networks or by consulting data intermediation platforms or other electronic systems enabled for this purpose.

Once this period has elapsed, the interested party will be informed that they may provide this report or wait for its submission by the competent body.

Notwithstanding the above, the GDPR, which applies directly, has come to displace those rules that oppose it, in the matter that the GDPR regulates, such as the protection of personal data of natural persons. Specifically, it is worth noting at this moment its Articles 4.11, regarding the definition of consent, and 6.1, in that consent is only one of the possible legal bases for data processing to be considered “lawful” (Article 5.1.a) GDPR).

The GDPR does not accept “tacit” consent, as Article 4.11 only considers “consent of the data subject”: any manifestation of free, specific, informed, and unambiguous will by which the data subject accepts, either through a declaration or a clear affirmative action, the processing of personal data concerning them. Since tacit consent does not derive from a “declaration” or a “clear affirmative action,” “tacit consent” would not be valid. Furthermore, as stated in Recital 43 of the GDPR, [t]o ensure that consent has been given freely, it should not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, particularly when the controller is a public authority and it is therefore unlikely that consent has been given freely in all the circumstances of that particular situation. It is not so much a matter of whether there is express or tacit consent as a possible legal basis for processing, but rather that consent will not be, regarding the processing of non-sensitive data (as the provisions of Article 9 GDPR would apply to these), a valid legal basis for processing. In this sense, Article 28 of Law 39/2015, which requires the consent of the interested party, should be understood as displaced by the GDPR, insofar as it applies to natural persons, since the GDPR does not apply to legal persons (Article 1 GDPR). The legal bases for processing would be those established in paragraphs c) and e) of Article 6.1 GDPR.

Only by law, which in any case must respect its essential content, may the exercise of such rights and freedoms be regulated, which shall be protected in accordance with the provisions of Article 161.1.a). The fundamental right to the protection of personal data is contained in Article 18.4 CE, and therefore the need for a law to limit it applies.

Regarding the scope of the expression in art. 6.1.e) GDPR as a legal basis for the processing of personal data, the inquiry asks whether "any processing of personal data by a public Administration would be covered by letter e) in all cases." The answer must be negative, but not in the sense that the question suggests, rather in a more general sense since art. 6.1.e) GDPR only considers the processing of personal data lawful based on that provision if it is necessary for the fulfillment of a mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred to the data controller. Therefore, if a specific processing is not "necessary" for the fulfillment of the mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of the public powers conferred by the legal system, such processing would not only lack sufficient legal basis as provided in paragraph e), but would also violate the principle of data minimization contained in article 5.1.c) GDPR, which is equally applicable to data processing carried out by the public Administration.

CONCLUSION: the data processing carried out by the Administration is subject to the principles established in the GDPR, including the principle of minimization (art. 5.1.c) GDPR), so only those personal data processing activities that are adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which such data are processed are covered.

To continue addressing the questions raised in the inquiry, it is important to remember that the Administration is bound by the principle of legality, such that, unlike individuals, it can only carry out what the legal system expressly permits. This is the meaning of the provisions in articles 9.1 and 103 of the Constitution, so that when the law and the legal system have not attributed to the Administration the corresponding powers to act in a certain situation, that action cannot be carried out unless the legal system previously attributes such powers to it. Therefore, there is no empty space where, in the absence of law, the Administration can act. This has been referred to as the doctrine of the positive binding of the Administration to legality (García de Enterría). Consequently, for the Administration to act, it requires prior legal authorization (understood here as normative authorization). This applies whether the Administration acts in the field of public law or in the field of private law. Thus, we can see that even in the latter field, where it would act on the same level as individuals, the Administration needs a norm that authorizes it to act. As an example, it can be mentioned that the State can be an heir (example of action in private law), either testamentary or intestate (art. 20 of

Law 33/2003, of November 3, on the Heritage of Public Administrations. As such, the heir has the same position as the other individuals called to the succession, but the possibility of accepting the inheritance is regulated by this law, so in any case, acceptance must be made with the benefit of inventory (art. 20.1). The data processing that the Administration needs to carry out for this purpose would, in principle, have as its legal basis the very basis established in Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR, that is, the processing must be necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest, since the Administration is not only positively bound to the law and to rights in such a way that it cannot act without it or behind its back, but also for that same reason, all its actions are guided, in broad terms, by the public interest, even though its actions take place within the realm of private law.

CONCLUSION: It should be considered, in principle, that since the Administration must always act bound to the law and to rights, its actions in both the field of public law and private law would be protected by the concept of “public interest” provided for in art. 6.1.e) of the GDPR.

In any case, the GDPR excludes the possibility that the processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions (without distinguishing whether such functions are subject to public or private law) can have as its legal basis the letter f) of art. 6.1 of the GDPR, that is, legitimate interest. This is undoubtedly because it is based on the premise, as demonstrated by Recital 45 of the GDPR, that when data processing is carried out in compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller or if it is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers, such processing must have a basis in Union Law or the laws of the Member States. Ultimately, such data processing will be covered by letter e) of art. 6.1 if the applicable law has attributed a competence to the Administration, and it will not be covered otherwise. Obviously, such powers can lead to specific legal bases; thus, for example, no one disputes that the Administration can enter into contracts, whether under public law or private law. In both cases, this contracting power must be established by law, as it regulates the competence and powers of the body to contract, but nothing prevents that in these cases the processing may be based on a different legal basis, such as that of art. 6.1.b) of the GDPR, that is, that the processing is necessary for the execution of a contract, since art. 6.1, first clause, establishes that it will be sufficient for lawful data processing that at least one (there may be more than one) of the conditions regulated by the provision is met.

III 

On the other hand, the reflection made in the inquiry regarding the "lesser" nature of the necessity of certain data processing is not shared. If the data processing is necessary, in a broad sense, so that without such processing the exercise of the competence or authority attributed to the Administration could not be carried out, then such data processing would be covered by letter e) of art. 6.1. If the processing were not necessary, it could not be considered covered by that legal basis; it would be superfluous and contrary, as mentioned, to the principle of minimization. In fact, in the example posed by the inquiry regarding the exercise of administrative powers in which it can declare the interested party to be withdrawn, there is already, in fact, a processing of personal data from the moment the interested party submits the license application, whose legal basis can certainly be that of letter e) of art. 6.1, even if the interested party is subsequently declared withdrawn, or even if the application is rejected due to lack of remedying formal requirements. 

Finally, in this section, it should be mentioned that article 6.3 of the GDPR requires that the purpose of the processing must be determined in the legal basis that is intended to be used in relation to letters c) and e) of paragraph 1 of art. 6, and furthermore, it adds that such purpose will be "necessary" for the fulfillment of the mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers, so it is difficult to conceive a legal norm that does not specify the concrete purposes for which such processing is considered lawful as being in accordance with data protection regulations. But in any case, and as already mentioned earlier, Recital 43 of the GDPR would not consider consent acceptable in this case as a legal basis for processing: [t]o ensure that consent has been freely given, it must not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, particularly when the data controller is a public authority and it is therefore unlikely that consent has been freely given in all the circumstances of that particular situation.

Article 5.1.b) of the GDPR establishes that personal data shall be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and that they shall not be processed further in a manner incompatible with those purposes. In short, a transfer of personal data, such as the address of the individual concerned, between Public Administrations, for the purpose of notifying an administrative act, constitutes a true processing of data, and it must be considered whether such processing is for the same purpose for which it was originally obtained, or if it is for a different purpose, whether that purpose is lawful and compatible with the initial purpose for which the data subject communicated that data to another administrative body, or even if it is not (i.e., the purpose is incompatible), whether it can be understood as included in a provision of Union Law or that of the Member States that constitutes a necessary and proportional measure in a democratic society to safeguard the objectives indicated in Article 23, paragraph 1 (Article 6.4 GDPR).  
  
It should be added that the transfer or transmission of data is a “processing,” as defined by Article 4.2) of the GDPR: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or modification, extraction, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, limitation, deletion, or destruction; 
 
Among the lawful bases for processing personal data, Article 6.1 c) or 6.1 e) of the GDPR recognizes as such that the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller; or when it is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller. In both cases, the functions of the bodies would be established by law, and both would fulfill a public interest mission. Therefore, there would be a lawful legal basis for such processing.

The consulting body has requested, before becoming aware of this Report, an extension of the inquiry, in which it raises "whether it is lawful for the personal data that a Public Administration has collected and processes in the exercise of its own competence (e.g., an autonomous Administration for the regulation of a specific economic activity and that is recorded in a Registry), to transfer them en masse at the request of another Public Administration, such as a Local Administration, so that the latter processes them for the exercise of a completely different competence (e.g., the exercise of sanctioning and disciplinary competence in relation to urban planning). All this citing the duty of collaboration between two Public Administrations that, moreover, operate in the same territorial scope, in accordance with Article 3.1. k) of Law 40/2015" (…).

In short, it asks "whether the legal basis of Article 6.1 c) or Article 6.1 e) of the GDPR would be sufficient for the transfer/communication of data or whether, on the contrary, since the personal data would be processed for purposes different from those for which they were collected and, therefore, in principle incompatible (one, the regulation of an activity, and the other, the disciplinary and sanctioning in relation to a materially different competence), prior consent from the data subjects would be necessary." It seems appropriate to also address these issues in the present Report.

In this regard, it should be mentioned first, as already referenced above in this report, that the GDPR does not consider consent as a "valid legal basis" for the processing of data by a Public Administration, since it does not guarantee that consent has been given "freely," as required by the GDPR (see Recital 43 GDPR, cited). Likewise, Recital 42 GDPR states that "Consent should not be considered freely given when the data subject does not have genuine or free choice or cannot refuse or withdraw consent without detriment." Therefore, this conception is surpassed by the GDPR, in that it cannot be asserted that the consent that a data subject may give, for example, for the processing of their driver's license, is truly given in a "free" manner (in the sense advocated by the GDPR), since if they were to withdraw it and this were the only legitimate basis for processing, it is obvious that they would suffer a detriment, as there is no alternative to obtaining the driver's license.

This does not mean, it is clarified, that there cannot be "consent" from the interested party for the Administration to process their data, because it can even be...

Secondly, and moving on to the specific response to the last inquiry, it is asked whether a “massive” transfer of data to other Administrations is permissible, adding that the purposes for the use of such data would be “different” and “incompatible” with the purposes for which they were collected. The answer must be negative. As already stated in section II of this Report in relation to the rulings of the Constitutional Court 17/2013, of January 31, and STC 292/2000, of November 30, the Constitutional Court has interpreted the requirements and circumstances of data transfer between Public Administrations (FJ 4 to 10):

Therefore, the transfer of data between Public Administrations without the consent of the affected party, when transferred for the exercise of different competencies or concerning different matters than those that motivated their collection, will only be possible, outside the expressly provided cases by the Organic Law on data protection itself, if there is an express legal provision for it [art. 11.2 a) in relation to 6.1 LOPD], since, according to the provisions of art. 53.1 CE, the limits on the right to consent to the transfer of data for purposes other than those for which they were collected are subject to legal reservation. The same principle arises from the GDPR. Its Recital 45, already cited, establishes that:

When [processing] is carried out in compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller, or if it is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority, the processing must have a basis in Union law or the law of the Member States. This Regulation does not require that each individual processing operation be governed by a specific rule. A rule may be sufficient as a basis for several data processing operations based on a legal obligation applicable to the data controller, or if the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority. The purpose of the processing must also be determined under Union law or the law of the Member States. Furthermore, such a rule could specify the general conditions of this Regulation governing the lawfulness of the processing of personal data, establish specifications for determining the data controller, the type of personal data being processed, the affected data subjects, the entities to which personal data may be communicated, the limitations of purpose, the retention period of the data, and other measures to ensure lawful and fair processing. It must also be determined under Union law or the law of the Member States whether the data controller carrying out a task in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority must be a public authority or another natural or legal person under public law, or, when carried out in the public interest, including health-related purposes such as public health, social protection, and the management of health services, a private law entity, such as a professional association.

From this section, it follows that processing based on public interest or the exercise of public power must be established in a rule of Union law or the law of the Member States.

Therefore, within this duty of collaboration among the different Public Administrations, and based on the respect that such collaboration must have for the personal data protection regulations (which, it is reiterated, only affects natural persons, and not therefore legal entities) and in accordance with constitutional jurisprudence, the following CONCLUSION can be reached: there can be no mass and indiscriminate access to personal data, and therefore, conversely, when there is the possibility of transfer established by law, such access must always be “specific in each case adjusted to the data that are necessary for the processing of a specific file and not for mass and indiscriminate access”; “such access could only occur when that data is necessary or pertinent in relation to the processing of a specific file, which allows for the analysis or determination in each case of the conformity of the access with what is established in the General regime that is applicable.” (STC 19/2013, FJ 7º).

Now, it should not be overlooked that the different bodies of the Public Administration that carry out data processing are “responsible” for such processing, and that the possibility of “transferring” such data can be viewed from two perspectives. The first of these is from the perspective of administrative law, as the Administrations are subject to the duty of collaboration among themselves as provided by Laws 39/2015 and 40/2015. However, this duty of collaboration is subject to the personal data protection regulations concerning the interested natural person, as we have seen (Article 13.h) of Law 39/2015), which implies that the Administration is also subject to the data protection regulations.

Currently, this regulation is the GDPR, which replaces the LOPD, so it can be considered that Article 21 of the LOPD 1999 has become inapplicable. Therefore, the transfer of data between Public Administrations must be governed by the general rules provided in the GDPR, and specifically, attention should now be paid to Articles 5.1, letter b); 6.2, 6.3, and 6.4 of the GDPR.

First of all, all processing must be carried out for a specific purpose (“determined, explicit, and legitimate purposes”), and they shall not be processed further “in a manner incompatible” with those purposes, with the treatments for archiving purposes, scientific research, or statistical purposes not being considered incompatible, as directly established by the regulation, in the terms that the GDPR outlines.

However, if such limitations did not exist, this does not mean that data can be transferred between the different Administrations without any limits. The GDPR regulates the possibility not only for a single regulation to foresee several possible treatments or purposes (see Recital 45 GDPR: This Regulation does not require that each individual processing be governed by a specific rule. Given that processing based on consent is not applicable to Public Administrations, it must first be determined, in the case that the purpose is different, whether there is a rule of Union Law or of the Member State that allows the processing of data to safeguard the objectives of Article 23.1 GDPR (which constitutes a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society to safeguard the objectives indicated in Article 23, paragraph 1).

CONCLUSION: If such a rule existed, processing based on it would be “lawful,” by virtue of the GDPR itself even if its purpose were incompatible with the purpose for which the data was initially collected.

Therefore, it would be lawful, from the perspective of personal data protection regulations, for one Administration to transfer personal data to another, even if such data had been collected for a purpose different from the purpose for which the data will be used by the receiving Administration, provided that the purposes for which the data will be used are contained in a rule of Union Law or of the Member State that constitutes a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society to safeguard the objectives indicated in Article 23, paragraph 1 (Article 6.4 GDPR). It is also worth noting that these objectives are as follows:

a) 
the security of the State;

b) 
defense;

c) 
public security;

d) 
the prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offenses or the execution of criminal sanctions, including protection against threats to public security and their prevention;

e) 
other important objectives of general public interest of the Union or of a Member State, in particular an important economic or financial interest of the Union or of a Member State, including in the fields of taxation, budgeting, and monetary policy, public health, and social security;

f) 
the protection of judicial independence and judicial proceedings;

g)

h)

i) 
the protection of the data subject or the rights and freedoms of others;

j)

If such a rule does not exist, or is not applicable to the case, and therefore the intended transfer cannot be supported by it, it is up to the data controller, the transferring Administration in this case, to carry out a balancing assessment in the terms of Article 6.4, paragraphs a) to e) of the same, to determine whether, from the perspective of data protection regulations, such a transfer to another Administration would be possible.

If the purpose of the processing is different from the initial one, the data controller must determine whether the processing for that other purpose is “compatible with the purpose for which the personal data were initially collected.” For this purpose, Article 6.4 establishes a non-exhaustive list of criteria to determine such compatibility of purposes, which by definition will be different from the initial one. If based on these criteria, and for each case

specifically, the responsible party considers that the purpose for which the personal data provided by the transferring Administration would be used is compatible with the initial purpose, such data could be transferred to the requesting Administration.

These criteria (it is reiterated, not exhaustive, as weighting may be carried out on others, provided they are reasonable), in accordance with the GDPR, are as follows:

a) any relationship between the purposes for which the personal data was collected and the purposes of the subsequent processing intended;

b) the context in which the personal data was collected, particularly regarding the relationship between the data subjects and the data controller;

c) the nature of the personal data, specifically when special categories of personal data are processed, in accordance with Article 9, or personal data related to convictions and criminal offenses, in accordance with Article 10;

d) the possible consequences for the data subjects of the intended further processing;

e) the existence of adequate safeguards, which may include encryption or pseudonymization.

CONCLUSION: even in those cases where the purpose of the processing for which the transfer of data is requested by one Administration to another is different from the initial purpose for which such data was collected, such transfer could take place, provided that the responsible party (the transferring Administration), after conducting an analysis of the circumstances in accordance with the criteria set forth in Article 6.4 GDPR (or other reasonable criteria, as they are not exhaustive), considers that this different purpose for which the data is requested is compatible with the purpose for which the personal data was initially collected.

Ultimately, it is about trying to avoid what has come to be called "glass citizens," so that any Administration does not have an expanded knowledge of all possible aspects of the citizens' personality, even those aspects that are unnecessary or superfluous for the exercise of their powers, conferred by the legal framework for the public interest; but likewise, it cannot be considered that data protection regulations may result in an unreasonable limitation on the exercise of such public powers, through the processing of strictly necessary data ("principle of minimization," Article 5.1.c) GDPR) so that the Administration, even through its transfer by other Public Administrations, can process that data (i) for the fulfillment of its functions as long as the purposes or matters are the same, (ii) even if incompatible, they are considered necessary by a law or community regulation as long as they are deemed a necessary and proportional measure to guarantee the objectives indicated in Article 23, paragraph 1 GDPR for the safeguarding of a democratic society, or (iii) they are "compatible" – in the terms outlined – even if they were collected for incompatible purposes.

All of the above refers to the personal data of natural persons that do not have the character of "special categories of personal data," since for the processing of these (which includes their transfer) there are specific and special rules in Article 9 GDPR, which must be adhered to in all cases.

VI

Finally, a brief additional reference could be made to the Data Intermediation Platforms between Public Administrations, which are ultimately the instrument that, in practice, the Administration uses to enforce the citizen's right not to provide documentation that has been prepared by any Administration (Article 28.2 Law 39/2015, already cited).

This inter-administrative consultation is, in broad terms, a mechanism for inter-administrative relations, which seeks "efficiency in the management of public resources, sharing the use of common resources" (Article 140.1.f) Law 40/2015). From a more specific perspective, Law 40/2015 considers it a mechanism for "electronic relations between Public Administrations" (Article 155 Law 40/2015), and subjects these obligations of "data transmission between Public Administrations" to the provisions of data protection regulations. This implies that the use, procedures, and systems of these tools must be adapted, following the GDPR, to the new legal bases for processing by public authorities, in which consent no longer plays the same role as it did under the LOPD regime, so that they continue to be, as they have been so far, an effective tool that allows the citizen to be relieved of the burden of providing, documenting, and verifying data in administrative procedures.
