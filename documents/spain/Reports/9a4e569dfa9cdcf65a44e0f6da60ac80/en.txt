The inquiry raises the question of whether it is compliant with the provisions of data protection regulations for professional associations whose future members may have regular contact with minors in the development of their activities and professional functions, to request a negative certificate from the Central Register of Sexual Offenders from the professionals who wish to join for the exercise of certain professions expressly mentioned in the inquiry itself.  

In turn, in the event that such a request is not compliant with the aforementioned data protection regulations—since these professions are not exclusively dedicated to the minor public—the consulting entity asks whether it would be compliant with data protection regulations for the corresponding Professional Association to ask the professional whether, in the course of their activity, they will regularly deal with minors, and, in the case of an affirmative response, to request such certification from the mentioned register. The inquiry also refers to the possibility of requesting such documentation, in any case, in relation to specialties concerning minors, such as pediatric or child-related specialties.  

Finally, the inquiry document raises the appropriateness of the provisions regarding data protection concerning the publication within the "professional qualification" section of the professional association's "single window" of the data referred to as "qualification to work regularly with minors" in relation to professionals who provide a negative certificate from the Central Register of Sexual Offenders.  

I

It should be recalled here that Convention No. 108 of the Council of Europe, dated January 28, 1981, for the protection of individuals with regard to the automated processing of personal data includes data related to criminal convictions as sensitive data. In this regard, Article 6 states that "Personal data revealing racial origin, political opinions, religious beliefs or other beliefs, as well as personal data related to health or sexual life, shall not be processed automatically unless domestic law provides appropriate safeguards. The same rule shall apply to personal data concerning criminal convictions."  

Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, dated April 27, 2016, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, which repeals Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation), applicable in the field of data protection since May 25, 2018, displacing the application of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, on the Protection of Personal Data, provides in Article 10 that "The processing of personal data related to criminal convictions and offenses or related security measures based on Article 6, paragraph 1, may only be carried out under the supervision of public authorities or when authorized by Union or Member State law that establishes adequate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. A complete register of criminal convictions may only be maintained under the control of public authorities."  

Thus, it can be concluded, as this Agency has repeatedly indicated, that the legislator intended to completely exclude files that could contain the aforementioned data from any private sphere, determining that only public authorities may hold such files, making it legally impossible to require job candidates or those already employed to provide a criminal record certificate, except in exceptional cases where such a measure is authorized by a law or community legal norm, and with the necessary safeguards.  

This would be the case contemplated in Article 13.5 of Organic Law 1/1996, of January 15, on the Legal Protection of Minors, partially amending the Civil Code and the Civil Procedure Law, following the amendment made by Law 26/2015, of July 28, on the modification of the child and adolescent protection system, which establishes the following:

II
At this point, it is necessary to highlight that data protection regulations contemplate different scenarios that may give rise to the processing of personal data. 
Specifically, according to Article 6 –“Lawfulness of processing”– of REGULATION (EU) 2016/679 OF THE EUROPEAN PARLIAMENT AND OF THE COUNCIL of April 27, 2016 (GDPR), regarding the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, among others, such processing is lawful and legitimate when:
“1. The processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the implementation at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures;
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;
d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller;
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that such interests are not overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require the protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.””
Thus, according to the content of the transcribed report, it is inferred that, also in the case submitted to this consultation, the legal basis required for the accreditation of the personal circumstances to which it refers would be present, mediated by the presentation by the qualified individual seeking their registration of the corresponding certificate, resulting in the processing of the data related to criminal records in the Central Registry of Sexual Offenders being “necessary for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller,” when the professionals whose registration is concerned will maintain regular contact with minors.
In this way, also for the case submitted for consultation, this Agency extracts the existence of a legal norm with the rank of formal law, such as Article 13.5 of Organic Law 1/1996, of January 15, previously transcribed, from which the validity and legality of the processing of personal data is inferred, within the strict respect and observance of the provisions of the rest of the data protection regulations. That is, the regulatory provision contained in this precept is aligned with the provisions of letter c) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, as the processing of the data is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation imposed by law on the data controller, as long as the professionals whose registration is concerned will maintain regular contact with minors.

The Central Register of Sexual Offenders is in response to the development of Law 26/2015, of July 28, amending the system for the protection of children and adolescents, which provides for its creation in its final provision seventeenth, integrating into the system of registers supporting the Administration of Justice. Its specific regulation is contained in Royal Decree 1110/2015, of December 11, which regulates the Central Register of Sexual Offenders.
The guiding principle that the Central Register of Sexual Offenders responds to is structured on the basis of the fundamental right of the minor for their best interest to be a priority, as proclaimed by Organic Law 8/2015, of July 22, amending the system for the protection of children and adolescents. Through the creation of the Central Register of Sexual Offenders, the aim is to prevent and protect minors from sexual offenses, in accordance with national and supranational regulations, and in line with the registration systems of other countries in our environment. In turn, through this Register, a system is developed to ascertain whether those who intend to access and exercise professions, trades, and activities that involve regular contact with minors have no convictions, both in Spain and in other countries, for the offenses referred to in this royal decree.
The nature and purpose of the Central Register of Sexual Offenders are described in Article 3 of Royal Decree 1110/2015, of December 11:
“1. The Central Register of Sexual Offenders constitutes a non-public and free information system, relating to the identity, genetic profile, penalties, and security measures imposed on those persons convicted by a final judgment for any crime against sexual freedom and integrity or for human trafficking for sexual exploitation purposes, including pornography, regulated in Organic Law 10/1995, of November 23, of the Penal Code, regardless of the age of the victim. This information will refer to convictions issued both in Spain and in other countries, particularly the Member States of the European Union and the Council of Europe.
2. The purpose of the Register is to contribute to the protection of minors against sexual exploitation and abuse, regardless of who the perpetrator of the crime is, by establishing a prevention mechanism that allows for the determination of whether those who intend to access and exercise professions, trades, and activities that involve regular contact with minors have or do not have criminal convictions for the offenses referred to in the previous paragraph. Likewise, the Register aims to facilitate the investigation and prosecution of the crimes referred to in this royal decree in order to protect minor victims of sexual crime, introducing effective measures that contribute to the identification of their perpetrators and cooperation with the judicial and police authorities of other countries, particularly with the Member States of the European Union and the Council of Europe.

III
Next, it is necessary to analyze whether, in any case, at the time of processing the registration of the graduates in the different Professional Associations referred to in the inquiry, the requirement analyzed in the previous point will be in accordance with data protection regulations. 
In relation to this particular matter, it is important to observe the breadth of functions, skills, and powers that derive from the exercise of regulated professions, such as those mentioned in the inquiry, to note that many of the regulated professionals will not maintain a “regular contact with minors,” which serves as a factual basis for the general application of the regulatory provisions mentioned in the preceding sections.
Thus, among others, the inquiry expressly refers to the disciplines specific to doctors, dentists, psychologists, optometrists, speech therapists, physiotherapists, nurses, podiatrists, and/or social workers. Furthermore, it should be taken into account the broad population spectrum to which these professional activities may refer.
Consequently, due to the difficulties arising from the prior lack of knowledge by the consulting entity regarding the actual and effective “regular contact with minors” of the future members, the applicability of the regulatory provision in question is negated, which cannot be used generally for each and every one of the regulated professions mentioned in the inquiry.

In this regard, it is appropriate to bring to attention the provisions of Article 5 of the GDPR, specifically the provisions of this article concerning the principle known as “data minimization”:
“Article 5 Principles relating to processing 
1. Personal data shall be: 
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject (“lawfulness, fairness, and transparency”); 
b) collected for specified, legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (“purpose limitation”); 
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (“data minimization”); 
d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable steps must be taken to ensure that personal data that are inaccurate are erased or rectified without delay (“accuracy”);
e) kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of the processing of personal data; personal data may be stored for longer periods as long as they are processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, without prejudice to the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures imposed by this Regulation to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject (“storage limitation”); 
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, by applying appropriate technical or organizational measures (“integrity and confidentiality”). 
2. The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and be able to demonstrate it (“accountability”).”

In summary, personal data may only be collected for processing, as well as subjected to such processing, when they are adequate, relevant, and not excessive in relation to the scope and the specific, explicit, and legitimate purposes for which they have been obtained. It follows that, in general, the consulting entity is not authorized to require new graduates who intend to join the corresponding professional Corporation to obtain and/or present the negative certificate from the Central Registry of Sexual Offenders. 

Conversely, the "implication" referred to by the enabling legal norm, when it stipulates the necessity for "access and practice in professions, trades, and activities (that) involve regular contact with minors," will occur in all those professional fields from which such circumstance can necessarily be inferred. 

In conclusion, the requirement regarding the obtaining of a negative certificate from the Central Registry of Sexual Offenders will only be applicable in cases—such as those specified in the consultation itself—related to specialties concerning minors, such as pediatric or child-related professions. 

In this regard, it should be noted that, according to the provisions of Article 3 of the current Civil Code—application of legal norms—“1. The norms shall be interpreted according to the proper meaning of their words, in relation to the context, historical and legislative background, and the social reality of the time in which they are to be applied, fundamentally considering the spirit and purpose of those.” 

Furthermore, the previous conclusion is fully compatible with the analysis of the nature and functions that, according to Articles 5 and 9 of Law 2/1974, of February 13, on Professional Colleges, these Corporations hold, particularly concerning the regulation of the practice of professions, the exclusive representation of professions, and the defense of the professional interests of the members. In these same terms, the conclusions reached in this report are compatible with the provisions of Article 13 of Law 19/1997, of June 11, on Professional Colleges of the Community of Madrid, which regulates the essential purposes and functions of the professional colleges of that Community. 

Finally, from the public-administrative functions of the Professional Colleges of the Community of Madrid, as outlined in Article 14 of Law 19/1997, of July 11, on Professional Colleges of the Community of Madrid, and, in particular, those related to the regulation of the activities of their members, the exercise of disciplinary authority over the professional members, the adoption of necessary measures to prevent professional intrusion and unfair competition, and collaboration in judicial and administrative procedures of the Community of Madrid that affect matters within the competence of each profession, no legal authorization is derived regarding the requirement for the certificate referred to in this report.

In conclusion:
- According to the principle of "data minimization," professional colleges may only require the obtaining and/or presentation of a negative certificate from the "Central Registry of Sexual Offenders" from new members whose registration is to take place in professional colleges that enable the practice of professions specifically directed towards minors.
- In relation to the principle of "data minimization," the regulations on professional colleges at the state level and in the Community of Madrid do not contain exorbitant norms that, based on the purposes and functions of the colleges, empower these colleges to require the aforementioned documentary requirement from graduates seeking their registration, beyond the specific cases related to professions that involve necessary and regular contact with minors.

IV
Regarding whether it would be compliant with data protection regulations for the corresponding Professional College to ask the graduate if, in the course of their activity, they will regularly deal with minors, and, in the case of an affirmative response, to request such certification from the mentioned registry, in our opinion, there is no objection to carrying out such inquiry. 

At this point, following what was stated by the Constitutional Court in Legal Foundation 5 of STC number 89/1989, of May 11, “Professional Colleges, in fact, constitute a typical type of Corporation recognized by the State, aimed not only at achieving strictly private ends, which could be achieved through simple association, but essentially at ensuring that the practice of the profession—which constitutes a service to the public—complies with the norms or rules that ensure both the effectiveness and the eventual responsibility in such practice, which, in principle, has already been guaranteed by the State with the issuance of the enabling title.”

V
Finally, in the consultation document, the adequacy of the provisions of data protection regulations regarding the publication within the "professional qualification" section of the professional association's "single window" of the data referring to "qualification to work regularly with minors" in relation to professionals who provide a negative certification from the Central Register of Sexual Offenders is raised.

Law 25/2009, of December 22, amending various laws to adapt to the Law on free access to service activities and their exercise, added article 10 -entitled "Single Window"- to Law 2/1974, of February 13, on Professional Associations, establishing that:
“2. Through the aforementioned single window, for the better defense of the rights of consumers and users, the professional organizations will provide the following information, which must be clear, unequivocal, and free of charge:
a) Access to the Register of members, which will be permanently updated and in which at least the following data will be included: name and surname of the registered professionals, registration number, official titles held, professional address, and professional qualification status.”

Thus, through article 10 of Law 2/1974, of February 13, on Professional Associations, the legislator's intention is to publish -in such a way that consumers and users can consult it- certain personal data of the registered members.

Consequently, the publication of the personal data specifically referred to in the transcribed provision is fully authorized, and even mandated by a normative provision with the value and rank of "formal law," for the purpose of making such data known for the better defense of the rights of consumers and users.

However, the introduction of the mention "at least" cannot imply the incorporation into the list of personal data published through the professional association's "single window" of any other data, unless one of the causes that determine that the processing is lawful and legitimate, in accordance with the provisions of article 6.1 of the GDPR, is present. Otherwise, it would constitute a violation of the principle of "data minimization," and a transfer "erga omnes" of personal data via the internet.

This conclusion is even more compelling when analyzed in the sense that, conversely, the recipients of the published information could assume that individuals whose data regarding specific qualifications were not published in the professional association's "single window" would be affected by a hypothetical positive concurrence of criminal records, thus obtaining an undesirable, perverse effect contrary to the provisions that -ad cautelam- are contained in article 10 of the GDPR.

Consequently, with the express reference to the "professional qualification status" contained in that provision, it is clear that the requirements of the principle of "data minimization" are duly met, without the need to incorporate the data relating to the more specific "qualification to work regularly with minors."

In summary, the processing by collegiate bodies of the information derived from the negative or positive certificates obtained from the members unfolds its effects in determining the "professional qualification" required in each case. However, such processing does not authorize the publication of the data related to the specific qualification "to work regularly with minors," which cannot be carried out through the website or institutional electronic channel of the Professional Association. The opposite would constitute a violation of the principle of "data minimization" and would go against the safeguards set out in Article 10 of the GDPR.
