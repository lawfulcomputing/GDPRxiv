RESOLUTION OF RECOURSE FOR RECONSIDERATION  
Examined the recourse for reconsideration filed by DIGI SPAIN TELECOM, S.L.U.  
(hereinafter, the appellant) against the resolution issued by the Director of the  
Spanish Agency for Data Protection dated November 11, 2024, and based on the following  
FACTS  
FIRST: On November 11, 2024, a resolution was issued by the Director of the  
Spanish Agency for Data Protection in file EXP202404509, in which it was agreed to uphold  
the claim for the exercise of rights filed by A.A.A. against DIGI SPAIN TELECOM, S.L.U.  
SECOND: The resolution now being appealed was duly notified to the appellant on  
November 11, 2024, as evidenced by the acknowledgment of receipt in the file.  
THIRD: The appellant has submitted a recourse for reconsideration to this Spanish  
Agency for Data Protection, in which it expresses its disagreement with the resolution in the  
following terms:  
1. On the regulatory limits applicable to the processing of data related to incoming calls.  
DIGI indicates that, as it has already stated in previous allegations, the basis for  
legitimacy is solely for retaining data related to outgoing calls, for the purpose of billing  
them, and that this information has already been made available to the claimant.  
It adds that this legitimacy would not apply to the retention of data related to incoming  
calls, or calls made from other lines owned by third parties.  
It states that the possibility of processing information related to incoming calls only arises  
for compliance with Law 25/2007, of October 18, on the retention of data related to  
electronic communications and public communications networks (Law 25/2007), which  
establishes that such data must be retained for the fulfillment of specific obligations and can  
only be made available in specific cases to the Security Forces and Bodies, always  
authorized by the corresponding and proper judicial resolution (art. 6.1).  
DIGI understands that this would constitute a limitation on the right of access under  
Article 23 of the GDPR, and that it would require a specific legal provision, in order to  
preserve security, public safety, and the prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution  
of criminal offenses.  
Therefore, DIGI believes that it lacks a legal basis that allows for the provision of  
information related to incoming calls to the claimant, given the absence of legal basis for its  
processing and the lack of legal authorization both in the special regulation and in the  
general one.  
On the other hand, DIGI wishes to state that it has responded to the claimant by  
providing the outgoing calls, as well as sufficiently justifying the grounds that prevent it from  
making available the calls made by third parties.  
DIGI also wishes to state that, after receiving the Resolution of November 11, it has  
sent a new communication to the claimant, which has also been forwarded to this Agency,  
reasonably justifying the legal and regulatory reasons that prevent the provision of incoming  
calls.  
2. On the violation of the rights of third parties.  
DIGI points out that, on pages 20 and 21 of the Resolution now being appealed, it is  
stated that it must be DIGI who evaluates and demonstrates that the provision of this  
information would negatively affect the rights and freedoms of third parties.  
Thus, and as previously noted, the fact that the claimant accesses the telephone records  
of incoming calls would imply an impact on the rights of third parties, and it substantiates this  
on the following grounds:  
i) because it may occur that the applicant or the holder of the contract is not the user of  
the telephone line, so information about calls in which the claimant has not intervened could  
be provided, which is relevant in the case of minors and individuals in vulnerable situations,  
and could lead to situations of control over the use of the line in cases where the claimant has  
not intervened, and against the protection of privacy.  
In this regard, DIGI wishes to state that the telephone device itself contains a record of  
incoming and outgoing calls, so it is likely that the request for the record is made by someone  
who does not have access to the mobile device.  
ii) because providing this information implies the provision by DIGI of personal data of  
third parties who have made the call, for which it is not authorized.  
This lack of authority would be evident in cases of calls from withheld numbers, where the  
caller chooses not to identify themselves, considering that it is a right expressly recognized in  
Article 65 of Law 11/2022, of June 28, General Telecommunications.

At this point, DIGI wishes to express that, in compliance with Law 25/2007, the register it maintains regarding incoming calls does not differentiate between hidden and non-hidden calls, as the Law does not provide for this distinction. Therefore, it understands that granting this list could constitute a violation of the rights of those third parties who exercise their right to make calls with hidden numbers.  
DIGI also states that the resolution now being appealed included regulations regarding the removal of suppression marks at the source of the line identification, in accordance with Article 46 of Law 32/2003, General Telecommunications Law.  
However, it understands that this is not applicable as there are no records at DIGI of any claims, complaints, or objections from the claimant regarding calls made by hidden numbers as being bothersome or malicious.  
For all the above reasons, DIGI finds it necessary to deny this request and the exercise of the right of access concerning information related to incoming calls, given the lack of standing of this party to address them, in order to ensure that the rights of third parties are not negatively affected, in accordance with the provisions of Article 15.4 and 23.1 of the GDPR.  
Additionally, it is the regulation itself that provides for the partial attention to the right of access, as DIGI has done, taking into account Recital 63 of the GDPR.

I  
Competence  
The Presidency of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to resolve this appeal, in accordance with the provisions of Article 123 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP) and Article 48.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD).

During the processing of the procedure, the respondent has indicated that the balancing of rights and obligations should be taken into account not only for DIGI and the claimant but also with respect to third parties, as well as the restrictions described in Law 25/2007 of October 18, on the retention of data related to electronic communications and public communications networks. In response to such statements, it should be noted, first of all, regarding the alleged restrictions that Law 25/2007 would impose on the right of access, that the Guidelines 1/2022, on the rights of data subjects — Right of access Version 2.1 of March 28, 2023, from the European Data Protection Board, state that  
“6.1. General Observations  
165. The right of access is subject to the limits resulting from Article 15, paragraph 4, of the GDPR (rights and freedoms of others) and from Article 12, paragraph 5, of the GDPR (manifestly unfounded or excessive requests).  
Furthermore, Union or Member State legislation may restrict the right of access in accordance with Article 23 of the GDPR. Exceptions relating to the processing of personal data for scientific, historical, or statistical purposes or for archiving purposes in the public interest may be based on Article 89, paragraph 2, and Article 89, paragraph 3, of the GDPR, and exceptions for processing carried out for journalistic purposes or for academic, artistic, or literary expression may be based on Article 85, paragraph 2, of the GDPR.  
166. It is important to note that, apart from the aforementioned limits, exceptions, and possible restrictions, the GDPR does not allow for other exemptions or exceptions to the right of access. This means, among other things, that the right of access does not have any general reservation to proportionality concerning the efforts that the data controller must make to comply with the requests of data subjects under Article 15 of the GDPR. Furthermore, it is not permitted to limit or restrict the right of access in a contract between the data controller and the data subject.”  
Similarly, the same guidelines interpret the scope of the aforementioned right of access, denying an excessively restrictive interpretation and allowing the

possibility that in its exercise it may include data that could affect other individuals:  
The scope of the right of access is determined by the scope of the concept of personal data as defined in Article 4, paragraph 1, of the GDPR. Apart from basic personal data, such as name, address, phone number, etc., a wide variety of data may be included in this definition, such as medical findings, purchase history, creditworthiness indicators, activity logs, search activities, etc. Personal data that has been subjected to pseudonymization remains personal data rather than anonymous data. The right of access refers to the personal data of the person making the request. This should not be interpreted too restrictively and may include data that could also affect other individuals, for example, the communication history involving incoming and outgoing messages.  
Finally, regarding access to traffic and location data retained in compliance with Law 25/2007, of October 18, on the retention of data related to electronic communications and public communications networks, it should be noted that the main objective of said Law 25/2007 is to establish the obligation of telecommunications operators to retain certain data generated or processed by them, in order to make them available to authorized agents. Authorized agents include, among others, members of the Police Forces authorized for this purpose within the framework of a criminal investigation for the commission of a crime.  
In this regard, the Law respects the pronouncements that the Constitutional Court has issued concerning the right to the secrecy of communications, a respect that is articulated, in particular, through two guarantees: firstly, that the data subject to the retention obligation are data exclusively related to the communication, whether by telephone or made via the Internet, but in no case revealing the content of it; and secondly, that the transfer of such data affecting a specific communication or communications will always require prior judicial authorization.  
Article 1.1 of the aforementioned Law 25/2007 states that  
“1. This Law aims to regulate the obligation of operators to retain the data generated or processed in the context of providing electronic communications services or public communications networks, as well as the duty to transfer such data to authorized agents whenever they are required through the corresponding judicial authorization for the purposes of detecting, investigating, and prosecuting serious crimes contemplated in the Penal Code or in special criminal laws.”

In the present case, from the examination of the documentation provided by the parties, it has been established that the claimant requested the respondent access to the record of incoming and outgoing calls from their mobile phone during a period in 2023, and that the respondent only provided the record of outgoing calls.

In this way, the data controller has the burden of demonstrating that the rights and freedoms of others would be affected in order to limit the access of the data subject to the personal data concerning them.  
In this regard, the Guidelines 1/2022 indicate that “  
The data controller must be able to demonstrate that the rights or freedoms of others would be negatively affected in the specific situation. Therefore, it follows from such provisions that the regulations have delineated the assumptions and circumstances under which the recipient of a call with a hidden number may know the identification of the originating line. Outside of these cases, the holder of the originating line has an expectation that their telephone line will remain hidden from the recipient of the call, that is, they have an expectation of privacy that may be compromised by the subsequent knowledge of this information by the interested party.  
Consequently, the right of access to this information could imply an interference with the right to privacy of the holders of the originating lines of the calls. And on this matter, Article 15.4 of the GDPR states that the right of access must not negatively affect the rights and freedoms of third parties.  
In any case, as already indicated, while the right to data protection is not an absolute right, Article 15.4 of the GDPR should not be interpreted in such a way that its application results in denying the information to the interested party.  
The aforementioned Guidelines 1/2022 provide that when interpreting Article 15, paragraph 4, of the GDPR, special precautions must be taken not to unjustifiably extend the restrictions established in Article 23 of the GDPR, which are only permitted under strict conditions. It will be the data controller who reconciles the conflicting rights in each case. They state the following:  
173. Regarding Recital 4 of the GDPR and the justification of Article 52, paragraph 1, of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, the right to the protection of personal data is not an absolute right. Therefore, the exercise of the right of access must also be weighed against other fundamental rights in accordance with the principle of proportionality. When the assessment of Article 15, paragraph 4, of the GDPR demonstrates that compliance with the request has negative effects on the rights and freedoms of other participants (Phase 1), the interests of all participants must be weighed, taking into account the specific circumstances of the case and, in particular, the likelihood and severity of the risks present in the communication of the data. The data controller must attempt to reconcile the conflicting rights (Phase 2), for example, by implementing appropriate measures that mitigate the risk to the rights and freedoms of others. As emphasized in Recital 63, the protection of the rights and freedoms of others under Article 15, paragraph 4, of the GDPR must not result in the refusal to provide all the information to the interested party. This means, for example, when applying the limitation, that information relating to other individuals must be rendered unreadable to the extent possible rather than refusing to provide a copy of the personal data. However, if it is impossible to find a solution to reconcile the relevant rights, the data controller must decide in a subsequent step which of the conflicting rights and freedoms prevails (Phase 3).  
Therefore, it must be assumed that the claiming party has the right to access the requested information, but DIGI, as the data controller, will have to assess whether there are rights and freedoms of others that are negatively affected, what these rights and freedoms are, if applicable, as well as weigh the rights in...

III  
Conclusion  
Having examined the appeal for reconsideration submitted by the interested party, it does not provide new facts or legal arguments that would allow for a reconsideration of the validity of the contested resolution, therefore it is appropriate to agree to its dismissal.  
IV  
Late Resolution  
Due to operational reasons of the administrative body, which are not attributable to the appealing party, as of the date of this document, the mandatory pronouncement of this Agency regarding the present appeal has not been issued.  
According to the provisions of art. 24 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (LPACAP), the effect of administrative silence in the procedures for challenging acts and provisions is dismissive.
