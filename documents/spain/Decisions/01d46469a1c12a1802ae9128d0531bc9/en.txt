LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Jurisdiction  
The Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to resolve the present appeal, in accordance with the provisions of Article 123 of the LPACAP and Article 48.1 of the LOPDGDD.  

II  
Response to the allegation regarding legitimate trust in the judiciary  
The appellant states that the sanctioning procedure "was initiated because the Criminal Court responsible for the investigation made available to CONECTA 5 the recording of the affected party's statement without applying any voice filter and without making any express mention or warning about the need to apply it, contravening the 2020 Justice Communication Protocol approved by the General Council of the Judiciary."  
Such allegation was already made by the appellant during the processing of the sanctioning procedure, having been analyzed and dismissed in Legal Ground IV of the contested resolution, which indicates that the treatment subject to the procedure is the dissemination of the victim without distortion that has been carried out by the appellant, "not entering into the scope of this other treatments, such as the sending made by the Court to the media of the material disseminated by the claimed party."  
It should be noted, as indicated in the aforementioned legal ground, that the appellant is responsible for the treatment, "as it has the power to do so by having decisive influence over it. Thus, the purpose is informative and the means encompass the decision-making power from the way in which the information is distributed or made available to the public, to its content. The media, for the purposes of fulfilling its purpose, once it has gathered all the necessary information in the exercise of its journalistic work, decides what information to provide and through which medium, in what terms and with what personal data."  
Therefore, such legal ground indicates that "The transfer of the video of the hearing with the undistorted voice of the victim by the Court to the media is another data treatment different from the one being analyzed. Therefore, the fact that the origin of the information was the Court is not sufficient to exempt the claimed party from its responsibility, because what is being judged is not the provision of information by the Court to the media, but the treatment for which the claimed party is responsible, such as the dissemination of personal data like the voice of the victim.  
As for the information reaching the media, it, as the responsible party for the treatment, is responsible for complying with data protection regulations, without being able to justify non-compliance on the grounds that the Court sent the information in that manner, assuming that such provision allows it to publish it without adhering to the prescriptions of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD."  
The appellant considers it "key to take into account that the publication of the recording of the victim's voice without distortion on a website was the result of the concurrence of the following factors: (i) the urgency in communication due to public interest and the significance of the process; (ii) that it was the Court itself that distributed the recording; and, (iii) that there was no express warning from the Court's communication offices on how to publish the provided information."  
It has already been indicated that non-compliance with data protection regulations cannot be justified by the fact that the Court sent the information in that manner.  
In the same vein, the appellant, as the responsible party for the treatment, is responsible for complying with data protection regulations, without being able to justify non-compliance on the grounds of urgency in reporting the news.  
Regarding the statement that the Court's communication offices did not make any express warning about how to publish the provided information, the aforementioned Legal Ground IV of the contested resolution states that "the communication offices of the courts cannot issue mandates to the media, but rather warnings and recommendations.  
(...)  
And if one follows the interpretation defended by the claimed party, the treatment carried out by the media would be totally subordinated or conditioned by the indications received from the judicial body, which is not the case."  
The appellant states that "the principle of legitimate trust constitutes a guiding principle of the actions of Public Administrations that expresses citizens' trust that the actions of public administrations cannot be altered arbitrarily and provides the framework for individuals' actions in their relations with public administrative powers, characterized by the notes of predictability and legal certainty.  
In this sense, it was the very act of the Court to freely and without any masking disseminate the voice of the victim that generated in CONECTA5 the trust that it was acting correctly, and therefore, the belief that its conduct was in turn correct.

It is in Article 3.1 of Law 40/2015, of October 1, on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector, where reference is made to the principle of legitimate trust, a provision that states that “Public Administrations serve the general interests objectively and act in accordance with the principles of efficiency, hierarchy, decentralization, deconcentration, and coordination, fully subject to the Constitution, the Law, and the Law.”  
They must respect the following principles in their actions and relationships:  
(...)  
e) Good faith, legitimate trust, and institutional loyalty.”  
The principle of legitimate trust can be understood as the trust of citizens in the future actions of Public Administrations based on their past actions, considering the expectations they generate, while always safeguarding the principle of legality, so that this principle cannot be invoked to justify situations contrary to the norm.  
This is derived from the Judgment of the Supreme Court of February 9, 2004 (rec. 4130/2001): “The principle of protection of legitimate trust, related to the more traditional principles in our legal system of legal certainty and good faith in the relationships between the Administration and individuals, entails, according to the doctrine of the Court of Justice of the European Communities and the jurisprudence of this Chamber, that the public authority cannot adopt measures that are contrary to the hope induced by the reasonable stability in its decisions, and based on which individuals have made certain decisions. (…) In the same sense, it must be taken into account that ‘the principle of protection of the citizen's legitimate trust’ in the actions of the Administration does not apply to cases of any type of subjective psychological conviction in the individual, but rather when such ‘trust’ is based on signs or external facts produced by the Administration that are sufficiently conclusive to induce the individual to trust in the ‘appearance of legality’ that the administrative action through specific acts reveals” (Cf. SSTS November 15, 1999, June 4, 2001, and April 15, 2002, among others).” (the underlining is ours).  
Meanwhile, the Judgment of the Supreme Court of February 1, 1999 (rec. 5475/1995) indicates that “this principle cannot be invoked to create, maintain, or extend, in the field of Public Law, situations contrary to the legal order.”

legal, or when the preceding act results in a contradiction with the purpose or interest protected by a legal norm that, by its nature, is not susceptible to supporting discretionary conduct by the Administration that implies the recognition of rights and/or obligations arising from its own acts. In other words, the invoked doctrine of "own acts" without the limitation just exposed could introduce into the realm of public law the principle of autonomy of will as an organizing method for matters regulated by imperative norms, in which the public interest safeguarded by the principle of legality prevails; a principle that would be violated if an action by the Administration contrary to the legal order were validated solely because it has been decided by the Administration or because it responds to a precedent set by it.  
One thing is the irrevocability of one's own acts declaring rights outside the review channels established by law (arts. 109 and 110 of the LPA of 1958, 102 and 103 of the Law on the Legal Regime of Public Administrations and Common Administrative Procedure, Law 30/1992, amended by Law 4/1999), and another is the respect for the legitimate trust generated by one's own action, which must necessarily project into the realm of discretion or autonomy, not into the regulated aspects or normative requirements against which, in Administrative Law, what has been resolved in an act or in a precedent that was contrary to those cannot prevail. In other words, it cannot be said that the trust placed in an act or precedent that is contrary to an imperative norm is legitimate." (the underlining is ours).  
Furthermore, as we have mentioned, that hope or trust generated must be "legitimate" and based on previous external acts whose meaning is undoubtedly contrary to what has been agreed upon subsequently, without including in this principle of legitimate trust a mere psychological conviction of the individual.  
We must emphasize that the principle of legitimate trust operates in practice between a specific Public Administration and an individual, within the framework of the existing legal relationship between them. This means that the action taken by another Public Administration, different from the acting one, does not bind the latter - each exercises its own competencies - nor can it, therefore, generate legitimate trust for the individual.  
The AEPD has not carried out any action that has allowed the appellant to conclude the suitability of its action. It cannot provide any pronouncement or action from this Agency that would lead it to think that it could disseminate the voice of the victim without distortion, simply because there is no action in that sense.  
Moreover, it is worth reiterating what has been previously expressed regarding the prevalence of the principle of legality, which prevents invoking legitimate trust to save situations contrary to the norm, especially when we are talking about the regulated powers of the AEPD as set out in Article 58 of the GDPR, such as the sanctioning power.

Therefore, the requirements stated in the Supreme Court Judgment of February 22, 2016 (rec. 4048/2013) to determine the existence of a legitimate trust are not met:  
1. “that it is based on undeniable and external signs.” Regardless of whether these undeniable and external signs, as already indicated, must be from the AEPD and, in this case, do not exist, it must be remembered that the court carries out a different treatment that is not the subject of this sanctioning file. This implies that what the media, in this case the appealing party, subsequently does with the information derived from the provision of the institutional signal is not the responsibility of the Court.  
2. “that the hopes generated in the administered must be legitimate.” As indicated in Legal Basis XI of the contested resolution, this is not about denying the exercise of the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information or giving precedence to one fundamental right over another, having to choose which carries more weight in a specific case, but rather about finding a balance between both to achieve the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information without undermining the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data. “That is, the freedom of information of the media is not questioned but the weighting with the right to data protection based on the proportionality and necessity of publishing the specific personal data of the voice. Such a situation could have been resolved by using technical procedures to prevent the recognition of the voice, such as, for example, the distortion of the victim's voice or the transcription of the account of the multiple rape, both security measures applied, depending on the case, ordinarily by the media.” (Legal Basis XI of the contested resolution)  
In the present case, the claimed party proceeded, following the request made by the AEPD on ***DATE.4, to remove the news from the link ***URL.1. However, it could have opted, as indicated in the aforementioned request, to have distorted the victim's voice, without prejudice to which the information would have continued to be available and would still be provided in all its breadth, which shows that to provide this specific information it was not necessary, in the terms of art. 5.1.c) of the GDPR, to disseminate the victim's voice.  
3. “that the final conduct of the Administration is contradictory to previous acts, is surprising and incoherent.” It must be emphasized that the contradictory final conduct must be from the same body, which does not happen in the present case since what the appealing party intends is to contrast the action of the jurisdictional body with that of the AEPD, not two contradictory actions of the Agency.

V  
Response to the allegation regarding the abuse of power in the use of preliminary actions  
The appealing party argues that “the AEPD has replaced the purpose intended for the sanctioning procedure with the own (and more limited) purpose of the preliminary investigative actions,” since “the preliminary investigative actions do not aim to unequivocally establish the facts in question, but merely to gather a series of indicative elements that suggest that, probably, some apparently typical facts have occurred and it is possible to identify their author,” and therefore understands that the appealed act incurs in an abuse of power.  
Article 55.2 of the LPACAP establishes that “In the case of sanctioning procedures, the preliminary actions shall be aimed at determining, as precisely as possible, the facts that may motivate the initiation of the procedure, the identification of the person or persons who may be held responsible, and the relevant circumstances that apply to both.”  
For its part, Article 67.1 of the LOPDGDD states that “Before the adoption of the agreement to initiate the procedure, and once the complaint has been admitted for processing, if any, the Spanish Agency for Data Protection may carry out preliminary investigative actions in order to achieve a better determination of the facts and circumstances that justify the processing of the procedure.”

Thus, regarding the allegation that the circumstance of intent or negligence does not exist because the appellant emitted the audio exactly as it was disseminated by the court itself, such that if there were negligence, it would lie with said court, the aforementioned legal basis indicates that what has been analyzed in the sanctioning procedure is the dissemination of the victim's voice without distortion, as the media are responsible for the treatment that habitually distorts the voice in order to prevent the speaker from being recognized.

If this is the case, the interested party must formally communicate this fact by means of a written document addressed to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, submitting it through the Electronic Registry of the Agency [https://sedeagpd.gob.es/sede-electronica-web/], or through any of the other registries provided for in article 16.4 of the aforementioned LPACAP. They must also provide the Agency with documentation that proves the effective filing of the contentious-administrative appeal. If the Agency does not have knowledge of the filing of the contentious-administrative appeal within two months from the day following the notification of this resolution, it will consider the provisional suspension to be concluded.
