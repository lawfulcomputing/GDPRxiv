LEGAL GROUNDS  
I Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of 5/12, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: "The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued for its development, and, insofar as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures."  
II The image as personal data. Data controller  
The GDPR aims to guarantee the right to the protection of personal data of individuals. Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines "personal data" as: "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ('the data subject'); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person."  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines "processing" as "any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment or combination, restriction, erasure or destruction."  
Article 4.7 of the GDPR defines "data controller" or "controller" as: the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; where the Union or Member State law determines the purposes and means of processing, the controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided by Union or Member State law;

Following the STC 292/2000, of 30/11, it must be affirmed that the fundamental right to data protection, enshrined in Article 18.4 of the Spanish Constitution, unlike the right to privacy established in Article 18.1 CE, which shares the objective of providing effective constitutional protection of personal and family private life, excluding external knowledge and intrusions by third parties against one’s will, seeks to guarantee that individual a power of control over their personal data, its use and destination, with the purpose of preventing its illicit and harmful trafficking to the dignity and rights of the affected individual.  
The right to data protection, therefore, has a broader scope than the right to privacy, as the fundamental right to data protection extends its guarantee not only to privacy in its constitutionally protected dimension under Article 18.1 CE, but also to the sphere of personal rights that belong to the realm of private life, inseparably linked to the respect for personal dignity, such as the right to honor, and to the full exercise of individual rights. The fundamental right to data protection expands the constitutional guarantee to those data that are relevant or have an impact on the exercise of any rights of the individual, whether or not they are constitutional rights and whether or not they relate to honor, ideology, personal and family privacy, or any other constitutionally protected good.  
Thus, the object of the fundamental right to data protection is not limited solely to the intimate data of the individual, but to any type of personal data, whether intimate or not, whose knowledge or use by third parties may affect their rights, whether or not fundamental—such as those that identify or allow for the identification of the individual, which may serve to create their ideological, racial, sexual, economic profile, or of any other nature, or that serve any other utility that, under certain circumstances, constitutes a threat to the individual—because its object is not only individual intimacy, already protected by Article 18.1 CE, but personal data. Consequently, it also encompasses those public personal data, which, by being accessible to anyone, do not escape the power of disposition of the affected individual because their right to data protection guarantees it.  
In this specific case, the image of the claimant, a teacher at the local IES, captured in the photo used for the electoral program of the local elections in May 2023, is a piece of personal data that appeared and is recorded from a photograph published by the City Council, to which the claimant is not shown to have opposed. Subsequently, the responding party, a private entity, a political party related to the Concello, used the photo in the printed electoral program of the political party, as the same individual was running for Mayor in the May 2023 elections. The photograph that the City Council previously obtained was for a specific, determined purpose desired by the claimant (availability, self-determination of the will of its holder), posing with the rest of its components that make up the photo. The claimant expresses that the data of her image, which fully identifies her, has been exposed by a political party, for which she indicates she did not grant her consent.  
The actions of the responding party confirm the requirements defined as the processing of personal data, being responsible for the processing.

For its part, Article 6.1 of the GDPR establishes the circumstances under which the processing of personal data can be considered lawful:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the implementation at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures;  
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;  
d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller;  
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.  
The provision in paragraph f) of the first subparagraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”  
Furthermore, Recital 40 of the aforementioned GDPR states: “In order for processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on another legitimate basis laid down by law, whether in this Regulation or by virtue of other Union or Member State law to which this Regulation relates, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject or the necessity of performing a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the purpose of taking steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.”

The responding party must demonstrate that it has a legitimate basis for the processing it carries out, as the legitimate basis is not presumed in this case, in any way, despite having been previously published by the Concello. Furthermore, the exceptional nature of the processing must be taken into account, as indicated in Article 6 of the GDPR, which states that “it shall be lawful only if it meets at least one of the following conditions.”  
The judgment cited by the responding party from the Supreme Court is not applicable to this case, as it concerns the initiation of different content. The judgment deals with journalistic information about individuals of public relevance in relation to a judicial investigation into alleged political and economic corruption versus the right to honor.  
Freedom of information would encompass the communication of facts that can be contrasted with objective data and consists of communicating or receiving truthful information by any means of dissemination (see all STC 5/2021, of January 25, 2021).  
In this case, there is no such journalistic interest nor is it a news item, but rather an electoral program that cannot be classified as information susceptible to contrast; therefore, there is no conflict of rights, nor does it relate to the institutionalization of public opinion formation. The information does not review the achievements made by the City Council, but rather what it offers in an electoral program, and is perfectly understandable without the photo, since, in fact, it does not explain anything about it, nor does it relate to the program, beyond the appearance of the Mayor, the candidate, in the photo.

V Determination of the sanction  
The corrective powers of the Spanish Data Protection Agency, as the supervisory authority, are established in Article 58.2 of the GDPR. Among them is the power to impose an administrative fine in accordance with Article 83 of the GDPR - Article 58.2 i) - or the power to order the controller or processor to ensure that the processing operations comply with the provisions of the GDPR, when appropriate, in a specified manner and within a specified time frame - Article 58.2 d).  
According to the provisions of Article 83.2 of the GDPR, the measure provided for in Article 58.2 d) of the aforementioned Regulation is compatible with the sanction consisting of an administrative fine.  
In the present case, considering the facts presented, it is deemed that the sanction to be imposed is an administrative fine. The fine imposed must be, in each individual case, effective, proportionate, and dissuasive, in accordance with Article 83.1 of the GDPR. g) To have, when not mandatory, a data protection officer.  
h) The voluntary submission by the responsible party or processor to alternative dispute resolution mechanisms, in those cases where there are disputes between them and any interested party.  

In the initiation agreement, it was considered a mitigating factor of the infringement that the respondent is an entity whose activity is not related to the processing of personal data, thus applying Article 76.2.b) of the LOPDGDD. This provision derives from the development of Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR, as a principle applicable to the circumstances of the case, to contemplate “any other aggravating or mitigating factor,” providing as examples the “financial benefits obtained or losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement.”  

However, the fact that its main activity does not appear to be directly linked to the processing of data, such as might be the case for business entities, financial institutions, or telecommunications operators, should not serve as a spur or benefit for a potential reduction in the sanction for which it has not been deserving, understanding that not participating in the usual and daily traffic of personal data does not proportionally affect the seriousness of the infringing act. In fact, it is difficult to specify an activity that does not involve the processing of data to a greater or lesser extent and with different scopes. The respondent is a centralized political organization, of national scope, that manages data across that entire area, including that of its affiliates, categorized as special category data, although this is not the case here, but its purpose may be political, as was the case when it dealt with personal data in an electoral program. Therefore, the respondent must assume the risk that such data may be processed in a manner contrary to the law. Considering the link between the infringer's activity and the limited processing of data as a mitigating factor can only nullify the deterrent effect of the fine, defaulting the circumstances that may concur, which would be tantamount to considering that the non-habitual processing of data or the lack of connection of the infringer's activity with the processing of data could not produce negative effects, but rather positive ones, pre-configuring the mitigating circumstance without considering the elements that concur on a case-by-case basis, as in this case.  

With its deterrent effect, administrative fines contribute to strengthening the protection of individuals regarding the processing of personal data and constitute, therefore, a key element in ensuring respect for the rights of such individuals, in accordance with the purpose of the aforementioned Regulation to ensure a high level of protection for those individuals regarding the processing of personal data. In conclusion, this mitigating factor is not deemed to be present in the conduct of the respondent.
