5.2. On the origin and current situation of the implementation of biometric systems in first and second division football stadiums.  
In accordance with the information gathered, and before analyzing the specific case of BURGOS CF, a brief reference must be made to the actions (chronologically...
6/54  
ordered) carried out by the inspector in order to clarify what was the origin and what is the current status of the implementation of biometric systems for fingerprint or facial recognition that was generally carried out to access the football stadiums of the first and second division clubs of LALIGA.  
-  
On September 23, 2015, the CSD authorized the new version of Book XII of the League Regulations (hereinafter, RGLALIGA), which is currently in force, whose Article XII establishes the following in its sections 2, 3, and 4:  
“2. The sale of season or half-season access tickets or documents, regardless of their designation, in the stands of encouragement with the characteristics described in section 1, will require that the fan provides, along with the data specified in Article 1 of this Regulation, the biometric data that is determined, and the consent of the interested party must be obtained by clearly informing them of the specific purposes for the processing of the aforementioned personal data, in accordance with the current regulations on personal data protection. At the time of the acquisition of the access title by the affiliated Club/SAD, the fan will be associated with the provided affiliation and the biometric data.  
3. THE ACCESS TITLES for season or half-season in the stands of encouragement will be personal and non-transferable, regardless of the policy that the Club/SAD has regarding these for the rest of the venue. To this end, the Club/SAD will establish, both in the document for acquiring the access title and in the corresponding internal regulation, that in these areas, spectators will be subject to all identity verification controls in force at any given time, including those related to automatic biometric systems, as well as the exhibition of the access title along with the document proving their identity.  
4. Access to the stands of encouragement will only be allowed to fans who have obtained the access title to that area and who, at the time of entry, submit to the reading of their biometric data. Access will be denied if the person does not provide, if required, along with the biometric data, a document proving their identity that matches the affiliation associated with the biometric data and the access title.”  
-  
There is no record that LALIGA had adopted measures aimed at enforcing the obligations set forth in the aforementioned article of the RGLALIGA until CEVRXID urged it to do so. Specifically, it is documented that CEVRXID urged LALIGA on two occasions to promote the implementation by the clubs of the biometric control measures indicated in Book XII of its General Regulations, exclusively for the stands of encouragement in the first and second divisions. In its agreements, it also refers to the fact that non-compliance will lead to “the corresponding proposals for the opening of sanctioning files in the exercise of its oversight and control function provided for in the Law.”  
-  
The first agreement directed by CEVRXID to LALIGA was on March 15, 2022, and led to the initiation of the implementation of these systems by various clubs, warning them of the possibility of incurring sanctioning liability, which was communicated to them by LALIGA.  
-  
Once its first agreement of March 15, 2022, was issued, CEVRXID, in its meeting on October 20, 2022, agreed to consult the AEPD

Specifically, regarding the legitimizing cause and exception applicable to these referred biometric treatments, they could be carried out under the protection of articles 6.1.e and 9.2.g of the personal data protection regulations, in consideration of the competence attributed to CEVRXID by article 13.1 of Law 19/2007, of July 11, against violence, racism, xenophobia, and intolerance in sports. The legal office of the AEPD issued Report 98/2022 on December 22, 2022, in response to the previous consultation (attached by the inspector in the aforementioned diligence). The report is based on several previously reported precedents and maintains that in the present case “there was no legal norm in the Spanish legal system that met the requirements of article 9.2.g) of the GDPR, so the processing could only be based on the consent of the affected parties, provided that it is guaranteed to be free.”  
-  
After receiving the aforementioned report, CEVRXID communicated on March 21, 2023, to LALIGA that “according to the information provided by the AEPD, access to the animation stands through biometric data will be carried out with the consent of the interested party,” so that, “in the event that there is no consent from the interested party to access the animation stands through biometric data, it will be mandatory for the clubs/SAD to have a procedure that allows the identification of all those who access outside of biometric control.”  
-  
Upon receiving the previous communication, LALIGA adopted on March 23, 2023, “Circular No. 19 of the 2022/2023 Season, which conveyed to the clubs what was indicated by the aforementioned Commission, also informing them of the AEPD report of December 22, 2022.” The Circular informs the clubs that “access to the animation stands through biometric data can be maintained but only if there is free consent from the interested party, prior information on the specific purposes for the processing of personal data. In the absence of consent from the interested parties, CEVRXID reminds that spectators in these stands will not be exempt from undergoing all identity verification controls that are in force at any given time and, to that end, it is mandatory for the Club or SAD to have a procedure that allows them to be identified.”

To verify what security measures have been adopted regarding data protection, they provide as Annex VIII the document prepared by the entity SEFPSAU, as the provider of the hardware and software on which the processing of biometric data is executed. This document provides descriptive information about the operation of the biometric system installed in the Burgos stadium, from which the following main points are extracted:  
.  
- The personal data collected from each interested party in the registration process (enrollment in the system) are name, surname, ID number, an identifier generated by the system, and the biometric pattern of the fingerprint.  
- The registration of the biometric pattern of the fingerprint is carried out at the microinformatics service points for members. Access to the cheering section is done through a single door that has three turnstiles with the possibility of access via biometric identification. Each of these turnstiles has several access methods (in addition to biometric reading): optical reading of barcodes or QR codes, and wireless reading of cards with an embedded chip.  
- The comparison process that is performed is “biometric identification” (one to many). Thus, the readers installed in the turnstiles send the encrypted biometric pattern of the fingerprint of the interested party accessing the stadium to the Biometric Identification Management Server (SGI) located at the club's facilities, which only the club has access to. The comparison performed on this server returns a positive or negative result of the identification.  
- The biometric patterns are encrypted at all times with the keys of the device manufacturer. The latter certifies that the decryption keys are solely safeguarded by them and are not available to their clients. Additionally, it certifies that once the template of the fingerprint image is extracted, the latter is deleted.  
- In point 6, it refers only to specific security measures of the elements or technical equipment.

2.1. Definition and characteristics of biometric data.

Biometric data processing systems are based on collecting and processing personal data related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of individuals, which may include their neural characteristics, through devices or sensors, creating biometric templates (also referred to as signatures or patterns) that enable the identification, tracking, or profiling of such individuals.  
The GDPR defines biometric data in Article 4.14 as “personal data obtained through a specific technical processing, related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person that (…) is unique to that person, such as facial images or fingerprint data.”  
As noted in Opinion 4/2007 of the Article 29 Working Party (ART. 29 of Directive 95/46/EC, as an independent advisory body of the EU), regarding the concept of personal data (WP136), dated 20/06/2007, biometric data can be defined as:  
“… biological properties, physiological characteristics, personality traits, or tics, which are, at the same time, attributable to a single person and measurable, even if the models used in practice to technically measure them involve a certain degree of probability. Typical examples of biometric data include those provided by fingerprints, retinal patterns, facial structure, voices, but also the geometry of the hand, venous structures, and even certain deeply rooted skills or other behavioral characteristics (such as handwriting, pulse rates, a particular way of walking or speaking, etc.). A particularity of biometric data is that they can be considered both as content of information about a specific person (So-and-so has these fingerprints) and as an element to link information to a specific person (this object has been touched by someone who has these fingerprints, and these fingerprints belong to So-and-so; therefore, So-and-so has touched this object). As such, they can serve as “identifiers.” Indeed, as they correspond to a unique person, biometric data can be used to identify that person. This dual nature is also present in the case of DNA data, which provide information about the human body and allow for the unequivocal identification of one, and only one, person.”  
Any biometric access control system for the stadium, in order to be used, must first register the identity of the user in the system by capturing a series of biometric parameters (in this case, the fingerprints of subscribers who purchase access tickets for the BURGOS CF stadium's cheering section). The aim is to process those parameters to identify the person each time they enter and exit through the access point.  
A biometric data point contained in a system is stored in the form of a biometric template or pattern, commonly referred to as a “vector.” A biometric template is a representation of a human biometric characteristic, such as a face or a fingerprint, in a way that is interpretable by a machine efficiently and effectively for a specific purpose or purposes. The biometric template is not intended to be interpreted by a person, like a photograph, but is designed to be processed in an automated process, that is, to be efficiently and effectively interpretable by a machine. This form of storage would allow for the individualization of a person and eje-

automatically take actions, profile or infer information about a subject such as attitudes or patterns of behavior, etc.  
This technology can be truly intrusive and requires a calm ethical and legal debate, as it can have very adverse effects on fundamental values and human integrity. Consider just some of its special characteristics and think about the significant impact they produce when this data is compromised, compared to when other types of personal data are processed:  
- Biometric systems are closely linked to a person, as they can use a specific unique property of an individual for identification. Each individual has unique fingerprints that show specific characteristics that can be measured to determine whether a fingerprint matches a registered sample. Therefore, they are unique, permanent, or definitive over time, and a person cannot free themselves from them; they can never be changed, not even with age, so the damage created in the event of compromise, loss, or intrusion into the system is irreparable in this case. Unlike a password, in the event of loss, the data of our fingerprint or face cannot be changed.  
- Furthermore, because biometric data is unique to a person and perpetual, the user can use the same data across different systems.  
- While traditional authentication methods such as passwords require a 100% character-by-character match to allow the user to access, for example, an account or application (deterministic methods), biometric methods are referred to as "probabilistic" because they are based on the probability that the user attempting to access a particular device or application is the same person as the registered user. We can measure the performance of a biometric system based on three main characteristics. These are: false rejection rate (FRR), false acceptance rate (FAR), and equal error rate (ERR). The false rejection rate represents the probability of detection errors by a biometric system, meaning that it cannot recognize a user whose biometric characteristics are already in the database. In the event of a rejection, the person must verify their identity again. From a security and protection perspective, this rate does not necessarily mean that it is a negative outcome. Each biometric method, whether facial recognition, fingerprint, palm print, iris, etc., has different values for different rates based on which a system rejects or accepts inputs.

V On the requirement that the processing is necessary, suitable, and proportional  
One of the obligations that corresponds to every data controller is to ensure that the processing complies with the principles set forth in Article 5 of the GDPR.  
In the case of biometric data, due to its special category and high risk, it is essential to highlight the importance of respecting the principle of data minimization, as provided in Article 5.1.c), which states:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
a) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’).”  
Respecting this principle must be the starting point for the initiation of any processing, with the controller first considering whether this processing will truly be necessary, suitable, and proportional before commencing it. If this processing is of high risk—such as in the case of biometrics—it must reflect this prior assessment of necessity and proportionality in a specific document called a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), in accordance with the provisions of Article 35.7.b) of the GDPR, which states that “an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations in relation to their purpose must be carried out and passed.”  
This is confirmed by Recital 39 of the GDPR, which emphasizes the importance of the processing being necessary, stating that “Personal data should only be processed if the purpose of the processing cannot reasonably be fulfilled by other means.”  
In the same vein, the Article 29 Working Party, in its Opinion 3/2012 on the evolution of biometric technologies, indicates that “When analyzing the proportionality of a proposed biometric system, it is necessary to consider beforehand whether the system is necessary to meet the identified need, that is, whether it is essential to satisfy that need, and not just the most suitable or cost-effective. A second factor to consider is the likelihood that the system will be effective in addressing the need in question in light of the specific characteristics of the biometric technology to be used. A third aspect to weigh is whether the resulting loss of privacy is proportional to the expected benefits. If the benefit is relatively minor, such as increased convenience or slight savings, then the loss of privacy is not appropriate. The fourth aspect to evaluate the suitability of a biometric system is to consider whether a less invasive means of achieving the desired end would suffice.”  
This idea is reiterated in section 72 of the Guidelines 3/2019 on the processing of personal data through video devices, dated January 29, 2020, from the EDPB, which states:  
“The use of biometric data and, in particular, facial recognition entails high risks to the rights of data subjects. It is essential that the use of such technologies occurs while duly respecting the principles of legality,

necessity, proportionality, and data minimization as established by the GDPR.  
Although the use of these technologies may be perceived as particularly effective, data controllers must first assess the impact on the fundamental rights and freedoms and consider less intrusive means to achieve their legitimate purpose of processing. In other words, it is necessary to address the question of whether this biometric application is truly essential and necessary, or merely "convenient."  
Therefore, processing personal data that is not suitable (appropriate), necessary, and proportional is always prohibited and constitutes, in itself, the commission of an administrative infringement of Article 5.1(c) of the GDPR.  
Since the processing of biometric data involves restricting the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, the obligation to process only "the personal data that are adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed," as provided by the principle of data minimization/processing in Article 5.1(c) of the GDPR, must be interpreted in accordance with the provisions of the repeated jurisprudence of our Constitutional Court regarding the necessity of establishing that any restrictive measure of fundamental rights (biometric processing in this case) surpasses what is referred to as "the triple test of proportionality."  
This implies that, first and foremost, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions referred to by the Constitutional Court: "whether such a measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test); whether, furthermore, it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other, more moderate measure to achieve such a purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test); and finally, whether it is balanced or proportionate, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (strict sense proportionality test)."

LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection will be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, this organic law, and the provisions

II  
Termination of the procedure  
Article 85 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), under the heading "Termination in sanctioning procedures" provides as follows:  
“1. Once a sanctioning procedure has been initiated, if the offender acknowledges their responsibility, the procedure may be resolved with the imposition of the appropriate sanction.  
2. When the sanction is solely of a pecuniary nature or when it is possible to impose a pecuniary sanction and another non-pecuniary sanction but the imposition of the latter has been justified as inappropriate, voluntary payment by the presumed responsible party, at any time prior to the resolution, will imply the termination of the procedure, except regarding the restoration of the altered situation or the determination of compensation for damages caused by the commission of the infringement.  
3. In both cases, when the sanction is solely of a pecuniary nature, the competent authority to resolve the procedure shall apply reductions of at least 20% on the amount of the proposed sanction, which shall be cumulative. The aforementioned reductions must be specified in the notification of initiation of the procedure, and their effectiveness will be conditioned upon the withdrawal or renunciation of any action or administrative appeal against the sanction. The percentage of reduction provided for in this section may be increased by regulation.”  
III  
Elevation of provisional measure to definitive. Adoption of corrective measures.  
Article 58.2 of the GDPR provides as follows:  
“Each supervisory authority shall have all of the following corrective powers indicated below:  
d) to order the controller or processor to ensure that the processing operations comply with the provisions of this Regulation, where appropriate, in a specified manner and within a specified time;”  
f) to impose a temporary or definitive limitation on processing, including its prohibition; […]”  
i) to impose an administrative fine in accordance with Article 83, in addition to or instead of the measures mentioned in this section, depending on the circumstances of each particular case;”  
Regarding the temporary or definitive limitation of processing, reference should be made to Article 69 of the LPACAP, which determines:

“1. Article 56 of the LPACAP states in its fifth section that:  

“5. Provisional measures may be lifted or modified during the processing of the procedure, either ex officio or at the request of a party, by virtue of unforeseen circumstances or circumstances that could not be taken into account at the time of their adoption. In the present procedure, as it is not recorded that BURGOS CF had suspended the processing of data related to access to the animation stand of the stadium through fingerprint recognition—which was maintained as a voluntary access system for members who opted for it—the initiation agreement of the present procedure ordered the “temporary suspension of all processing of biometric personal data and, in particular, those related to the fingerprint recognition system for access to the El Plantío stadium,” since the provisional suspension of processing was deemed necessary, proportionate, and effective to guarantee the rights and freedoms at stake for the affected individuals and less burdensome for the accused.  

According to what is provided in the initiation agreement of the present procedure, the provisional measure should have been adopted from the notification of the initiation agreement of the sanctioning procedure until its final resolution, in which it should be confirmed, modified, or lifted, without prejudice to the provisions of Article 56.5 of the LPACAP.  

As of the date of this resolution, BURGOS CF has acknowledged its responsibility and payment, requesting the termination of the procedure, without referring to the status of the biometric data processing in the animation stand of its stadium, so it is unknown whether this system has been provisionally suspended, as ordered in the initiation agreement of the sanctioning procedure, or if it has been definitively suspended.  

That said, BURGOS CF has acknowledged its responsibility, and the infringements that were attributed in the initiation agreement have been duly established, making it necessary to impose on the responsible party the adoption of appropriate corrective measures to adjust its actions to data protection regulations, as was already indicated in the initiation agreement.  

It is estimated that the same risks that motivated the provisional suspension or limitation of processing in the initiation agreement currently persist without doubt.
