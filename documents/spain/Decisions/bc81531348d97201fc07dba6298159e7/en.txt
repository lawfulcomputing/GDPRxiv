This principle of European Union law and the scope of corporate liability take precedence and should not be undermined by limiting it to the acts of certain officials (such as senior executives) in contradiction to national law. It is not relevant which natural person acted on behalf of which entity. Therefore, the supervisory authority and national courts should not be required to determine or identify a natural person in investigations or in the decision to impose a fine. In the present case, taking into account the concurrent circumstances, the regulation of the GDPR, and what is provided in the aforementioned Guidelines, the administrative fine should be considered based on the group's turnover and not just on Openbank's turnover.

III  
Obligation Breached  
Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines "personal data" as "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ('the data subject'); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person."  
Article 6 of the GDPR, "Lawfulness of processing," states in its paragraph 1 the circumstances under which the processing of third-party data is considered lawful:  
"1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary in order to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child. The provisions of point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks."  
In this regard, Recital 40 of the GDPR states:  
“(40) In order for processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on another legitimate basis laid down by law, whether in this Regulation or under other Union or Member State law to which this Regulation relates, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject or the necessity of performing a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.”

In this case, it is considered that the respondent violated Article 6.1 of the GDPR, as it processed the personal data of the complainant without legal justification. The personal data of the complainant were incorporated into the company’s information systems, without having demonstrated that it had a legal basis for the collection and subsequent processing of their personal data.  
Consequently, it has processed personal data without having demonstrated that it has the legal authorization to do so.  
In this regard, and this is essential, the respondent does not prove the legitimacy for the processing of the complainant's data.  
Respect for the principle of lawfulness, which is at the core of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, requires that it be demonstrated that the data controller exercised the necessary diligence to prove this aspect. Failing to act in this manner—and failing to require this from this Agency, which is responsible for ensuring compliance with the regulations governing the right to the protection of personal data—would result in emptying the principle of lawfulness of its content.  
Thus, it is considered that the facts submitted for the evaluation of this Agency constitute a violation of Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
In the present case, it is established that Openbank improperly processed the data of the complainant, as it opened a bank account in their name without legal justification, even though the impersonator had the complainant's data; the respondent did not verify that the signature on the ID did not match that of the contract.  
The actions of the respondent are considered serious, as by processing the complainant's data without one of the lawful bases of Article 6 of the GDPR, it has caused harm such as identity theft or damage to their reputation. This harm occurred from the time the account was opened on August 16, 2021, until it was canceled on March 31, 2022.  
It should be emphasized that Openbank did not verify the identity of the person who requested the opening of the bank account and did not take the necessary precautions to prevent these events from occurring.  
Based on the above, the diligence employed by Openbank to identify the person who requested this account opening is called into question.  
In accordance with the available evidence, it is considered that Openbank's conduct violates Article 6.1 of the GDPR, constituting the infringement classified in Article 83.5.a) of the aforementioned Regulation 2016/679.
