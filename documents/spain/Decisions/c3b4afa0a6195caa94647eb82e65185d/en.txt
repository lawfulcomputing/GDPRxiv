In response to the allegations presented by DIGI, the following should be noted:  
Regarding the fact that identity theft and access to the data of the data subject in an illegitimate manner occurs prior to any contact with DIGI and is the cause that triggers the incident and the issuance of the duplicate SIM card. Consequently, it is not possible to associate DIGI with the performance of unauthorized processing of personal data, given that its actions are limited to complying with its processes and obligations.  
Indeed, the issuance of a duplicate is not sufficient to carry out banking operations in the name of the data subjects; certainly, to complete the fraud, it is necessary for a third party to "impersonate" the identity of the data subject before the financial entity.  
This, in principle, leads to processing outside the principle of lawfulness since a third party is processing data, as they have access to it, without any legal basis, in addition to the violation of other principles such as confidentiality.  
For this reason, this is a process where the diligence provided by the operators is fundamental to prevent this type of fraud and violations of the GDPR. Diligence that translates into the establishment of adequate measures to ensure that data processing is in accordance with the GDPR.  
Identical considerations apply to the actions of banking entities that provide payment services, in which this type of fraud begins, as the third party has access to the credentials of the affected user and poses as them.

Insofar as these entities are responsible for the processing of their clients' data, they bear the same obligations as those indicated so far for the operators regarding compliance with the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, as well as those arising from Royal Decree-Law 19/2018, of November 23, on payment services and other urgent measures in financial matters.  
Well, DIGI has provided a duplicate of the SIM card to a third party other than the legitimate holder of the mobile line, after third parties bypassed the existing security policy, which evidences a breach of the duty to protect customer information.  
Denying the occurrence of negligent conduct on the part of DIGI would be equivalent to acknowledging that its behavior—by action or omission—has been diligent. Obviously, we do not share this perspective of the facts, as the lack of due diligence has been established. The Administrative Court ruling of October 17, 2007 (rec. 63/2006) is very illustrative; starting from the premise that these are entities whose activities involve continuous processing of customer data, it indicates that “...the Supreme Court has understood that there is negligence whenever a legal duty of care is neglected, that is, when the offender does not behave with the required diligence. It is established in the file that adequate security has not been guaranteed in the processing of personal data, given the result produced by the identity theft. That is to say, a third party has managed to access the personal data of the holder of the line.

In other cases, it bases the lawfulness of the processing on the grounds provided in Article 6.1.a), c), e), and f) of the GDPR.

However, the manner of attributing responsibility to legal entities does not correspond with the forms of culpability that are attributable to human conduct, whether intentional or negligent. Thus, in the case of violations committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present, it is necessarily applied differently than it is with natural persons.  
According to STC 246/1991, "(...) this distinct construction of the attribution of the authorship of the infringement to the legal entity arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the rules to which they are subject. The capacity to infringe and, therefore, the direct reproachability that derives from the legal good protected by the norm that is infringed and the necessity for that protection to be genuinely effective, along with the risk that, consequently, the legal entity must assume, which is subject to compliance with that norm" (in this sense, STS of November 24, 2011, Rec 258/2009).  
To the above, it should be added, following the judgment of January 23, 1998, partially transcribed in the SSTS of October 9, 2009, Rec 5285/2005, and October 23, 2010, Rec 1067/2006, that "although the culpability of the conduct must also be subject to proof, it must be considered in relation to the assumption of the corresponding burden, that ordinarily the volitional and cognitive elements necessary to appreciate it form part of the proven typical conduct, and that their exclusion requires evidence of the absence of such elements, or in its normative aspect, that due diligence has been exercised by the one who claims its non-existence; in short, the mere invocation of the absence of fault is not sufficient for exculpation against a typically unlawful behavior."  
Consequently, the lack of culpability is dismissed. The ultimate responsibility for the processing remains attributed to the responsible party, who determines the existence of the processing and its purpose. Let us remember that, generally speaking, operators process their customers' data under the provisions of Article 6.1 b) of the GDPR, as it is considered necessary for the execution of a contract in which the data subject is a party (...). In this regard, DIGI has a network of sales representatives, points of sale, and authorized distributors through a distribution contract to offer DIGI's services. Among these services offered from its points of sale is the issuance of duplicates of SIM cards corresponding to a mobile phone line.

In accordance with the evidence available, it is estimated that the conduct of the responding party violates Article 6.1 of the GDPR and may constitute the infringement typified in Article 83.5.a) of the aforementioned Regulation 2016/679.  
In this sense, Recital 40 of the GDPR states:  
“(40) For processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on some other legitimate basis established in accordance with law, whether in this Regulation or under another Union or Member State law to which this Regulation relates, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller or the necessity of performing a contract to which the data subject is party or for the purpose of taking steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.”  
III  
Typification and qualification of the infringement  
The infringement is typified in Article 83.5 of the GDPR, which considers as such:  
“5. Infringements of the following provisions shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 20,000,000 EUR or, in the case of an undertaking, an amount equivalent to up to 4% of the total annual global turnover of the preceding financial year, opting for the higher amount:  
a) The basic principles for processing, including the conditions for consent under Articles 5, 6, 7, and 9.”  
The LOPDGD, for the purposes of the prescription of the infringement, qualifies in its Article 72.1 as a very serious infringement, with a prescription period of three years in this case, “b) The processing of personal data without any of the conditions for lawful processing established in Article 6 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”

The ruling made by the National Court in its SAN of May 5, 2021 (Rec. 1437/2020) regarding section e) of Article 83.2 of the GDPR, concerning the commission of prior infringements:  
“On the other hand, it considers that the absence of a prior infringement should be regarded as a mitigating factor. Well, Article 83.2 of the GDPR establishes that, among other circumstances, the following must be taken into account for the imposition of the administrative fine: 'e) any prior infringement committed by the controller or the processor.' This is an aggravating circumstance; the absence of the conditions for its application means that it cannot be taken into consideration, but it does not imply or allow, as the claimant intends, its application as a mitigating factor.”  
(III) Article 83.2.f) of the GDPR refers to the “degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority in order to remedy the infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects of the infringement.” The response of the respondent to the information request from the Inspection Subdirectorate did not fulfill these purposes, so it cannot be framed within that mitigating circumstance.  
(IV) Regarding the application of Article 76.2.c) of the LOPDGDD, in connection with Article 83.2.k), the non-existence of benefits obtained, it should be noted that such a circumstance can only operate as an aggravating factor and in no case as a mitigating circumstance.  
Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR refers to “any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement.” And Article 76.2.c) of the LOPDGDD states that “2. In accordance with the provisions of Article 83.2.k) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following may also be taken into account: [...] c) The benefits obtained as a result of the commission of the infringement.” Both provisions mention as a factor that can be taken into account in the assessment of the sanction the “benefits” obtained, but not the “absence” of these, which is what DIGI claims.  
Furthermore, according to Article 83.1 of the GDPR, the imposition of fines is governed by the following principles: they must be individualized for each particular case, be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. The admission that the absence of benefits operates as a mitigating factor is contrary to the spirit of Article 83.1 of the GDPR and to the principles governing the determination of the amount of the fine. If, as a result of the commission of a GDPR infringement, it is classified as a mitigating factor that no benefits have existed, the dissuasive purpose fulfilled through the sanction is partially nullified. Accepting DIGI's thesis in a case like the one at hand would mean introducing an artificial reduction in the sanction that truly should be imposed; the one resulting from considering the circumstances of Article 83.2 GDPR that must indeed be assessed.  
The Administrative Litigation Chamber of the National Court has warned that the fact that in a specific case not all elements that constitute a modifying circumstance of liability, which by its nature has an aggravating character, are present, cannot lead to the conclusion that such circumstance is applicable as a mitigating factor.
