1. Applicable sectoral regulations  
1.1. Pharmacies  
Pharmacies are, according to Article 86.6 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, which approves the consolidated text of the Law on guarantees and rational use of medications and health products, private health establishments of public interest.  
Their regulation is found, among other applicable norms, in Law 14/1986, of April 25, General Health Law; Law 16/1997, of April 25, on the Regulation of Pharmacy Office Services; Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, which approves the consolidated text of the Law on guarantees and rational use of medications and health products; as well as in the applicable regional legislation such as, in the case of Catalonia, Law 31/1991, of December 13, on the Pharmaceutical Regulation of Catalonia.  
Both Law 14/1986, of April 25, and Law 16/1997, of April 25, state:  
“The pharmacist owner of the pharmacy, assisted, where appropriate, by assistants or auxiliaries, must provide the following basic services to the population:  
1. The acquisition, custody, conservation, and dispensing of medications and health products.  
2. The monitoring, control, and custody of dispensed medical prescriptions (…)”.  
For its part, Article 3 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, is more emphatic in stating that the custody, conservation, and dispensing of medications for human use corresponds exclusively to:  
“a) Pharmacies open to the public, legally authorized.  
b) The pharmacy services of hospitals, health centers, and primary care structures of the National Health System for their application within such institutions or for medications that require particular monitoring, supervision, and control by the multidisciplinary health care team, in accordance with the qualification granted by the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products for such medications (…)”.  
Pharmacies are obliged, according to Article 86.3 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, to “dispense medications requested by both individuals and the National Health System under the established regulatory conditions.” However, the principle of free choice of pharmacy must be respected. Thus, this regulation establishes as a serious infringement in its Article 111 b):  
“26th. To restrict the user's freedom in choosing the pharmacy through any act or omission.”  
It is also classified as a serious infringement in the same article:  
“22nd. To defraud the National Health System or its beneficiaries regarding the billing and collection of official prescriptions.”  
It is also emphasized that, according to the aforementioned Royal Legislative Decree, it constitutes a very serious infringement according to Article 111 c):  
“23rd. For pharmacies to carry out activities of distributing medications to other pharmacies, authorized distribution entities, or other entities, centers, or individuals without authorization for distribution activities or to carry out shipments of medications outside the national territory.”  
For its part, Law 16/1997, of April 25, enshrines the figure of the pharmacist as an essential and indispensable element for the operation of a pharmacy by determining in its Article 5:  
“1. The presence and professional action of a pharmacist is an inexcusable condition and requirement for the public dispensing of medications. The collaboration of assistants or auxiliaries does not excuse the pharmacist's action in the pharmacy while it remains open to the public, nor does it exclude their professional responsibility.  
2. The Autonomous Communities may regulate the minimum number of assistant pharmacists, in addition to the owner, who must provide services in pharmacies to ensure adequate professional assistance to users. This regulation must take into account, among other factors, the volume and type of activity of the pharmacies and the service hours regime.  
3. Without prejudice to the action of the assistant, the pharmacist owner shall be responsible for ensuring service to users.”  
Furthermore, Article 103 of Law 14/1986, of April 25, General Health Law states:  
“1. The custody, conservation, and dispensing of medications shall correspond to:  
a) Legally authorized pharmacies.  
b) The pharmacy services of hospitals, health centers, and primary care structures of the National Health System for their application within such institutions or for those that require particular monitoring, supervision, and control by the multidisciplinary health care team.  
2. Pharmacies open to the public are considered health establishments for the purposes provided in Title IV of this Law.  
3. Pharmacies will be subject to health planning under the terms established by the special legislation on medications and pharmacies.  
4. Only pharmacists may be owners and holders of pharmacies open to the public.”

2. On the presumption of veracity of the acts of the Pharmacy Inspection  
Article 108 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, which approves the consolidated text of the Law on guarantees and rational use of medications and health products, states that it is the responsibility of the health administrations within their competencies to carry out the necessary inspections to ensure compliance with the provisions of this law.

3. Responsibility for processing in the field of personal data protection  
Article 4.1) of the GDPR defines “personal data” as: “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  
Article 4.2) of the GDPR defines “processing” as: “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction.”  
Article 4.7) of the GDPR defines the “data controller” or “controller” as: “the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; where the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of processing, the controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided by the law of the Union or of the Member States.” In turn, Article 4.8) of the GDPR defines the “processor” as the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the controller.  
For its part, the Guidelines 07/2020 of the European Data Protection Board on the concepts of “data controller” and “data processor” (hereinafter, Guidelines 07/2020) emphasize that the concepts of data controller and data processor are functional concepts, being necessary.

establish them by virtue of their specific activities in the analyzed case and not based on the formal designation that may appear in the contract:  
“12. The concepts of ‘data controller’ and ‘data processor’ are functional concepts: their purpose is to assign responsibilities based on the actual role of each party. This implies that the legal status of ‘data controller’ or ‘data processor’ of the participants must be established, in principle, by virtue of their specific activities in a given situation and not based on the formal designation of a participant as ‘data controller’ or ‘data processor’ (e.g., in a contract). This implies that the assignment of the role of controller or processor must normally derive from an analysis of the facts or circumstances of the case and, consequently, is not negotiable.” (underlined by the AEPD).  
In short, the identification of the controller must be carried out according to material criteria in accordance with the actual conditions and circumstances of each processing operation; it cannot be understood as a mere formal acknowledgment of what is stated, for example, in a contract signed between parties. Thus, there will be occasions when the participants in a chain of processing operations have been identified respectively as controller and processor, and yet, based on who determines the purpose of the processing and the essential means thereof, the role of controller corresponds to the one who was formally identified as such.  
In addition to what has been stated so far, it should be noted that these Guidelines from the EDPB highlight:  
“20. (…) The data controller is the one who decides on certain essential aspects of the data processing. The responsibility for processing can be established in the regulation or inferred from an analysis of the facts or circumstances of the case. It is necessary to focus on the specific processing activities in question and understand who determines them. To do this, the following questions must first be examined: ‘Why is the processing taking place?’ and ‘Who has decided that the processing should be carried out for a specific purpose?’.” (underlined by the AEPD).

Obligation not fulfilled. Article 32 of the GDPR. Security of processing.  
Article 32 of the GDPR establishes:  
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d) a process for regularly testing, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
2. In assessing the appropriateness of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the data processing, in particular as a result of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data.  
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this article.  
4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data only processes such data on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law.”  

The obligation to adopt all technical and organizational measures imposed by Article 32 of the GDPR on the data controller is a manifestation of the two essential pillars of the GDPR: the proactive accountability enshrined in Article 5.2 and developed in Article 24.1 of the GDPR and the risk-based approach. Thus, Article 32, while establishing a set of measures that could be considered minimal to be adopted by the controller, such as pseudonymization or data encryption, cannot in any case be understood as a numerus clausus since these must address the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of likelihood and severity affecting personal data.

LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Presidency of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states that: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection will be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, to the extent that they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  
II  
Termination of the procedure  
Article 85 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), under the heading “Termination in sanctioning procedures” provides the following:  
“1. Once a sanctioning procedure has been initiated, if the offender acknowledges their responsibility, the procedure may be resolved with the imposition of the appropriate penalty.  
2. When the penalty is solely pecuniary or when a pecuniary penalty and another non-pecuniary penalty can be imposed, but the imposition of the latter has been justified as inappropriate, voluntary payment by the alleged responsible party, at any time prior to the resolution, will imply the termination of the procedure, except regarding the restoration of the altered situation or the determination of compensation for the damages caused by the commission of the infringement.  
3. In both cases, when the penalty is solely pecuniary, the competent authority to resolve the procedure will apply reductions of at least 20% on the amount of the proposed penalty, which will be cumulative. The aforementioned reductions must be specified in the notification of initiation of the procedure, and their effectiveness will be conditioned upon the withdrawal or waiver of any action or administrative appeal against the penalty. The percentage of reduction provided for in this section may be increased by regulation.”
