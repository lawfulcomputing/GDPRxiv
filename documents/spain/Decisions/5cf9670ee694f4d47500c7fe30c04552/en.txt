LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of the LOPDGDD, the Director of the Spanish Data Protection Agency is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states that: "The procedures processed by the Spanish Data Protection Agency shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, this organic law, the regulatory provisions issued for its development, and, in so far as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures."

Investigation of the required information at the time it is requested. The contrary would imply accepting that it is not possible to demand compliance with the legal obligation to respond to the information requests of this Agency as long as the proceedings are not concluded and the outcome is determined.  
Regarding the argument of the respondent that, in application of Law 25/2007, of October 18, on the retention of data related to electronic communications and public communications networks, it could not provide this Agency with the requested data without the proper judicial authorization, it is noted that in this case the aforementioned exclusion from the duty of cooperation imposed by Article 52 of the LOPDGDD does not apply, since it cannot be accepted that the mere confirmation of a specific call made between two numbers can be considered as traffic data processed "exclusively" to comply with the obligations set forth in Law 25/2007.  
In this regard, Article 52 of the LOPDGDD expressly provides within the duty of cooperation that, in the event that the conduct was carried out using a fixed or mobile telephone service, information must be supplied regarding "3. The mere confirmation that a specific call has been made between two numbers on a certain date and time."  
In the currently disputed case, what was being requested was not the transfer of specific data, but merely the confirmation of information that the AEPD already had and that it transmitted to VDF for the purpose of confirmation, since the AEPD was aware of the calling number, as well as the specific time and date when that call was made to the claimant's number.  
And all of this in addition to the fact that this confirmation of information is expressly provided for and determined by the legislator within the duty of cooperation of the aforementioned Article 52 of the LOPDGDD.  
Regarding the inability to appreciate the existence of culpability, it is recalled that, indeed, the principle of responsibility set forth in Article 28.1 of Law 40/2015, of October 1, on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector, states that: "Only individuals and legal entities, as well as, when a law recognizes them the capacity to act, groups of affected parties, unions and entities without legal personality, and independent or autonomous assets, that are responsible for the same by way of intent or negligence, may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative infractions."  
However, according to the ruling in STS 7887/2011 of November 24, 2011, Rec. 258/2009, "(...) since its ruling 76/1990, of April 26, the Constitutional Court has declared that objective or non-fault liability is not applicable in the administrative sanctioning sphere, a doctrine that is reaffirmed in ruling 164/2005, of June 20, 2005, whereby the possibility of imposing sanctions solely based on the result is excluded, without proving a minimum level of culpability even in terms of mere negligence. However, the method of attributing responsibility to legal entities does not correspond to the forms of intentional or negligent culpability that are attributable to human conduct."

It follows that, in the case of infringements committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present (see the ruling of this Chamber of the Supreme Court dated November 20, 2011 (appeal for cassation in the interest of the law 48/2007), it is necessarily applied differently than it is with natural persons. According to STC 246/1991, “(...) this distinct construction of the attribution of the authorship of the infringement to the legal person arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the rules to which they are subject. The capacity for infringement and, therefore, direct reproachability derives from the legal right protected by the rule that is infringed and the necessity for that protection to be genuinely effective, as well as the risk that, consequently, the legal person subject to compliance with that rule must assume.”  
To the above, it should be added, following the ruling of January 23, 1998, partially transcribed in STS 6262/2009, of October 9, 2009, Rec 5285/2005, and STS 6336/2009, of October 23, 2009, Rec 1067/2006, that “although the culpability of the conduct must also be subject to proof, it should be considered in terms of the assumption of the corresponding burden, that ordinarily the volitional and cognitive elements necessary to appreciate it form part of the proven typical conduct, and that their exclusion requires evidence of the absence of such elements, or in its normative aspect, that due diligence has been exercised by the party claiming its non-existence; it is not sufficient, in summary, for exculpation against a typically unlawful behavior to invoke the absence of fault.”  
Therefore, the lack of culpability is dismissed since the responding party did not adequately respond to the requests made by this Agency, thereby calling into question the diligence employed.  
Finally, the absence of benefits cannot be considered a mitigating factor according to the Judgment of the AN, dated 05/05/2021, rec. 1437/2020, which states: “On the other hand, it considers that the non-commission of a previous infringement should be appreciated as a mitigating factor. Well, Article 83.2 of the GDPR establishes that the following circumstance must be taken into account for the imposition of the administrative fine, among others: 'e) any previous infringement committed by the controller or the processor.' This is an aggravating circumstance; the fact that the prerequisite for its application does not exist means that it cannot be taken into consideration, but it does not imply or allow, as the claimant intends, its application as a mitigating factor”; applied to the case under review, the lack of the prerequisite for its application regarding Article 76.2.c) of the LOPDGDD, that is, obtaining benefits as a consequence of the infringement, does not allow its application as a mitigating factor.”  
This criterion of gradation is established in the LOPDGDD in accordance with the provisions of Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR, according to which administrative fines will be imposed taking into account any “aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement,” understanding that avoiding a loss has the same nature for these purposes as obtaining benefits.  
If we add to this that the sanctions must be “in each individual case” effective, proportionate, and dissuasive, in accordance with the provisions of Article 83.1 of the GDPR,

Admitting the absence of benefits as a mitigating factor is not only contrary to the factual premises contemplated in Article 76.2.c), but also contrary to what is established in Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR and the principles indicated. Thus, valuing the absence of benefits as a mitigating factor would nullify the deterrent effect of the fine, as it diminishes the impact of the circumstances that effectively influence its quantification, providing the responsible party with a benefit they have not earned. It would be an artificial reduction of the sanction that could lead to the understanding that violating the regulation without obtaining benefits, whether financial or of any other kind, would not produce a negative effect proportional to the severity of the infringing act.  
In any case, the administrative fines established in the GDPR, in accordance with what is established in Article 83.2, are imposed based on the circumstances of each individual case and it is not considered that the absence of benefits is an adequate and determining factor for assessing the severity of the infringing conduct. Only in the case that this absence of benefits is relevant for determining the degree of unlawfulness and culpability present in the specific infringing action can it be considered as a mitigating factor, in application of Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR, which refers to "any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case."  
For all these reasons, the request of the accused party regarding the filing of this sanctioning file cannot be granted, nor can the reduction of the initially proposed sanction based on the indicated mitigating factors.

IV  
Obligation not fulfilled  
In light of the facts presented, it is considered that the respondent party has not provided the Spanish Agency for Data Protection with the information it requested.  
The respondent entity did not respond to the requests made by this Agency in order to confirm the receipt of the calls made to the complainant's numbers, limiting itself to stating that the calling number belonged to another operator, instead of answering the question of whether the calls had been received or not, information that must be known since VODAFONE ESPAÑA, S.A.U. is the entity that operates the complainant's numbers through one of its brands (LOWI).  
With the aforementioned conduct of the respondent party, the investigative power that Article 58.1 of the GDPR confers on supervisory authorities, in this case, the AEPD, has been obstructed.  
Therefore, the facts described in the section "Proven Facts" are deemed to constitute an infringement, attributable to the respondent party, for violation of Article 58.1 of the GDPR, which provides that each supervisory authority shall have, among its investigative powers:  
“a) to order the controller and the processor and, where applicable, the representative of the controller or the processor, to provide any information required for the performance of their tasks; b) to conduct investigations in the form of data protection audits; c) to review the certifications issued pursuant to Article 42, paragraph 7; d) to notify the controller or the processor of the alleged infringements of this Regulation; e) to obtain from the controller and the processor access to all personal data and all information necessary for the performance of their tasks; f) to obtain access to all premises of the controller and the processor, including any equipment and means of processing data, in accordance with Union or Member State procedural law.”

Classification and qualification of the infringement  
The facts presented are deemed to constitute an infringement, attributable to the respondent party.
