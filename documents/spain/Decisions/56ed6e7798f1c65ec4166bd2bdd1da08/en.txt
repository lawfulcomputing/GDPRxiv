II  
Applicable legal norms  
The GDPR refers to the principles governing the processing of personal data in Article 5, “Principles relating to processing,” which states:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
[…]  
f) processed in a manner that ensures an adequate level of security of personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, by applying appropriate technical or organizational measures (‘integrity and confidentiality’);  
[…]  
2. The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and shall be able to demonstrate it (‘proactive accountability’).”  
Article 4 of the GDPR, “Definitions,” establishes that, for the purposes of this Regulation, “processing” shall mean “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”

1. The documentation in the file evidences that there was an employment relationship between JEG'S LIFE and the claimant that began on 28/02/2019 and was terminated on 26/05/2020 due to the dismissal of the worker.

IV  
Violation of the principle of confidentiality  
1. JEG'S LIFE is held responsible for a violation of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR concerning the principle of confidentiality.  
Recital 39 of the GDPR states that “Personal data must be processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security and confidentiality of personal data, including to prevent unauthorized access to or use of such data and the equipment used for processing.”  
The Judgment of the Constitutional Court (STC) 292/2000 states regarding the content of the right to the protection of personal data (Legal Basis 7):

“[…] it results that the content of the fundamental right to data protection consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that enables the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which data this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose such possession or use. These powers of disposition and control over personal data, which are part of the content of the fundamental right to data protection, are legally concretized in the ability to consent to the collection, obtaining, and access to personal data, its subsequent storage and processing, as well as its possible use or uses, by a third party, whether the State or a private individual. And this right to consent to the knowledge and processing, whether computerized or not, of personal data requires, as indispensable complements, on the one hand, the ability to know at all times who disposes of that personal data and to what use they are subjecting it, and, on the other hand, the power to oppose such possession and uses.” (The underlining is ours)  
The documentation in the file certifies that JEG'S LIFE, through its legal representative, the joint administrator 1, provided third parties with information concerning the claimant to which it had access within the framework of the employment relationship that both maintained.  
By virtue of the principle of proactive responsibility (“accountability”) established in Article 5.2 of the GDPR, it is the responsibility of the data controller to comply with the principles of Article 5.1 of the GDPR, particularly the principle of confidentiality, as well as to be able to demonstrate compliance.  
As stated by the Legal Office of the AEPD (among others, in reports 17/2019 and 64/2020), the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to personal data protection, which is now based on the principle of “proactive responsibility” (“accountability”). In this sense, the explanatory memorandum of the LOPDGDD states that “the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or processor of the risks that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the appropriate measures based on that assessment.”  
Opinion 3/2010, from the Article 29 Working Party (GT29) - WP 173 - issued during the validity of Directive 95/46/EC, which has been repealed by the GDPR, but whose reflections are applicable today, states that the “essence” of proactive responsibility is the obligation of the data controller to apply measures that, under normal circumstances, ensure that the rules on data protection are complied with in the context of processing operations and to have available documents that demonstrate to data subjects and to the supervisory authorities what measures have been adopted to achieve compliance with data protection regulations.  
In the factual case at hand, the processing of the claimant's personal data constitutes a violation of Article 5.1(f) of the GDPR.

Recital 74 of the GDPR states on this matter:  
“The responsibility of the data controller for any processing of personal data carried out by themselves or on their behalf must be established. In particular, the controller must be obliged to implement appropriate and effective measures and must be able to demonstrate compliance of the processing activities with this Regulation, including the effectiveness of the measures. In the initiation agreement of the sanctioning procedure, having appreciated as an aggravating circumstance the concurrence of the circumstance of Article 83.2.b) of the GDPR - “Intentionality or negligence in the infraction” - this statement was included: “In the present case, in light of the documentation in the file, it is clearly evidenced that the party being complained against acted with the direct intention of disclosing to third parties information concerning the complainant.”  
Intent must always be subject to proof. In the case we are examining, liability due to intent was inferred from the repeated conduct of the party being complained against in which it disclosed to third parties information concerning the complainant.  
It should be noted, however, that while the liability due to intent is not sufficiently established, the severely culpable conduct or gross negligence of the data controller is fully evidenced. Thus, the element of culpability, necessary for the emergence of sanctioning liability, would be constituted by the lack of diligence demonstrated by the party being complained against in complying with the confidentiality principle to which it is obliged by Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR.

VI  
Administrative fine sanction  
Article 70.1 of the LOPDGDD provides that those responsible for processing are subject to the sanctioning regime established in the GDPR and in this organic law: “a) The controllers of the processing.”  
The corrective powers attributed to the AEPD as the supervisory authority are detailed in article 58.2 of the GDPR, paragraphs a) to j), among which the provision includes the power to impose an administrative fine in accordance with article 83 of the GDPR (article 58.2.i).  
As indicated in the initiation agreement of the sanctioning file, in the present case it is considered appropriate to sanction JEG'S LIFE for the infringement of article 5.1.f) of the GDPR with an administrative fine.  
Article 83 of the GDPR, “General conditions for the imposition of administrative fines,” states in paragraph 1 that the supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of fines for the infringements of this Regulation indicated in its paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 complies, in each individual case, with the principles of effectiveness, proportionality, and deterrence.  

The principle of proportionality entails a correlation between the infringement and the sanction, with the prohibition of unnecessary or excessive measures, so that it must be suitable to achieve the purposes that justify it. The requirement imposed by article 83.2 of the GDPR that administrative fines be not only effective and deterrent but also proportionate obliges consideration of the turnover or activity figure of the respondent. On this aspect, the available information is only the amount of the entity's share capital, which according to the Commercial Registry amounts to 173,726 euros.  
On the other hand, article 83.2 of the GDPR provides a list of criteria or factors that must be taken into account to determine the amount of the fine. In the initiation agreement of the procedure, the concurrence of the following circumstances of article 83.2 of the GDPR that operate as aggravating factors was appreciated:  
- Article 83.2.a) GDPR: the severity of the infringement, taking into account the scope or purpose of the processing operation in question.  
As stated then, various criteria indicate the severity of the conduct contrary to the GDPR from the perspective of the “scope” or “purpose” of the processing carried out:  
(i) There have been three data processing operations carried out by the respondent party in which it has violated the principle of confidentiality.  
(ii) The breach of the confidentiality of the complainant's personal data has had a huge scope as there have been numerous recipients of the emails sent by the respondent in which personal information of the affected party was revealed.

- Article 83.2.g) GDPR: “The categories of personal data affected by the infringement.”  
Among the data concerning the complainant whose confidentiality has been violated are not only their name and surname, their habitual address, their email address, the contribution group they had in the respondent company, the dates of entry and exit from the company, the date of medical leave due to work incapacity, and the “Contribution bases for common contingencies and for AT/EP, according to TC-2, during the last 180 days prior to the termination of the employment contract,” but also others such as their Social Security affiliation number or their NIF, which identify them unequivocally.  
On this point, it should be noted that, although the literal wording of Article 83.2.g) of the GDPR seems to connect this criterion of graduation exclusively with Article 9 of the GDPR and the classification that is implicit in that provision between particularly protected personal data and the rest, there are references in the GDPR to other classifications of data that respond to the purpose pursued by Article 83.2 of the GDPR for the graduation of the administrative fine to be imposed, for the individual case, respecting the principles of proportionality and effectiveness.  
Recitals 51 and 75 of the GDPR distinguish a group of personal data that, by their nature, are particularly “sensitive” due to the significant risk that their processing may pose to fundamental rights and freedoms. Their common denominator is the risk they pose to rights and fundamental freedoms, as their processing can lead to physical, material, or immaterial harm.

This group or category includes the specially protected data regulated by Article 9 of the GDPR -recital 51 of the GDPR- and, additionally, many others that are not mentioned in that provision. Recital 75 details the personal data whose processing may entail varying degrees of risk and probability for the rights and freedoms of individuals as a consequence of potentially causing physical, material, or immaterial harm. Among these, it refers to those whose processing "may lead to issues of discrimination, identity theft or fraud, financial losses, damage to reputation, loss of confidentiality of data subject to professional secrecy, unauthorized reversal of pseudonymization, or any other significant economic or social harm;”  
The numerical identifier of the DNI along with the corresponding verification character of the tax identification number unequivocally identifies a natural person. This quality makes it a particularly sensitive data since, to the extent that its processing is not accompanied by the necessary technical and organizational measures to ensure that the person identifying themselves with it is indeed its holder, a third party can easily impersonate the identity of a natural person, or, in other words, can commit identity fraud, with the risks that this entails for the privacy, honor, and assets of the impersonated individual.  
The breach of the confidentiality principle for which the respondent is held responsible in this resolution has involved, among other data, the DNI/NIF of the complainant, which allows for their unequivocal identification, impacting the severity of the conduct. Reasons that lead to the consideration of the circumstance in section g) of Article 83.2 of the GDPR as an aggravating factor, as was also deemed in the initiation agreement of the sanctioning procedure.  
No circumstances under Article 83.2 of the GDPR that would mitigate the responsibility of the respondent or the unlawfulness of their conduct are observed.  
In light of these facts, it is agreed to sanction the respondent entity for the violation of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.5.a) of the GDPR, with an administrative fine (Article 58.2.i) set at €20,000 (twenty thousand euros), considering the circumstances of Article 83.2 GDPR that operate as aggravating factors -sections a), b), and g)-.
