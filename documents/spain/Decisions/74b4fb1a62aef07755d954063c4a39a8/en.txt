Transfers of Personal Data to Third Countries  
Article 44 "Transfers of personal data to third countries or international organizations" of the GDPR establishes:  
"Transfers of personal data that are subject to processing or will be processed after their transfer to a third country or international organization may only take place if, subject to the other provisions of this Regulation, the controller and the processor comply with the conditions laid down in this chapter, including those relating to further transfers of personal data from the third country or international organization to another third country or another international organization. All provisions of this chapter shall apply in order to ensure that the level of protection of natural persons guaranteed by this Regulation is not undermined."  
Chapter V of the Regulation provides various instruments to ensure a level of protection that is substantially equivalent to that guaranteed in the European Union, in accordance with Article 44 of the Regulation:  
- adequacy decisions (Article 45);  
- appropriate safeguards (Article 46);  
In the absence of an equivalent level of protection, it establishes exceptions for specific situations (Article 49).

2. On the qualification of the data subject to processing as personal data  
It can be asserted that the data collected under the Google Analytics function and transferred to the United States of America constitutes personal data.  
Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines personal data as "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ('the data subject'); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific factors relating to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person."  
It should be noted that online identifiers, such as IP addresses or information stored in cookies, can commonly be used to identify a user, especially when combined with other similar types of information. This is illustrated in Recital 30 of the GDPR, which states that the assignment of online identifiers such as IP addresses and cookie identifiers to natural persons or their devices can "leave traces that, in particular when combined with unique identifiers and other information received by the servers, can be used to create profiles of natural persons and identify them." In the particular case where the data controller claims not to have the ability to identify the user through the use (alone or combined with other data points) of such identifiers, it would be expected to disclose the specific means deployed to ensure the anonymity of the collected identifiers. Without such details, they cannot be considered anonymous.  
Therefore, it is necessary to examine to what extent the implementation of Google Analytics on a website allows the website administrator and Google to identify a data subject (a visitor to the relevant website).

For this reason, when several elements are combined, they can allow for the individual identification of visitors to the webpage of "***URL.1", where Google Analytics is implemented. It is not necessary to know the name or the (physical) address of the visitor, as, according to Recital 26 of the GDPR, such qualification of individuals is sufficient for the visitor to be identifiable.

If the opposite were decided, the scope of the right to data protection, guaranteed by Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, would be undermined, as it would allow companies to specifically select individuals along with personal information (for example, when visiting a specific website) while simultaneously denying them any right to protection against such singling out. Such a restrictive opinion that would undermine the level of protection for individuals is also not in line with the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter, CJEU), which has repeatedly ruled that the scope of the GDPR must be understood very broadly (see, for example, judgment C-439/19, paragraph 61).  
Google LLC claims that it has "no intention" of using online identifiers to identify the claimant (or other individuals), as stated in the last paragraph of the response to question 13 of the document dated April 9, 2021, and that it "does not do this." It should be noted that Article 4, paragraph 1, of the GDPR does not require an entity to have a specific intention to identify a person. According to the clear wording of Article 4, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, the term "personal data" is fulfilled when an entity can (has the possibility to) do so.  
Apart from this, even after a more restrictive interpretation of Article 4, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, which in any case would be contrary to the jurisdiction of the CJEU, the definition of "personal data" would be understood to apply to the exposed data. In the event that any visitor to the website ***URL.1 had logged into a Google account at the time of their visit, as can be seen in the statement from Google LLC dated April 9, 2021, the implementation of Google Analytics on a website allows Google to receive information that a specific user of the Google account has visited that website.  
In the context of the use of Google Analytics, and depending on some settings in the Google user account configuration (see response to question 9 of the Google document dated April 9, 2021), it allows Google to receive information that a user logged into a Google account has visited a particular website. Therefore, personal data related to this account is collected. For all these reasons, the data in question must be considered personal data in the sense of Article 4.1 of the GDPR.

b.  
Appropriate safeguards: Standard data protection clauses  
Article 46, “Transfers subject to adequate safeguards,” of the GDPR, establishes in its paragraph 1 that “In the absence of a decision pursuant to Article 45(3), the controller or processor may only transfer personal data to a third country or international organization if it has provided adequate safeguards and on condition that the data subjects have enforceable rights and effective legal remedies.”  
Article 46, paragraph 2, of the GDPR provides that “The adequate safeguards referred to in paragraph 1 may be provided, without requiring any express authorization from a supervisory authority, by:  
(...)  
c) standard data protection clauses adopted by the Commission in accordance with the examination procedure referred to in Article 93(2).  
(...).”  
The Google Standard Contractual Clauses for the transfer of personal data to the United States, in its version of September 21, 2022, titled "Google Ads & Measurement: Standard Contractual Clauses (Module 3: Processor-to-Processor)," which could be translated as: “Google Ads Data Processing Terms: Standard Contractual Clauses, Processor-to-Processor Standard Contractual Clauses” (hereinafter, SCC). These clauses comply with those published by the European Commission in Decision 2010/87/EU.  
In this context, it is noteworthy that the Standard Contractual Clauses are a transfer instrument in the sense of Chapter V of the Regulation and were not challenged as such by the CJEU in its judgment of July 16, 2020 (C-311/18). However, the CJEU considered that the contractual nature of these clauses implied that they could not be binding on the authorities of third countries. In particular, the CJEU stated that: “...Therefore, while there are situations in which, depending on the legislation and practices in force in the third country in question, the recipient of such a transfer is in a position to ensure the necessary protection of the data solely on the basis of Standard Contractual Clauses for data protection, there are others in which the content of such standard clauses may not constitute a sufficient means to ensure, in practice, the effective protection of the personal data transferred to the third country in question. This is particularly the case when the legislation of that third country allows its public authorities to interfere with the rights of the data subjects to...

"that refer to such data" (C-311/18, paragraph 126, emphasis added).  
However, no further analysis of the legal situation in the United States is necessary, as the CJEU has already provided it in its aforementioned judgment. Indeed, the CJEU considered that regulatory surveillance programs such as Section 702 of FISA and E.O. 12333 in conjunction with PPD-28 do not meet the minimum requirements established by Union Law regarding the principle of proportionality, so that surveillance programs based on these provisions cannot be considered limited to what is strictly necessary (C-311/18, paragraph 184). Furthermore, the CJEU found that the legal framework in question did not confer on the parties concerned rights that could be enforced in court against U.S. authorities, from which it follows that these individuals do not have the right to effective judicial protection (C-311/18, paragraph 192).  
The CJEU's analysis is relevant in the present matter, as Google LLC (as the importer of data to the U.S.) must be classified as a provider of electronic communications services within the meaning of subsection (b) of point 4 of Section 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code and, therefore, is subject to surveillance by U.S. intelligence services in accordance with subsection (a) of Section 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code ("FISA 702"). Therefore, Google LLC is obliged to provide personal data to the U.S. government when requested in accordance with subsection (a) of Section 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code (FISA 702).

To the extent that these standard data protection clauses cannot, by their very nature, provide guarantees beyond a contractual obligation to ensure compliance with the level of protection required by Union Law, they may require, depending on the prevailing situation in a particular third country, the adoption of supplementary measures by the controller to ensure compliance with that level of protection" (C-311/18, paragraph 133).

In its Recommendations 01/2020, dated June 18, 2021, (which can be consulted on the website ***URL.11, although the transcriptions included in this document refer to its translation into Spanish) the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) clarified that when the assessment of the legislation or current practices of the third country may affect the effectiveness of the adequate safeguards of the transfer instruments on which the exporter relies, in the context of its specific transfer, as is the case here following the assessment of the CJEU, the exporter must suspend the transfer or implement appropriate supplementary measures. The EDPB notes in this regard that “Any supplementary measure can only be considered effective in the sense of the CJEU ruling ‘Schrems II’ to the extent that it addresses the specific deficiencies identified in its assessment of the legal situation in the third country. If, ultimately, it cannot ensure a level of protection essentially equivalent, personal data must not be transferred.” (see Recommendations 01/2020, point 75).  
The measures to complement the standard data protection clauses can be classified into three categories: contractual, technical, or organizational (see Recommendations 01/2020, point 74).  
Regarding contractual measures, the EDPB stated that: “In some situations, these measures can complement and reinforce the guarantees that the transfer instrument and the relevant law of the third country can provide when, considering the circumstances of the transfer, they do not meet all the necessary conditions to ensure a level of protection essentially equivalent to that guaranteed in the Union. Given the nature of contractual measures, which generally cannot bind the authorities of that third country when they are not part of the contract, these must be combined with other technical and organizational measures to provide the required level of data protection (...).” (see Recommendations 01/2020, point 99, emphasis added).  
As for organizational measures, the EDPB highlighted that: “... Selecting and applying one or more of these measures will not necessarily and systematically ensure that your transfer complies with the essential equivalence standard required by Union law. Regarding technical measures, the EDPB stated that “... Complementary measures implemented by Google LLC  
Google LLC, as the recipient of user data from its Google Analytics services, adopted contractual, organizational, and technical measures to complement the SCC. In the document dated April 9, 2021, sent by Google LLC to the Austrian data protection authority, which it shared with the other authorities within the framework of the TF101 Working Group, Google LLC described the measures taken in detail.

On July 10, 2023, the Commission approved the Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1795 regarding the adequacy of the level of protection of personal data in the EU-U.S. Privacy Framework in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, so that international transfers carried out after this date may be covered by the aforementioned Decision.

However, it must be concluded that, on the date when the events subject to the claim occurred, FNMT cannot invoke any of the tools provided for in Chapter V of the GDPR to justify the international transfers of personal data of visitors to its website, particularly unique identifiers, IP addresses, browser data, and metadata, to Google LLC in the United States, being fully applicable the doctrine established by the Court of Justice of the European Union in the judgment of the Schrems II case, which invalidated the decision on the "EU-U.S. Privacy Shield."
