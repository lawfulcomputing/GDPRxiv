LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR) and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of the LOPDGDD, the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, in so far as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  

II  
Unfulfilled obligation. Excessive data processing  
Article 5 of the GDPR refers to the principle of data minimization in paragraph 1, letter c) in the following terms:  
“Article 5 Principles relating to processing  
1. Personal data shall be:  
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’).”  
The entity being claimed against is a company that provides medical services and operates several dental clinics, including the Sagrada Familia Dental Clinic in Barcelona, of which the claimant is a client.  
For reasons unknown, the services received by the claimant resulted in a balance in their favor, which entails an obligation for the entity being claimed against to reimburse amounts. According to the claimant, the clinic must reimburse them for an amount overpaid for a treatment received.  
To carry out these reimbursements, the entity being claimed against has required its clients, prior to the reimbursement, to provide a photocopy of their identity document. The reimbursement of the amount in question is conditioned by the Clinic on the delivery of a photocopy of this document, as stated by the Clinic, to verify the identity of the client and ensure that the reimbursement is made to the correct person.  
This has occurred in the present case, in which the Clinic has rejected, according to the claimant, all the alternatives proposed to effectuate the reimbursement, among which they mention reimbursement via transfer to the bank account that has already been communicated to the entity being claimed against, a nominative or cash check, with the corresponding receipt signed.  
This Agency understands that the contractual relationship that entails the provision of dental services to a client (the claimant) justifies the access by the entity being claimed against to the personal data of the individuals receiving them, as long as it is necessary for the fulfillment of the obligations arising from such service provision; but it does not justify access to all the information contained in the client's identity document and, even less, the collection and retention of a photocopy of this document without a legal basis justifying it.  
Given that the purpose is to return an amount to a person due to the cancellation of a treatment or another reason, it means that this is an already identified client of the Clinic who has made previous payments, so that reimbursement can be made using the information already available and without the need to collect the additional information referred to in the claim.  
If, as the claimant states, the entity being claimed against already had their bank account and accepted the return of the amount via transfer to that account, there are no grounds for requiring a copy of the client's identity document. The same can be said regarding the other means proposed by the claimant for the amount in question to be satisfied, such as payment via nominative check or cash payment simply by verifying their identity and signing the corresponding receipt for the delivery of cash.  
Thus, the collection of the photocopy of the client's identity document, generally required by the entity being claimed against, with all the information contained in that document, is understood as a processing of personal data that is inadequate, irrelevant, and unnecessary for the specific purpose of processing, contrary to the principles of data protection, specifically, the principle of “data minimization,” regulated in Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR.  
Consequently, in accordance with the evidence presented, the aforementioned facts constitute a violation by the entity being claimed against of the provisions of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, which gives rise to the application of the corrective powers that Article 58 of the aforementioned Regulation grants to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection.

The respondent has indicated that the complaint directed by the complainant to the DPO of the entity was not forwarded to them due to an oversight, without intent, by the person who received the communication, and the cause of this omission could not be determined.  
However, this Agency understands that failing to forward the complaint to the DPO amounts to not processing it, to not handling it due to a malfunction of the internal channels established by the entity, which cannot be accepted as an unintentional error.  
The incident occurs within the scope of responsibility of the respondent, and this entity must be held accountable for it. It cannot be considered that the alleged error excludes their responsibility, since, according to established case law, the existence of such an error cannot be deemed to exist when it is attributable to the one suffering it or could have been avoided with greater diligence. In this case, the alleged error is incompatible with the diligence that the respondent is obliged to observe, which must be manifested in the specific case being analyzed, regarding which the error is alleged.  
In the specific case of the complainant, it cannot be accepted that the action of the respondent, not processing the complaint directed to the DPO, was diligent. To admit that it is not appropriate to demand responsibility for this fact, based on a supposed unintentional error consisting of not processing the complaint, would be tantamount to admitting that the application of the GDPR and the LOPDGDD can be ignored, undermining the entire system established in relation to the figure of the data protection officer, in which the obligation to respond to such complaints is expressly contemplated in any case, within a period of two months.

In this regard, if the error demonstrates a lack of diligence, the type is applicable, since although the principle of culpability governs in sanctioning matters, as inferred from a simple reading of Article 130 of Law 30/1992, it is true that the expression "simple noncompliance" in Article 130.1 of Law 30/1992 allows for the imposition of a sanction, undoubtedly in cases of intent, and also in cases of negligence, as mere noncompliance with the duty of care suffices.

"The appellant also maintains that there is no culpability in her actions. The principle of culpability is required in the sanctioning procedure, and thus the STC 246/1991 considers it inadmissible in the realm of administrative sanctioning law to have liability without fault. However, the principle of fault does not imply that only intentional or voluntary actions can be sanctioned, and in this regard, Article 28 of Law 40/2015 on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector, under the heading "Liability," states the following:  
"1. Only individuals and legal entities, as well as, when a law recognizes them the capacity to act, groups of affected parties, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous assets, that are responsible for the same by way of intent or negligence, may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative infractions."  
The facts presented demonstrate that the party being claimed did not act with the diligence to which it was obligated and acted with a lack of diligence. The Supreme Court (Judgments of April 16 and 22, 1991) considers that from the culpability element it follows that "the action or omission, classified as an administratively sanctionable infraction, must, in any case, be attributable to its author, by intent or imprudence, negligence, or inexcusable ignorance." The same Court reasons that "it is not enough... for exculpation against a typically unlawful behavior to invoke the absence of fault," but it is necessary "that the diligence required by the one claiming its nonexistence has been employed" (STS January 23, 1998).  
Also connected with the degree of diligence that the data controller is obliged to exercise in fulfilling the obligations imposed by data protection regulations is the SAN of October 17, 2007 (Rec. 63/2006), which specified: "(...) the Supreme Court has understood that there is imprudence whenever a legal duty of care is neglected, that is, when the infringer does not behave with the required diligence."

V  
Corrective Powers  
In the event of a violation of the provisions of the GDPR, among the corrective powers available to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, as the supervisory authority, Article 58.2 of that Regulation includes the following:  
“2 Each supervisory authority shall have all of the following corrective powers:  
(…)

(…)  
d) order the controller or processor to bring the processing operations into compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, when appropriate, in a specific manner and within a specified time frame;

According to the provisions of Article 83.2 of the GDPR, the measure provided for in the previous letter d) is compatible with the sanction consisting of an administrative fine.

. Article 83.2.b) of the GDPR: “b) the intentionality or negligence in the infringement."  
Intentionality or negligence in the infringement: although the Agency considers that there was no intentionality on the part of the defendant party, it concludes that its actions are negligent, as it established excessive requirements for the reimbursement of amounts.  
. Article 83.2.g) of the GDPR: “g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement.”  
Categories of personal data affected by the infringement: although "Special categories of personal data," as defined by the GDPR in Article 9, have not been affected, the personal data referred to in the proceedings (image of the identity documentation of clients) has a particularly sensitive nature and increases the risks to their privacy.  
. Article 76.2.b) of the LOPDGDD: “b) The link between the infringer's activity and the processing of personal data.”  
The high link of the infringer's activity with the processing of personal data, considering the activity it develops. This circumstance determines a higher degree of demand and professionalism and, consequently, of responsibility of the defendant entity in relation to the processing of the data.  
Considering the factors presented, the assessment of the fine for the infringement of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR is 20,000 euros (twenty thousand euros).

The following aggravating criteria are considered concurrent:

. Article 76.2.b) of the LOPDGDD: “b) The link between the infringer's activity and the processing of personal data.”  
The high link of the infringer's activity with the processing of personal data, already justified in relation to the previous infringement.

. Article 83.2.a) of the GDPR: “a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing operation in question as well as the number of affected parties and the level of damage and harm they have suffered.”  
The infringement only affects the claimant party.  
Considering the factors presented, the assessment of the fine for the infringement of Article 38 of the GDPR is 10,000 euros (ten thousand euros).
