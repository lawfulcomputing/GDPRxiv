In response to such allegation, it should be indicated that the Judgment of the Supreme Court, dated November 12, 2020, Appeal 4739/2019, in its legal basis IV states the following:  
“Doctrine on the issue that presents objective cassational interest.  
The admission order issued in this appeal on November 15, 2019 established that the issue raised in the appeal that presented objective cassational interest for the formation of jurisprudence consisted of 'determining whether the concept of certain, due, enforceable debt that has gone unpaid includes the total amount of the debt claimed through administrative or judicial means, or whether, for the purposes of possible inclusion in the ASNEF register, that amount which has not been claimed can be deducted, in this case the principal of the debt and the remunerative interests, since only those amounts derived from accessory clauses of the contract have been claimed.'  
Well, to respond to the aforementioned issue, we must clarify what the true purpose pursued with the inclusion of data in the so-called 'delinquent registers' is, and also, what requirements must be met for such inclusion to be considered legitimate.  
In this regard, the First Chamber of this Supreme Court - in its SSTS 463/2019, of September 11; 174/2018, of March 23; 68/2016, of February 16; 740/2015, of December 22; and 176/2013, of March 6, among others - has established a doctrine that we fully share and that can be summarized as follows:  
- The data included in these delinquent registers must be true and accurate; but compliance with these requirements alone is not sufficient to meet the demands of the principle of data quality in this type of register. There are data

that may be certain and accurate without being relevant, as they are not determinative for assessing the economic solvency of the interested parties.  
- If the debt is subject to controversy because the data subject legitimately believes that they do not owe what is being claimed and the matter is under judicial or arbitral decision, the lack of payment is not indicative of the insolvency of the affected party. The debt may ultimately be recognized, in whole or in part, by the judgment or arbitral award and therefore may be considered as a true fact. However, it was not a pertinent and proportionate fact for the purpose of the automated file, as its purpose is not merely to ascertain debts, but to evaluate the asset solvency of the affected parties. Therefore, only the inclusion in these files of those debtors who cannot or do not want to pay their debts without justification is pertinent, but not of those who are legitimately disputing with the creditor the existence or amount of the debt.  
- When it comes to files related to the fulfillment of monetary obligations, the debt must also be due and enforceable, certain, that is, unequivocal, undoubted, and a prior payment request is also necessary. For this reason, it is not permissible to include personal data in these records due to uncertain, doubtful, non-peaceful, or litigated debts.  
- The inclusion in the records of defaulters cannot be used by large companies to seek to collect amounts they deem pertinent, relying on the fear of personal discredit of the affected party, the detriment to their professional prestige, and the denial of access to the credit system that appearing in a defaulter file entails, thereby avoiding the costs that would come with initiating the corresponding judicial procedure, which often exceeds the amount of the debts they claim. Therefore, the inclusion in the defaulter registry when the client has legitimately questioned the existence or amount of the debt, in judicial or arbitral proceedings, constitutes illegitimate pressure.  
Consequently, in light of the aforementioned doctrine, it is appropriate to respond to the question posed by the Admission Section in the following terms:  
In consideration of the purpose of such records—which is not to ascertain the non-payment of debts, but to evaluate the asset solvency of the debtor— for the inclusion of the debtor's data in a defaulter registry to be considered legitimate, it is not enough for the amount of the unpaid debt to be accurate and true; it is also necessary for the inclusion in the registry to be pertinent. And it will not be pertinent when the debtor has legitimately questioned, in administrative, judicial, or arbitral proceedings, the existence or amount of the debt.  
Therefore, we must understand that, although a prior judicial declaration is certainly not required for a debt to be included in a credit information system, it is necessary that the debt is not uncertain, doubtful, non-peaceful, or subject to litigation, which is why preventive cancellation should have been carried out, as is being claimed in this case.

Thus, it follows from the proven facts of this procedure the serious lack of diligence of the respondent in maintaining the claimant's data registered in the credit information system despite the existence of an open judicial proceeding affecting the amount of the debt. The inclusion or maintenance of the debt in credit information systems, when the affected party has legitimately questioned the existence or the amount of the debt, either judicially or arbitrarily, affects the very purpose of the common file, which is none other than to facilitate information about the debtor's solvency. The mere lack of payment of the debt when it is disputed is not indicative of insolvency, since, as stated by the Supreme Court in its Judgment of November 12, 2020, Appeal 4739/2019, only the inclusion in these files of those debtors who cannot or do not want, without justification, to pay their debts is pertinent, but not of those who are legitimately disputing with the creditor the existence or the amount of the debt.  
Therefore, diligence must be heightened in these cases to ensure that personal data related to debts subject to litigation are not included or remain in common credit information systems. Such inclusion or permanence constitutes an illegitimate pressure that the debtor, who justifiably disputes the veracity of the debt or its amount, should not have to endure.  
The infringement attributed in this case may be sanctioned with a fine of up to €20,000,000 or, in the case of a company, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 4% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount, as dictated by Article 83.5 of the GDPR. Given these circumstances, it follows that the amount set as a sanction for the respondent is not disproportionate.  
IV  
In light of the above, it should be noted that the violation of data protection regulations occurs when the respondent maintained the claimant's data in the credit information system from the challenge of the settlement until January 12, 2023, the date on which the cancellation becomes effective; therefore, during this period, there has been processing without a basis for legitimization of the claimant's personal data.

To ensure its useful effect, the Council of State wishes to point out, as an alternative solution, the possibility of introducing, through legislative means, in specific cases, simple iuris tantum presumptions favorable to the prevalence of the legitimate interest of the data controller when certain requirements or conditions are met.  
This solution could align with the flexibility in the balancing of interests and the principle of proactive responsibility of the data controller that, as indicated, is pursued by the new community regulation. Furthermore, the provision of simple presumptions unless proven otherwise would fit within the analyzed jurisprudence, which prohibits establishing in a national norm the result of the aforementioned balancing "definitively (...), without allowing for a different outcome," an obstacle that the alternative solution proposed here would, in principle, allow to avoid.  
Following this provision, the LOPDGDD introduces a "iuris tantum" presumption of the prevalence of the legitimate interest of the data controller in certain specific cases, including the processing of data in credit information systems. Thus, when the guarantees provided in Article 20 of the LOPDGDD are observed, the processing may be presumed lawful under Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR, without prejudice to the fact that the legitimacy must be assessed on a case-by-case basis and without prejudice to the fact that the controller may carry out the legally required balancing when the aforementioned guarantees are not met, as clarified in the preamble of the law, which states:  
“In Title IV, ‘Provisions applicable to specific treatments’ are included, incorporating a series of cases that should in no case be considered exhaustive of all lawful treatments. Among them, it is worth noting, first of all, those for which the legislator establishes a ‘iuris tantum’ presumption of the prevalence of the legitimate interest of the controller when carried out with a series of requirements, which does not exclude the lawfulness of this type of processing when the conditions set forth in the text are not strictly met, although in this case the controller must carry out the legally required balancing, as the prevalence of their legitimate interest is not presumed.”  
Among the aforementioned guarantees, Article 20 of the LOPDGDD includes the one stating that “the data refer to certain, due, and enforceable debts, the existence or amount of which has not been subject to administrative or judicial claim by the debtor or through a binding alternative dispute resolution procedure between the parties” (Article 20.1.b) GDPR).  
And as has been stated, in this case, the amount of the debt was disputed, so the presumption of lawfulness cannot be applied, and the claimed party must carry out its own balancing as the legally established balancing does not apply. However, in this case, there is no indication that the claimed party has carried out such balancing and consequently that its legitimate interests prevail over the interests, rights, and freedoms of the claimed party.

Therefore, in accordance with the available evidence, it is considered that the described facts constitute a violation of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, as the inclusion of personal data in credit information systems due to a disputed debt by the claimant, which is pending judicial resolution, is not covered by the presumption of lawfulness provided for in Article 20 of the LOPDGDD. This implies processing of personal data without a legal basis, as there is no indication that the respondent has conducted the necessary balancing to determine the prevalence of its legitimate interest over the interests, rights, and freedoms of the claimant, since this processing is not protected by the presumption of lawfulness contemplated in Article 20 of the LOPDGDD, given that the debt subject to inclusion in the solvency files was not yet certain as it was in an ongoing judicial process.  

V  
Article 58.2 of the GDPR states the following: “Each supervisory authority shall have all of the following corrective powers:  
b) to impose a warning on any controller or processor when the processing operations have infringed the provisions of this Regulation;  
d) to order the controller or processor to bring the processing operations into compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, when appropriate, in a specific manner and within a specified time;  
i) to impose an administrative fine in accordance with Article 83, in addition to or instead of the measures mentioned in this paragraph, depending on the circumstances of each particular case;  
Furthermore, Article 72.1 b) of the LOPDGDD indicates that “based on what is established in Article 83.5 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following infringements shall be considered very serious and shall be subject to a three-year limitation period, which involve a substantial breach of the articles mentioned therein and in particular, the following:  
b) The processing of personal data without any of the conditions for lawfulness of processing in Article 6 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”
