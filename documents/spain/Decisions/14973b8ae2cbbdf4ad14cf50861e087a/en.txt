From the procedure instructed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  

BACKGROUND  
FIRST: Mr. A.A.A. and Ms. B.B.B. (hereinafter, the complainant) filed a complaint with the Spanish Agency for Data Protection on November 18, 2021. The complaint is directed against Servicios de la Comarca de Pamplona, Sociedad Anónima with NIF A31118441 (hereinafter, SCPSA). The reasons for the complaint are as follows:  
The complainant states that SCPSA has established a system for opening waste containers through an electronic card. They have distributed in the municipal area of Pamplona an envelope containing a brochure with two attached electronic cards, as well as a sheet with "user" data and a password for a mobile application. Each card contains an address, which is that of the property to which the envelope is delivered to the occupant (whoever they may be, without a request for identification).  
In the information provided to the interested parties, it is indicated that the data on the opening of the containers by each of the cards will be processed to analyze the use of the containers.  
The complainant states that the regulatory basis (Regulatory Ordinance on Waste Management in the Pamplona Comarca) allows for the collection of data on the use of the containers and of one in relation to the other (organic and residual), but not for the collection of data on the use of each container by each person, which is completely different, since this latter collection involves the immediate knowledge of multiple data points for each user: behavioral habits, days when the residence is empty, number of occupants of the residence, or personal differences regarding diet or the use of items that must go into the residual fraction container.  
The complainant further indicates that the association of the card to the interested parties derives from the linkage between the card and the personal data of the holder or owner of the property, with the aforementioned data obtained from the Territorial Wealth Registry of Navarra.  
The complainant concludes that the collection of data from the owners violates Article 5 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation) (hereinafter, GDPR), as it is not necessary to process the data of the owners, nor the data on the use of the containers by each owner, to achieve the objectives.

We must emphasize, beforehand, that while in the private sphere one can do anything that is not prohibited, in the public sphere only that which is permitted by the legal system can be carried out; this implies that public administrations can only do what the legal system expressly allows them to do, and they cannot rely, under the pretext of the silence of the norm, on the notion that something can be done because nothing in the regulatory provision indicates otherwise.  
As indicated in report 2018-0175 from the Legal Cabinet of the AEPD 175/2018, “the Administration is bound by the principle of legality, so that, unlike individuals, it can only carry out what the legal system expressly permits. This is the meaning of the provisions in articles 9.1 and 103 of the Constitution, such that when the law and the rights have not attributed to the Administration the corresponding powers to act in a certain situation, that action cannot be carried out without prior attribution of such powers by the legal system. Therefore, there is no empty space where, in the absence of law, the Administration can act. This is what has been termed the doctrine of the positive binding of the Administration to legality (García de Enterría). Consequently, for the Administration to act, it requires prior legal authorization (understood here as normative authorization).”  
In the present case, the norm does not foresee the treatment of any personal data, which the responding party labels as an omission. However, as we have made explicit, MPC can only do what the ordinance allows it to do, and the “omission” of the regulator cannot be supplanted by the mere will of MPC.

- That other specific provisions regarding data protection may be determined in such regulation with the rank of law, although it is not mandatory.  

In the area of waste management, the aforementioned state legislation on environmental matters has established that local entities must meet objectives regarding the separation at source of waste, while allowing them to determine the means to achieve this, means that must be specified and detailed in the corresponding local ordinances. That is, the Law only determines the ends, enabling local entities, through ordinances (the last level of regulatory specification), to determine the means. In this case, the ordinance should have necessarily included the processing of personal data and not just the processing of data from the containers.  

This implies, in the examined case, that although there is a prior legal authorization, this Law expressly enables a lower-ranking legal norm to contain specific provisions developing the competence conferred by the Law. Such specification, which must be found, in this case, in a local ordinance, should have been carried out by the regulator in whatever manner it deemed appropriate, including or not including data protection issues, in which case it would be under the terms of Article 6.3 of the GDPR. As can be seen, in the case examined in the present sanctioning procedure, the regulator has not provided for any processing of personal data as authorized by Article 17.2 of Law 22/2011, of July 28, and currently authorized by Article 20.3 of the LRSCEC.  

Furthermore, such determination of the means cannot be carried out in any manner regarding data protection, but rather with similar requirements to those provided in Article 6.3 of the GDPR for the Law, as it is an extension of it. The ordinance being the last normative specification instrument in which all means must be provided, including for these purposes the processing of personal data (by virtue of Article 6.3 of the GDPR and Article 5.1.a) of the principle of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency of the GDPR), MCP is bound to its strict content. The Regulatory Ordinance for Waste Management of MCP only contemplates the collection of data from the containers. It does not contemplate the collection of personal data. It does not contemplate the collection of data on the use made of the different containers by each household. No processing of personal data is provided for in the ordinance of MCP. Therefore, as we have pointed out previously, it cannot carry out what MCP itself has not normatively provided regarding the processing of personal data.

Recital 39 of the GDPR states that “All processing of personal data must be lawful and fair. For its part, the Judgment of the Constitutional Court 292/2000, of November 30, states that “the content of the fundamental right to data protection consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that empowers the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which data this third party may collect, and it also allows the individual to know who holds that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose such possession or use. These powers of disposition and control over personal data, which are part of the content of the fundamental right to data protection, are legally specified in the ability to consent to the collection, obtaining, and access to personal data, its subsequent storage and processing, as well as its possible use or uses, by a third party, whether the State or a private individual. And this right to consent to the knowledge and processing, whether computerized or not, of personal data requires as indispensable complements, on the one hand, the ability to know at all times who has that personal data and to what use it is being subjected, and, on the other hand, the power to oppose such possession and uses.” (the emphasis is ours).

11. If necessary, the controller shall examine whether the processing is in compliance with the data protection impact assessment, at least when there is a change in the risk posed by the processing operations.”  
As indicated in the WP 248 Guidelines by the Article 29 Working Party on data protection impact assessment (DPIA) and to determine whether the processing “likely results in a high risk” for the purposes of the GDPR, “The words ‘in particular’ in the introductory sentence of Article 35(3) of the GDPR refer to a non-exhaustive list. There may be ‘high risk’ processing operations that are not included in this list but that pose similarly elevated risks. These processing operations must also undergo a DPIA. For this reason, the criteria developed below sometimes go beyond a simple explanation of what should be understood from the three examples provided in Article 35(3) of the GDPR.  
In order to provide a more concrete set of processing operations that require a DPIA due to their inherent high risk, taking into account the particular elements of Article 35(1) and Article 35(3)(a) to (c), the list to be adopted at the national level under Article 35(4), and recitals 71, 75, and 91, as well as other references in the GDPR to processing operations that “likely result in a high risk,” the following nine criteria must be considered:”  
“3. Systematic observation: processing used to observe, monitor, and control data subjects, including data collected through networks or ‘systematic observation [...] of a publicly accessible area’ [Article 35(3)(c)].”  
Through the use of electronic cards and the mobile application for opening containers, there is the possibility to observe, monitor, and control how many times, when, and where the different containers (organic waste or residual waste) are opened by the service users.  
“5. Large-scale data processing: the GDPR does not define what is meant by large scale, although recital 91 offers some guidance. In any case, the GT29 recommends that the following factors be taken into account, in particular, when determining whether the processing is carried out on a large scale:  
a) the number of data subjects affected, either as a specific figure or as a proportion of the relevant population;

b) the volume of data or the variety of distinct data elements being processed;

“8. Innovative use or application of new technological or organizational solutions, such as combining the use of fingerprint and facial recognition to improve physical access control, etc.”

In this line, it should be indicated that interested parties must use the electronic cards or the mobile application to open the automatic containers if they wish to deposit their waste, as established in the Regulatory Ordinance on waste management in the MCP.

IN CONCLUSION:  
The respondent has been implementing, since autumn 2021, a system for opening waste containers equipped with electronic locks that correspond to Access Control Equipment (ECA) for such containers, activated by a card linked to a specific postal address, a transport card activated for the opening of containers, or a mobile application linked to a specific postal address.  
Although the electronic cards for opening waste containers were initially distributed by mail, subsequently individuals have been able to:  
1. Request a new card (in cases of loss, malfunction, breakage, change of address),  
2. Request the activation of the transport card for the opening of waste containers,  
3. Request credentials to activate the mobile application for opening waste containers.  
In these cases, in addition to processing the personal data of the postal address, new personal data has been processed derived from the collection and registration of the submission of the aforementioned requests: ID number, first name and last name, email address, phone number, postal address, transport card ID, signature.  
The processing carried out, and currently being carried out, by the respondent has infringed the following articles of the GDPR:  
1. Article 6.1 of the GDPR since there is no legitimizing cause that supports the processing of personal data subject to the sanctioning file.  
At the time of filing the complaint with the AEPD, the processing was not lawful because:  
1. The processing of personal data carried out by the respondent was not lawful solely for the achievement of the objectives of waste separation at source established by the regulations, since:  
a) The processing cannot be considered based on compliance with a legal obligation enforceable against the data controller because Law 22/2011, of July 28, on waste and contaminated soils, does not require it to carry out any processing of personal data to exercise its competencies in the collection, transport, and treatment of domestic waste.  
b) The processing also cannot be considered based on a mission carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred to the data controller. The Regulatory Ordinance on waste management of MCP, the last level of regulatory specification that Law 22/2011, of July 28, on waste and contaminated soils, enabled to determine the means of how to carry out such waste management, does not provide for the collection of personal data for the use made of the different containers by each household.

In the first case, we are talking about the information that must be provided when personal data has not been obtained from the data subject (Article 14 of the GDPR), while in the second case we must refer to the information that must be provided when personal data is obtained from the data subject (Article 13 of the GDPR).  
In both cases, the information provided was incomplete.

4.- Article 35 of the GDPR.  
The responding party submitted, along with its allegations to the proposed resolution, the data protection impact assessment of the project "Waste Card and Other Devices" dated June 20, 2023, in which the necessity analysis determines the obligation to carry out a DPIA.  
Since the circumstances of the aforementioned personal data processing have not changed since its inception, the responding party should have conducted such a DPIA before commencing the processing of personal data.  
Finally, it is necessary to highlight that the responding party, throughout the entire procedure, has attempted to comply with some of its obligations regarding data protection by presenting documentation prepared ad hoc (in response to the infringements highlighted by the AEPD), in an attempt to justify the adequacy of personal data processing to the regulations. Similarly, it has repeatedly expressed a series of commitments to comply with the GDPR without demonstrating any actual compliance.  
Moreover, in its attempt to justify the legitimacy of the processing, it has incurred numerous, continuous, and flagrant contradictions regarding the same, such as when it states that the processing of the postal address is not essential and yet processes it, or when it states that the postal address data cannot determine who throws the waste into a container and seeks to exercise inspection and sanctioning authority.  
Regarding the above, we must emphasize that compliance with data protection regulations does not consist of commitments, but in the effective, timely, and proper fulfillment of the obligations imposed by the GDPR on data controllers. Let us not forget that a fundamental right of citizens is at stake, which Public Administrations are obliged to guarantee.  
Many of these obligations, such as the preparation of the record of processing activities, the preparation of the information that must be provided to data subjects, or the conduct of the DPIA, should have been fulfilled by the responding party before effectively launching the processing of personal data. Doing so afterwards does not respond to the principle of proactive responsibility regulated in Article 5.2 of the GDPR nor to privacy by design regulated in Article 25.1 of the GDPR.
