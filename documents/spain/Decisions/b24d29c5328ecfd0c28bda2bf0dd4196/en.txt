LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
By virtue of the powers that Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR) recognizes to each supervisory authority and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, insofar as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  
II  
The voice as personal data

A person's voice, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is personal data as it makes them identifiable, and its protection is therefore subject to the GDPR:  
“‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person;”  
The voice is a personal attribute unique to each individual, defined by its pitch, intensity, and timbre. Endowed with unique and distinctive traits that directly individualize it, associating it with a specific individual, it is shaped while speaking, allowing one to infer the individual's age, sex, health status, personality, culture, origin, hormonal, emotional, and psychological state. Elements of expression, idiolect, or intonation are also considered personal data when considered together with the voice.  
Therefore, the report 139/2017 from the Legal Office of this Agency states that “the image as well as the voice of a person is personal data, just as any information that allows determining, directly or indirectly, their identity will be.”  
In fact, the ruling of the National Court dated March 19, 2014 (rec. 176/2012) states that “a person's voice constitutes personal data, as deduced from the definition provided by Article 3.a) of the LOPD, as ‘any information concerning identified or identifiable natural persons,’ a matter that is not controversial.”  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines ‘processing’ as: “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”  
The inclusion of a person's voice in journalistic publications, which identifies or makes a person identifiable, constitutes processing of personal data and, therefore, the data controller carrying out such processing is obliged to comply with the obligations established for data controllers in the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.

This was a highly publicized case. The voice of the victim was clearly heard as she recounted in graphic detail the multiple rapes she suffered. All of this constitutes the processing of the victim's personal data.  
Individuals have the power to dispose of their personal data, including their voice, as well as its dissemination, and undoubtedly, a person whose personal data is disseminated in violation of the legal framework deserves protection.  
Thus, the Constitutional Court ruling STC 292/2000, of November 30, states that "the content of the fundamental right to data protection consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that enables the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose such possession or use. These powers of disposition and control over personal data, which are part of the content of the fundamental right to data protection, are legally specified in the authority to consent to the collection, obtaining, and access to personal data, its subsequent storage and processing, as well as its possible use or uses by a third party, whether the State or a private individual. And this right to consent to the knowledge and processing, whether computerized or not, of personal data requires as indispensable complements, on the one hand, the authority to know at all times who has that personal data and what use they are subjecting it to, and, on the other hand, the power to oppose such possession and uses."  
In this regard, and regardless of the legal basis legitimizing the processing, every data controller must respect the principles of processing set out in Article 5 of the GDPR. We will highlight Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, which states that:  
“1. Personal data shall be  
c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’);”  
However, we are faced with a fundamental right that is not absolute, since, in certain cases, the Fundamental Right to Data Protection may yield to the prevalence of other rights and freedoms that are also constitutionally recognized and protected, such as, for example, the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information, weighing this on a case-by-case basis.  
However, in the present case, as we will explain, it must be considered that the processing carried out by the responding party within the framework of freedom of information has been excessive, as there is no prevailing public interest in the dissemination of the victim's voice—without adding any value to the information by maintaining the victim's real voice (without distortion, for example)—under which pretext it seems that such data has been disclosed; a voice that, combined with the fact that this is a highly publicized case, makes the victim clearly identifiable. When weighing the conflicting interests and considering the concurrent circumstances of this case, namely, the particularly sensitive nature of the personal data and the intense impact on the victim's privacy, the interest of the holder of the right to the protection of her personal data and to not have it disseminated deserves greater protection than the purported public interest in its dissemination.  
IV  
Right to Information  
In the conflict between the Fundamental Rights to Freedom of Information and the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, even though equal degrees of protection are recognized for both constitutional rights, the former is usually granted precedence by our courts after evaluating and weighing all the elements at play.  
However, preponderance does not mean prevalence when, considering all the concurrent circumstances in a specific case, the limits established by law and jurisprudence are exceeded.  
In this sense, the Article 29 Working Party in its Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC, when examining the legal basis of legitimate interest under Article 7.1.f) of Directive 95/46/EC, fully translatable to the current Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR, includes the right to freedom of expression or information as one of the scenarios in which the issue of legitimate interest may arise, asserting that "without prejudice to whether the interests of the data controller will ultimately prevail over the interests and rights of the data subjects when the balancing test is carried out."

Balance between the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information and the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data.  
In the specific case examined, as we have indicated, the party being claimed published, on the websites referred to in the facts, the audio of a statement made before the judge by a victim of a multiple rape, to illustrate the news of a highly publicized case.  
Thus, it is not a matter, as in other jurisprudentially examined cases, of giving precedence to one fundamental right over another, having to choose which one carries more weight in a specific case. Rather, it is about finding a balance between both in order to achieve the purpose of the former without distorting the latter. The reconciliation of both rights is nothing new, since the European legislator mandates such reconciliation in Article 85 of the GDPR.  
As we have seen previously, the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information is not unlimited, since the jurisprudential interpretation when confronting it with other rights and freedoms does not allow it in every case and to every extent, but rather, the prevalence that courts usually grant it can be limited by other fundamental rights that must also be respected. This limitation is observed when the personal data provided was unnecessary for the essence of the content of the information.  
We must consider the special circumstances present in the examined case. It involves a very young woman who has suffered a multiple rape. In the published recording, she can be heard recounting, with great emotional weight, the sexual assault suffered in all its harshness, narrating (...).  
Furthermore, we cannot lose sight of the victim's condition, whose voice, with all the nuances expressed, has been disseminated.  
Let us recall, for merely illustrative purposes, that Law 4/2015, of April 27, on the Statute of the Victim of Crime provides for a special need for protection for victims of crimes against sexual freedom or sexual integrity, as well as victims of violent crimes, both circumstances that apply in the examined case.  
In this case, the situation of the victim (who is not on the same level of equality as the accused) must be considered, as well as what the dissemination of her voice with all its nuances implies, along with the special protection that the legal system must provide her, which, without constraining the provision of information, must be compatible with the principle of data minimization, applicable to the manner, the medium in which the information is provided and disseminated due to the immediate impact on the personal data and the identification of the victim.  
Precisely because the evident public interest in the news is not denied, given the general interest in criminal cases, in this specific case, it is not a matter of diminishing the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information by the prevalence of the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, but rather of

Furthermore, we must emphasize that the victim is an anonymous person, and our Constitutional Court, in STC 58/2018 of June 4, states that public authorities, public officials, and public figures or those engaged in activities that involve public notoriety "voluntarily accept the risk that their subjective personal rights may be affected by criticism, opinions, or adverse revelations and, therefore, the right to information reaches, in relation to them, its maximum level of legitimizing effectiveness, in that their life and moral conduct participate in the general interest with greater intensity than that of private individuals who, without a vocation for public projection, are circumstantially involved in matters of public significance, to whom, consequently, a higher sphere of privacy must be recognized, which prevents granting general significance to facts or conduct that would have it if referred to public figures."  
The CJEU (Second Chamber) ruling of February 14, 2019, in case C 345/17, Sergejs Buivids, mentions various criteria for weighing the right to respect for privacy against the right to freedom of expression, among which are "the contribution to a debate of general interest, the notoriety of the affected person, the subject of the report, the previous behavior of the interested party, the content, the form and the repercussions of the publication, the manner and circumstances in which the information was obtained and its veracity (see, in this sense, the judgment of the ECHR of June 27, 2017, Satakunnan Markkinapörssi Oy and Satamedia Oy v. Thus, for a matter to be considered of general interest, of public relevance, it must be so not only because of the person involved but also because of the subject matter to which it refers. Both requirements must be met, resulting, further to what was stated in the previous section, that in the case examined, the victim is not a public person; rather, it is of great interest that they are not recognized by third parties, as this could imply a new penalty to that already suffered. The victim is an anonymous person and must remain so, in such a way that their fundamental rights are fully guaranteed.  
In the present case, (i) we are neither dealing with a person of public relevance, in the sense that such relevance is sufficient to understand that it entails, ex lege, a dispossession of their fundamental right to the protection of their personal data, and (ii) while we are dealing with "facts of public relevance," in the sense that they are revealed as "necessary" for the exposition of ideas or opinions of public interest, that necessity does not extend to providing data that identifies the victim.

VII  
Every data controller has obligations regarding data protection, as prescribed in the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, highlighting, in terms of our interest, the proactive responsibility, Article 5.2 of the GDPR, the assessment of risks, and the implementation of appropriate security measures. Obligations that are even more relevant when, as in the case we are examining, it is particularly sensitive.  
Such obligations do not diminish because we are dealing with a data controller that is a media outlet.  
If we combine the dissemination of the victim's voice (with all its nuances), which makes them identifiable and potentially recognizable by third parties, with the factual account they provide regarding the violation suffered, there is a very high and probable risk that they may suffer harm to their rights and freedoms. This has occurred in other cases of dissemination of personal data of victims of rape. Moreover, with the dissemination of the victim's voice, they are being condemned again to be recognized by third parties, which is neither a proportional nor necessary treatment in relation to the information purposes pursued.  
It is extremely significant that, in the examined case, the respondent party immediately withdrew the recording of the hearing in which the victim's voice was disseminated at the request of the AEPD in two cases and distorted the victim's voice in the third, notwithstanding which the information remains available and continues to be provided in all its breadth. This demonstrates that to provide this specific information, it was not necessary, in the terms of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, to disseminate the victim's voice.
