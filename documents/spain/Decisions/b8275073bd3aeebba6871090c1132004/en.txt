The physical image of a person, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is a personal data and its protection, therefore, is subject to this Regulation. Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines the concept of "processing" of personal data.  
Images generated by a system of cameras or camcorders are personal data, so their processing is subject to data protection regulations.  
It is, therefore, pertinent to analyze whether the processing of personal data (image of individuals) carried out through the reported video surveillance system is in accordance with the provisions of the GDPR.  
III  
Article 6.1 of the GDPR establishes the circumstances that allow the processing of personal data to be considered lawful, indicating the following:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child. The provisions of point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.  
Regarding processing for the purposes of video surveillance, Article 22 of the LOPDGDD establishes that natural or legal persons, public or private, may carry out the processing of images through camera or camcorder systems for the purpose of ensuring the safety of persons and property, as well as their facilities.”

In the present case, the uploading of sexual and personal content defaming the claimant on YouTube at the link ***URL.1.. has been confirmed.  
The defendant, in their defense, alleges that the images in question are the result of the materialization of a FICTION project carried out by mutual agreement between the claimant and the current defendant for the recording of videos about their relationship and their sailing trips, with the aim of editing and publishing them.  
Furthermore, it states that although the uploading of images with sexual content and for defamation has been processed in the Court of Violence Against Women in Marbella, it has been agreed to archive the case.  
In this regard, this Agency must indicate that the order XXX/20XX of April 10, 2023, issued by the Court of Violence Against Women No. 1 of Marbella in its first legal reasoning establishes the following:  
"From the proceedings, in particular, the statements of both parties, along with the videographic documentation, it is evident that Ms. A.A.A. and Mr. B.B.B., being a couple, mutually agreed to record videos about their relationship and their sailing trips, with the aim of editing and publishing them. In 2022, after their sentimental relationship had ended, Ms. A.A.A. carried out the editing and publication of some videos, the content of which is not included in the records. Mr. B.B.B. did the same, editing and publishing a video, about four minutes long, narrating a fictional story based on the same audiovisual material. However, once she became aware of and viewed what he had published, she expressed her disapproval of the publication, as she could be recognized, which led Mr. B.B.B. to retouch the editing and pixelate Ms. A.A.A.'s face, maintaining the publication.  
Based on the facts succinctly expressed, it is not considered that they fit within the crime of disclosure of secrets under Article 197.2 of the Penal Code, nor in the crime against honor under Article 208 of the Penal Code. In the first case, the essential requirements of being in the presence of secret or intimate material, as well as revealing it without consent, are lacking, since this is implicit in the agreement for recording and publication. In fact, Ms. A.A.A. acknowledged in her statement that she had carried out the publication of material recorded in the same project. In the second case, the subjective element of the crime is not present, which is the intent to offend or injure, since it is a fictional story in which both are the characters.  
In summary, the perpetration of any of the crimes that have given rise to the formation of the case is not adequately justified, so in accordance with the provisions of Article 641-1º, in relation to Article 779.1-1º of the Criminal Procedure Law, it is appropriate to decree the provisional dismissal of the proceedings. The above will be carried out with the reservation of civil actions in favor of Ms. A.A.A., under the protection of Organic Law 1/1982, on the protection of the right to honor, intimacy, and one's own image."  
Therefore, despite the archiving of the complaint filed with the Court of Violence Against Women No. 1 of Marbella, it must be taken into account that although said complaint is archived for not meeting the requirements to incur in a crime of disclosure of secrets or against honor in accordance with Article 197.2 and 208.

of the Penal Code, such filing is carried out, “with reservation of civil actions in favor of Mrs. A.A.A., under the Organic Law 1/1982, for the protection of the right to honor, privacy, and one's own image.” It should be noted that the existence of a filing of the complaint submitted to the Court of Violence against Women number 1 of Marbella does not imply that one cannot be sanctioned for the possible violation of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, since the judge has assessed from a criminal perspective whether the necessary elements to incur a crime are present, while the AEPD protects a different legal interest, evaluating whether the protection of personal data has been violated.  
Furthermore, it must be taken into account that the order itself highlights the lack of subsequent consent from the complainant in the following manner: “once she became aware of and viewed what was published by him, she expressed her disagreement with the publication, as she could be recognized, which led Mr. B.B.B. to edit and pixelate the face of Mrs. A.A.A., maintaining the publication.”  
In this regard, it should be noted that, even if there was a prior agreement between the parties for the dissemination of the images, the complainant has the right at any time to revoke her consent in accordance with Article 7.3 of the GDPR, which regulates the conditions for consent and states the following:  
“The data subject shall have the right to withdraw his or her consent at any time. The withdrawal of consent shall not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal. Before giving consent, the data subject shall be informed thereof. It shall be as easy to withdraw consent as to give it.”  
Therefore, upon revoking her consent for the dissemination of the video, and the respondent knowing that she has withdrawn her consent, the respondent must cease its dissemination, as it is not sufficient to have pixelated the complainant's face, since in that video not only her face is disclosed but also her naked body is visible and her voice is heard.  
By not ceasing the dissemination of the video despite the revocation of the complainant's consent, and knowing that the complainant has withdrawn her consent, it implies that the respondent has processed the complainant's personal data (her body and her voice) that would allow for her identification, without legitimate grounds for doing so, since the complainant has revoked the consent she had given to the respondent for that purpose, as noted in the order issued by the Court of Violence against Women of Marbella in its first legal reasoning, transcribed in this legal basis.  
In light of these facts, it is considered that the actions described, that is, posting on the internet address ***URL.1 a video in which, although the complainant's face is pixelated, her body can be seen and her voice can be heard, without the complainant's consent, taking advantage of its sexual content to defame the complainant, violates what is established in Article 6 of the GDPR, indicated in legal basis III, which could constitute the commission of an infringement classified in Article 83.5 of the GDPR, which states the following:
