RESOLUTION OF SANCTIONING PROCEDURE  
From the procedure conducted by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  

BACKGROUND  
FIRST: A.A.A. (hereinafter, the complainant) filed a complaint on December 29, 2020, with the data protection authority of Saxony, Saxon Data Protection Commissioner (hereinafter, SA Saxon). The complaint is directed against THE MAIL TRACK COMPANY, S.L. with NIF B66095670 (hereinafter MAILTRACK). The grounds for the complaint are as follows:  
The company offers free software for tracking emails so that the sender of an email is informed through the program that the recipient has opened it and when. Additionally, such emails provide a link to the homepage of mailtrack.io. If the recipient forwards the email, elements are inserted within the email, which could transfer their data to third parties.  
The complainant has received several emails that implement MAILTRACK software and believes that the sender, with the help of MAILTRACK software, transfers data to third parties without their consent. Furthermore, they consider that neither the link appearing in the email nor MAILTRACK's privacy policy sufficiently informs about what data is transferred to the sender of the email nor about the processing carried out on the data.

VIII  
Legality of Processing  
Regarding the collection of personal data from users and recipients by MAILTRACK for the provision of the service in question, Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of July 12, 2002, on the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (Directive on Privacy and Electronic Communications), hereinafter "ePrivacy Directive", provides in its Article 5.3:  
“‘3. Member States shall ensure that the storage of information, or the access to information already stored, in the terminal equipment of a subscriber or user is only allowed on condition that the subscriber or user has given his or her consent after having been provided with clear and comprehensive information, in particular regarding the purposes of the processing of the data, in accordance with the provisions of Directive 95/46/EC. This shall not prevent the storage of or access to information for the sole purpose of carrying out the transmission of a communication over an electronic communications network, or to the extent strictly necessary in order for the provider of a service of the information society to provide a service explicitly requested by the subscriber or user.’”  
By virtue of the principle of normative specialty, the special law must prevail over the general law regarding those issues specifically regulated by the special law. Therefore, Directive 2002/58 must prevail over the GDPR, as it is a special law, regarding the matters it regulates.  
On this issue, Recital 173 of the GDPR states that "This Regulation should apply to all matters related to the protection of the rights and freedoms of individuals in relation to the processing of personal data that are not subject to specific obligations with the same objective established in Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, including the obligations of the data controller and the rights of natural persons (...)."

It should also be noted that Article 95 of the GDPR specifies that “This Regulation shall not impose additional obligations on natural or legal persons regarding processing in the context of the provision of publicly available electronic communications services in public communication networks of the Union in areas where they are subject to specific obligations with the same objective established in Directive 2002/58/EC.”  
Therefore, with respect to the storage of information and access to already stored personal data through the use of the web beacon (tracking and information retrieval device) used by MAILTRACK for the tracking service it provides, the GDPR would not apply but rather the aforementioned “ePrivacy Directive,” which has been partially transposed into Spanish law by the LSSI, so the one-stop-shop system provided for by Article 60 of the GDPR could not be used and could not be subject to this sanctioning procedure.  
However, the GDPR would apply to subsequent processing of the personal data thus obtained. There is no doubt that MAILTRACK processes the data for the purposes of the service, primarily the tracking of the opening of emails sent by its users and their communication to the users, and that regarding these purposes it acts as the data controller as the instructions of the users are not defined and it would be disproportionate to shift the responsibility to individuals covered by the domestic exception.  
The legal status of MAILTRACK as a “controller” or “processor” must, in principle, be determined by its actual activities in a specific situation, rather than by the formal designation made in its privacy policy. According to the factual approach, the word “determines” means that the entity that actually influences the purposes and means of processing is the data controller. If one of the parties actually decides why and how personal data is processed, that party will be a controller even if a contract states that it is a processor.  
Typically, a processor agreement establishes who the decision-making party (controller) and the instructed party (processor) are. If there is no reason to doubt that this accurately reflects reality, there is nothing against following the terms of the contract. However, the mere use of the MAILTRACK service grants the status of data controller to all its users. Thus, any user of MAILTRACK would be responsible, including individuals, which makes it clear that this circumstance is not in line with the intention of the GDPR.  
While the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union indicates that it is irrelevant whether the administrator of a website has access to the data collected by Facebook (see CJEU C-40/17, paragraph 82), this circumstance would not apply in this case, as established in paragraph 3.2 of the MAILTRACK Privacy Policy, which states that it has effective access to personal data of the recipients of the emails.  
In this regard, Article 6, “Lawfulness of processing,” of the GDPR provides:

XII  
Principles relating to processing  
Article 5 “Principles relating to processing” of the GDPR states:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject (‘lawfulness, fairness, and transparency’); (…)”  
In this case, in the “Terms of Use and Contracting” of MAILTRACK, in its paragraph 1.1, in its last paragraph, it states: “Regarding the collection and processing of personal data, the Privacy Policy and the Cookie Policy will apply.” Therefore, the Privacy Policy is a legal document that, together with the Terms of Use and Contracting, binds MAILTRACK regarding the processing of data it carries out of its users and of the recipients of the emails sent using the tool. Point 6 of the Privacy Policy of MAILTRACK establishes the method of opting out of its service concerning the recipients of the emails whose personal data are subject to processing:  
“6. Exclusion from the Mailtrack Service  
6.1. The recipient of an email sent by the User of the Service may be excluded from the tracking carried out by the User if the recipient selects the ‘Opt-out’ checkbox available in the ‘User Privacy’ section.  
6.2. Due to technical limitations, the only way to exclude the recipient from the Service involves the installation of a cookie from the website mailtrack.io. Therefore, if the recipient proceeds to delete the cookies from their browser, the recipient will once again be included in the Service.  
6.3. The mentioned cookie will only function in the browser used by the recipient to select the exclusion. Therefore, if a different browser is used, the recipient will have to carry out the exclusion process in that browser.”

Due to the technical limitations outlined in clause 6 of the MAILTRACK Privacy Policy, the use of the service by the user entails, in any case, the necessary activity for the processing of the recipient's data, with the latter having to take the necessary actions to ensure that their data is not subject to processing. This is contrary to the GDPR, as it is not the data subject who should protect themselves against specific data processing; rather, the entity performing that processing must carry out the appropriate actions to terminate such processing.  
Furthermore, it should be noted that Article 5 of the GDPR requires that the processing of personal data be transparent, and in this regard, Article 12 of the GDPR states that “…all information referred to in Articles 13 and 14, as well as any communication pursuant to Articles 15 to 22 and 34 related to the processing, shall be provided in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language…”  
Well, the tool for opting out of the processing of the recipient's email data is not easily accessible, as:  
- Access to the “User Privacy” section is not available on the website https://mailtrack.io/es/  
- There is no link in the MAILTRACK Privacy Policy itself.  
- Performing a search for “the mail track company user privacy” in an internet search engine does not yield a direct result.  
Finally, according to the information available on the website “***URL.22”, the limitation on data processing occurs by disabling images in the recipient's email.  
Therefore, in this case, the privacy policy indicates that recipients of emails can be excluded from the tracking carried out by MAILTRACK if they select the “Opt-out” checkbox supposedly available in the “User Privacy” section, but this statement is misleading, as such a checkbox is not available on the MAILTRACK website.  
Additionally, the privacy policy also states that the only way for recipients to be excluded from the service provided by MAILTRACK is by installing a specific cookie, which must be reinstalled each time the cookies of the relevant browser are deleted, and which must be installed in all browsers used.  
The other option that a MAILTRACK user would have, but which is also not mentioned in the privacy policy of the aforementioned website but is explained in a section of articles about the tool, would be to disable images in the recipient's email.  
The processing as carried out would violate the principles of fairness and transparency of the GDPR, as the Guidelines 4/2019 regarding Article 25 Data Protection by Design and by Default Version 2.0, in its section 3.3, establish that:

“Loyalty is a general principle that requires that personal data not be processed in a manner that is unjustifiably detrimental, unlawfully discriminatory, unexpected, or misleading to the data subject.”
