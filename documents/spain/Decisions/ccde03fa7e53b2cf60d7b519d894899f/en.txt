This requirement of culpability in the field of administrative offenses is reiterated ad nauseam by the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court.

Administrative responsibility requires not only that the conduct be unlawful and typical, but also that it be culpable, that is, a consequence of an action or omission attributable to its author due to malice or recklessness, negligence, or inexcusable ignorance (...) that is to say, as a requirement derived from Article 25.1 of the Constitution, no one can be condemned or sanctioned except for acts that can be attributed to them by way of intent or fault (principle of culpability).  
Consequently, culpability must be assessed, in administrative offenses, based on the voluntariness of the infringing subject in the unlawful action or omission. Therefore, the principle of culpability constitutes a basic element when qualifying a person's conduct as sanctionable; that is, it is an essential element in any administrative offense (Supreme Court rulings, among others, of February 28, 1996, and July 6, 1995).  
In summary, and in the words of the Supreme Court, culpability must be as demonstrated as the active or omissive conduct that is sanctioned.  
In this context, Article 28 of Law 40/2015 on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector states: “Only individuals and legal entities... who are responsible for acts constituting an administrative offense can be sanctioned.”

Second: Regarding the application in this case of the "principle of culpability" alleged by the respondent in point "two" of their writing, it should be noted that it is true that this principle also governs in matters of administrative infractions, as declared repeatedly by the Constitutional Court (TC), and that objective or no-fault liability is not applicable in the administrative sanctioning sphere, whereby the possibility of imposing sanctions solely based on the result is excluded, without demonstrating a minimum level of culpability even in terms of mere negligence (SSTC 76/1990 and 164/2005), requiring that the imposition of the sanction be based on the requirement of the subjective element of fault, to guarantee the principle of responsibility and the right to a sanctioning procedure with all guarantees (STC 129/2003, of June 20, and STS of March 1, 2012, Rec 1298/2009).  
Certainly, the principle of culpability, provided for in Article 130.1 of Law 30/1992, of November 26, on the Legal Regime of Public Administrations and the Common Administrative Procedure, states that only those responsible for acts constituting administrative infractions can be sanctioned, even in terms of simple non-compliance. Obviously, this implies that such responsibility can only be demanded in terms of intent or fault, excluding from the realm of sanctioning administrative law the so-called "objective liability," and understanding the culpable title to include recklessness, negligence, or inexcusable ignorance.  
This "simple non-compliance" cannot therefore be understood as the admission in sanctioning administrative law of objective liability, as the majority jurisprudence of our Supreme Court (starting from its rulings of January 24 and 25 and May 9, 1983) and the doctrine of the Constitutional Court (after its STC 76/1990) highlight that the principle of culpability, even without explicit recognition in the Constitution, is inferred from the principles of legality and prohibition of excess (Article 25.1 CE), or from the very requirements inherent to a Rule of Law, thus requiring the existence of intent or fault (in this sense, STS of January 21, 2011, Rec 598/2008).  
However, the way of attributing responsibility to legal entities does not correspond with the forms of culpability that are attributable to human conduct, whether intentional or negligent. Thus, in the case of infractions committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present, it necessarily applies differently than it does with natural persons. The capacity for infringement and, therefore, direct reproachability that derives from the legal good protected by the norm that is infringed and the necessity for that protection to be truly effective, and the risk that, consequently, must be assumed by the legal entity that is subject to compliance with that norm."  
(In this sense, see STS of November 24, 2011, Rec 258/2009).  
To the above, it should be added, following the judgment of January 23, 1998, partially transcribed in the SSTS of October 9, 2009, Rec 5285/2005, and of October 23, 2010, Rec 1067/2006, that:  
"although the culpability of the conduct must also be subject to proof, it must be considered in terms of the assumption of the corresponding burden, that ordinarily the volitional and cognitive elements necessary to assess it form part of the proven typical conduct, and that their exclusion requires that the absence of such elements be accredited, or in its normative aspect, that the diligence that was required by the one who claims its non-existence has been employed; it is not enough, in summary, for exculpation against a typically unlawful behavior to invoke the absence of fault."  
In the case at hand, the existence of unlawfulness and culpability in the infringing conduct of the entity responsible for the processing of personal data is evident, as the data controller decides on the purpose, content, and use, and therefore has the obligation to act with greater diligence when providing the necessary information to the user when collecting their personal data. This condition imposes a special duty of diligence when carrying out the processing of personal data, regarding compliance with the duties established by data protection legislation to guarantee the fundamental rights and public freedoms of individuals; SAN 392/2015, of November 17.  
In Judgment 292/2000, of November 30, the TC considers as an autonomous and independent right the power of disposition and control that a person has over their personal data, which enables them to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, or which this third party may collect, and which also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose it will be used, being able to oppose that possession or use if they so wish.  
Therefore, the conduct of the respondent is considered to respond to the title of fault, as a custodian of personal data and thus should have been especially diligent and careful in its processing. That is to say, from the perspective of culpability, we are faced with a surmountable error since, with the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures, the irregularity on the website under its ownership, providing incorrect information about the processing of personal data could have been easily avoided.

In this regard, Recital 74 of the GDPR states the following:  
The responsibility of the data controller must be established for any processing of personal data carried out by themselves or on their behalf. In particular, the controller must be obliged to implement appropriate and effective measures and must be able to demonstrate compliance of the processing activities with this Regulation, including the effectiveness of the measures. Such measures must take into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.  
Furthermore, Recital 79 states:  
The protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, as well as the responsibility of controllers and processors, including with respect to the supervision by the supervisory authorities and the measures adopted by them, requires a clear allocation of responsibilities under this Regulation, including cases where a controller determines the purposes and means of processing jointly with other controllers, or where processing is carried out on behalf of a controller.  
Therefore, in the case at hand, it is evident that, apart from the unlawful and typical conduct of the respondent, there is also culpability since, through actions or omissions attributable to the website owner, incorrect information was provided regarding the processing of personal data to users at the time they registered on their website.

III.  
Information that must be provided to the interested party  
Article 13 of the GDPR establishes the information that must be provided to the interested party when the data is collected directly from them:  
“1. When personal data relating to an interested party is obtained from them, the data controller, at the time of obtaining this data, shall provide: a) the identity and contact details of the controller and, where applicable, of their representative; b) the contact details of the data protection officer, where applicable; c) the purposes of the processing for which the personal data are intended and the legal basis for the processing; d) when the processing is based on Article 6, paragraph 1, letter f), the legitimate interests of the controller or a third party; e) the recipients or categories of recipients of the personal data, where applicable; f) where applicable, the intention of the controller to transfer personal data to a third country or international organization and the existence or absence of an adequacy decision by the Commission, or, in the case of transfers referred to in Articles 46 or 47 or Article 49, paragraph 1, second subparagraph, reference to the appropriate or suitable safeguards and the means to obtain a copy of these or the fact that they have been provided.

2. In addition to the information mentioned in section 1, the data controller shall provide the data subject, at the time when personal data is obtained, with the following necessary information to ensure fair and transparent data processing:

f) the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling, referred to in Article 22(1) and (4), and, at least in such cases, meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences of such processing for the data subject.  

In the case at hand, it was found that on the website https://www.motorpasion.com, personal data of users could be entered through the registration form available in the dropdown menu located at the top left. Before being able to submit the form and register, the option “_I have read and accepted the <<Privacy and Participation Policy>>” had to be clicked. It was also noted that at the time of collecting personal data from users through the form, no information regarding the actual data controller was provided anywhere on the form page, nor was there any other link or direct reference that redirected the user to the true “Privacy Policy” or “Legal Notice.”
