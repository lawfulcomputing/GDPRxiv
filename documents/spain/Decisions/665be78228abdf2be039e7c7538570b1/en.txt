RESOLUTION OF TERMINATION OF THE PROCEDURE DUE TO VOLUNTARY PAYMENT  
From the procedure instructed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  
BACKGROUND  
FIRST: On January 11, 2023, the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection agreed to initiate a sanctioning procedure against BBVA in order to impose fines totaling 1,640,000 euros, in accordance with the provisions of Articles 63 and 64 of the LPACAP, for the alleged commission of the following infractions:  
Three related to the specific case reported by the complainant:  
- For the alleged infringement of Article 6.1 of the GDPR classified under Article 83.5 of the GDPR, concerning the unauthorized contracting of products.  
- For the alleged infringement of Article 32 of the GDPR classified under Article 83.4 of the same Regulation, due to the lack of security measures regarding the processes of communication, inclusion, and maintenance in credit information systems of personal data.  
- For the alleged infringement of Article 6.1 of the GDPR classified under Article 83.5 of the same Regulation, concerning the incorporation of personal data into credit information systems.  
And two related to the manner in which the procedures implemented by BBVA were established and revealed through the information provided by the financial entity during the investigation:  
- For the alleged infringement of Article 25 of the GDPR classified under Article 83.4 of the same Regulation.  
- For the alleged infringement of Article 32 of the GDPR classified under Article 83.4 of the same Regulation, due to the lack of security measures regarding the procedures for contracting financial products.  
In the aforementioned initiation agreement, BBVA was informed that it had a period of ten days to submit allegations.  
SECOND: On February 10, 2023, a letter from BBVA was received by this Agency, in due time and form, in which, on the one hand, allegations were made regarding the initiation agreement concerning the alleged infringement of Article 25 of the GDPR and the alleged infringement of Article 32 of the GDPR due to lack of security measures regarding the procedures for contracting financial products, and, on the other hand, responsibility was accepted for the commission of the following infractions included in the initiation agreement related to the specific case reported to the Agency that led to the investigation conducted:  
- The infringement of Article 6.1 of the GDPR classified under Article 83.5 of the GDPR, concerning the unauthorized contracting of products.

Judicial Procedure Status  
The claiming party attaches the ordinary lawsuit against the defendant submitted on September 1, 2020, which contains the description of the facts.  
The defendant states the following regarding the judicial procedure opened and its responsibility in the identity theft suffered by the claiming party:  
"The claiming party argues that it did not give consent for the transactions carried out between April 10 and 13, 2020. However, we cannot consider Article 31 of the Royal Decree-Law on Payment Services applicable, which provides that 'in the event that an unauthorized payment transaction is executed, the payment service provider of the payer shall immediately refund the amount of the unauthorized transaction and, where appropriate, restore the payment account to the state that would have existed had the unauthorized payment transaction not been executed,' as the transactions were authenticated and accurately recorded and accounted for, and were not affected by a technical failure or any other deficiency.  
Article 30 of the Payment Services Law establishes that 'when a user of payment services denies having authorized a payment transaction already executed or claims that it was executed incorrectly, it shall be the responsibility of their payment service provider to demonstrate that the payment transaction was authenticated, accurately recorded, and accounted for, and that it was not affected by a technical failure or any other deficiency.  
For these purposes, within the framework of the judicial procedure processed by the Court of First Instance No. 10 of Barcelona (***PROCEDIMIENTO.1), the appropriateness of such a claim is being discussed, where, according to the evidence provided by the parties, it will be resolved whether there was a lack of diligence on the part of the claiming party in the custody of the security mechanisms provided by the Bank."

2. Security  
2.1. Introduction  
2.1.1. Concepts  
Below, we will outline some of the concepts that will be handled later in order to unify criteria in this matter: […]  
Identity Theft:  
Linked to the precautions regarding identification, there is identity theft. In most cases, observing the recommendations given regarding identifying false or manipulated IDs and conducting some verification through questions only known to the holder or a call to the customer's phone number listed in our database will be sufficient to unmask the majority of attempts.  
However, another aspect to consider is that, on some occasions, this action is carried out by abusing a hypothetical relationship of trust, for example, when a person presents themselves to carry out some operation with documentation belonging to another person without that person being present.  
It should be noted that cases of scams against direct relatives, manipulation of accounts of deceased relatives (in some cases for tax reasons), or the improper manipulation of accounts due to inheritances, etc., are not uncommon. If the bank does not operate correctly, it may incur serious liability, so these practices should not be carried out. It is also worth mentioning the use of impersonation to obtain information (account numbers, balances, etc.) that is then used to commit fraud through, for example, fraudulent withdrawals. Therefore, precautions regarding identification must be heightened when providing certain data. […]  
Transactions without the physical presence of the card.- In these cases (internet purchases and mail/telephone sales), it is the merchant who contractually assumes responsibility, except in the case of secure commerce with authentication of the holder. This does not exempt us from paying attention to this type of fraud as it indirectly affects the reliability of this product and, consequently, its use and profitability. […]

2.2.2. How to Act  
It should not be forgotten that fraudsters often try to pressure the employee, make them nervous, or demand all kinds of urgencies for various reasons that are not always clearly justified. They do this so that their victim makes a mistake, allowing them to seize their loot. One should never fall into their trap and should always act calmly. At the slightest doubt, and to feel more supported, one should consult with colleagues, the Security Delegate in the Territory, or Banking Security-Fraud Prevention.  
As a general rule of great importance, in case of the slightest doubt, legible photocopies of the Identification Document (both front and back if it is an ID card or similar) must always be made, in addition to the cases specified later, in which the photocopy must necessarily be made. In the event that fraud is consummated and in cases determined as such, the corresponding complaint will be filed, formulated with the advice and support of the Security Delegate in the Territory. 2.2.3. Identification Precautions  
Correct identification of the client, whether an individual or a representative of a legal entity, is fundamental for the early prevention of fraud.  
Identification Documents:  
The only valid documents for correct identification are:  
* National Identity Document.  
* Passport.  
* Foreign Identification Number (NIE), with its various types of Cards (residence, asylum, student, etc.).  
* National identification document from a European Union country with a photograph.  
Only originals will be valid, never photocopies, and only if they are valid. Expired documents will not be accepted under any circumstances.  
How to Identify:  
Regarding the correct identification process, the first step is to determine if the person holding the document is the same as the one appearing in the photograph of the identification document, then we will verify that the presented document is valid.  
Other Issues to Consider:  
Theft/loss of ID/book/check/promissory note:  
In the event that the client reports the theft or loss of their ID/book/check, for example, when informing about the loss or theft of a card or another document, it is mandatory to add that information into the Bank's applications, so that it serves as an alert for the Network.

In these cases, it is advisable for the office to obtain from the client a written statement in which they communicate the loss of the documentation.  
[…] Remote operations:  
As a general principle, for proper identification, the physical presence of the person who will carry out the operation is essential, in addition to being necessary to obtain their consent and to ensure that all required documents are duly signed. Experience indicates that frauds frequently occur in which communications via telephone, email, or fax are used to give certain orders.  
Poor identifications occur, whereby the fraudster poses as a client or even as an employee.  
Orders via telephone communications:  
As a general rule, they should NOT be accepted. If, in exceptional cases, they are accepted, they should be restricted to very well-known clients. In case of doubt, they should not be executed. It is advisable to ask verification questions to ensure the identity of the interlocutor or to request their details and call a phone number obtained from databases (BD), never the one provided by the communicator themselves. Information can also be verified through the BD. In these exceptional cases, the operations must be documented immediately.  
Sending orders via email/fax:  
The procedures for scanning documents, due to their simplicity and cost-effectiveness, have facilitated the increase of this type of fraud. For this reason, as a general rule, they should NOT be accepted. They will be restricted to the maximum, being allowed only in exceptional cases (exclusively very well-known clients), and it is essential to comply with the procedure regulating the authorization for the processing of payment orders (National and International) received from clients via email/fax, which requires making a phone contact to verify the authenticity of the received order. […]  
It is very common for fraudsters, posing as clients, to call the offices (especially others than that of the actual holder) repeatedly to check that their order has been processed. It is not sufficient to receive those calls to consider the order valid.

Now delving into the examination of non bis in idem, the Judgment of the National Court of July 23, 2021 (rec. 1/2017) states that “(...) According to the legislation and jurisprudence presented, the principle of non bis in idem prevents punishing the same subject twice for the same act based on the same grounds, understood as the same legal interest protected by the sanctioning norms in question. Indeed, when there is a triple identity of subject, act, and grounds, the accumulation of sanctions creates a sanction foreign to the proportionality assessment carried out by the legislator and materializes the imposition of a sanction not legally provided for, which also violates the principle of proportionality.  
But for "bis in idem" to be applicable, there must be a triple identity among the compared terms: objective (same facts), subjective (against the same subjects), and causal (for the same grounds or reason for punishment):  
a) The subjective identity implies that the affected subject must be the same, regardless of the nature or judicial or administrative authority that judges and independently of who the accuser or specific body that has resolved is, or whether it is judged alone or in conjunction with other affected parties.  
b) The factual identity implies that the facts being judged must be the same, and excludes cases of real concurrence of infringements where there is not a single unlawful act but several.  
c) The identity of grounds or causal identity implies that the sanctioning measures cannot coincide if they respond to the same nature, that is, if they share the same teleological foundation, which occurs between criminal and administrative sanctions.

Secondly, Article 29 of Law 40/2015, of October 1, on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector (hereinafter, LRJSP) is not applicable to the sanctioning regime imposed by the GDPR for the following reasons:  
1. The GDPR is a closed and complete system.

The GDPR is a community regulation directly applicable in the Member States, which contains a new, closed, complete, and global system aimed at ensuring the protection of personal data uniformly across the European Union.  
In relation, specifically and also, to the sanctioning regime provided therein, its provisions apply immediately, directly, and integrally, foreseeing a complete and gapless system that must be understood, interpreted, and integrated absolutely, fully, and integrally, thus leaving its ultimate purpose intact, which is the effective and real guarantee of the fundamental right to the Protection of Personal Data. The contrary results in a reduction of the guarantees of the rights and freedoms of citizens.  
In fact, a specific example of the absence of gaps in the GDPR system is Article 83 of the GDPR, which determines the circumstances that may operate as aggravating or mitigating factors regarding an infringement (Art. 83.2 of the GDPR) or specifies the existing rule concerning a possible concurrent offense (Art. 83.3 of the GDPR).  
To this, we must add that the GDPR does not allow the development or specification of its provisions by the legislators of the Member States, except for what the European legislator has specifically provided, delimiting it very concretely (for example, the provision of Art. 83.7 of the GDPR). The LOPDGDD only develops or specifies some aspects of the GDPR to the extent that it allows and with the scope that it permits.  
This is so because the purpose intended by the European legislator is to implement a uniform system throughout the European Union that guarantees the rights and freedoms of individuals, corrects behaviors contrary to the GDPR, promotes compliance, and enables the free movement of these data.  
In this sense, Recital 2 of the GDPR states that “(2) The principles and rules regarding the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of their personal data must, regardless of their nationality or residence, respect their fundamental freedoms and rights, in particular the right to the protection of personal data. This Regulation aims to contribute to the full realization of an area of freedom, security, and justice and of an economic union, to economic and social progress, to the strengthening and convergence of economies within the internal market, as well as to the well-being of individuals.” (the emphasis is ours)  
Recital 13 of the GDPR further indicates that “(13) To ensure a consistent level of protection for individuals throughout the Union and to avoid divergences that hinder the free movement of personal data within the internal market, a regulation is necessary that provides legal certainty and transparency to economic operators, including micro-enterprises and small and medium-sized enterprises, and offers individuals from all Member States the same level of enforceable rights and obligations and responsibilities for controllers and processors, in order to ensure consistent supervision of personal data processing and equivalent sanctions across all Member States, as well as effective cooperation between supervisory authorities.”

of the different Member States. The proper functioning of the internal market requires that the free movement of personal data within the Union is not restricted or prohibited for reasons related to the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data.” (the underlining is ours)  
In this system, what is decisive in the GDPR is not the fines. The corrective powers of the supervisory authorities provided for in Article 58.2 of the GDPR, combined with the provisions of Article 83 of the GDPR, demonstrate the prevalence of corrective measures over fines.  
Thus, Article 83.2 of the GDPR states that “Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure for the measures referred to in Article 58, paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j).”  
In this way, the corrective measures, which are all those provided for in Article 58.2 of the GDPR except for the fine, take precedence in this system, relegating the economic fine to cases where the circumstances of the specific case determine that a fine is imposed alongside the corrective measures or in substitution thereof.  
And all this with the aim of enforcing compliance with the GDPR, preventing non-compliance, promoting compliance, and ensuring that the infringement is not more profitable than compliance.  
Therefore, Article 83.1 of the GDPR provides that “Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines in accordance with this article for the infringements of this Regulation indicated in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 are, in each individual case, effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.”  
Fines must be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive to achieve the purpose intended by the GDPR.  
For this system to function with all its guarantees, it is necessary for several elements to be deployed in an integral and complete manner. The application of rules external to the GDPR regarding the determination of fines in each of the Member States applying their national law, whether due to aggravating or mitigating circumstances not provided for in the GDPR - or in the LOPDGDD in the Spanish case - or due to the application of a different mediating competition than that provided for in the GDPR, would undermine the effectiveness of the system, losing its meaning and teleological purpose, resulting in fines imposed for different infringements ceasing to be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. And in this way, the interested parties would also be deprived of the effective guarantee of their rights and freedoms, weakening the uniform application of the GDPR. The mechanisms for protecting the rights and freedoms of citizens would be diminished, which would be contrary to the spirit of the GDPR.  
The GDPR is endowed with its own principle of proportionality that must be applied in its strict terms.

Article 25.1 GDPR  
Principle of data protection by design  
Article 25.1 of the GDPR states the following:  
“Data protection by design and by default  
1. Taking into account the state of the art, the cost of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity posed to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall implement, both at the time of determining the means of processing and at the time of the processing itself, appropriate technical and organizational measures, such as pseudonymization, designed to effectively apply the principles of data protection, such as data minimization, and to integrate the necessary safeguards into the processing to meet the requirements of this Regulation and protect the rights of data subjects.”  
In line with these provisions, Recital 78 of the GDPR states:  
“The protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data requires the adoption of appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure compliance with the requirements of this Regulation. In order to demonstrate compliance with this Regulation, the data controller must adopt internal policies and implement measures that specifically comply with the principles of data protection by design and by default. Such measures could consist, among others, of minimizing the processing of personal data, pseudonymizing personal data as soon as possible, providing transparency regarding the functions and processing of personal data, allowing data subjects to monitor the processing of data, and enabling the data controller to create and improve security elements. When developing, designing, selecting, and using applications, services, and products that are based on the processing of personal data or that process personal data to fulfill their function, producers of products, services, and applications should be encouraged to take into account the right to data protection when developing and designing these products, services, and applications, and to ensure, with due regard to the state of the art, that data controllers and processors are in a position to fulfill their obligations regarding data protection. The principles of data protection by design and by default must also be taken into account in the context of public contracts.  
In particular, in light of Recital 78 of the GDPR, the principle of data protection by design is the key to be followed by the data controller to demonstrate compliance with the GDPR, as “the data controller must adopt internal policies and implement measures that specifically comply with the principles of data protection by design and by default.”  
The principle of privacy by design is a demonstration of the shift from reactivity to

www.aepd.es 28001 – Madrid

the proactivity and direct manifestation of the risk-based approach imposed by the GDPR.  
Part of the proactive responsibility requires that, from the earliest stages of planning a processing operation, this principle must be considered: the data controller, from the moment an eventual processing of personal data is designed and planned, must determine all the elements that make up the processing, in order to effectively apply the principles of data protection, integrating the necessary safeguards in the processing with the ultimate goal of, in compliance with the provisions of the GDPR, protecting the rights of the data subjects.  
Thus, regarding the risks that may be present in the processing, the data controller shall carry out an exercise of analysis and detection of risks throughout the entire data processing cycle, with the primary and ultimate purpose of protecting the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, and not only when the processing actually takes place. This is expressed in the Guidelines 4/2019 of the EDPB regarding Article 25 Data Protection by Design and by Default, adopted on October 20, 2020.  
In the aforementioned Guidelines, it is indicated that:  
“35. The ‘moment of determining the means of processing’ refers to the period of time in which the controller is deciding how the processing will be carried out and how it will occur, as well as the mechanisms that will be used to carry out such processing. In the process of making such decisions, the data controller must evaluate the appropriate measures and safeguards to effectively apply the principles and rights of the data subjects in the processing, and take into account elements such as risks, the state of the art, and the cost of implementation, as well as the nature, scope, context, and purposes. This includes the moment of acquiring and implementing the software and hardware and data processing services.  
36. Taking into account Data Protection by Design and by Default from the outset is crucial for the correct application of the principles and for the protection of the rights of the data subjects. Furthermore, from a cost-effectiveness perspective, it is also in the interest of data controllers to consider Data Protection by Design and by Default as early as possible, as it may later become difficult and costly to introduce changes to already formulated plans and designed processing operations.”  
To this end, the data controller must refer to the principles set out in Article 5 of the GDPR when designing the processing, which will serve to assess the effective compliance with the GDPR. Thus, the aforementioned Guidelines 4/2019 of the EDPB state that “61. To make Data Protection by Design and by Default effective, data controllers must apply the principles of transparency, lawfulness, fairness, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality, and proactive responsibility. These principles are set out in Article 5 and Recital 39 of the GDPR.”  
The Privacy by Design Guide of the AEPD states that “Privacy by Design (hereinafter, PbD) involves using a risk management and proactive responsibility-oriented approach to establish strategies that incorporate privacy protection throughout the entire lifecycle of the object (whether it is a system, hardware or software product, service, or process). By lifecycle

The object encompasses all the stages it goes through, from its conception to its withdrawal, including the phases of development, production, operation, maintenance, and withdrawal.”  
The Guide states that “Privacy must be an integral and indissoluble part of systems, applications, products, and services, as well as of the business practices and processes of the organization. It is not an additional layer or module added to something pre-existing, but must be integrated into the set of non-functional requirements from the moment it is conceived and designed (…) Privacy is born in the design, before the system is operational, and must be guaranteed throughout the entire data lifecycle.”  
Therefore, privacy by design, an obligation of the data controller that arises before the system is operational, is not a patch that is applied to a system built in disregard of the GDPR. Linked to the establishment of a true culture of data protection within the organization, it also implies, due to proactive responsibility, the ability to document all decisions made with a “privacy design thinking” approach, demonstrating compliance with the GDPR in this aspect as well.  
The risk-based approach refers directly and immediately to a preventive system aimed at visualizing, regarding the processing of personal data, the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Therefore, other risks to which the organization may be subjected and that affect its business scope must be excluded from the data protection risk-based approach.  
In relation to the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, risks must be identified, their impact evaluated, and the probability of their occurrence assessed. Thus, it is not the data that is protected, but the individuals behind it.  
The risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons arising from the processing of personal data can vary in severity and probability and can cause physical, material, or immaterial harm, tangible or intangible consequences, to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Recital 75 of the GDPR and Article 28.2 of the LOPDGDD exemplarily compile some of those considered by the legislator, but they are not the only ones. It will depend on the processing and the context in which it is carried out, the personal data processed, the individuals involved, the means used, etc.

Article 32 of the GDPR  
Lack of security measures  
Article 32 "Security of processing" of the GDPR establishes:  
"1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d) a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
2. In assessing the adequacy of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the processing of data, in particular as a consequence of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data.  
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this Article.  
4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data only processes such data on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law."

LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued for its development and, in so far as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  
II  
Termination of the procedure  
Article 85 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), under the heading “Termination in sanctioning procedures” provides as follows:  
“1. Once a sanctioning procedure has been initiated, if the offender acknowledges their responsibility, the procedure may be resolved with the imposition of the appropriate sanction.  
2. When the sanction is solely of a pecuniary nature or when it is possible to impose a pecuniary sanction and another of a non-pecuniary nature but the imposition of the latter has been justified as inappropriate, voluntary payment by the presumed responsible party, at any time prior to the resolution, will imply the termination of the procedure, except regarding the restoration of the altered situation or the determination of compensation for the damages caused by the commission of the infringement.  
3. In both cases, when the sanction is solely of a pecuniary nature, the competent body to resolve the procedure shall apply reductions of at least 20% on the amount of the proposed sanction, which shall be cumulative. The aforementioned reductions must be specified in the notification of initiation of the procedure, and their effectiveness shall be conditioned upon the withdrawal or waiver of any action or appeal in the administrative route against the sanction. “THIRD.- ON THE NON-EXISTENT VIOLATION OF ARTICLE 25 OF THE GDPR”  
First of all, and before examining the allegations made regarding the violation of Article 25 of the GDPR,  
1. Article 25 of the GDPR starts from the need to take into account a series of elements:  
   - State of the art  
   - Cost of application  
   - Nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing  
   - Risks posed by the processing to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.  
2. It imposes an obligation on the controller, who determines the purposes and means of the processing, giving, in this case, special relevance to the means.  
3. The controller must apply, both when determining the means of processing and at the time of the processing itself, appropriate technical and organizational measures (for example, pseudonymization), designed to effectively implement the principles of data protection and integrate the necessary guarantees into the processing.  
4. With this, a dual purpose is pursued:  
   - To comply with the requirements of the GDPR  
   - To protect the rights of the affected parties.  
The GDPR aims to achieve the protection of the rights of the affected parties through the application of its provisions. Therefore, the focus must always be directed towards the identification and assessment of risks to the rights and freedoms of the affected parties, with the subsequent adoption of technical and organizational measures of all kinds aimed at preventing their materialization.  
Thus, Article 25 of the GDPR refers to the “Risks posed by the processing to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.”  
If the approach adopted by the company or organization is not oriented towards the risks to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects but rather to the risks for the company or organization itself, not only will effective protection for the data subjects not be sought (since where you do not look, you do not see), but Article 25 of the GDPR will be violated.  
It may be the case that the measures adopted by the data controller to cover the risks of their organization may eventually prevent the materialization of risks to the rights and freedoms of their clients, but compliance with the PDDD cannot be assumed within the framework of proactive responsibility when the prescribed approach in Article 25 of the GDPR, which focuses on identifying the risks of varying probability and severity that processing poses to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, with the ultimate aim of protecting the rights of the data subjects, has not been applied.

Starting from this basis, in response to the meticulous evaluation referred to by BBVA in its allegations to the resolution proposal, the Constitutional Court stated in STC June 16, 1982 (RTC 1982, 36) "that brief or succinct reasoning, if sufficiently indicative, does not equate to a lack of reasoning nor does it lead to its nullity." Jurisprudence employs a very flexible criterion and allows this reasoning to be made, either directly or by reference to reports or opinions contained in the proceedings (STS January 21, 2003 [RJ 2003, 893]), through the incorporation of the resolution proposal (STS November 10, 1993 [RJ 1993, 8201]), or resulting from the same administrative file (STS July 29, 2002 [RJ 2002, 7385]). IV  
Imposition of measures  
In accordance with the provisions of the initiation agreement and the proposed resolution, BBVA is ordered to adapt its procedures to data protection regulations within a period of 9 months, complying with the principle of data protection by design in the organization's fraud detection system, and the adoption of personal data security procedures both in the contracting of financial products and in the inclusion and maintenance of customer data in credit information systems, in accordance with the provisions of the aforementioned Article 58.2 d) of the GDPR, according to which each supervisory authority may “order the controller or processor to ensure that the processing operations are in compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, where appropriate, in a specific manner and within a specified time frame…”. The imposition of this measure is compatible with the sanction consisting of an administrative fine, as provided for in Article 83.2 of the GDPR.  
Regarding the infringement of Article 25 of the GDPR, the respondent must analyze the risks, as well as establish appropriate technical and organizational measures of all kinds necessary to prevent the contracting of banking and financial services without the consent of the holder of the identified documentation and access devices in cases of theft or loss of identification documentation or online banking access devices, including the necessary measures so that communications made to the entity by its customers regarding the loss, misplacement, or theft of identity documents, phones, or credit cards, take effect in its various customer relationship channels related to the operations that the customer may carry out.  
Regarding the infringement of Article 32 of the GDPR, the respondent must analyze the risks, as well as establish the necessary security measures to ensure that no online contracts are made in the name of the data subject and, on the other hand, that customer data is included in credit information systems when the debt is disputed.
