LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
                                   Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection will be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, to the extent that they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  
II  
                       Allegations to the Resolution Proposal  
The responding party in its written allegations has stated that public authorities have the obligation, among others, to guarantee the constitutional right to the physical integrity of citizens, allocating measures for risk control and prevention of situations that may arise for this purpose. In this regard, Law 17/2015, of July 9, on the National Civil Protection System (hereinafter LSNPC), establishes that the heads of centers, establishments, and public dependencies that generate a risk of emergency are obliged to adopt self-protection measures.  
Under this framework, Royal Decree 393/2007, of March 23, which approves the Basic Self-Protection Standard for centers, establishments, and dependencies dedicated to activities that may give rise to emergency situations, is issued, as this is an essential document to respond to possible emergency situations in this area that may occur in the different police dependencies and that for its implementation requires the management of personal data, including telephone data, which are subsequently reflected in the “Communication Directory,” as determined by that royal decree; in the R.D. its Annex II “Minimum content of the self-protection plan” contains, in turn, an Annex I “Communication Directory,” which requires this data in its section “Emergency Personnel Phones.”

Firstly, it is true, as the LSNPC states in its preamble, that “This new law aims to strengthen the mechanisms that enhance and improve the functioning of the national system for the protection of citizens in the face of emergencies and disasters, which was already anticipated by the previous law. This civil protection system is understood as an instrument of public safety, integrated into the National Security policy. A system that will facilitate the cooperative, coordinated, and efficient exercise of the competencies distributed by constitutional doctrine among the Public Administrations, in light of new circumstances and social demands, by openly and flexibly interconnecting the plurality of services and actions aimed at the common objective.”  
On the other hand, it should be noted that the LSNPC has not been complemented by the Basic Civil Protection Regulation, approved by Royal Decree 407/1992, of April 24, nor by the Basic Self-Protection Regulation, approved by Royal Decree 393/2007, of March 23, as can be seen, they are prior to the LSNPC; although they were complementary to the latter, which came to replace the former, namely, Law 2/1985, of January 21, on Civil Protection.  
Under the LSNPC, Royal Decree 524/2023, of June 20, was issued, which approves the Basic Civil Protection Regulation, in whose derogatory provision it is stated that:

III  
                            Unfulfilled obligation: infringement of Article 6.1 GDPR  
The facts revealed are materialized in the accreditation arising from the practice of a fire drill, in the so-called Emergency Plan, where personal data along with the personal mobile phone numbers of each of the police officers were recorded, these last data which have not been provided or authorized by them, which could imply a violation of the regulations on the protection of personal data.  
This processing could constitute an infringement of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, Lawfulness of processing, which states:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary in order to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;

f) The processing is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that these interests do not prevail over the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject requiring the protection of personal data, particularly when the data subject is a child.  
The provisions in letter f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions.”  
Also, Recital 40 of the aforementioned GDPR states that “For processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on some other legitimate basis established in accordance with the law, whether in this Regulation or under another law of the Union or of the Member States to which this Regulation relates, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller or the necessity of executing a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the purpose of taking steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.”  
On the other hand, Article 4 of the GDPR, Definitions, in its paragraphs 1, 2, and 11, states that:  
“1) ‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person;  
“2) ‘processing’: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of making available, alignment or combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction;  
“11) ‘consent of the data subject’: any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject’s wishes by which they, by a statement or by a clear affirmative action, signify their agreement to the processing of personal data relating to them.”  
The processing of personal data requires the existence of a legal basis that legitimizes it.  
In accordance with Article 6.1 of the GDPR, in addition to consent, there are other possible bases that legitimize the processing of data without the need for the authorization of its owner, in particular, when it is necessary for the execution of a contract in which the affected party is involved or for the application, at their request, of pre-contractual measures, or when it is necessary for the satisfaction of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that these interests do not prevail over the interests or the rights and freedoms of the data subject.
