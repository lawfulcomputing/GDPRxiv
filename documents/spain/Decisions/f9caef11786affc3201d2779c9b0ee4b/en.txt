Lawfulness and security of processing  
Article 6 of the GDPR, under the heading "Lawfulness of processing," details in its paragraph 1 the circumstances under which data processing is considered lawful:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if it meets at least one of the following conditions:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, particularly when the data subject is a child.  
The provisions of point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”  
In turn, Article 6.1 of the LOPDGDD states, regarding the processing of personal data based on the consent of the affected party, that: “1. In accordance with the provisions of Article 4.11 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the consent of the affected party is understood as any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes, by which they signify agreement, either by a statement or by a clear affirmative action, to the processing of personal data concerning them (...).”

It should be noted that a Certification Authority (CA) is a trusted entity responsible for issuing and revoking digital certificates used in electronic transactions and signatures. This trust is achieved through the role of the CA, which acts as an intervening party in the relationship between companies or between companies and individuals. Thus, when any transaction occurs between two parties, the CA provides trust to the documents managed and signed as it is not an interested party.  
Thanks to the infrastructure of cryptographic keys that a Certification Authority possesses, the identity of the signer as well as the content of the transactions carried out are trusted and guaranteed.  
Some of the functions of the Certification Authority include: providing services such as the publication of certificates, lists of revoked certificates, validity checks of certificates, etc. Additionally, the CA records the exact date and time when a document has been electronically signed, which is known as timestamping.  
Likewise, the client companies of CIRBOX act as a Physical Verification Point (PVP) in order to verify the identity of the end user when applying for the electronic certificate.  
Regarding the accreditation of the identity of the data holder, the following controls are evident:  
- CIRBOX signs a PVP agreement with its client company.  
- These PVP agreements stipulate that identification will be carried out by internal personnel of the client company and according to the criteria established in the eIDAS Regulation.  
- At the time of identification, a certificate must be issued, which is sent to CIRBOX to verify the identification, along with an image of the identity document.  
- The specific employees of the client who will carry out the identification tasks of the holders of the digital certificates sign a document where they are authorized to do so and informed of the instructions for identification, including among other instructions the verification of the accuracy and authenticity of the information provided, and the comparison of copies of the documentation against the original presented.  
Client companies sign a service provision contract with CIRBOX with a Data Protection annex.  
In the case at hand, within the process of applying for digital certificates and reports, the phone number and email address of the end user are used, informing them through these channels of both the terms and conditions of the service and the privacy policy, the latter in two layers. They are also informed of the issuance of the certificate, indicating that it will be used solely for the purpose of obtaining the reports and providing them to the company that requests them, and of the certification authority (CA) that issues it. Acceptance for the issuance of the certificate and the reports is obtained through a process with an OTP key directed to the end user's mobile.

III  
The appealing party also requests the unarchiving of the actions based on two new issues that had not been previously reported: that CIRBOX does not have a data protection impact assessment (DPIA), nor a data protection officer.  
Well, these were not mentioned in the initial complaint, so it is not appropriate to resolve them at this stage of review.  
The appealing party cannot claim that in the appeal phase, facts that were not stated in a previous procedural phase be taken into account. The LPACAP provides in its article 118 the following procedural rule: "Facts, documents, or allegations of the appellant will not be taken into account in the resolution of appeals when, having been able to present them during the allegations phase, they have not done so. The practice of evidence cannot be requested when its lack of execution in the procedure in which the appealed resolution was issued is attributable to the interested party." This rule contains a provision that is nothing more than the positive specification for the common administrative sphere of the general principle that the law does not protect the abuse of rights (article 7.2 of the Civil Code). This principle aims, among other things, to prevent the allegations and evidence phases of application procedures from becoming useless, as would be the case if interested parties could choose, at their discretion, the moment to present evidence and allegations, since this would be contrary to a basic procedural order.
