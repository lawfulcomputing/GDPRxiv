BACKGROUND  
FIRST:  
On October 19, 2022, A.A.A., representing B.B.B. and C.C.C. (hereinafter, claimant 1 and claimant 2 respectively) filed a complaint with the Spanish Data Protection Agency.  
The complaint is directed against CAIXABANK, S.A. with NIF A08663619 (hereinafter, the respondent).  
The grounds for the complaint are as follows:  
The representative of the claimants states that her clients are customers of the respondent. Claimant 1 has three accounts open with this entity, appearing as the holder on two of them and her mother as authorized. In the third account (ending in ***REFERENCE.1, hereinafter shared account), claimant 1 and claimant 2 are listed as co-holders, with no other person appearing either as holder or as authorized.  
She states that when claimant 1 attempts to carry out any operations with her accounts through online banking, the signature of the authorized person is required not only if the operation is performed from one of the two accounts where she is authorized but also if they wish to carry out an operation in the account of which claimant 2 is a co-holder (the one ending in ***REFERENCE.1) and in which claimant 1's mother is not listed as authorized.  
Furthermore, when the authorized person for two of the accounts of which claimant 1 is a holder accesses the personal area through "CaixaBankNow," she can view not only the information related to the accounts and the products linked to them where she is listed as authorized, but she can also view information related to the cards linked to the third account (ending in ***REFERENCE.1) for which she has no capacity, either as a holder or as an authorized person.  
As a result of what occurred, numerous complaints were filed with the respondent, as well as a complaint with the Bank of Spain, without the issue being resolved.  
In the complaint, it is stated that “The initial response given to claimant 1 is that at some point in the year 2009 she signed a 'future contract,' without specifying which one or providing the supposed signed document by B.B.B. and D.D.D., by which the latter would have access to all that information.”

The shared ownership of a legal situation requires determining the regime to which the exercise of rights by the subjects that are part of that shared ownership must be subject.  
The exercise of rights by the holders of a shared legal situation can be organized in two ways: through “solidarity” or by means of “joint ownership.”  
Solidarity implies that each of the co-holders can exercise all the rights related to the shared legal situation individually and independently of the others, so that each of the co-holders has “the right to demand…in full the things subject to it” (Article 1,137 of the Civil Code), as if they were a single holder. In contrast, joint ownership (arg. Article 1,139 of the Civil Code) requires the simultaneous concurrence of all holders for the legal act to be validly produced. The rights must be exercised “in common hand” by all co-holders, who cannot exercise the rights individually, as is typical of solidarity.  
In this sense, the difference between a bank account being solidary or joint is that in the first case, the co-holders may exercise the rights attributed to them by the contract with the banking entity independently and without requiring the concurrence of the other co-holder. In contrast, if the bank account is subject to the regime of joint ownership, the consent of all co-holders will be necessary to exercise any right provided for in the current account contract.

Representation is defined in generic terms as that legal figure by virtue of which a person (representative) is granted the authority to act legally on behalf and in the interest of another person (represented).  
Depending on its foundation, representation can be voluntary (if it arises from an act of private autonomy of the representative) or legal (if the representation is imperative as it emanates from a legal provision). In relation to the difference between voluntary representation and legal representation, the ruling of the Civil Chamber of the Supreme Court dated June 22, 1979 (RJ 1979/2908) is particularly illustrative as it establishes the following:  
“[...] it should be noted that there is a clear and precise distinction between legal representation and voluntary representation, in that while the former strictly finds its foundation in the Law, and therefore outside the scope of private autonomy, the latter has its cause in the fact that the represented voluntarily grants their representation to the attorney, consequently originating from that private autonomy and finding in it the reason for its effectiveness, ultimately revealing that in the Spanish legal system while voluntary representation is merely a concession of legitimacy of the representative, legal representation emanates from a power of representation that rests immediately on a legal provision [...]”.  
Therefore, unlike legal representation, voluntary representation is configured as a manifestation of the private autonomy of the holder of individual rights, who freely decides that a third party shall substitute them in the exercise of such rights under the entrusted terms.  
The legal transaction through which the representative relationship is materialized for external purposes is the granting of power of attorney, which is precisely the legal act by virtue of which one person voluntarily grants another a power of representation with the specific scope determined.  
It should be noted that the essence of the granting of power of attorney lies in the fact that the representative acts on behalf of the represented, which has effects in the legal sphere of the latter. Therefore, representation is the institution by which one subject (representative) substitutes another (represented) in the performance of legal activity.  
Consistent with the above, and insofar as representation determines the substitution of the represented by the representative in legal transactions, the basis of the representative relationship is trust and fidelity between the representative and the represented, thus configuring it as a personal relationship.

II Preliminary Issues  
It should be noted that, according to Article 4.1 GDPR, "personal data" means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ("the data subject"); an identifiable natural person is one whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, particularly by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.  
Having established the above, let us recall that the bank transactions of a bank account contain data concerning its holder and, therefore, must be considered personal data in the sense indicated in the preceding paragraph. In the same vein, credit or debit card transactions are also personal data.  
For its part, Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines processing as "any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction."  
Finally, it should be noted that, according to paragraph 7 of that provision, the data controller is the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the data controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States.

To clarify this point, it should be indicated that the party being claimed is therefore considered responsible for the processing, as it is the one that determines the purposes and means of such activity, by virtue of Article 4.7 of the aforementioned GDPR:  
Article 4, paragraph 12 of the GDPR broadly defines "personal data security breaches" (hereinafter referred to as security breaches) as "all breaches of security that lead to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or unauthorized communication or access to such data."  
In this context, it should be noted that allowing access to a third party, in this case, the mother of claimant 1, to the account movements and the products associated with it, in which she is not listed as an authorized person, constitutes a breach of personal data, categorized as a breach of confidentiality.  
According to the Article 29 Working Party (GT29), a "Breach of confidentiality" occurs when there is an unauthorized or accidental disclosure of personal data, or access to it.  
Within the principles of processing provided for in Article 5 of the GDPR, the integrity and confidentiality of personal data are guaranteed in paragraph 1.f) of Article 5 of the GDPR.  
On the other hand, Article 32 of the GDPR states the obligation to adopt organizational and security measures adapted to the risk of processing.

In fact, a specific example of the non-existence of gaps in the GDPR system is Article 83 of the GDPR, which determines the circumstances that may operate as aggravating or mitigating factors regarding an infringement (Article 83.2 of the GDPR) or that specifies the existing rule concerning a possible concurrent infringement (Article 83.3 of the GDPR), which, unlike Article 29.5 of Law 40/2015, does not consider that only the sanction corresponding to the most serious infringement committed should be imposed, but rather that in the case of infringement of several provisions of the GDPR "the total amount of the fine shall not exceed the amount provided for the most serious infringements."
