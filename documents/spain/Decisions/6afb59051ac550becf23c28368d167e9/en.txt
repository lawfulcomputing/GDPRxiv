LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states that: "The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued for its development, and, as long as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures."  
II  
The physical image of a person, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is a personal data and its protection, therefore, is the subject of said Regulation. Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines the concept of "processing" of personal data.  
Images generated by a system of cameras or camcorders are personal data, so their processing is subject to data protection regulations.  
It is, therefore, pertinent to analyze whether the processing of personal data (image of the claimant) carried out by the respondent is in accordance with the provisions of the GDPR.  
III  
Article 6.1 of the GDPR establishes the circumstances that allow the processing of personal data to be considered lawful, indicating the following:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.”

The respondent also indicates that he disseminates the video based on a legitimate interest; however, such legitimate interest does not justify his processing as it contravenes a fundamental right of the claimant, such as his right to honor and dignity, regulated in Article 18 of the Constitution, thus violating what is indicated in Article 6.1 f) of the GDPR.

In this regard, it should be noted that the Constitutional Court ruling STC 6/1988 of January 21 establishes that the rights recognized in Article 20 of the Spanish Constitution (CE) are not absolute and have limitations, as Article 20.1, in its section 4, states the following: “These freedoms have their limit in respect for the rights recognized in this Title, in the provisions of the laws that develop it, and, especially, in the right to honor, to privacy, to one’s own image, and to the protection of youth and childhood.” This means that reference is made to the external limits of freedom of expression, keeping in mind, therefore, the conflict of rights that it poses.  
The publication of personal data of individuals on the Internet constitutes the processing of personal data, and therefore, the rules on the protection of personal data will apply.  
It should be emphasized that when the information refers to the publication on web pages of personal data, a balancing should occur; otherwise, it would lead to the indiscriminate publication of individuals' data without any limitations.  
In this specific case, the respondent publishes personal data of the claimant, such as the claimant's image and voice.  
Regarding the respondent's assertion that the publications are protected by freedom of expression and information, we must emphasize that in the conflict between the Fundamental Rights to freedom of expression and freedom of information in relation to the Fundamental Rights to privacy and honor and to the protection of personal data, notwithstanding the preponderance that both our Constitutional Court and the Supreme Court have granted to the former, we must always weigh the elements at play.  
Because preponderance does not mean prevalence when the limits set by regulations and jurisprudence are exceeded.  
In this sense, the Article 29 Working Group in its Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC, when examining the legal basis of legitimate interest under Article 7.1.f) of Directive 95/46/EC, fully transferable to the current Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR, includes the right to freedom of expression or information as one of the scenarios in which the issue of legitimate interest may arise, asserting that “without prejudice to whether the interests of the data controller will ultimately prevail over the interests and rights of the data subjects when the balancing test is carried out.”  
It is not necessary and proportionate if, under any circumstances, any data of any person or professional is published, even if it is not relevant, without being necessary to exercise the right to freedom of expression or information, as is the case here.  
This may entail, within the framework of balancing, the impossibility of publishing personal data or any information that makes them identifiable, while also guaranteeing the right to honor and privacy.  
Thus, in the balancing of rights, a series of elements must be considered, among which is the relevance of the published information.

Regarding this matter, the Judgment of the National Court dated April 2, 2019, in its third legal foundation asserts that “Thus, the protection of these other constitutional rights recognized by Article 20.4 of the Spanish Constitution is weakened against the freedoms of expression and information, when exercised in connection with matters of general interest, due to the subjects they refer to and the individuals involved, which consequently contribute to the formation of public opinion, as occurs when it affects public figures who perform public functions or are involved in matters of public relevance, thereby obliged to endure a certain risk that their subjective personal rights may be affected by opinions or information of general interest (Constitutional Court Judgments 107/1988, of June 8 (RTC 1988, 107), 20/2002, of January 28 (RTC 2002, 20), 160/2003, of September 15 (RTC 2003, 160), 151/2004, of September 20 (RTC 2004, 151), and 9/2007, of January 15 (RTC 2007, 9)).”  
We also add the Judgment of the Supreme Court dated November 2, 2016, Appeal 2538/2015, which states that the data provided in the information must be necessary for the formation of that public opinion.  
It considers in its second legal foundation that although “…both rights [of freedom of information and expression] cannot be reduced to the media, especially when currently the media facilitated by the internet can confer greater social relevance than those (Judgments 79/2014, of May 28, and the aforementioned 39/2016, of March 3),” it understands that “ultimately, the appellant has not even attempted to justify, in response to the request from the affected party for the cancellation of those data in its publication, that those specific data were necessary for the formation of public opinion beyond a purported interest in the work performed by that third party regarding tasks that the appellant values in a particular way but which, in light of what has transpired, does not transcend beyond their own personal motivations.”  
In conclusion, and as clarified by the aforementioned judgment, “what prioritizes the right to freedom of expression [over the fundamental right to the protection of personal data] is justified to the extent that the facts are revealed as necessary for the exposition of ideas or opinions of public interest.”  
We will add that the Court of Justice of the European Union in the judgment of February 14, 2019, C-345/17, BUIVIDIS, understands, in its paragraph 58, that it cannot be considered that any information published on the Internet, relating to personal data, falls within the concept of “journalistic activities.”  
Paragraph 59 adds that it must be verified whether the publication had as its sole purpose the dissemination to the public of information, opinions, or ideas, and its paragraph 62 states that if it turns out that the publication did not have as its sole purpose the dissemination to the public of information, opinions, or ideas, it cannot be considered that the processing of personal data in dispute in the main litigation was carried out “for exclusively journalistic purposes.”  
For all these reasons, for a matter to be considered of general interest, of public relevance, it will be so not only because of the person involved but also because of the subject matter it refers to and its effects on shaping public opinion.  
The data provided through the information must be necessary to shape that public opinion.

Upon examining the content of the publications, the respondent reports an event that renders it controversial or personally criticizable.  
Public relevance, therefore, does not exist as it solely responds to the personal interest and motivation of the respondent, as the data controller.  
From the documents in the file, it does not appear that the respondent has the purpose of disclosing information, opinions, or ideas to the public with the publication of personal data; rather, they publish and comment on the internet a video featuring the image and voice of the complainant that does not correspond to that purpose.  
In the present case, we are neither dealing with a person of public relevance, in the sense that such relevance is sufficient to understand that it entails, ex lege, a dispossession of their fundamental right to the protection of their personal data, nor are we dealing with facts "of public relevance," in the sense that they are revealed as "necessary" for the exposition of ideas or opinions of public interest, whose sole purpose is the dissemination of the same to the public.  
Furthermore, the exercise of freedom of expression and information, in cases where it outweighs other rights and legitimate interests of a person, cannot overlook data protection regulations.  
In the disseminated video in question, there are personal data that are neither precise for providing information nor for expressing any opinion.  
Given these circumstances, this Agency considers that the facts presented, namely, the dissemination of a video that may harm the image of a person, without their consent, means that the processing of their image as personal data may be considered unlawful, thus violating what is established in Article 6 of the GDPR, indicated in legal basis III, which could imply the commission of an infringement classified in Article 83.5 of the GDPR, which states the following:
