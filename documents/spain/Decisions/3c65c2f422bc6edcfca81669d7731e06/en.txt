LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
The Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to resolve this appeal, in accordance with the provisions of Article 123 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter LPACAP) and Article 48.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD).  
II  
Regarding the statements made by the appellant, reiterating basically the allegations already presented throughout the sanctioning procedure, it should be noted that all of them were already analyzed and dismissed in the Legal Grounds from II to VII, inclusive, of the contested Resolution, as transcribed below:  
II  
The reported facts are specified in that the complained company, when delivering packages to their recipients through courier companies, imposes as a necessary condition for receipt the taking of a photograph of the front and back of their ID card. 1. The documentation in the file provides clear evidence that the complained party violated Article 5 of the GDPR, principles relating to processing, by imposing as a condition for the delivery of the product the taking of a photograph of the front and back of the ID card; a photograph that is obtained through the application included in the mobile device of the delivery person from the company making the delivery and whose image is subsequently transferred and consulted by the company sending the package.  
Article 5 of the GDPR refers to the general principles for data processing. In its paragraph c) it refers to the principle of data minimization, indicating that data must be “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.”  
From the regulation, we can deduce that only adequate personal data that will be processed can be collected, that is, those that are strictly necessary for the processing; that they can only be collected...

In this same regard, Recital 39 states that:  
(39) All processing of personal data must be lawful and fair. For natural persons, it must be entirely clear that personal data concerning them is being collected, used, consulted, or otherwise processed, as well as the extent to which such data is or will be processed. This principle particularly refers to the information provided to data subjects about the identity of the data controller and the purposes of the processing, as well as the additional information to ensure fair and transparent processing with respect to the affected natural persons and their right to obtain confirmation and communication of the personal data concerning them that is subject to processing. This requires, in particular, ensuring that the retention period is strictly limited to a minimum. Upon examining the documentation, it is evident that there are other procedures for delivering products through which the identity of the recipient can be verified, confirming that they are the person to whom it is addressed, without the need to photograph their ID using the application contained in the distributor's mobile device and to certify its delivery to the responsible party. The processing of images of the ID is excessive for the purpose of verifying and determining that the recipient to whom the goods are delivered is the same person who entered into the contract.  
Article 5.1.c) enshrines the principle of data minimization and implies that the processing of personal data must be adequate and proportional to the purpose it is directed at, clearly determining the purposes for which the data is collected and processed, and restricting the processing of data that is excessive or proceeding to their deletion.

Likewise, the relevance in the processing of data must occur both at the stage of collection and in the subsequent processing carried out on the same.  
Regarding whether the procedure adopted by the respondent is necessary to meet this need or if it is essential to respond to this need, it should be noted that the purpose of the same, as repeatedly asserted by the respondent, is that it provides greater security that the product is indeed delivered to its intended recipient, who is the same person that signed the contract, proving that the delivery is made to the person who actually contracted; although it should also be added that it involves the fight against fraud in this type of products and its economic aspect, as it should not be forgotten that these deliveries in the so-called distance selling have had and have a direct relationship with fraudulent activities, which requires reasonable diligence on the part of such companies at the time of contracting by implementing appropriate measures, verifying through the relevant documentation the identity of the person providing the data, to avoid such behaviors.  
In this regard, Recital 47 states that “The processing of personal data strictly necessary for the prevention of fraud also constitutes a legitimate interest of the data controller in question.”  
However, the procedure employed by the respondent is relevant when assessing the interference with the fundamental right to data protection and the necessity of achieving the intended purpose. It should be noted that the GDPR limits the use or processing of data by necessity and not by excess; that is, personal data must be adequate, necessary, relevant, and limited to the purpose for which they were collected.  
Previously, the Constitutional Court jurisprudence has established that if the objective can be achieved without processing data, the data should not be processed.  
On the other hand, this limitation to what is necessary must be evaluated both from a quantitative point of view (volume of data processed) and qualitative (category of data processed). In this same sense, Recital 39 states that “…This requires, in particular, ensuring that its retention period is limited to a strict minimum. Personal data should only be processed if the purpose of the processing cannot reasonably be achieved by other means…”

However, such a claim cannot be accepted, as access to the image of the ID card, photographing both the front and back of it, and its processing for the purpose of delivering a product through the mobile terminal of the distributor's delivery person, is considered excessive and not limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which it is processed.  
It should be noted that the ID card contains not only the name and surname, its number, and the photograph of the recipient but also incorporates many other data: signature, address, place and date of birth, access key to the information contained therein, date of issuance, expiration date, alphanumeric code, issuing equipment and office, etc., completely additional data that are neither adequate nor relevant nor limited for the purpose of delivering the goods and that to prove that the person collecting the product is the holder who provided the data at the time of contracting, other means that are less harmful and aggressive to the privacy of individuals should be used.  
The above, regardless of the fact that the image of the ID card, as stated by the respondent, cannot be retained by the delivery person and is transmitted through an encrypted channel to the logistics company's system, where it is stored on its internal server, to which only a technician assigned to its maintenance has access, and through the ERP consultation module of the computer system, the respondent can access the collected photograph.  
Therefore, the processing does not correspond to what is indicated in Recital 39, nor in Article 5.1.c) which states that data must be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" for the intended purpose.  
This diligence that the respondent claims must be exercised at the time of contracting by adopting the necessary precautions and guarantees to uniquely identify the contractor.  
Thus, if the contracting is done correctly, there can be no difficulty in providing proof that the recipient of the product is indeed the one who entered into the contract and to whom the product is delivered.

It is at the moment of the contract's execution that the processing of the identifying document of the interested party is deemed necessary so that the respondent can prove that the person with whom they are contracting is indeed who they claim to be and, therefore, this would demonstrate an adequate level of diligence.

However, the cases referred to bear no resemblance to the one we are examining, no matter how much the respondent has an interest in proposing it, as they involve fraudulent contracts, due to a violation of the principle of legitimacy (Article 6.1 of the GDPR) in the first case and the principle of consent (Article 6.1 of the LOPD) in the second, which the respondent must be well aware of since in the sanctioning procedure they were sanctioned by the AEPD and were a party to the appeal in the contentious case that led to the first of the judgments, which dismissed the appeal filed and imposed a fine of 80,000 euros.  
Therefore, the processing of the image of the ID card at the time of delivery of a product contracted remotely is not only considered unlawful, but is also deemed inadequate, not pertinent, nor limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which it is processed, as it should not be forgotten that the ID card contains data that exceeds and has nothing to do with the intended purpose.  
The AEPD in the current case is not only not in contradiction with the resolutions issued, but is in accordance with the interpretation that the National Court has been making in its judgments.  
This is why the respondent's allegation reiterating that the AEPD sanctioned them for their lack of diligence in not retaining proof of the verification of the identity of the interested party, and in the opening of the current procedure in which they are blamed for the exact opposite, retaining proof of the verification of the identity of the interested party, is not true and cannot be accepted as it does not involve contradictory administrative resolutions nor affect legal certainty, nor generate uncertainty and defenselessness.  
Therefore, it is considered that there is a violation of the principle of data minimization, with the respondent being responsible for the infringement of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, an infringement classified in Article 83.5.a).

V  
The respondent has expressed their disagreement with the content of the grounds set forth in the Proposal of the procedure, reaffirming what has already been indicated in previous writings.  
They argue that they are being sanctioned for displaying an excessive level of diligence in identifying their clients by requiring a copy of the ID card, the only document that, in accordance with Organic Law 4/2015, of March 30, on the Protection of Citizen Security, allows for the verification of the identity and personal data of its holder.  
It is true that Article 8.1 of the LOPSC establishes that the ID card "is the only document with sufficient value by itself for the accreditation, for all purposes, of the identity and personal data of its holder" and that the verification by the respondent of the identity of the interested party at the time of delivery of the product to their home is considered lawful both for executing the object of the contract, such as the delivery of the contracted product, and for preventing the commission of fraud. However, what is in question is whether the method, protocol, procedure, or system carried out for this purpose is appropriate; photographing the front and back of the ID card at the time of delivery is deemed excessive, inappropriate, and intrusive to the privacy of individuals, unless it is justified by other reasons, such as that reasonable diligence was not exercised during the contracting process and the corresponding documents were not requested. What need does the respondent have to photograph this document if it is already in their possession?  
They have also argued that the processing of images of the ID card of the interested party is not only considered necessary to prove that the product has been delivered to the person listed as the contractor, but that this criterion has been recognized by the Agency not only in specific resolutions but is also described by the Agency in the ‘Plan for Official Inspection on Distance Contracting in Telecommunications Operators and Energy Marketers of the AEPD’, where it identifies compliance with regulations in the sector and proposes improvements to make that duty of diligence effective.

On the other hand, if the contracting carried out by the respondent, for which a copy of the document signed by the contractor has been provided, was correctly identifying (copy of the DNI and banking document showing the account number), verifying that the identity of the recipient and the contractor match should be done without the need to request the copy of the DNI again (photograph of its front and back).

In order to establish the administrative fine that should be imposed, the provisions contained in Articles 83.1 and 83.2 of the GDPR must be observed, which state:  
“1. Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines under this article for the infringements of this Regulation indicated in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 are, in each individual case, effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.  
2. Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure to those provided for in Article 58, paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j). In deciding on the imposition of an administrative fine and its amount in each individual case, the following shall be duly taken into account:  
a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing operation in question, as well as the number of data subjects affected and the level of damage and harm they have suffered;  
b) the intentionality or negligence in the infringement;  
c) any measures taken by the controller or processor to mitigate the damage and harm suffered by the data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the controller or processor, taking into account the technical or organizational measures they have implemented pursuant to Articles 25 and 32;  
e) any previous infringement committed by the controller or processor;  
f) the degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority in order to remedy the infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects of the infringement;  
g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement;  
h) the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, in particular whether the controller or processor notified the infringement and, if so, to what extent;  
i) when the measures indicated in Article 58, paragraph 2, have been previously ordered against the controller or processor in relation to the same matter, the compliance with such measures;  
j) adherence to codes of conduct under Article 40 or to certification mechanisms approved under Article 42, and  
k) any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement.”  
In relation to letter k) of Article 83.2 of the GDPR, the LOPDGDD, in its Article 76, “Sanctions and corrective measures,” establishes that:  
“2. In accordance with the provisions of Article 83.2.k) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following may also be taken into account:
