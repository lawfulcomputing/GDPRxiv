II  
Violation of Article 5.1(f) of the GDPR  
Article 5 of the GDPR establishes the principles relating to processing, indicating, among other issues, that personal data shall be:  
“f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, by means of appropriate technical or organizational measures (‘integrity and confidentiality’).”  
Similarly, Recital 39 of the GDPR states that: “Personal data must be processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security and confidentiality of the personal data, including to prevent unauthorized access or use of such data and the equipment used for processing.”  
The principle of confidentiality, according to the aforementioned Article 5.1(f) of the GDPR, requires the protection of personal data against unauthorized access, use, and disclosure. This principle is essential to ensure the security of personal data, constituting a key pillar in the protection of privacy and individual rights in this area. Conversely, the violation of this principle can have unintended consequences, reflecting its importance within the regulatory framework of data protection.  
In the present case, in the terms set out in the PROVEN FACT FIRST, the accidental delivery of certain personal data of the mother and legal representative of a patient, such as the partial address, phone number, and email to an unauthorized party (in this case, the father of the minor) demonstrates a violation of the aforementioned principle.  
First of all, it should be noted that Article 4 defines personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”

In this context, the entity's statement that the improper delivery was a consequence of an employee's negligence does not exempt it from its presumed responsibility for such events. It should be noted that, by virtue of the principle of proactive responsibility recognized in Article 5 itself, the data controller is responsible for complying with the principles set forth in the aforementioned article, as well as being able to demonstrate such compliance. Therefore, ensuring the principles of personal data protection and ensuring their proper processing falls on the data controller.  
In the present case, it is not possible to ignore the potential impact that unauthorized disclosure of personal information can have. Furthermore, the disclosure of additional data, such as email or phone number, increases the risk of possible misuse of such data in the future.  
On the other hand, the necessity of not communicating personal information of the mother to the father of the minor, as evidenced in FACT PROVEN FIRST, is a key factor to consider in this case. The relevance of this fact lies in the obligation of the data controller to respect the preferences of individuals regarding the personal data that will be subject to processing. In the case at hand, the delivery of certain personal information of the mother to the father, despite the necessity not to do so, implies a manifestation of the violation of the aforementioned principle of confidentiality, which entails an infringement in the terms indicated below.  
Finally, the allegation by CDIS S.L. that it communicated the security breach to both the authority and the affected party does not exempt it from the responsibility of having violated the principle of confidentiality. It should be noted that such communications are not discretionary but are mandatory under the GDPR. The notification of such an incident, while demonstrating a reactive compliance with this regulation, cannot eliminate the inherent responsibility for failing to prevent the exposure of the affected personal data from the outset. Consequently, the entity that communicates the breach remains responsible for the infringement of the principle of confidentiality and must face the corresponding legal and administrative consequences.

IV  
Non-compliance with Article 32 of the GDPR  
Article 32 “Security of processing” of the GDPR establishes:  
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the varying likelihood and severity of risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d) a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
2. In assessing the appropriateness of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the processing of data, in particular as a result of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data.  
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this Article.

4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data may only process such data in accordance with the instructions of the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law.  
It is necessary to point out that the aforementioned provision does not establish a list of specific security measures according to the data being processed, but rather establishes the obligation for the controller and the processor to apply technical and organizational measures that are appropriate to the risk posed by the processing, taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, and the risks of likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  
Furthermore, the security measures must be adequate and proportionate to the identified risk, determining those technical and organizational measures that are appropriate considering pseudonymization and encryption, the ability to ensure confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience, the ability to restore availability and access to data after an incident, verification processes (not audits), evaluation, and assessment of the effectiveness of the measures.  
In any case, when assessing the adequacy of the level of security, particular attention must be paid to the risks presented by the processing of data, such as the destruction, loss, or accidental or unlawful alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data that could cause physical, material, or immaterial harm.  
For its part, Recital 83 of the GDPR states that “(83) In order to maintain security and prevent processing from infringing the provisions of this Regulation, the controller or the processor must assess the inherent risks of the processing and implement measures to mitigate them, such as encryption. These measures must ensure an adequate level of security, including confidentiality, taking into account the state of the art and the cost of their implementation in relation to the risks and the nature of the personal data to be protected. When assessing the risk in relation to data security, the risks arising from the processing of personal data must be taken into account, such as the destruction, loss, or accidental or unlawful alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data, particularly those that could cause physical, material, or immaterial harm.”  
In the present case, the non-compliance with the content of the aforementioned Article 32 of the GDPR is based on a detailed analysis of the circumstances surrounding the data breach incident. In this regard, as deduced from the article itself, the current regulations require that entities responsible for processing personal data apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure an adequate level of security relative to the risk.

It is worth mentioning that the failure to comply with these obligations in this case is not a consequence of the breach communicated by the entity, but rather it indicates or manifests a failure to adequately assess the risks associated with data processing or to implement security measures commensurate with these risks.  
This is particularly relevant in the present case given that, considering the corporate purpose of CDIS S.L., as indicated in the FOURTH PROVEN FACT, the processing may affect data of minors. Logically, the sensitive nature of such data requires a higher level of protection due to the potentially greater risks to the rights of this more vulnerable group.  
The allegation presented by CDIS S.L., consisting of the decision to delete any information related to phone numbers, postal addresses, and email addresses from all reports generated for delivery to the interested parties following the situation described in the security breach, in fact manifests the aforementioned violation of Article 32 of the GDPR. This article requires that the data controller apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk in a prior and continuous manner. In contrast, the measures indicated were adopted reactively after the security breach and not prior to it, which evidences that the initial preventive measures were insufficient to adequately protect personal data and comply with the mandate of the infringed article.  
Similarly, security measures must be particularly stringent and reviewed in environments where sensitive data is processed, as is the case with minors, in order to ensure their confidentiality, as well as their integrity and availability. The entity must not only implement security measures but also ensure that these measures remain effective against the various risks in the processing of personal data, including potential human error or negligence, especially when such data is sensitive.  
In conclusion, the attribution of the infringement is based on the inadequacy and ineffectiveness of the implemented security measures, which leads to the violation of Article 32 of the GDPR.
