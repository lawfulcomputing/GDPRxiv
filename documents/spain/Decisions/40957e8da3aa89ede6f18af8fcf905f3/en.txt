2.- As mentioned, the sanctioning power of the Public Administration is directly linked to the principles that inspire criminal law, given that both powers are expressions of the Legal System of the State, as expressed in the Constitution itself (art. 25) and recognized by the jurisprudence of the Constitutional Court since STC 18/1981, of June 8, and a very reiterated jurisprudential doctrine of the Supreme Court (STS September 29, 1980, and STS November 4, 1980, and STS November 10, 1980, among others).  
The sanctioning power of the Administration has its constitutional basis in Article 25 of the CE. It is a reiterated doctrine of the Constitutional Court (STC 77/83, of October 3, STS 42/87, of April 7; STC 29/1989, of February 6) that the administrative sanctioning system includes a double guarantee: the first, of a material nature, implies the need for normative predetermination of unlawful conduct and corresponding sanctions, through legal provisions that allow predicting, with sufficient certainty, the conduct that constitutes an infringement and the penalties or sanctions applicable.  
This arises from the mandate of taxativity or "lex certa" and is specified in the requirement of normative predetermination of unlawful conduct and corresponding sanctions, which places upon the legislator the duty to configure them in the sanctioning laws with the greatest possible precision (principle of typicity) so that citizens can know in advance the scope of what is prohibited and thus foresee the consequences of their actions. (STC 242/2005, of October 10, and STC 162/2008, of December 15). The second, of a formal nature, refers to the rank of the norms typifying the infringements and regulating the sanctions, since the term "current legislation" contained in art. 25.1 CE expresses a reservation of Law.  
Therefore, the formal guarantee implies that the law must contain the determination of the essential elements. Well, the material guarantee constitutes the aforementioned principle of typicity, which "implies the imperative need for normative predetermination of infringing conduct and corresponding sanctions, that is, the existence of legal provisions (lex

For its part, Article 27 of Law 40/2015 of October 1, of LRJSP, incorporates the so-called principle of typicity, according to which only violations of the legal order that are expressly provided as such by a Law constitute administrative infractions.  
The principle of typicity, related to the principle of legality, requires that the act that allegedly has an illicit character is expressly provided as an infraction in some provision of the administrative legal order. For a behavior to be classified as typical, and therefore, unlawful and punishable, it is necessary that there is a coincidence between the action performed by the actor and the behavior described in the applicable legal provision.  
Through the application of the principle of typicity, it is intended to verify whether an event that occurred in reality meets all the characteristics described in the law as prerequisites for the infringement of a sanctioning norm. Well, we must begin this section by indicating that, as this Agency has pointed out on several occasions, the greatest novelty presented by the GDPR is the evolution from a model based fundamentally on the control of compliance with current legality to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the controller or processor of the risk that the processing of personal data may generate, in order to adopt the necessary measures based on that assessment.

Section 4 of CHAPTER IV of the GDPR - Articles 37 to 39 - regulates in detail the figure of the DPO. Regarding the interpretation and application of these provisions, reference can be made to the guidelines contained in the Article 29 Working Party document “Guidelines on Data Protection Officers” - WP243, last revised and adopted on April 5, 2017.  
This regulation is complemented by the provisions in CHAPTER III of TITLE V of the LOPDGDD, in which Articles 34 to 37 contain some specialties directly applicable to our internal law. Specifically, the designation of the data protection officer is included in Article 37 of the GDPR, with the scope of subjects obliged to its appointment being expanded in Article 34 of the LOPDGDD.

From the above, it can be inferred that the requirement for the appointment of a DPO (Data Protection Officer) should not be interpreted merely as a formality, but must comply with the requirements established in the applicable legal norms. Consequently, it is necessary to conduct a brief analysis of the functions and resources that the DPO must have. Thus, it should start from the important functions assigned by Article 39.1 of the GDPR:  
“1. The Data Protection Officer shall have at least the following functions:  
a) to inform and advise the controller or the processor and the employees who are involved in processing of their obligations under this Regulation and under other Union or Member State data protection provisions;  
b) to monitor compliance with the provisions of this Regulation, other Union or Member State data protection provisions, and the policies of the controller or the processor in relation to the protection of personal data, including the allocation of responsibilities, awareness-raising and training of staff involved in processing operations, and the relevant audits;  
c) to provide advice as requested regarding the impact assessment related to data protection and to monitor its implementation in accordance with Article 35;  
d) to cooperate with the supervisory authority;  
e) to act as a contact point for the supervisory authority on issues related to processing, including prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and to consult, where appropriate, on any other matter.”  
These are, therefore, advisory and supervisory functions aimed at ensuring adequate compliance with the regulations on personal data protection, with Article 39.2 stating that “The Data Protection Officer shall perform their tasks with due regard to the risks associated with processing operations, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing.”  
Furthermore, Article 38.1 clearly establishes that: “The controller and the processor shall ensure that the Data Protection Officer is involved properly and in a timely manner in all issues related to the protection of personal data.”  
In addition to the important advisory functions assigned to the DPO, including cases where it is necessary to carry out an impact assessment due to high-risk processing, and specifying the supervisory functions, Article 36 of the LOPDGDD (Organic Law on Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights) provides that: “The DPO may inspect the procedures related to the subject matter of this organic law and issue recommendations within the scope of their competencies,” that “In the exercise of their functions, the Data Protection Officer shall have access to personal data and processing operations, and the controller or processor may not invoke any duty of confidentiality or secrecy, including that provided for in Article 5 of this organic law, to oppose this access,” and that, “When the Data Protection Officer identifies a relevant breach in data protection, they shall document it and communicate it immediately to the management and administration bodies of the controller or the processor.”

On the other hand, Article 39.1.e) of the GDPR also establishes as functions of the DPO “to act as a contact point for the supervisory authority on issues related to processing, including the prior consultation referred to in Article 36, and to carry out consultations, where appropriate, on any other matter.”  
To adequately fulfill these duties, the GDPR requires certain training requirements for the DPO, and that they are provided with the necessary resources. The training requirements are referred to in Article 37.5 of the GDPR, which states that “The data protection officer shall be designated based on their professional qualities and, in particular, their specialized knowledge of law and practice in the field of data protection and their ability to perform the tasks specified in Article 39.” Furthermore, Article 35 of the LOPDGDD adds that “Compliance with the requirements established in Article 37.5 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 for the designation of the data protection officer, whether a natural or legal person, may be demonstrated, among other means, through voluntary certification mechanisms that particularly take into account the obtaining of a university degree that certifies specialized knowledge in law and practice in the field of data protection.”  
In order to better interpret and apply these provisions, one may refer to the guidelines contained in the Article 29 Working Party document “Guidelines on Data Protection Officers” -WP243-, last reviewed and adopted on April 5, 2017, which, in relation to the knowledge and skills of the DPO, points out the following:  
-  
Level of knowledge: The required level of knowledge is not strictly defined, but it must be commensurate with the sensitivity, complexity, and volume of the data that an organization processes. For example, when the data processing activity is particularly complex or involves a large amount of sensitive data, the DPO may require a higher level of knowledge and support. There is also a difference depending on whether the organization systematically transfers personal data outside the European Union or if such transfers are occasional.  
Thus, the DPO must be chosen carefully, taking into account the data protection issues that arise within the organization.  
-  
Professional qualities: It should be noted that, although Article 37, paragraph 5, does not specify the professional qualities to be considered when designating the DPO, an important factor is that they have knowledge of national and European legislation and practices in the field of data protection and a deep understanding of the GDPR.  
-  
Ability to perform their functions: The ability of the DPO to perform their functions should be interpreted both in reference to their personal qualities and knowledge as well as their position within the organization.

Personal qualities must include, for example, integrity and a high level of professional ethics; the primary concern of the DPO must be to enable compliance with the GDPR.  
The DPO plays a fundamental role in promoting a culture of data protection within the organization and contributes to the implementation of essential elements of the GDPR, such as the principles related to data processing, the rights of data subjects, data protection by design and by default, the record of processing activities, processing security, and the notification and communication of data security breaches.  
On the other hand, the need to provide the DPO with the necessary resources to perform their functions is established as an obligation of the controller in Article 38.2 of the GDPR: “The controller and the processor shall support the data protection officer in performing the tasks mentioned in Article 39, providing the necessary resources for the performance of those tasks and access to personal data and processing operations, and for the maintenance of their specialized knowledge.”  
The following aspects must be particularly considered:  
· Active support for the DPO’s work by senior management (at the board level).  
· Sufficient time for the DPO to fulfill their functions, which is particularly important when a part-time internal DPO is appointed or when an external DPO carries out data protection in addition to other obligations.

As we can see, there is no classification in the GDPR of very serious, serious, or minor infringements typical of the Spanish legal system, nor can it be deduced from its wording that the violation of the provisions of Article 83.4 of the GDPR corresponds to minor infringements and the provisions of Article 83.5 or Article 83.6 of the GDPR correspond to serious infringements.

However, special attention must be paid to the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, its intentional character, the measures taken to mitigate the damages suffered, the degree of responsibility, or any relevant prior infringement, the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, compliance with measures ordered against the responsible party or processor, adherence to codes of conduct, and any other aggravating or mitigating circumstances.”  
For all these reasons, the severity of an infringement is determined for the purposes of the GDPR and with the elements provided by it.

to involve a similar and greater severity than those contemplated in paragraph 4 of the same Article 83 of the European Regulation. The European rule thus limits itself to establishing two categories of infringements based on their severity.  
The limitation periods for infringements are not provided for in the European Regulation and, therefore, there is a tacit but peaceful understanding that Member States have the competence to establish such periods. The determination of such periods must be based, as is well known, on the severity of the infringement.  
Well, the infringements provided for in paragraph 4 of Article 83, on the one hand, and in paragraphs 5 and 6 of Article 83 of the European Regulation, on the other, have a different maximum limit—€10,000,000 or 2% of the turnover in the first case, €20,000,000 or 4% of the turnover in the second—but the same minimum limit, which in both cases is €1. The existence of such broad quantitative margins indicates that the infringements of Article 83, whether those of paragraph 4 or those of paragraphs 5 and 6, can be of very different magnitude and that, for this reason, they cannot have the same limitation period; those infringements that, due to their severity, are close to the upper quantitative limit cannot have the same limitation period as those that, due to their minor nature, are closer to the lower quantitative limit.  
In such circumstances, the establishment of limitation periods would not be satisfactorily resolved by applying a longer period to the infringements of the provisions mentioned in paragraphs 5 and 6 of Article 83 than to the infringements of the provisions mentioned in paragraph 4 of Article 83, given that the infringements contemplated by both provisions, in the case of being minor, would require a shorter limitation period.  
From this perspective and solely for the purpose of establishing their limitation period, a distinction has been made between "merely formal infringements" and "substantial violations" of such provisions, considering the former as "minor infringements" with a limitation period of one year and the latter as "serious" and "very serious infringements" with limitation periods of two and three years respectively. In the opinion of the Council of State, this classification of infringements, to the extent that it is made solely for the purpose of determining limitation periods for infringements not provided for in the European Regulation, cannot be understood as contrary to the provisions of the European rule.  
This classification, however, does not have significance regarding the amount of the fines. The determination of the amount of fines to be imposed for the violation of the provisions mentioned in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 of Article 83 of the European Regulation is, according to the European rule, the responsibility of the supervisory authorities, in accordance with the grading criteria established in paragraph 2 of this same provision, among which is the "nature" or "severity" of the infringement.  
Within the quantitative limits established by the European Regulation, the supervisory authorities, taking into account the greater or lesser severity of the infringement, must set the amount of the fines. Certainly, the margins available to the supervisory authorities are very broad—from €1 to €10,000,000 for infringements of the provisions mentioned in paragraph 4 of Article 83 and from €1 to €20,000,000.
