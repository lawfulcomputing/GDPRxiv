RESOLUTION OF SANCTIONING PROCEDURE  
From the actions carried out by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  

BACKGROUND  
FIRST: A.A.A. (hereinafter, the complainant) filed a complaint with the Spanish Agency for Data Protection on November 10, 2022. The complaint is directed against AYUNTAMIENTO DE TELDE with NIF P3502600D (hereinafter, the respondent/the City Council).  
The complainant requests an analysis of the legality of the implementation of clocking in via facial recognition established as a method of controlling the workday for municipal employees of the City Council, reviewing whether a prior impact assessment has been conducted before processing biometric data and to be informed whether the respondent has fulfilled the obligation to appoint a data protection officer.  
The reasons on which the complaint is based are as follows:  
- On January 19, 2022, the complainant, an employee of the municipal police of the City Council of Telde, submitted a written request to the City Council's registry, in which he requested that:  
  “He be recognized the right to clock in electronically via mobile with geolocation and consequently, the Human Resources Department provide the applicant with the necessary technical means to clock in through this procedure as it is proportional to the intended purpose, since it is less invasive regarding the fundamental right to data protection than the alternative facial recognition procedure; and under equal conditions as other municipal employees.”  
The complainant states that this request is based on the Circular of January 10, 2021, issued by the Department of Economy and Human Resources of the City Council, and the subsequent Regulation of the Full City Council, which established three possible ways of clocking in for the workday: clocking in via facial recognition, clocking in through the web, and clocking in via mobile with geolocation.  
He also indicates that this request was initially made by email to the Human Resources Department, which was denied on January 11, 2021, as this type of clocking in was reserved for specific services, indicating that in his case he should clock in using the facial recognition system at the terminal located in the municipal police facilities.  
- The request made on January 10, 2021, was denied by the City Council by resolution dated January 26, 2022, on the grounds that the clocking in system was not the employee's choice but part of the organizational authority of the City Council, which was appealed by the complainant through a request for reconsideration, which was dismissed for the same reason on April 28, 2022.  
- On May 4, 2022, the complainant requested the City Council to assign a place, date, and time for the collection of biometric data. He included a request for the data protection officer (hereinafter, DPO) to supervise compliance with data protection regulations and determine: whether a prior impact assessment has been conducted before processing biometric data, to comment on the obligation to carry out clocking in via facial recognition instead of other less invasive methods, and to ensure that at the time of collecting biometric data, all requirements established by current regulations on personal data protection are duly complied with, especially that the information provided for in Article 13 and Articles 3 and 11 of the General Data Protection Regulation is provided in writing.  
- On May 12, 2022, the City Council responded to the written request, scheduling an appointment for the collection of biometric data for May 19, 2022, without addressing the part regarding the review of the legality of the biometric systems implemented that the complainant included in his letter.  
- The complainant states that on the date scheduled for the collection of biometric data, May 19, 2022, he was verbally informed that the data collection was canceled until further notice.  
Along with the complaint, he submits the referenced documents in the complaint.  
SECOND: On December 27, 2022, a resolution of inadmissibility of the complaint was issued due to lack of sufficient evidence of the implementation of the facial recognition biometric system by the City Council.  
THIRD: On January 23, 2023, a request for reconsideration against the inadmissibility was filed, in which new documentation was submitted. This request was resolved by Resolution dated April 17, 2023, in which it was resolved to uphold the appeal and admit the complaint for processing, considering that: “along with the request for reconsideration, new documentation relevant to the issues raised has been submitted, which must be analyzed by the General Subdirectorate of Data Inspection.”

LEGAL GROUNDS  
I. Competence and procedure  
In accordance with the powers granted by Article 58.2 of the GDPR and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states that: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, this organic law, the regulatory provisions issued for its development, and, in so far as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  
II. Preliminary issues  
2.1. Description of the work schedule control system implemented by the City Council.  
According to the documentation in the procedure, and as stated by the City Council itself and the data processor, the City Council progressively implemented a new time control system called TARA, which established the attendance systems that would be applicable to control the working hours of municipal employees, through different municipal regulations:  
- On 10-1-21, the Circular from the Department of Economy, Finance, and HR was issued regarding the attendance control of municipal employees, in which it is communicated to employees that:  
“(…) A new time control system called TARA (Time Applied to Resources and Actions) will be implemented. Daily attendance in the TARA system must be carried out at the terminals installed in the respective municipal offices, only allowing attendance at the terminal located in the office where services are provided. 3.1. Regarding the risks arising from the processing of biometric personal data and personal location data.  
In accordance with the Principle of Risk Management by Design and by Default, as well as the Principle of Proactive Accountability, which inspire the new regulation, the new GDPR emphasizes that the data controller must seriously assess the risks of the processing they wish to establish concerning the rights and freedoms of the data subjects (always prior to initiating any processing, and continuously if they decide to do so), opting for a risk analysis approach by design and by default, in order to identify them, determine the likelihood of their occurrence and their impact, and foresee measures and guarantees that eliminate or, at the very least, mitigate the identified risks, preventing their occurrence. This analysis must be documented by the controller and included in a mandatory DPIA in cases where it is likely that the processing entails a "high risk" as provided for in Article 35 of the GDPR.  
This prior risk analysis is especially important when the processing of personal data is carried out by Public Administrations (hereinafter, PAs), as noted in the Guide on Technologies and Data Protection in PAs published by this Spanish Data Protection Agency:  
"The processing of personal data in PAs involves distinct risks compared to the risks of processing that any other controller may carry out, which arise, at least, from the volume of affected subjects, the extent of the data collected, the impossibility, in many cases, of opposing the processing, and the inherent power or asymmetry that exists between PAs and the citizens or data subjects whose data they process. Furthermore, regardless of the fact that all processing in PAs is guided by a spirit of public service, the same spirit that encourages the work of their employees, these potential risks could materialize for citizens in certain situations, such as, for example, situations of breakdowns in the rule of law, situations of abuse by public controllers, in circumstances of massive or selective filtering of personal data as a result of security breaches, in cases of possible legislative changes even in third countries to which the data may have been transferred, in cases of corruption, in situations of uncontrollable emergencies, etc."  
Consequently, PAs, as they are responsible for processing citizens' data, before launching new processing activities or modifying already provided services that utilize technologies they have not previously used, being the first occasion they implement them for a specific purpose, must identify the risks to which the processing may be exposed. They must also adopt the necessary technical and organizational measures that, by design and by default, allow for the elimination or at least acceptable mitigation of the risks that could arise from the processing concerning the rights and freedoms of individuals.

See just some of the special characteristics of unique identification systems based on biometric data and consider the significant impact they produce when these data are compromised, compared to other methods (signature and completion of sheets, magnetic cards, etc.):  
- Biometric data can be processed and stored in different ways. Sometimes, the biometric information captured from a person is stored and processed in raw form, allowing the source from which it comes to be recognized without special knowledge; for example, a photograph of a face, a photograph of a fingerprint, or a voice recording. Other times, the raw biometric information captured is processed in such a way that only certain characteristics or traits are extracted and saved as a biometric template, referred to here as "fingerprint minutiae" for the fingerprint; and "facial pattern" for facial recognition.  
- Unlike other personal data, biometric data are unique, permanent, or definitive over time, and the person cannot free themselves from them; they can never be changed, not even with age, which means that the damage caused in the event of compromise-loss or intrusion into the system is irreparable in this case. Unlike a password, in the event of loss, the data of our fingerprint or face cannot be changed.  
- Furthermore, because biometric data are specific to a person and perpetual, the user can use the same data across different systems, which poses an extra risk.  
- While traditional authentication methods such as passwords require a 100% match of character by character to allow the user to access, for example, an account or application (deterministic methods), biometric methods are referred to as "probabilistic," because they are based on the probability that the user attempting to access a particular device or application is the same person as the registered user. We can measure the performance of a biometric system based on three main characteristics. These are: false rejection rate (FRR), false acceptance rate (FAR), and equal error rate (ERR). The false rejection rate represents the probability of detection errors by a biometric system, meaning that it cannot recognize a user whose biometric characteristics are already in the database. In the event of a rejection, the person must verify their identity again. From a security and protection perspective, this rate does not necessarily mean that it is a negative outcome. Each biometric method, whether facial reading, fingerprint, palm print, iris, etc., has different values for different rates based on which a system rejects or accepts inputs. Error rates, as stated by the City Council, were high in this case.

3.2. On the necessity of conducting and exceeding a DPIA.  
The Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) appears as the tool required by the GDPR to ensure compliance with this aspect when processing is considered to be of "high risk," as established in Article 35, paragraph 1 of the GDPR (the emphasis is ours):  
“When it is likely that a type of processing, in particular if it uses new technologies, by its nature, scope, context, or purposes, entails a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller shall carry out, prior to the processing, an assessment of the impact of the processing operations on the protection of personal data…”  
This assessment shall be conducted prior to the commencement of processing, but it must be understood as a continuous or periodic assessment, in the sense established by Article 35.11 of the GDPR, which states: “Where necessary, the controller shall review whether the processing is in accordance with the data protection impact assessment, at least when there is a change in the risk posed by the processing operations.”  
A DPIA must meet the minimum requirements or content outlined in Article 35.7 of the GDPR, which states:  
“The assessment shall include at least:  
a) a systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes of the processing, including, where applicable, the legitimate interest pursued by the controller;  
b) an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations in relation to their purpose;  
c) an assessment of the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects referred to in paragraph 1, and  
d) the measures envisaged to address the risks, including safeguards, security measures, and mechanisms to ensure the protection of personal data, and to demonstrate compliance with this Regulation, taking into account the rights and legitimate interests of the data subjects and other persons concerned.”  
In summary, exceeding a DPIA requires that the controller of high-risk processing documents in writing that it surpasses the assessment of suitability, necessity, and proportionality of the processing, and that it manages the specific risks of the processing from the design stage, with the practical application of measures aimed at these risks in such a way as to ensure an acceptable risk threshold throughout the entire lifecycle of the processing, as established in Article 35 of the GDPR.

3.3. On the requirement that the processing is necessary, suitable, and proportional.  
Failure to carry out and complete the mandatory DPIA in this case means, among other things, that the City Council initiated the processing operations of the new time control system without previously conducting the assessment of its necessity, suitability, and proportionality as required by Articles 5.1.c) and 35.4.b) of the GDPR.  
Thus, Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR establishes the so-called “Principle of data minimization,” which states the following:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
a) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’).”  
Respect for this principle must be the starting point for the initiation of any processing, and the data controller must consider whether this processing will truly be necessary, suitable, and proportional before commencing it. Therefore, this prior analysis must be conducted before the initiation of any personal data processing; however, when there is a likelihood that the processing entails a high risk (as is the case with biometric data and location data processed in this time control system), there is an obligation to document it within the DPIA, since it must contain “b) an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations with respect to their purpose,” in accordance with Article 35.7.b) of the GDPR.

In the same vein, the Article 29 Working Party expresses this in its Opinion 3/2012 on the evolution of biometric technologies.  
The obligation to process only “the personal data that are adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed” as provided by the principle of data minimization in Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, and to assess the necessity and proportionality of the processing in the DPIA according to Article 35.7.b) of the GDPR, must be interpreted in accordance with the established jurisprudence of our Constitutional Court regarding the necessity to ascertain that any restrictive measure of fundamental rights (processing operations that include biometric and geolocation data in this case) exceeds what is referred to as “the triple test of proportionality.”

According to the definition provided by Article 4.14 of the GDPR, biometric data processed by these systems will become personal data as long as the purpose of the processing is the identification or authentication of a person, as intended in Article 4.1 of the GDPR.  
There is no doubt that the minutiae (fingerprints) and the facial pattern necessary for the biometric registration provided by the City Council are personal biometric data, since the purpose of the implemented system is to determine the identity, directly or indirectly, of the municipal employees of the claimed City Council, in order to record their entry and exit from the workplace, and to verify the time and date of the beginning and end of their workday. According to Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the processing of biometric data is prohibited when it consists of: “biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person.”  
In the specific case of facial recognition techniques (FRS), the general registration system implemented by the City Council, there is no doubt that the “facial pattern” extracted from each person allows for unique identification. The use of the face in FRS is capable of validating identity, containing unique information about natural persons. The software algorithm, based on the biometric sample (photograph of the person's face), extracts the biometric characteristics of a person's face through a photograph, reduces and transforms that sample into labels or numbers, constituting a mathematical representation of the original biometric characteristic, which is the biometric template (facial pattern or vector). The template is stored for comparison in the final phase in which, with the biometric sample - in the reader - and with the previously recorded template, it uniquely identifies the user each time they enter by placing their face in front of the reader, which is why it is considered that the data falls within the scope of special data, due to being a unique identification.

It is recorded in the file that the DPO of the City Council – whose appointment is, in this case, mandatory according to the provisions of Article 37.1.a) of the GDPR – did not adequately and timely participate in all matters related to the personal data protection raised by the new time control system TARA implemented by the City Council. That is, there is no record of the DPO's participation, nor of the prior consultation of the City Council in any of the phases of implementation of this processing, neither before, during, nor after the initiation of any of the 3 processing operations carried out to control the working hours of its municipal employees starting from 2-11-21.  

When questioned about this issue by the inspector during the investigation proceedings, the City Council clearly acknowledges that: “No report was requested from the Data Protection Delegate prior to the commencement of the aforementioned processing, although there is a report dated November 24, 2020, regarding suitability concerning the possibility of implementing labor timekeeping through facial recognition in relation to the hygiene measures that must be applied due to the health situation caused by the COVID-19 pandemic when it comes to biometric verification/authentication (one-to-one).”  

The aforementioned PRODAT Report of 24/11/2020 provided by the City Council does not contain a signature footer nor does it identify the issuing person or body. It confines its analysis to the following: “Having been consulted by the M.I. CITY COUNCIL OF TELDE regarding the suitability of implementing labor timekeeping through facial recognition, it must be analyzed whether the implementation of the facial recognition system complies with the current state regulations on the protection of personal data.”  

By providing this report, in which the issuing body or person cannot be identified, it cannot be understood as proven that this Report was issued by the DPO of the City Council, as unsigned documents lack legal validity.

VIII. Qualification and classification of the infringement of Article 38 of the GDPR.  
The violation of Article 38 of the GDPR is classified in Article 83.4.a) of the aforementioned GDPR in the following terms:  
“4. Violations of the following provisions shall be sanctioned, in accordance with paragraph 2, with administrative fines of up to 10,000,000 EUR or, in the case of an enterprise, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 2% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount:  
a) the obligations of the controller and the processor pursuant to Articles 8, 11, 25 to 39, 42, and 43.”  
For its part, the LOPDGDD in its Article 73, for the purposes of prescription, qualifies as “Serious infringements:  
According to what is established in Article 83.4 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following are considered serious and shall prescribe after two years:  
w) Failing to enable the effective participation of the data protection officer in all matters related to personal data protection, not supporting him or interfering with the performance of his duties.”

In this regard, such measures must consist of carrying out and demonstrating to this Agency within 7 months compliance with the following corrective measures concerning the compliance with Articles 9 and 35 of the GDPR:

Article 58.2 of the GDPR provides as follows:

Regarding the temporary or definitive limitation of processing, reference should be made to Article 69 of the LPACAP, which states:  
“1. During the conduct of preliminary investigation actions or the initiation of a procedure for the exercise of sanctioning authority, the Spanish Data Protection Agency may reasonably agree to the necessary and proportionate provisional measures to safeguard the fundamental right to data protection and, in particular, those provided for in Article 66.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the precautionary blocking of data and the immediate obligation to comply with the requested right.  
2. In cases where the Spanish Data Protection Agency considers that the continuation of the processing of personal data, its communication or international transfer would cause serious harm to the right to personal data protection, it may order the controllers or processors to block the data and cease its processing and, in the event of non-compliance with these mandates, proceed to immobilize it.”
