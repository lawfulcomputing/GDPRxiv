III  
Infringement of the principle of minimization  
1. The image of a natural person is a piece of personal data. Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person;”  
Recital 26 of the GDPR clarifies that “[...] To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means reasonably likely to be used by the data controller or any other person to identify the natural person, directly or indirectly, must be taken into account. To determine whether there is a reasonable likelihood that means will be used to identify a natural person, all objective factors must be considered, such as the costs and time necessary for identification, taking into account both the technology available at the time of processing and technological developments. [...]”  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines processing as “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”  
All processing of personal data must respect the principles that govern it, including, and as relevant here, the principle of minimization, according to which only data that is necessary (“limited to what is necessary”) in relation to the purposes of the processing will be processed (Article 5.1.c GDPR).  
Recital 39 of the GDPR specifies that “Personal data should only be processed if the purpose of the processing could not reasonably be fulfilled by other means.” (The emphasis is ours)

Opinion 3/2010, from the Article 29 Working Party (WP29) - WP 173 - issued during the validity of Directive 95/46/EC, which has been repealed by the GDPR, but whose reflections are applicable today, states that the “essence” of proactive accountability is the obligation of the data controller to implement measures that, under normal circumstances, ensure that data protection standards are met in the context of processing operations and to have available documents that demonstrate to data subjects and supervisory authorities what measures have been adopted to achieve compliance with data protection standards.  
Article 5.2 is developed in Article 24 of the GDPR, which obliges the controller to adopt appropriate technical and organizational measures “to ensure and be able to demonstrate” that processing is in compliance with the GDPR. The provision states:  
“Responsibility of the data controller”  
“1. Taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with this Regulation. Such measures shall be reviewed and updated as necessary.  
2. Where appropriate in relation to the processing activities, the measures referred to in paragraph 1 shall include the implementation, by the data controller, of appropriate data protection policies.  
3. Adherence to approved codes of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may be used as elements to demonstrate compliance with the obligations of the data controller.” (The emphasis is ours)  
In turn, Article 25 of the GDPR, “Data protection by design and by default,” refers to the technical and organizational measures that the data controller must adopt and provides:  
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the cost of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying probability and severity that the processing entails for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall implement, both at the time of determining the means of processing and at the time of the processing itself, appropriate technical and organizational measures, such as pseudonymization, designed to effectively apply the principles of data protection, such as data minimization, and integrate the necessary safeguards into the processing, in order to comply with the requirements of this Regulation and protect the rights of data subjects.  
2. The data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure that, by default, only personal data that are necessary for each specific purpose of the processing are processed. Such measures shall ensure in particular that, by default, personal data are not accessible, without the intervention of the data subject, to an indeterminate number of natural persons.

IV  
Sanction that could be imposed  
The corrective powers attributed to the AEPD as the supervisory authority are outlined in Article 58.2 of the GDPR, subsections a) to j). Among them, the provision includes the authority to impose an administrative fine in accordance with Article 83 of the GDPR (Article 58.2.i). It also includes the authority to “order the rectification or deletion of personal data or the limitation of processing in accordance with Articles 16, 17, and 18 and the notification of such measures to the recipients to whom personal data have been communicated in accordance with Article 17, subsection 2, and Article 19” (Article 58.2.g) and the authority to order the data controller or processor to ensure that the processing operations comply with the provisions of the GDPR, when appropriate, in a specified manner and within a specified timeframe (Article 58.2.d). Furthermore, Article 83.2 of the GDPR warns that administrative fines shall be imposed “as an additional or substitute measure for the actions contemplated in Article 58, subsection 2, letters a) to h) and j).”  

In the present case, without prejudice to the outcome of the investigation, an administrative fine could be imposed on the REAL ESTATE AGENCY for the alleged violation of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, in accordance with Article 58.2.i) of the GDPR.  

Article 83 of the GDPR, “General conditions for the imposition of administrative fines,” states in its subsection 1 that the supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of fines for the violations of this Regulation indicated in subsections 4, 5, and 6 complies in each individual case with the principles of effectiveness, proportionality, and deterrence.  

The principle of proportionality entails a correlation between the violation and the sanction, prohibiting unnecessary or excessive measures, so that it must be suitable to achieve the purposes that justify it. Article 83.2 of the GDPR determines the technique to be followed to achieve that adequacy between the sanction and the violation committed and provides a list of criteria or factors that will be taken into account to calibrate the sanction. Subsection 2 of Article 83 of the GDPR establishes:  
“Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure for the actions contemplated in Article 58, subsection 2, letters a) to h) and j). - Circumstance of Article 83.2.b) of the GDPR: “the intentionality or negligence of the infringement.”  
It should be noted at the outset that the principle of culpability governs our sanctioning law, which prevents the imposition of sanctions based on the objective responsibility of the alleged infringer. The presence of the element of culpability in a broad sense, as a condition for the emergence of sanctioning responsibility, has been recognized by the Constitutional Court, among others, in its ruling STC 76/1999, in which it states that administrative sanctions share the same nature as criminal ones as they are one of the manifestations of the State's ius puniendi and that, as a requirement derived from the principles of legal certainty and legality enshrined in Articles 9.3 and 25.1 of the Constitution, its existence is essential for imposing it. Therefore, even if there is unlawful conduct, when the data controller demonstrates having acted with the diligence that the circumstances of the case require to fulfill the obligations imposed by data protection regulations, given that objective responsibility is prohibited in our sanctioning Administrative Law, the AEPD proceeds to archive the file.

The principle of culpability is expressly enshrined in Law 40/2015 on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector, as provided in Article 28, under the heading "Responsibility":  
“1. Only individuals and legal entities, as well as, when a law recognizes their capacity to act, groups of affected parties, unions and entities without legal personality, and independent or autonomous assets, that are responsible for acts constituting administrative infractions may be sanctioned.”  
Regarding the element of culpability within the framework of the sanctioning procedure, the SAN of 30/05/2015 (Rec. 163/2014) has highlighted the differences that exist between attributing responsibility to an individual and to a legal entity, and connects the “blameworthiness” of a certain conduct to a “legal entity” with the circumstance that it “would have provided or not effective protection to the legal right protected by the norm.” The second Legal Ground of the aforementioned Judgment states:  
<<However, the manner of attributing responsibility to legal entities does not correspond with the forms of culpability that are imputable to human conduct, whether intentional or negligent. Thus, in the case of infractions committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present, it necessarily applies differently than it does with respect to individuals. According to STC 246/1999 “(…) this distinct construction of the attribution of authorship of the infraction to the legal entity arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the norms to which they are subject. The capacity for infringement and, therefore, the direct blameworthiness that derives from the legal right protected by the norm that is infringed, and the necessity for that protection to be truly effective, along with the risk that, consequently, the legal entity subject to compliance with that norm must assume”>> (Emphasis added).  
Now, in the analyzed case, not only is the element of culpability present—as indicated, an indispensable condition for demanding sanctioning responsibility—but there is also a very serious lack of diligence on the part of the respondent in fulfilling the obligations imposed by the GDPR, a serious lack of diligence that has a direct consequence on the determination of the amount of the administrative fine deemed appropriate to impose.  
In this case, it has been established that the respondent did not exercise the slightest diligence to comply with the principles governing the processing of personal data, particularly the principle of minimization. The entity in question omitted the diligence it was obliged to observe when, in the course of its business activity, it captured images of the claimant's home and collected personal data of his minor daughters (their image) that were in no case necessary for the purpose pursued by its activity and when, subsequently, it disseminated those images on its website. We refer to the SAN of 17/10/2007 (Rec. 63/2006) cited in Legal Ground III, point 3, above.
