From the procedure instructed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  

BACKGROUND  
FIRST: A.A.A. (hereinafter, the complainant) filed a complaint on August 5, 2021, with the data protection authority of Bavaria (Bavarian Lander Office for Data Protection Supervision). The complaint is directed against OPEN BANK, S.A. with NIF A-28021079 (hereinafter, OPENBANK). The reasons on which the complaint is based are as follows:  
The banking entity OPENBANK has requested the complainant to prove the origin of several amounts received in their bank account, in compliance with anti-money laundering regulations. However, no mechanism has been offered to facilitate this information encrypted or through direct upload on the web portal. The only valid option has been sending via email.  
Along with the notification, the following is attached:  
- Copy of an email sent from the address ***EMAIL.1 to ***EMAIL.2 (hereinafter, the complainant's email) dated July 7, 2021. In this email, the complainant is requested to provide the necessary documentation to prove the origin of the funds of three deposits made by the complainant, in compliance with anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing legislation; and it is indicated that, in the event of not receiving this documentation within 15 days, OPENBANK must block the execution of new payments in their account in accordance with current regulations.  
- Copy of an email sent from the complainant's email to ***EMAIL.1 dated July 10, 2021. In this email, the complainant indicates that they are providing the corresponding documentation for the year 2019 under protest via an unencrypted email because, as stated in a phone conversation, there is no possibility of sending that documentation electronically in any other way.  
- Automatic response to the previous email of July 10, 2021 sent from ***EMAIL.3 to the complainant indicating that their email has been received and they will respond soon.

Data Protection by Design and by Default  
Article 25 “Data Protection by Design and by Default” of the GDPR establishes:  
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the cost of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying probability and severity that the processing entails for the rights and freedoms of individuals, the data controller shall implement, both at the time of determining the means of processing and at the time of the processing itself, appropriate technical and organizational measures, such as pseudonymization, designed to effectively apply the principles of data protection, such as data minimization, and integrate the necessary safeguards into the processing, in order to comply with the requirements of this Regulation and protect the rights of the data subjects.  
2. The data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure that, by default, only personal data that are necessary for each specific purpose of the processing are processed. This obligation shall apply to the amount of personal data collected, the extent of their processing, the duration of their storage, and their accessibility.”

These measures will ensure, in particular, that, by default, personal data is not accessible, without the intervention of the individual, to an indeterminate number of natural persons.

IX
Security Measures
Article 32 “Security of processing” of the GDPR establishes:
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the varying likelihood and severity of risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;
d) a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.
2. In assessing the appropriateness of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the processing of data, in particular as a consequence of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data.
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this article.
4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data only processes such data on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law.”
In this case, neither in the protocol of March 2021 nor in the email sent by OPENBANK to the claimant on July 7, 2021, was any means of communication indicated for sending the documentation requested by OPENBANK. The only communication channel for sending documents was to reply to the email itself, as no other was offered to the client. 
In this specific case, OPENBANK did not provide its client with an appropriate means to submit the documentation, even despite the warnings from the claimant in this regard, so the submission was made without adequate security measures. 
This is despite the fact that documents 4 and 5 submitted by OPENBANK along with its allegations, titled “Impact Assessment - Monitoring of Clients and Sensitive Operations,” version August 2021 and October 2022, respectively, in section “13. Security” have classified the risk as high impact. Only in the October 2022 version, on page 43, was the following indication included regarding “Control and residual risk”: “It has been ensured that communication channels with clients concerning matters related to the prevention of money laundering and terrorist financing have the necessary technical measures to guarantee the protection of their personal data. Clients will be identified by their ID and access key to the private client area.”
In this regard, email cannot be considered an appropriate means to ensure a level of security adequate to the risk in the sending of documentation containing personal data provided under Chapter II of the LPBCFT, which requires special protection, taking into account the regulations on the prevention of money laundering, the nature of the data being processed, and the GDPR. 
Regarding the security of email, the “Good Practices Report” of May 2021, CNN-CERT BP02, from the National Cryptologic Center, a service attached to the National Intelligence Center, whose mission is to contribute to the improvement of Spanish cybersecurity, outlines a series of vulnerabilities of email and the various ways in which they can be attacked, as well as security recommendations. In section 4.2 of this Report, the “Security of communications via email” is described, with the following assertions on pages 37 to 39:
“The protocol involved in this sending process is SMTP. This protocol has been used since 1982 and when it was implemented, security measures were not taken into account.”

of security such as encryption or authentication of communications. This means that the entire sending process described above would be carried out in plain text, meaning that at any point in the transmission, an attacker could see and manipulate the content of the emails. Due to these shortcomings in SMTP, various technologies and extensions have been developed that allow for the incorporation of security measures to ensure authentication, integrity, and encryption of email communications. Some of the most well-known technologies are STARTTLS, SPF, DKIM, and DMARC... Although well-known email providers such as Google, Yahoo, and Outlook encrypt and authenticate emails using these types of technologies, many organizations continue to use email carelessly. It should also be noted that these technologies must be implemented both at the source and at the destination for them to be utilized. Furthermore, some of these measures are susceptible to attacks. For example, STARTTLS is vulnerable to downgrade attacks, where an attacker in a man-in-the-middle situation can force the TLS negotiation not to take place (it would be sufficient to replace the STARTTLS string). 

Even in the case that TLS communication is successfully established, the mail servers through which the email passes until it reaches its destination would have access to its content. Due to these facts, it is concluded that it is not sufficient to delegate the security of email to the underlying technologies responsible for delivering it to its recipient. 

In light of the aforementioned security shortcomings, the need to adopt reinforced measures to appropriately ensure the integrity and confidentiality of personal data sent by email is evident, especially when communicating personal data that deserves special protection, as in the present case, measures that have not been applied, which has posed a greater risk for OPENBANK clients sending personal data through this medium. 

It should be noted that the GDPR does not establish a list of security measures applicable according to the data being processed, but rather, under the principle of proactive responsibility of Article 5.2 of the GDPR itself, which entails the requirement that the data controller ensures the effective privacy and integrity of the data, both the controller and the processor shall apply technical and organizational measures that are appropriate to the risk posed by the processing, taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, and the risks of probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. 

Moreover, the controller must be in a position to demonstrate that it has implemented those measures and that they are adequate to achieve the intended purpose. 

Additionally, the security measures must be appropriate and proportionate to the identified risk, indicating that the determination of the technical and organizational measures should be made taking into account: pseudonymization and encryption, the ability to ensure confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience, the ability to restore availability and access to data after an incident, verification process (not audit), evaluation, and assessment of the effectiveness of the measures.

In any case, when assessing the adequacy of the level of security, particular attention shall be paid to the risks presented by data processing, such as the destruction, loss, or accidental or unlawful alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data that could cause physical, material, or immaterial harm.  
In this same sense, Recital 83 of the GDPR states:  
“(83) In order to maintain security and prevent processing from infringing the provisions of this Regulation, the controller or processor must assess the risks inherent in the processing and implement measures to mitigate them, such as encryption. These measures must ensure an adequate level of security, including confidentiality, taking into account the state of the art and the costs of their implementation in relation to the risks and the nature of the personal data that must be protected. When assessing the risk in relation to data security, the risks arising from the processing of personal data must be taken into account, such as the destruction, loss, or accidental or unlawful alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data, particularly those that could cause physical, material, or immaterial harm.”  
In light of the above, the technical and organizational measures applied by OPENBANK in the request for information from its clients (specifically from the claimant) in compliance with anti-money laundering regulations did not guarantee an adequate level of security commensurate with the risk, as required by Article 32 of the GDPR, due to the nature of the personal data being processed, which deserves special protection regarding its confidentiality and integrity.  
Subsidiarily, regarding the application of enhanced technical and organizational measures to the processing in question, it can be stated that the fact that a processing operation as a whole is not considered high risk and that a data protection impact assessment does not need to be carried out does not mean that appropriate security measures should not be applied to the risk presented by any of the activities or stages of the processing in question, in accordance with the provisions of Article 32 of the GDPR.  
In the processing cycle, which includes various and distinct activities, not all risks need to be uniform; there may be different levels of risks at different stages of the processing, depending on the activities that constitute it. And if there is a high risk in one phase, even if not all processing is high risk, appropriate measures should be applied.
