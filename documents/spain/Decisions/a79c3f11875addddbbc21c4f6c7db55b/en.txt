RESOLUTION OF RECOURSE FOR RECONSIDERATION
Examined the recourse for reconsideration filed by ORANGE ESPAGNE, S.A.U. (hereinafter, the appellant) against the resolution issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection dated January 31, 2023, and based on the following

FACTS
FIRST: On January 31, 2023, a resolution was issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection in file EXP202204288, by virtue of which ORANGE ESPAGNE, S.A.U. was imposed a fine of 70,000 euros (seventy thousand euros) for an infringement of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.5 of the GDPR. This resolution, which was notified to the appellant on February 2, 2023, was issued following the processing of the corresponding sanctioning procedure, in accordance with the provisions of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD), and supplementary to Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), regarding the processing of sanctioning procedures.

SECOND: As proven facts of the aforementioned sanctioning procedure, PS/00297/2022, the following was recorded:
FIRST: The complainant states that on January 17, 2022, they lost coverage on their mobile line ***TELEFONO.1, due to the fact that the entity being complained about provided a third party with a duplicate of the SIM card for that mobile line, without the consent of the corporate entity that owns the line, nor of the user (complainant).
SECOND: The complainant has provided a copy of the complaint filed with the Police and its extension dated January 18 and February 14, 2023, stating that the duplicate of the card was made on January 14, 2022, around 7 PM, according to information provided to the complainant by Orange's customer service on January 19 of the same year.
THIRD: Orange acknowledged both in the response letter dated May 27 and in the allegations of October 18, 2022, to this Agency that the incident was due to a specific human error by the Agent of the Orange Point of Sale “that on January 18, 2022, a request was made to issue a duplicate of the SIM card. This occurred through 'Attention to the Channel', a support service for employees and agents of Orange's points of sale. Thus, the incident is due to a specific human error.”
THIRD: The appellant submitted a recourse for reconsideration to this Spanish Agency for Data Protection on March 1, 2023.

ORANGE is the controller of the data processing referred to in the aforementioned background, as it determines the purposes and means of the processing carried out for the purposes indicated in its Privacy Policy, in accordance with the definition in Article 4.7 of the GDPR.  
Furthermore, the issuance of a duplicate SIM card involves the processing of the personal data of its holder, as any person whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, particularly by means of an identifier (Article 4.1) of the GDPR, will be considered an identifiable natural person.  
In Spain, since 2007, through the Single Additional Provision of Law 25/2007, of October 18, on the retention of data related to electronic communications and public communications networks, it is required that the holders of all SIM cards, whether prepaid or contract, are duly identified and registered. This is important because the identification of the subscriber will be essential to activate the SIM card, which means that when obtaining a duplicate of this, the person requesting it must also identify themselves and that their identity must match that of the holder.  
In summary, both the data processed to issue a duplicate SIM card and the SIM card (Subscriber Identity Module) that uniquely and unequivocally identifies the subscriber on the network are personal data, and their processing must be subject to data protection regulations.

Identical considerations apply to the actions of banking entities that provide payment services, within which this type of fraud begins, as the third party gains access to the credentials of the affected user and impersonates them.  
As these entities are responsible for processing their clients' data, they have the same obligations as those outlined so far for the operators regarding compliance with the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, as well as those arising from Royal Decree-Law 19/2018, of November 23, on payment services and other urgent financial measures.  
From the Proven Facts, it can be inferred that ORANGE has provided a duplicate SIM card to a third party other than the legitimate holder of the mobile line, after a third party bypassed the existing security policy, which evidences a breach of the duty to protect customer information.  
This unauthorized access to the personal data of the affected individuals is crucial for the subsequent actions carried out by the impersonators, as they take advantage of the time that elapses until the user detects the failure in the line, contacts the operator, and the operator identifies the problem, to carry out fraudulent banking operations that would have been impossible without the duplicate SIM card.  
Denying the occurrence of negligent conduct on the part of ORANGE would be equivalent to acknowledging that its behavior—by action or omission—has been diligent. Obviously, we do not share this perspective of the facts, as the lack of due diligence has been established. The SAN of October 17, 2007 (rec. 63/2006) is very illustrative; starting from the premise that these are entities whose activity involves continuous processing of customer data, it indicates that “...the Supreme Court has understood that there is imprudence whenever a legal duty of care is neglected, that is, when the offender does not behave with the required diligence. And in assessing the degree of diligence, special consideration must be given to the professionalism or lack thereof of the subject, and there is no doubt that, in the case now examined, when the activity of the appellant involves constant and abundant handling of personal data, it must be insisted upon the rigor and exquisite care to comply with the legal provisions in this regard.”  
It is established in the file that adequate security has not been guaranteed in the processing of personal data, given the outcome produced by the identity theft. That is to say, a third party has managed to access the personal data of the line holder without the security measures that ORANGE claims exist being able to prevent it. Thus, we are faced with the concurrence of typical, unlawful, and culpable conduct.

Diligence translates into the establishment of adequate measures to ensure that appropriate security measures are implemented and maintained to effectively protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of all personal data for which they are responsible, or that they have been entrusted with by another controller.  
The Constitutional Court indicated in its Judgment 94/1998, of May 4, that we are dealing with a fundamental right to data protection, which guarantees individuals control over their data, any personal data, and over its use and destination, to prevent the illicit trafficking of such data or that which is harmful to the dignity and rights of the affected individuals; thus, the right to data protection is configured as a citizen's power to oppose the use of certain personal data for purposes other than those that justified its acquisition.  
For its part, in Judgment 292/2000, of November 30, it considers it an autonomous and independent right that consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that empowers the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which data this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose such possession or use.  
Regarding ORANGE's conduct, it is considered to fall under the title of fault. As a large-scale holder of personal data, therefore, accustomed or specifically dedicated to the management of customers' personal data, it must be especially diligent and careful in its processing. That is to say, from the perspective of culpability, we are facing a remediable error, since with the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures, these identity thefts could have been avoided.  

It is Recital 74 of the GDPR that states: “The responsibility of the controller for any processing of personal data carried out by him or on his behalf must be established. In particular, the controller must be obliged to apply appropriate and effective measures and must be able to demonstrate compliance of the processing activities with this Regulation, including the effectiveness of the measures. Such measures must take into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Likewise, Recital 79 states: The protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, as well as the responsibility of controllers and processors, also with regard to the supervision by the supervisory authorities and the measures adopted by them, requires a clear allocation of responsibilities under this Regulation, including cases where a controller determines the purposes and means of processing jointly with other controllers, or where processing is carried out on behalf of a controller.”  
The computer system and the intervening technologies must be adequate to prevent identity theft and be properly configured.

It is true that there are protocols to prevent identity theft in these processes; that they have been communicated to those involved in the processing; that improvements have been introduced after identifying certain vulnerabilities; that there are penalties for non-compliance. However, we do not agree that these protocols or internal procedures can be considered adequate as they are susceptible to improvement. Regarding due diligence, it is acknowledged that ORANGE has acted diligently in minimizing the impact on potential victims by implementing new security measures to prevent the recurrence of similar incidents in the future.  
Certainly, the principle of responsibility provided for in Article 28 of the LRJSP states: “Only individuals and legal entities, as well as, when a law recognizes their capacity to act, groups of affected parties, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous estates, may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative infractions, who are responsible for them by way of intent or negligence.”  
However, the manner of attributing responsibility to legal entities does not correspond to the forms of culpability that are attributable to human conduct. Thus, in the case of infractions committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present, it is necessarily applied differently than it is with individuals.  
According to STC 246/1991, “(...) this distinct construction of the attribution of the authorship of the infraction to the legal entity arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the norms to which they are subject. Capacity to infringe and, therefore, direct reproachability that derives from the legal good protected by the norm that is infringed and the necessity for that protection to be truly effective and the risk that, consequently, the legal entity subject to compliance with that norm must assume” (in this sense, STS of November 24, 2011, Rec 258/2009).  
To the above, it should be added, following the ruling of January 23, 1998, partially transcribed in the STS of October 9, 2009, Rec 5285/2005, and of October 23, 2010, Rec 1067/2006, that “although the culpability of the conduct must also be subject to proof, it must be considered in relation to the assumption of the corresponding burden, that ordinarily the volitional and cognitive elements necessary to appreciate it form part of the proven typical conduct, and that their exclusion requires evidence of the absence of such elements, or in its normative aspect, that the diligence required by the one claiming its non-existence has been employed; it is not enough, in short, for exculpation against a behavior.”

Consequently, the lack of culpability is dismissed. The ultimate responsibility for the processing remains attributed to the data controller, who is the one that determines the existence of the processing and its purpose. Let us remember that, generally speaking, operators process their customers' data under the provisions of Article 6.1 b) of the GDPR, as it is considered necessary for the execution of a contract in which the data subject is a party (...). In this regard, ORANGE has a network of sales representatives, points of sale, and authorized distributors through a distribution contract to offer ORANGE services. Among these services offered from their points of sale is the issuance of duplicates of SIM cards corresponding to a mobile phone line.  
In the present case, it is established that Orange provided a duplicate of the claimant's SIM card to a third party, without their consent and without verifying the identity of that third party, who accessed information contained in the mobile phone, such as banking data, passwords, email address, and other personal data associated with the device. Thus, the respondent did not verify the identity of the person who requested the duplicate SIM card and did not take the necessary precautions to prevent these events from occurring.  
Based on the above, in the analyzed case, the diligence employed by the respondent to identify the person who requested a duplicate of the SIM card is called into question.  
It is established that Orange acknowledged in its response letter dated May 27 and in its statements dated October 18, 2022, to this Agency that the incident was due to a specific human error by the Orange Point of Sale Agent, who, due to the insistence and knowledge of the criminals of the company’s 'jargon'—believing they were dealing with a colleague—managed to get the Agent to reveal their credentials, violating all protocols and instructions communicated to them regarding the confidentiality of such credentials.  
In accordance with the available evidence, it is estimated that the conduct of the respondent violates Article 6.1 of the GDPR and may constitute the infringement typified in Article 83.5.a) of the aforementioned Regulation 2016/679.  
In this regard, Recital 40 of the GDPR states:  
“(40) For processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on some other legitimate basis established under law, whether in this Regulation or under another Union or Member State law to which this Regulation relates, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject or the necessity of performing a contract to which the data subject is party or for taking steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.”

Sanction  
The determination of the sanction to be imposed in this case requires observing the provisions of Articles 83.1 and 2 of the GDPR, which respectively state the following:  
“1. Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines under this article for the infringements of this Regulation referred to in paragraphs 4, 9, and 6 are, in each individual case, effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.”  
“2. Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure to those provided for in Article 58, paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j). In deciding to impose an administrative fine and its amount in each individual case, the following shall be duly taken into account:  
a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing operation in question, as well as the number of data subjects affected and the level of damage suffered;  
b) the intentionality or negligence in the infringement;  
c) any measures taken by the controller or processor to mitigate the damage suffered by data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the controller or processor, taking into account the technical or organizational measures they have implemented in accordance with Articles 25 and 32;  
e) any previous infringements committed by the controller or processor;  
f) the degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority in order to remedy the infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects of the infringement;  
g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement;  
h) the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, in particular whether the controller or processor notified the infringement and, if so, to what extent;  
i) when the measures referred to in Article 58, paragraph 2, have previously been ordered against the controller or processor in relation to the same matter, compliance with those measures;  
j) adherence to codes of conduct under Article 40 or to approved certification mechanisms under Article 42, and  
k) any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement.”

According to the transcribed provisions, for the purpose of determining the amount of the fine to be imposed on the entity claimed as responsible for an infringement classified in article 83.5.a) of the GDPR and 72.1 b) of the LOPDGDD, the following factors are considered concurrent in this case:  
As aggravating factors:  
- The evident link between the business activity of the claimed entity and the processing of personal data of clients or third parties (article 83.2.k of the GDPR in relation to article 76.2.b of the LOPDGDD).  
The Judgment of the National Court of 17/10/2007 (rec. 63/2006), which, regarding entities whose activity involves continuous processing of client data, indicates that “…the Supreme Court has understood that there is negligence whenever a legal duty of care is neglected, that is, when the infringer does not behave with the required diligence. As mitigating factors:  
The claimed party proceeded to block the line as soon as it became aware of the facts (art. 83.2 c).  
It is appropriate to graduate the sanction to be imposed on the claimed party and set it at the amount of €70,000 for the alleged infringement of article 6.1) classified in article 83.5.a) of the aforementioned GDPR.
