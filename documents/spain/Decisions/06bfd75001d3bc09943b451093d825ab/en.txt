LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
The Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to resolve this appeal, in accordance with the provisions of Article 123 of the LPACAP and Article 48.1 of the LOPDGDD.

Article 8.2 of the LOPDGDD states that “The processing of personal data may only be considered based on the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of public powers conferred on the controller, in the terms provided for in Article 6.1 e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, when it derives from a competence attributed by a norm with the rank of law.”  
Since the actions of the media do not derive from a competence attributed by a norm with the rank of law, but from the exercise of the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information regulated in Article 20.1.d) of the Constitution, the processing carried out by the appellant is not legitimized by Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR, and therefore the invoked judgment is not applicable to the present case.  
On the other hand, Judgment 10747/2020 of the National Court, dated September 9, 2022, was already invoked by the appellant during the processing of the sanctioning procedure. Regarding it, the Legal Basis IV of the resolution now appealed indicates that “the aforementioned Judgment of the National Court of September 9, 2022 is also not applicable to the present case, in which it was not judged whether there had been a violation of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, which is the one under study in the present procedure, but whether there had been a violation of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR.”  
Notwithstanding the above, this does not mean that the resolution now appealed denies that the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data is not an absolute right, as was indicated earlier. In fact, in the same line as the judgment of the National Court, Contentious Chamber, of September 21, 2021 (rec. 1986/2019), invoked by the appellant, Legal Basis XII of the resolution states that:  
“In the conflict between the Fundamental Rights to Freedom of Information in relation to the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, even when equal degrees of protection are recognized for both constitutional rights, ordinarily the former tends to be given precedence by our courts, after evaluating and weighing all the elements at play.  
However, preponderance does not mean prevalence when, considering all the circumstances present in a specific case, the limits set by regulations and jurisprudence are exceeded.”

Subsequently pointing out in its Legal Basis XIV that “this is not, as in other jurisprudentially examined cases, about giving precedence to one fundamental right over another, having to choose which one weighs more in a specific case. In short, the appellant, as the data controller, has committed an infringement of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, as it could have opted for means of publishing the news that did not involve the dissemination of the victim's voice without distortion. That is, it could have exercised the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information without needing to violate the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data of the victim.  
Therefore, the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information of the appellant has not been violated, so the contested resolution does not suffer from nullity in accordance with Article 47.1.a) of the LPACAP.  
III  
Response to the allegation regarding the voice as personal data  
In relation to the statements made by the appellant regarding the consideration of the voice as personal data, reiterating basically the allegations already presented throughout the sanctioning procedure, it should be noted that all of them have already been analyzed and dismissed in Legal Bases IV and V of the resolution now being appealed.  
The voice of any person is a personal data and identifies or makes them identifiable in a unique way, regardless of any additional data that may exist in the case. Thus, the publication of the personal data of the victim's voice alone and without distortion put them at a certain risk of being identified by individuals who were unaware of their status as a victim.  
Among the allegations of the appellant, special attention should be paid, due to its novelty, to the invocation of report 190/2009 of the AEPD regarding, as stated by the appellant, that “the voice is not a personal data per se.” However, the cited report does not make such a statement at any time. Moreover, report 139/2017 of the Legal Department of this Agency states that “the image, as well as the voice of a person, is a personal data, just as any information that allows determining, directly or indirectly, their identity will be…” a report referred to in Legal Basis X of the resolution now being appealed.

Well, Article 83.2 of the GDPR establishes that, among other circumstances, the following must be taken into account for the imposition of the administrative fine: ‘e) any previous infringement committed by the data controller or the data processor.’ This is an aggravating circumstance; the fact that the prerequisite for its application does not occur means that it cannot be taken into consideration, but it does not imply or allow, as the claimant intends, its application as a mitigating factor.’” (Legal Basis IX)  
The following statement from the appellant is novel: “According to Article 76 of the LOPD, in relation to point k) of Article 83.2 of the GDPR, there are also some circumstances that must be taken into account to modulate the eventual liability of ABC, namely that the infringement did not have a continuous nature as the news was removed, in addition to the fact that the informative activity carried out by ABC is undoubtedly linked to the processing of personal data.”  
Article 29.6 of Law 40/2015, of October 1, on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector establishes that “the carrying out of a plurality of actions or omissions that infringe the same or similar administrative provisions, in execution of a preconceived plan or taking advantage of the same opportunity, shall be sanctionable as a continued infringement.”  
In the present case, the video was published on April 6, 2021, and it was known to be removed on May 17, 2021, the day on which the AEPD notified the urgent content removal order. That is to say, the video with the victim's voice without distortion was available for just over a month. Thus, we are faced with a single action characterized by its durability over time, as indicated in Legal Basis V of the contested resolution, since while the news was published, it remained online, making it possible to access its content, and therefore the victim's voice without distortion.  
In other words, we are dealing with a permanent infringement, as indicated by the Supreme Court ruling of October 23, 2015 (Sec. 3, rec 384/2013): “[P]ermanent infringements are distinguished from continued ones in that in the former, the offender carries out a single punishable action, but this action is characterized by its prolongation in time, so that the consummation of the infringement begins at the moment the offender carries out the typical action, but what could be called the period of consummation does not stop there but persists from that initial period until the offender terminates the typical conduct constitutive of the infringement, so that the consummation of the crime perpetuates until the conduct ends. It has been said, therefore, that in permanent infringements, by the will of the author, the typical action is continuously renewed, thus maintaining the unlawful situation until the offender puts an end to it.”  
However, while we are not faced with a continued infringement, it should be noted that, just as the aforementioned circumstance regulated in Article 83.2.e) of the GDPR is considered by the aforementioned Judgment of the National Court,
