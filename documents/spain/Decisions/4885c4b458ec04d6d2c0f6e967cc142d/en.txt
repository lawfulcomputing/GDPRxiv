2. The regulations regarding the protection of personal data allow you to exercise your rights of access, rectification, opposition, deletion, limitation of processing, portability, and not to be subject to individualized decisions before the data controller.  
Therefore, this right can be exercised before the controller by requesting the deletion of your personal data when any of the circumstances established in Article 17 of the GDPR are met.

It should be remembered, as stated in the proven facts, that on 25/08/2020 the same claimant sent a letter to the respondent informing them of the harassment he had been suffering on his mobile phone line, demanding payment of a debt arising from the financing of a vehicle, and threatening that failure to pay would result in being included in solvency files, commonly referred to as delinquency files; financing that he claimed to be unaware of, indicating “That, since the past 22nd of July of this year... he has been harassed by the company we are addressing, through calls and SMS sent to the phone line associated with the number ***TELÉFONO.2, requesting the cancellation of his data, and the response was that ‘with the data provided, no person appears as a client of this entity.’  
One of the main innovations and the principal concept presented by the GDPR is the principle of proactive responsibility. This principle is defined as the necessity for the data controller to apply appropriate technical and organizational measures in order to ensure and demonstrate that the processing of personal data is in compliance with the Regulation. In other words, it is not enough to comply with data protection regulations; it is also necessary to be able to demonstrate that compliance is being achieved.  
This principle is enshrined in Article 5.2 of the GDPR, which states:  
“2. The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and shall be able to demonstrate it (‘proactive responsibility’).”  
This principle therefore requires a conscious and diligent attitude towards all personal data processing activities carried out, an attitude that has not existed in the present case since, in response to the letter sent by the claimant on 25/08/2020, the claimant should have acted with greater diligence and proactivity in order to preserve his rights regarding the processing of his personal data and its legitimacy, which is the opposite of what occurred on 18/04/2022 when the AEPD sent him its second request, asking for information related to the phone number ***TELÉFONO.2 belonging to the claimant.  
And that in light of these actions, the respondent had proceeded to delete the claimant's phone number before responding to the request, a measure that should have been carried out in light of the claimant's letter, verifying and checking that he was not a client of the entity and that he could not belong to TRANSPORTES A.A.A., S.L., and from that moment on, the claimant's personal data should not have been subject to processing as the respondent was not legitimized to do so, having neither consent nor authorization for it.

5. Regarding the absence of culpability alleged by the respondent, it should be noted that the principle of culpability is required in the sanctioning procedure, and thus the STC 246/1991 considers a responsibility without fault inadmissible in the field of sanctioning administrative law.

The Supreme Court (STS April 16, 1991, and STS April 22, 1991) considers that from the element of culpability it follows “that the action or omission, classified as an administratively sanctionable infringement, must, in any case, be attributable to its author, either by intent or negligence, imprudence, or excusable ignorance.” The same Court reasons that “it is not enough...for exculpation against a typically unlawful behavior to invoke the absence of fault,” but rather it is necessary “that the diligence required by the one claiming its non-existence has been employed.” (STS January 23, 1998).  
This doctrine is further elaborated in STC (Second Chamber) 129/2003, of June 30, 2003, which, citing the previous STC 246/1991, states in its F.J. 8:  
(...) the admission in our administrative sanctioning law of the direct responsibility of legal entities, thus recognizing their infringing capacity, entails that responsibility is configured based on the capacity for infringement and the responsibility, “which derives from the legal right protected by the norm that is infringed and the necessity for that protection to be truly effective... and from the risk that, consequently, the legal entity subject to compliance with that norm must assume” (STC 246/1991, FJ 2). In the present case, given that there was evidentiary activity against the facts attributed to the now-appellant company, it was up to them to provide the administrative bodies that intervened in the processing of the file with a principle of proof, however minimal, that would allow them to think that the infringement of the norm was not attributable to them (...).  
Therefore, the entity has suffered from a serious lack of diligence as evidenced by the fact that its internal procedures or action protocols were ineffective and were unable to detect that the data provided by the claimant and the AEPD, especially their phone number, did not belong to the actual debtor, Transportes A.A.A., S.L., with whom a loan contract had been made, and that the non-payment notices continued to be sent to their mobile phone, demanding payment of a debt that did not belong to them.  
Regarding the good faith alleged, it should be noted that the National Court in its rulings, among others that of 05/24/02, has stated that “good faith in action, to justify the absence of fault –as is done in the present case–; it is sufficient to say that this allegation is undermined when there is a specific duty of vigilance derived from the professionalism of the infringer. In this traditional line of reasoning, the STS of March 12, 1975, and March 10, 1978, reject the allegation of good faith when the infringer is burdened with duties of vigilance and diligence derived from their professional status.”

As provided, it must be developed considering, in any case, the concurrent circumstances, in order to achieve the necessary and due proportion between the alleged facts and the responsibility required, given that any sanction must be determined in accordance with the gravity of the infringement committed and according to a criterion of proportionality in relation to the circumstances of the act. Thus, proportionality constitutes a normative principle that is imposed on the Administration and that reduces the scope of its sanctioning powers.  
Well, in accordance with the circumstances present in this case, the resolution does not violate the principle of proportionality in determining the imposed sanction, resulting in a balanced and proportionate response to the severity of the infringement committed, the importance of the facts, as well as the circumstances taken into account to grade the sanction, without any reasons being appreciated that further justify the reduction made, especially considering the amount to which such a sanction may ascend in accordance with Article 83.5.b) of the RGDP, which provides for an infringement of Article 17 of the RGDP, "… administrative fines of up to 20,000,000 EUR or, in the case of a company, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 4% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount."  
Therefore, it is considered that the imposed sanction is highly balanced and proportionate to the severity of the facts presented in the contested resolution.
