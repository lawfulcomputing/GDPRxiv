Examined the reconsideration appeal filed by RUBIATO PAREDES, S.L, with NIF B85227635 (hereinafter, the appellant or RUBIATO) against the resolution issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection on 07/03/2023, and based on the following  

FACTS  
FIRST: On 07/03/2023, a resolution was issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection in file EXP202100106, by virtue of which two administrative fines were imposed on the appellant for two infringements of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (hereinafter, GDPR), and, under Article 58.2.d) of the GDPR, a corrective measure:  
- Administrative fines:  
1. For an infringement of Article 9 of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.5.a) of the GDPR, a fine of €20,000 (twenty thousand euros) was imposed.  
2. For an infringement of Article 6.1. of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.5.a) of the GDPR, a fine of €10,000 (ten thousand euros) was imposed.  
- Corrective measure:  
It was agreed to “Order RUBIATO PAREDES, in accordance with Article 58.2.d) of the GDPR, to proceed to delete the data of its employees related to vaccination against Covid-19 and to certify compliance within one month from the date the sanctioning resolution became enforceable. To this end, a report signed by the legal representative of the company must be submitted, along with more detailed documentation to prove compliance with the imposed measure.”  
This resolution, which was notified to the appellant on 08/03/2023, was issued following the processing of the corresponding sanctioning procedure, in accordance with the provisions regarding the processing of sanctioning procedures in Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD) and, subsidiarily, in Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (LPACAP).

1. The arguments put forth by the appellant in their defense are, essentially, a reiteration of those made during the processing of the sanctioning file. The most notable considerations presented in the appeal document are examined below.  
1.1. Through the first allegation, the appellant defends the legality of the processing of health data related to the vaccination status of its employees, which has been carried out through its employee, Ms. A.A.A., who was appointed in 2020 as the coordinator for occupational risk prevention. The issues raised within the framework of this allegation, in general terms, are analyzed and addressed in the Grounds III to VI of the contested resolution.  
The appellant considers, as they stated during the sanctioning procedure, that the general prohibition on processing health data established in Article 9.1 of the GDPR was lifted under Article 9.2.b) of the GDPR, in connection with Law 31/1995, of November 8, on the Prevention of Occupational Risks (LPRL), and that the legal basis for the processing would be constituted by Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR in connection with the aforementioned LPRL, Article 22.4.  
The appellant does not introduce new arguments to support the claimed legality of the processing. The new considerations made within the framework of the first allegation of the appeal are either irrelevant, as they have nothing to do with the legality of the processing, or pertain to the particular "interpretation" that they have made of the applicable LPRL regulations, or they include statements that do not align with the reality of the examined facts.  
- In one of the paragraphs of the first allegation, the appellant seems to imply—given the ambiguity of the wording, as it links a comment with the text of a provision—that Ms. A.A.A., who was appointed "coordinator and point of contact" between RUBIATO and the external prevention service, had not come to know the "personal" vaccination status of RUBIATO employees but only the "conclusions," as it states:  
<<the coordinator for occupational risk prevention only has knowledge of the "conclusions derived from the assessments made regarding the worker's fitness for the performance of their job or the need to introduce or improve protective and preventive measures" (Article 22.4 of the LPRL).>> (The underlining is ours)  
Regardless of what was intended to be expressed in that paragraph, the fact is that it does not correspond to the factual situation that was subject to evaluation in the contested resolution. That paragraph refers to a situation in which the occupational risk prevention services conduct an examination and assessment of the worker's fitness for the performance of their job and subsequently provide access to their "conclusions" to the occupational risk coordinator.  
However, neither the transcribed paragraph nor the mentioned provision of the LPRL corresponds to the factual scenario presented here, in which it was Ms. A.A.A. who, from her corporate account (***USUARIO.1@rubiatoparedes.com), sent an email to the employee staff on 07/06/2021 signed by her, in which

Royal Decree 39/1997, which approves the Regulation for the development of the LPRL, whose Chapter III is dedicated to the “Organization of resources for preventive activities,” establishes in Article 10, “Modalities”:  
“1. The organization of the necessary resources for the development of preventive activities shall be carried out by the employer according to one of the following modalities:  
a) Personally assuming such activity.  
b) Designating one or more workers to carry it out.  
c) Constituting an own prevention service.  
d) Resorting to an external prevention service.” (The underlining is ours)  
As stated in the Sixth Proven Fact of the contested resolution, the appellant was asked during the evidentiary phase what model of organization of preventive activity it had implemented according to the Regulation for the development of the LPRL (Royal Decree 39/1997) and stated that it has the services of the entity PREVENACTIVA, S.L.U., in all its centers, by virtue of a contract signed on 26/05/2015 in which this entity had assumed responsibility.

4. Nature of the data regarding the vaccination status of employees that has been processed by RUBIATO: Health-related data.  
It is necessary to analyze whether the personal data concerning vaccination that RUBIATO collected from its workers falls under the category of "health-related data," which would subject it to the specific regime outlined in Article 9 of the GDPR. This is not a trivial matter, as this provision establishes a particular legal regime for special categories of data, whose "general rule" (paragraph 1) is precisely the prohibition of their processing.  
Article 9 of the GDPR, under the heading "Processing of special categories of personal data," specifies in paragraph 1 which data has this character and includes among them those "related to health." The provision states:  
“1. The processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data aimed at uniquely identifying a natural person, health-related data, or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation is prohibited.” (Emphasis added)  
In turn, the GDPR, Article 4.15, defines "health-related data" as "personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status." (Emphasis added)  
In contrast to the definition of "personal data related to health" provided by Article 5.g) of the Regulation implementing Organic Law 15/1999 on the Protection of Personal Data (LOPD), approved by Royal Decree 1720/2017 (RLOPD), the GDPR (Article 4.15) incorporates into the special category of health-related data "the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status." Recital 35 of the GDPR refers to them in the following terms:  
<<All personal data related to the health of the data subject that provide information about their past, present, or future physical or mental health status should be included among health-related personal data. This includes information about the individual collected during their registration for health care purposes, or during the provision of such care, in accordance with Directive 2011/24/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council (1); any number, symbol, or data assigned to a natural person that uniquely identifies them for health purposes; information obtained from tests or examinations of a part of the body or bodily substance, including that derived from genetic data and biological samples, and any information related, for example, to a disease, a disability, the risk of suffering from diseases, medical history, clinical treatment, or the physiological or biomedical status of the data subject, regardless of its source, for example, a doctor or other health professional, a hospital, a medical device, or an in vitro diagnostic test.>> (Emphasis added)

In short, recital 35 of the GDPR states that “the information about the natural person collected in connection with their registration for health care purposes, or in connection with the provision of such care, in accordance with Directive 2011/24/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council” is included in the special category of health data.  
Directive 2011/24/EU, to which recital 35 refers, indicates in its Article 3, “Definitions,” what is understood by: “a) ‘health care’: the services related to health provided by a health professional to patients to assess, maintain, or restore their state of health, including the prescription, dispensing, and provision of medicines and medical products;” (The underlining is ours).  
Article 3 of Directive 2011/24/EU also determines, in paragraphs f) and i), respectively, what is understood by “health professional” and “medicines.”  
Paragraph f) defines “health professional” as “any doctor of medicine, nurse responsible for general care, dentist, midwife, or pharmacist in accordance with the provisions of Directive 2005/36/EC or any other professional who carries out activities in the health care sector that are restricted to a regulated profession as defined in Article 3, paragraph 1, letter a), of Directive 2005/36/EC, or any person considered a health professional under the legislation of the Member State of treatment;” (The underlining is ours).  
In this regard, it is worth noting that Law 44/2003, of November 21, on the regulation of the health profession, qualifies as “health professionals,” among others, the Graduates in Nursing (Article 2) and the Technicians in Auxiliary Nursing Care (Article 3).  
And paragraph i) of Article 3 of Directive 2011/24/EU understands “medicine” as “any medicine as defined in Directive 2001/83/EC.” In turn, Directive 2001/83/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, of November 6, 2001, establishing a community code on medicines for human use, in its Article 1.2 understands “medicine” as “a) any substance or combination of substances presented as having properties for the treatment or prevention of diseases in human beings, or b) any substance or combination of substances that may be used in, or administered to human beings for the purpose of restoring, correcting, or modifying physiological functions by exerting a pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic action, or of making a medical diagnosis.” (The underlining is ours).  
In light of the provisions cited, the vaccination of a person against Covid-19 involves the provision of a health care service; a service provided by those who, according to Spanish legislation, Law 44/2003, are considered health professionals and through which a medicine is dispensed, as defined by Article 1.2 of Directive 2001/83/EC.  
Therefore, the information about whether an identified natural person has received or not received the Covid-19 vaccine has the nature of personal data related to the
24/47  
health, framed within the category of special or sensitive data regulated in Article 9 of the GDPR.  
Thus, the conduct contrary to the GDPR for which RUBIATO is held responsible is specified in the processing of health data of workers, data related to the vaccination of individuals with whom it maintained an employment relationship, which it accessed through the responses given to the message sent on 07/06/2021, which, as outlined in the following Grounds, violates Articles 9 and 6.1 of the GDPR.

As previously indicated, the GDPR, Article 9.1, generally prohibits the processing of "special data," including data related to health. However, subsection 2 of the provision introduces ten exceptions; ten scenarios in which the prohibition on processing may be lifted if any of them apply. These circumstances that exempt the general prohibition rule are connected to "any" of the legal bases that, according to Article 6.1 of the GDPR, legitimize the processing of data.  
RUBIATO has claimed that the processing carried out is covered by the circumstance of Article 9.2.b) of the GDPR, in connection with the LPRL, particularly its Article 14, which we referred to in the previous Legal Basis, and has repeatedly mentioned the context of the health emergency, citing Recitals 46 and 52 of the GDPR for this purpose.

Article 9.2 a) GDPR refers to the explicit consent of the data subject: "the data subject has given explicit consent to the processing of those personal data for one or more specified purposes, except where Union or Member State law provides that the prohibition referred to in paragraph 1 may not be lifted by the data subject."  
Consent is defined in Article 4.11 of the GDPR as "any freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes by which, by a statement or a clear affirmative action, signifies agreement to the processing of personal data relating to him or her."  
For consent to be "valid" (Guidelines 5/2020, of May 4, from the European Data Protection Board, EDPB), it must have been given freely, be specific, and informed.  
"The element of 'freely' implies a genuine choice and control for the data subjects. As a general rule, the GDPR prescribes that if the data subject has no real option, feels compelled to consent, or will suffer negative consequences if they do not consent, then the consent will not be valid." "[...], consent shall not be considered freely given if the data subject cannot refuse or withdraw their consent without detriment."

Freedom cannot be granted if there is a “clear imbalance” between the data subject and the data controller. In this regard, Recital 42 of the GDPR states:  
“Consent should not be considered freely given when the data subject does not have genuine or free choice or cannot refuse or withdraw consent without detriment.” And Recital 43 adds that, “To ensure that consent has been freely given, it should not constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of personal data in a specific case where there is a clear imbalance between the data subject and the data controller, [...].” (The emphasis is ours)  
Guidelines 5/2020 emphasize the element of freedom, necessary for the validity of consent, and specify that “The ‘free’ element implies a genuine choice and control for the data subjects.” and that, “Consequently, consent will not be considered free if the data subject cannot refuse or withdraw consent without detriment.” Regarding the imbalance between the data controller and the data subject, Guidelines 5/2020 make the following considerations:  
“There is also a power imbalance in the employment context. Given the dependency resulting from the employer/employee relationship, it is unlikely that the data subject can deny consent for data processing without experiencing the fear or real risk of detrimental effects as a result of a denial. [...] Therefore, the EDPB considers it problematic for employers to process the personal data of current or future employees based on consent, as it is unlikely to be given freely. For most of this data processing in the workplace, the legal basis cannot and should not be the consent of the workers (Article 6(1)(a)), due to the nature of the relationship between employer and employee.”  
In light of the above, it cannot be accepted that, in the case presented, the employees who provided their health data to RUBIATO in response to the received email had genuine freedom of choice.  
It is taken into account, following the EDPB's criteria, that there is a clear and overwhelming imbalance between the data controller and the data subject. Section (c) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR states: “the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller.”  
The first element that integrates this circumstance is the necessity of the processing carried out, an expression that should be understood as equivalent to essential and not as advisable or convenient. The term “necessity” has its own and independent meaning in community legislation. The Court of Justice of the European Union considers it to be an “autonomous concept of Community Law” (CJEU ruling of 16/12/2008, case C-524/2006, paragraph 52) and the European Court of Human Rights states that the “adjective necessary is not synonymous with ‘indispensable’ nor does it have the flexibility of the expressions ‘admissible,’ ‘ordinary,’ ‘useful,’ ‘reasonable,’ or ‘desirable’” (paragraph 97 of the ECtHR ruling of 25/03/1983).
