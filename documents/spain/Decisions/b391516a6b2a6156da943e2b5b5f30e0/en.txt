II  
Preliminary Issues  
The physical image of a person, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is a personal data and its protection is therefore subject to said Regulation. Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines the concept of "processing" of personal data.  
“‘processing’: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, restriction, erasure or destruction;”  
Images captured by a system of cameras or video cameras are personal data, so their processing is subject to data protection regulations, unless it is domestic processing.  
In this case, the claim dated 25/11/22 is examined, whereby the main fact reported is the “presence of a camera system without the proper signage affecting a public transit area.”  
The facts are specified according to the alleged capture of images of the adjacent public road, as well as the presence of cameras in the area of the establishment designated for recreation and service area for customers (e.g., main dining room).  
Individuals are responsible for ensuring that the installed video surveillance systems comply with current legislation, and must be able to demonstrate such compliance to the competent Authority.  
It is therefore pertinent to analyze whether the processing of personal data (image of natural persons) carried out through the reported video surveillance system is in accordance with the provisions of the GDPR.  
III  
Non-compliance with the obligation of Article 5.1.c) GDPR. Qualification of the infringement  
In accordance with the evidence available at this moment of the initiation agreement of the sanctioning procedure, and without prejudice to what results from the instruction, it is considered that the party being claimed has a video surveillance camera system that shows signs of disproportion in the capture of images.  
Article 22.1 of the LOPDGDD establishes that “1. Natural or legal persons, public or private, may carry out the processing of images through camera or video camera systems for the purpose of preserving the safety of persons and property, as well as their facilities.”  
The application of this provision is linked to what is established by Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, the so-called “data minimization principle.” This article states that “Personal data shall be (…) c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.”  
This implies that one of the obligations that the data controller has is to carry out the processing in accordance with its specific purpose and that the data processed for the achievement of that purpose are adequate, relevant, and limited in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.  
For the case of data processing for video surveillance purposes, in accordance with the provisions of Article 22 of the LOPDGDD, the intended purpose of preserving persons, property, and facilities must only encompass the adequate, relevant, and limited personal data in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.  
Based on what has just been indicated, it can be concluded that the installation of video surveillance cameras would be a proportional and justified measure if the following requirements are met:  
1. That it is a measure capable of achieving the proposed objective.  
2. That there is no other, more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness.  
3. That it is balanced or weighed, as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values.

Therefore, the person responsible for an establishment that carries out processing for the purposes of video surveillance as described above may install video surveillance cameras for the previously mentioned purpose only in places and under circumstances appropriate to preserve security within the establishment itself.  
When analyzing proportionality, special attention must be paid to whether the measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective and whether other less intrusive means can be adopted in relation to the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, as this involves preserving the power of disposition and control of personal data by the data subject, as well as preventing interference with other fundamental rights and freedoms such as individuals' privacy or the free development of their personality.  
Regarding proportionality, the Judgment of the Constitutional Court 207/1996, of December 16, establishes that it is "a common and constant requirement for the constitutionality of any restrictive measure of fundamental rights, including those that entail an interference with the rights to physical integrity and privacy, and particularly in the case of restrictive measures of fundamental rights adopted during a criminal process, which is determined by the strict observance of the principle of proportionality."  
The impact on the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data can only arise from the achievement of the intended purpose of the processing and subject to the principle of minimization, as mere invocation is not sufficient to sacrifice the fundamental right. Given the preeminent position of fundamental rights in our legal system, such modulation will only occur to the extent strictly necessary to achieve the purposes of the processing in accordance with the aforementioned rules. This entails the need to carry out an adequate balancing that respects the correct definition and constitutional valuation of the fundamental right at stake and the interest pursued with the processing.  
Therefore, once it is determined that processing for the purposes of video surveillance aimed at ensuring the safety of persons, property, and facilities is, in the specific case, a measure capable of achieving the proposed objective and that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness, it must be assessed that it is balanced or proportionate.  
In the examined case, as a result of the submission of a complaint, it is noted from the documents in the file that the data controller has installed a video surveillance system for the purpose provided in Article 22 of the LOPDGDD.  
The area captured by the cameras includes the tables of the restaurant where diners are located during their visit, in addition to the cash register area and the entrance door to the establishment.

The capture of the area of the cash register and the access door to the premises does not constitute a violation of the GDPR.  
Although it is observed that the camera focusing on the door of the premises captures a portion of the public road, such capture is proportionate as it only encompasses the essential part necessary to achieve the purpose of ensuring the safety of individuals, property, and facilities, in accordance with Article 22.2 of the LOPDGDD.  
However, the same does not occur regarding the capture of the tables in the restaurant where diners are located when they visit, as inferred from the evidence available at this stage of the procedure, since it involves a treatment that captures images constantly, covering all the tables that make up the dining area.  
By its very nature, the area of the dining tables is a location where the individuals affected by the treatment may remain for a long time, and in a situation where their Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data may be affected, as well as other rights and freedoms, such as their privacy or the free development of their personality, especially since these places are typically visited during leisure time.  
Furthermore, there is no special reason justifying the surveillance of places such as restaurant tables concerning the security of the establishment, as might exist in relation to confined spaces such as the entrance/exit or the cash register. In this sense, the permanent capture of the room and the tables when customers are present visiting the restaurant would not comply with the requirements of the principle of data minimization, which stipulates that personal data processing should be "adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed."  
In this regard, it is important to note that, according to the information provided by the responsible party, the captured images are retained for a period of fifteen days (document in response to the Agency) or one month (informative document about the cameras in the premises). Thus, the data of all diners are stored during that period. The impact on the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, understood as the power of disposition and control held by the data subject, is far greater than the alleged interest that the respondent seeks to achieve, which is the preservation of the safety of property and individuals in the area of the restaurant tables.  
From the information and documentation in the file, the respondent has not substantiated the necessity of carrying out this personal data processing based on the purpose concerning the area of the diners' tables, and according to the circumstances described above. Therefore, it cannot be concluded that it respects the data minimization principle established in Article 5.1(c) of the GDPR.

c) any measures taken by the controller or processor to mitigate the damage and harm suffered by the data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the controller or processor, taking into account the technical or organizational measures they have implemented under Articles 25 and 32;  
e) any previous infringement committed by the controller or processor;  
f) the degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority in order to remedy the infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects of the infringement;
