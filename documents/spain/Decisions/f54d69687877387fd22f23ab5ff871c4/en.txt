BACKGROUND  
FIRST: Complaints filed.  
On 4/11/2022, the Spanish Agency for Data Protection became aware through a complaint of certain facts that could violate data protection legislation.  
The complainant states that the Permanent Commission of the State Commission against Violence, Racism, Xenophobia, and Intolerance in Sport (CEVRXID), dependent on the Higher Sports Council (C.S.D.), has adopted an agreement whereby access to the cheering stands in football stadiums will be carried out through biometric control. It adds that some football teams are already adopting these forms of access, even for access to other areas of the stadium.  
The complainant believes that the processing carried out constitutes an "excess in the sense of Article 5.1 c) of the GDPR," and that "the same access controls can be carried out through nominal subscriptions and, if necessary for security reasons, by requesting the presentation of the ID card."  
Subsequently, on 22/03/2023, a new complaint (registration number ***REFERENCE.1) was acknowledged, pointing to the implementation by the NATIONAL PROFESSIONAL FOOTBALL LEAGUE (LNFP), through the GENERAL REGULATION OF THE NATIONAL PROFESSIONAL FOOTBALL LEAGUE (RGLNFP), of access control mechanisms to football stadiums based on biometric data. According to a news article accompanying ***URL.1., access via facial recognition has been implemented at the stadium "***STADIUM.1."  
This complainant expresses that these actions involve the processing of special categories of personal data, and in such cases, Article 9 of the GDPR must be complied with. A copy of the RGLNFP (version approved by the C.S.D. in the session of 7/11/2022) is provided, in which BOOK XII regulates the "Regulation for the sale of subscriptions and tickets" (RVAE) and Article XII.2 "conditions for the sale of subscriptions in cheering stands."  
SECOND: Agreement on preliminary investigation actions  
As a result of the complaint, the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection agreed on 2/1/2023 to carry out preliminary investigation actions in accordance with the provisions of Article 67 of Organic Law 3/2018, of 5/12, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD), in order to achieve a better determination of the facts and circumstances that justify the processing of the procedure.  
THIRD: Initiation of preliminary actions  
The General Subdirectorate for Data Inspection proceeded to carry out preliminary investigation actions to determine the facts in question, EXP202213792, AI/00444/2022, by the assignment of the functions attributed to the control authorities in Article 57.1 and the powers granted in Article 58.1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and in accordance with the provisions established in Title VII, Chapter I, Section two, of the LOPDGDD.  
3.1 Request for information from the LNFP  
On 17/02/2023, the LNFP was requested regarding the access controls to the stadiums of the Clubs and SAD (sports public limited companies) that are members of the LNFP, the following:  
3.1.1. Provide the set of rules, instructions, recommendations, etc., approved by the LNFP and currently in force regarding the various aspects of personal data processing (principles, bases of legitimacy, special categories of data, duty of information, rights of the data subjects, retention periods, etc.) resulting from the access control to the stadiums of its members. Indicate for each of the referred rules the date from which it has been in force, whether it is of a recommendation or obligation nature, and whether the approval of its terms derives from the promulgation of state regulations.  
The LNFP responded on 10/03/2023.  
It cites Article 56.3 of Law 39/2022 on Sport, of 30/12, BOE 31/12/2022, effective from the day following its publication (LD 2022), the ratification by the C.S.D. of the social statutes and regulations of the LNFP. It states that the regulations approved by the LNFP for the proper development of the competition do not stem from its mere unilateral will but from its approval by the C.S.D., constituting administrative acts emanating from that body, and are therefore subject to administrative contentious appeal.  
It indicates that the LNFP lacks the capacity to adopt regulatory norms for the competitions subject to it, requiring the endorsement of the Public Administration. It considers that all the requested norms were approved by the C.S.D.  
It cites the doctrine of the Supreme Court ruling of 19/02/2020, cassation appeal 670/2018 as an example of the legal nature of the LNFP in a matter resolved in the exercise of its competencies by the Basketball Clubs Association (Professional Basketball League) due to the refusal to register a Club, taken in development of its Regulation.  
The issue was whether the C.S.D. was competent in appeals against resolutions adopted by the aforementioned Association.

3.2 Request for information to the C.S.D.  
On 17/02/2023, a request was made to the C.S.D.:  
3.2.1 To inform whether provisions have been adopted that impose or recommend to Clubs or sports limited companies the installation of biometric systems for controlling access to the venues where sports competitions are held.  
If applicable, a copy of the aforementioned provisions is requested and, if any, of the assessments regarding the impact on the right to personal data protection that have been carried out in the process of adopting such provisions.  
On 28/03/2023, a response was received from CERVIXD. It informs that in its meeting on 3/03/2022, “an agreement was adopted establishing a series of additional security measures for the stands of support in the First and Second Division Football competitions. This is in accordance with the provisions of Article 13 of Law 19/2007, of July 11, against violence, racism, xenophobia, and intolerance in sports and Article 15.3 of Royal Decree 203/2010, of February 26, which approves the Regulation for the prevention of violence, racism, xenophobia, and intolerance in sports. Among the aforementioned additional measures, the installation of biometric systems for controlling all access to the stands of support was foreseen, allowing the unique identification of fans accessing these stands (document 1, copy of the agreement of 3/03/2022).”  
The literal text of the document is accessed, which is a communication to the President of the LIGA, in which, in addition to containing what is referred to in the previous paragraph, additional measures are agreed upon regarding the regime for access to the stands of support, reproducing the specific part of the RGLNFP, and communication is urged to indicate to the Clubs to adopt the measures for compliance, which may lead to the opening of sanctioning files in the exercise of their surveillance and control functions entrusted to them by Article 20.3.c)1 of Law 19/2007 (functions of CEVRXID: “surveillance and control for the purpose of proposing to the competent public authorities the adoption of sanctioning measures against those who fail to comply with the regulations provided in this Law and in the regulations that develop it”). All this without prejudice to the possibility that, if applicable, other additional measures may be adopted for these or other competitions.  
The C.S.D. indicates that on 20/10/2022, it requested a consultation (providing document 2) to the Legal Office of the AEPD (Legal Office of the AEPD) regarding whether the adoption of the agreement of 3/03/2022 was legally viable according to the data protection regulatory framework. 2.1. Legal Regime of the Sale of Season Tickets and Tickets to Stadiums - Relationship with Access to Sports Venues.  
The legal regime regarding the sale of tickets and access of spectators to sports facilities where official sports competitions are held is primarily regulated by Law 19/2007 of 11/07 against violence, racism, xenophobia, and intolerance in sports (Anti-Violence Law). One of the objectives of the Law, as stated in article b), is to “maintain public safety and order at sports events during the celebration of competitions and sports shows.”  
The CEVRXID was created under Law 10/90, of 15/10, on Sports, articles 60 to 69, whose Title IX was titled: “Prevention of Violence in Sports Events” and is a collegiate body responsible for formulating and implementing active policies against violence, intolerance, and the avoidance of racist, xenophobic, and LGTBIfobic practices in sports.”  
Article 3 of the Anti-Violence Law indicates as a general principle:  
“1. In general, the organizers of competitions and sports events must adopt appropriate measures to prevent the conduct described in sections one and two of article 2, as well as to ensure compliance by spectators with the conditions of access and permanence in the venue established in Chapter Two of this title.”  
2. It is particularly the responsibility of the organizers of competitions and sports events:  
a) To adopt the security measures established in this Law and its implementing provisions.  
b) To ensure respect for the obligations of spectators regarding access and permanence in the venue, through appropriate control instruments.  
Thus, on one hand, the purely sports organization of competitions is entrusted to the League, which does not organize these sports events held in the sports facilities where they take place, hence the terminological differentiation between the two terms: organizer of competitions, organizer of sports events.  
Regarding the relationship with the sale of tickets and access by spectators to the stadiums, the Anti-Violence Law transfers to the organizers of competitions and to the organizers of sports events the appropriate measures to prevent prohibited conduct, as well as to ensure compliance by spectators with the conditions of access and permanence in the venue through appropriate control instruments in safeguarding public order and safety.

3.2. Biometric Data  
Biometric data irrevocably changes the relationship between the body and identity, as it makes human body characteristics readable by machines and subject to subsequent use.  
Moreover, biometric systems are closely linked to a person, as they use a specific unique property of an individual for identification. For example, each individual has unique fingerprints that display specific characteristics that can be measured to determine whether a fingerprint matches a registered sample.

Biometric data has the particularity of being produced by the body itself and definitively characterizes it; these are data not about that person, but rather the data refers to the same person, which in principle cannot be modified by the individual's will. Furthermore, because biometric data is unique to a person and perpetual, in the event that the user accesses multiple biometric systems implemented by different controllers for different purposes, the user would have to use the same data, which would be accessible in different systems and by different controllers.  
As already pointed out in Opinion 4/2007 of the Article 29 Working Party (ART. 29 of Directive 95/46 EC, as a consultative and independent body of the EU), regarding the concept of personal data (WP136), dated 20/06/2007, biometric data can be defined as:  
“… biological properties, physiological characteristics, personality traits or tics, which are, at the same time, attributable to a single person and measurable, even if the models used in practice to technically measure them involve a certain degree of probability. Typical examples of biometric data include those provided by fingerprints, retinal patterns, facial structure, voices, but also hand geometry, venous structures, and even certain deeply rooted skills or other behavioral characteristics (such as handwriting, pulse, a particular way of walking or speaking, etc.). A particularity of biometric data is that they can be considered both as content of the information about a specific person (So-and-so has these fingerprints) and as an element to link information to a specific person (this object has been touched by someone who has these fingerprints, and these fingerprints correspond to So-and-so; therefore, So-and-so has touched this object). As such, they can serve as "identifiers." Indeed, as they correspond to a unique person, biometric data can be used to identify that person. This dual nature is also present in the case of DNA data, which provide information about the human body and allow for the unequivocal identification of one, and only one, person.”  
It should be considered that biometric data can be processed and stored in different ways. Sometimes, the biometric information captured from a person is stored and processed in raw form, allowing the source from which it comes to be recognized without special knowledge; for example, a photograph of a face, a photograph of a fingerprint, or a voice recording. Other times, the raw biometric information captured is processed in such a way that only certain characteristics or traits are extracted and stored as a biometric template.  
While traditional authentication methods such as passwords require a 100% character-by-character match to allow the user to access, for example, an account or application (deterministic methods), biometric methods are referred to as “probabilistic,” because they are probabilistic technologies that can automatically recognize individuals by their face or fingerprint for authentication or identification. In both the biometric identification and authentication systems, the facial recognition techniques used are based on an estimated match between templates: the one being compared and the previously registered reference template(s). From this perspective, they are probabilistic techniques: the comparison deduces a greater or lesser probability that the person is indeed the one to be authenticated or identified; if this probability exceeds a certain threshold in the system, defined by its user or developer, the system will understand that there is a match.

A biometric system works with biometric data obtained from a person, from which an algorithm selects features to create a biometric template. Subsequently, the system verifies the identity of the person against the biometric database. It can do this in a second while comparing hundreds of millions of biometric data points in the database.  
We can measure the performance of a biometric system based on three main characteristics. These are: false rejection rate (FRR), false acceptance rate (FAR), and equal error rate (ERR). The false rejection rate represents the probability of detection errors by a biometric system, meaning that it cannot recognize a user whose biometric features are already in the database. In the event of a rejection, the person must verify their identity again. From a security and protection perspective, this rate does not necessarily indicate a negative outcome.  
The false acceptance rate is the probability that a system fails and matches the biometric features of a person with an incorrect template, granting them permission to access. This can pose a potential threat, as the system allows an unauthorized person to access an account, facility, etc.  
The equal error rate is an essential indicator by which a system accepts or rejects biometric inputs. This rate is the equality value between FRR and FAR and represents the ideal number of errors of the two.  
Each biometric system, whether facial recognition, fingerprint, palm print, iris, etc., has different values for different rates based on which a system rejects or accepts inputs. Biometric data has the peculiarity of being produced by the body itself and is definitively characterized by it. Therefore, they are unique, permanent, or definitive over time, and a person cannot rid themselves of them; they can never be changed, not even with age, creating issues of liability in the event of compromise, loss, or intrusion into the system. Unlike a password, in the event of loss, they cannot be changed.  
The definition of biometric data refers to "technical processing," without specifying, except to note that the purpose of such processing must be to identify a person.  
To be considered biometric data in the sense of the GDPR, the processing of unprocessed data, such as the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person, must involve a measurement of those characteristics. Thus, from the concept, one must not lose sight of:  
- The nature of the data: data relating to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person;  
- The means and forms of processing: data "obtained from a specific technical processing"; which differentiates them, for example, from images of a person that appear in a surveillance system, which cannot be considered biometric data unless they have been technically processed in a specific manner to contribute to the unique identification of that person.

The concept of data controller is defined in Article 4.7 of the GDPR, which states:  
“the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States;”  
The concept of “data controller” is of fundamental importance in the application of the GDPR, as it determines who is responsible for compliance with the principles established in Article 5(1) of the GDPR and must be able to demonstrate compliance with those principles by adopting appropriate and effective measures to demonstrate that the processing activities comply with the GDPR. The principle of proactive responsibility is developed in Article 24 of the GDPR, which establishes that the data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the GDPR. Such measures shall be reviewed and updated when necessary.  
Since the ultimate objective of assigning the role of data controller is effective and comprehensive protection of personal data, the concept of “controller” should be interpreted broadly enough to promote, as far as possible, effective and complete protection of data subjects, in order to ensure full effectiveness in data protection, avoid gaps, and prevent possible evasion of the regulation, without this implying a reduction in the powers of the processor (see in this regard the judgments of 29/07/2019, Fashion ID, C-40/17, paragraph 66, and of 28/04/2022, Meta Platforms Ireland, C-319/20, paragraph 73 and cited case law).

A DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment) is a process designed to describe the processing of personal data, assess its necessity and proportionality, and help manage the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons arising from such processing, by identifying, evaluating, and determining measures to mitigate them. Defining it as a process means that it is not a one-off and isolated activity in time. The DPIA is an analysis process of a processing operation that extends over time, throughout the entire lifecycle of personal data processing, including its design phase, and must be continuously reviewed, "at least when there is a change in the risk posed by the processing operations" (Article 35.11 of the GDPR).  
DPIAs are important tools for accountability, as they help controllers not only to comply with the requirements of the GDPR, ensuring the rights and freedoms of individuals attending football stadiums, specifically in the stands of animation, but also to demonstrate that appropriate measures have been taken to ensure compliance with the Regulation.

The concept of an enterprise is an autonomous concept in EU competition law, defined by the jurisprudence of the Union courts.  
Enterprises are "economic units that consist of a unified organization of personal, material, and immaterial elements that pursue a specific long-term economic objective and may contribute to the commission of an infringement as contemplated in [Article 101 TFEU]." In simple terms, "enterprise" refers to any entity engaged in economic activity, regardless of its legal status and the manner in which it is financed. Therefore, the concept of an enterprise is of an economic nature. This is often the case with corporate groups: the parent company and the subsidiary—distinct legal entities (usually corporations)—constitute a single enterprise if the relationship between them is so close that, from an economic perspective, they form a "single economic unit."  
The reference to the enterprise should not be understood as including all specific jurisprudence of European competition law, as the GDPR has its own mechanisms to distinguish which entity has violated the law and how liability should be divided.  
Article 83 and Recital 150 of the GDPR should be understood to mean that, in the event that the data controller or processor is an enterprise in the sense of Articles 101 and 102 TFEU (which means it is "an entity engaged in economic activity" and not a natural person or a public body), the combined turnover of the enterprise as a whole may be used to calculate the maximum limit of the fine, in this case up to 10,000,000 euros or, in the case of an enterprise, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 2% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, "opting for the higher amount."  
In accordance with Article 83.1 of the GDPR, the administrative fines provided for in paragraphs 4 to 6 must be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. Three characteristics that can only be attributed to a fine whose amount is determined based on the actual or material economic capacity of its recipient. Hence the need to operate with a material or economic concept of enterprise, rather than employing a strictly formal notion for the calculation of the sanction. The real or material definition of enterprise characteristic of competition law, understood as an economic unit, is adopted by the European legislator for the determination of the amount of fines for violations of the GDPR.  
The interpretative criterion regarding the combined turnover of the enterprise is reflected in the criteria of the Guidelines 4/2022 on the calculation of fines under the GDPR, version 2.0 of 24/05/2023, as for example in paragraph 121 "in cases where the data controller or processor is (part of) an enterprise in the sense of Articles 101 and 102 TFEU, the combined turnover of that enterprise as a whole may be used to determine the dynamic maximum limit of the fine (see Chapter 6.2.2, "Legal maximum and corporate liability/determination of turnover and corporate liability of the enterprise/determination of turnover) and to ensure that the resulting fine complies with the principles of effectiveness, proportionality, and deterrence (Article 83, paragraph 1, of the GDPR)."
