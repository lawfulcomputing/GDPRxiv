11/19  
In relation to the facts subject to the claim, and pursuant to the provisions established in Articles 4.1 and 4.2 of the GDPR, in the case we are examining, there is evidence of the processing of personal data concerning the claimant carried out by the respondent, given that SIE collected and used the following personal data of the claimant to register contracts for electricity in her name: the first name and two surnames, the NIF, the postal address, the bank details, the CUPS numbers, and the location address of the two supply points of which she is the owner.

Regarding this entity, the data controller, it has been made clear in previous paragraphs that the name of the marketing company does not appear in the heading of the issued invoices. In the heading of both invoices, only the logo of “más Luz Energía” and the email address ***EMAIL.1 appear.  
The Resolution of the General Directorate of Energy Policy and Mines, dated 28/04/2021, issued under the authority granted to that General Directorate by the third additional provision of Royal Decree 148/2021, of March 9, “which establishes the minimum content and the model of electricity invoice to be used by reference marketers,” (BOE number 103, of 30/04/2021) determines the format of the invoices that marketing companies must issue.  
According to the aforementioned Resolution, the following information regarding the marketing entity must be included in the invoices: the “Logo, name, NIF, and registered office of the COR” in the cases of contracting provided for in point Third. And in the cases of contracting provided for in point Fourth of that Resolution, the minimum content to be included will be the “Identity, registered office of the marketing company and, where applicable, an indication that it is a supply in the free market.”  
Regarding the location of this information on the invoice, only for the cases contemplated in section Third of the aforementioned Resolution of the General Directorate of Energy Policy and Mines is it specified which part of the invoice must include it: in the heading of the invoice in the upper right corner. Applicable Provisions  
The GDPR addresses in its Article 5 the principles governing the processing of personal data, a provision that states:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (<<lawfulness, fairness, and transparency>>)  
[...]  
Paragraph 2 of Article 5 of the GDPR establishes that “The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and shall be able to demonstrate it (<<accountability>>)”.  
Article 6 of the GDPR under the heading “Lawfulness of processing” specifies in its paragraph 1 the circumstances under which the processing of third-party data is considered lawful:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the implementation of pre-contractual measures taken at the request of the data subject;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person.  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, provided that such interests are not overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.

IV  
Unfulfilled Obligation: Violation of Article 6.1 of the GDPR  
1. This resolution confirms that SIE has committed a violation of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, manifested in having processed the personal data of the claimant related to the registration of two electricity contracts without an adequate legal basis.  
All processing of personal data must be based on at least one of the lawful grounds provided in Article 6.1 of the GDPR. In this regard, Recital 40 of the GDPR states:  
“For processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on another legitimate basis established by law, whether in this Regulation or under another law of the Union or of the Member States to which this Regulation applies, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject or the necessity of performing a contract to which the data subject is a party or for taking steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.”  
The documentation in the file demonstrates that SIE processed personal data concerning the claimant linked to two electricity contracts: it collected their personal data, used it to register two electricity contracts -***CONTRACT.1 and ***CONTRACT.2- and retained it for a time related to those contracts, as it issued at least one invoice in the name of the claimant for each of them. And, as the incoming marketer, it managed the cancellation of the claimant in the contracts they had subscribed to until then with their marketer.

2. In the analyzed case, it is not evidenced that the processing of the claimant's data carried out by SIE was based on any of the lawful bases contemplated in Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
At this point, it is necessary to recall that the burden of proof lies with the respondent, SIE, to demonstrate that the processing it has carried out complies with the principle of lawfulness (Article 5.1.a) as it is based on one of the legal bases described in Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
The GDPR introduces the principle of proactive responsibility (Article 5.2), according to which the data controller must comply with the principles established in Article 5.1 - for the purposes now relevant, the principle of lawfulness - and be in a position to demonstrate its compliance. Consequently, the burden is placed on the controller to demonstrate that the processing it has carried out is covered by one of the circumstances in Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
As stated by the Legal Office of the AEPD (among others, in reports 17/2019 and 64/2020), the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to personal data protection, which is now based on the principle of "proactive responsibility" ("accountability"). The explanatory memorandum of the LOPDGDD states that "the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility."  
Following Opinion 3/2010 from the Article 29 Working Party (GT29) - WP 173 - issued during the validity of Directive 95/46/EC, which has been repealed by the GDPR but whose reflections are applicable today, the "essence" of proactive responsibility is the obligation of the data controller to apply measures that, under normal circumstances, ensure that the rules on data protection are complied with in the context of processing operations and to have available documents that demonstrate compliance to the data subjects and the Authorities of

Of the possible legal bases for processing described in Article 6.1. of the GDPR, the only one that could serve as a lawful basis for the processing carried out is that provided for in paragraph b): the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the implementation at their request of pre-contractual measures.” This circumstance refers to the existence of a contract between the claimant and the respondent for which the processing of personal data is necessary for its execution and development.  
However, in light of the documentation in the file, it has not been established in any case that the claimant had given consent to the conclusion of any of the electricity contracts that SIE registered in their name. In short, the status of “contracting party” of the claimant in the electricity contracts whose development and execution would supposedly constitute the legal basis for the processing carried out by the respondent is not evidenced.

VI  
Filing of the sanctioning procedure  
The principle of personal liability for sanctions or personal responsibility for one's own actions is enshrined in Article 25.1 of the Spanish Constitution (CE). The Constitutional Court has repeatedly held that this principle is one of the foundations of the punitive order of the State, applicable also in the administrative sanctioning field, which constitutes one of the bases or guarantees of the principle of culpability.  
In its ruling STC 146/1994, of May 12, the High Court states:  
“As for the constitutionality of the content of the norm, as we have indicated on repeated occasions, the principles inspiring the criminal order are applicable, with certain nuances, to the administrative sanctioning law, given that both are manifestations of the punitive order of the State. Among the informing principles of the criminal order is the principle of personal liability for punishment, protected by Article 25.1 of the Fundamental Norm (STC 254/1988, legal basis 5), also formulated by this Court as the principle of personal liability for punishment or sanction (STC 219/1988, legal basis 3), a designation sufficiently revealing of its applicability in the field of administrative sanctioning law.  
The final clause of section 4 of Article 31 of Law 44/1978 establishes the joint liability of (all) members of the family unit towards the Treasury; the literal wording of this provision allows the Administration to address the collection of the tax debt, including sanctions, not only to the member or members of the family unit who are responsible for the actions that generated the sanction but also to other members who have neither committed nor collaborated in the commission of the violations, thus violating the aforementioned principle of personal liability for punishment or sanction protected by Article 254.1 of the Constitution, thereby incurring a defect of unconstitutionality.” (The underlining is ours)  
The principle of personal liability for sanctions is enshrined in Law 40/2015, of October 1, on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector (LRJSP), which in its Article 28, “Responsibility,” states:  
“1. Only individuals and legal entities, as well as, when a Law recognizes them the capacity to act, groups of affected individuals, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous assets, who are responsible for the same by way of intent or negligence, may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative violations.” (The underlining is ours)
