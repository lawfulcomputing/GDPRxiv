II  
Preliminary Issues  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines "processing" as:  
“any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction.”  
For its part, Article 4.7 of the GDPR defines the "data controller" or "controller" as:  
“the natural or legal person, public authority, service or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; where the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of processing, the controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided for by the law of the Union or of the Member States.”  
In the present case, in accordance with the provisions of Articles 4.1 and 4.2 of the GDPR, there is evidence of the processing of personal data, since OCCIDENT collects and processes, through the Google Analytics service, among others, the following personal data of natural persons: unique user identifiers (_ga and _gid), the IP address, as well as other data associated with the browser and the browsing itself, among other processing activities.  
OCCIDENT carries out this activity in its capacity as data controller, as it is the one who determines the purposes and means of such activity, by virtue of Article 4.7 of the GDPR.  
Article 44 of the GDPR regulates the transfer of personal data to third countries.

III  
Response to the Allegations  
Next, a response is provided to the allegations presented by the entity being complained against, in the order in which they are formulated in their writing:  
“FIRST.- THE PRIOR INVESTIGATION ACTIONS THAT THE AEPD RELIES ON TO ADOPT THE INITIATION AGREEMENT HAVE EXPIRED”  
In response to this allegation, it should be noted that, in this case, after the admission of the complaint for processing, no prior investigation actions have been carried out, so the twelve-month duration of such actions, which Article 67.2 of the LOPDGDD established as a time limit, does not apply, according to the wording of that article in force at the time the complaint was admitted for processing. The time limit established in Article 67.2 of the LOPDGDD is the maximum period within which the initiation agreement must be issued, only in the event that prior investigation actions have been agreed upon, and this time limit cannot be applied, as OCCIDENT intends, even if such actions have not been agreed upon.  
After the admission of the complaint for processing, and not considering it necessary to carry out prior investigation actions, the only time limit that we must apply is the three-year statute of limitations provided for in Article 72.1.l) of the LOPDGDD for very serious infringements, as was substantiated in the initiation agreement. Considering the absence of an adequacy decision with the U.S. (following the CJEU ruling C-311/18) between July 16, 2020, and July 10, 2023, the facts referred to in the complaint persisted until at least this last date, which would be the start date of the statute of limitations, so that, as of the start date of the present procedure, the infringement had not expired.  
Regarding the incorporation of the document “Google Transparency Report, relating to statistics of access requests by U.S. intelligence services,” this was not produced within the framework of prior investigation actions. It was simply incorporated into the file as a publicly available document, which does not equate to carrying out prior investigation actions.  
On the other hand, the Judgment of the National Court cited by OCCIDENT refers to the case in which, after an agreement to open prior investigation actions has been issued, and the time limit established in the LOPDGDD has elapsed, the expiration of the prior investigation actions has not been declared, because when this circumstance occurs, the expired actions must be archived and, if applicable, the opening of new actions must be agreed upon. As explained previously, in the complaint that gave rise to the present procedure, the opening of prior investigation actions was not agreed upon, so the grounds of the Judgment of the National Court are not applicable in this case.

IV  
Transfers of personal data to third countries  
Article 44 “Transfers of personal data to third countries or international organizations” of the GDPR states:  
“Personal data that are subject to processing or are to be processed after their transfer to a third country or international organization may only be transferred if, subject to the other provisions of this Regulation, the controller and the processor comply with the conditions laid down in this chapter, including those relating to further transfers of personal data from the third country or international organization to another third country or another international organization. All provisions of this chapter shall apply in order to ensure that the level of protection of natural persons guaranteed by this Regulation is not undermined.”  
Chapter V of the Regulation provides various instruments to ensure a level of protection that is substantially equivalent to that guaranteed in the European Union, in accordance with Article 44 of the Regulation:  
- adequacy decisions (Article 45);  
- appropriate safeguards (Article 46);  
In the absence of an equivalent level of protection, exceptions are established for specific situations (Article 49).  
1.  
On the processing of data and the responsibility for processing.  
In the document dated April 9, 2021, sent by Google LLC to the Austrian data protection authority, which it shared with the other authorities within the framework of the TF101 working group, it is indicated that Google Analytics operates by including a block of Javascript code on the pages of a website. When a user visits a webpage, this Javascript code refers to a Javascript code that has been previously downloaded to the user's device, which then executes the tracking operation for Google Analytics. The tracking operation sends data about the requested page through various means and sends this information to the Analytics server via a series of parameters attached to a request for a one-pixel GIF image sent to the domain google-analytics.com. The data is then processed more extensively and ends up in the reports of the website owner, in this case, OCCIDENT. The data that Google Analytics collects on behalf of the website owner comes from the following sources:  
i.  
The user's HTTP request.  
An HTTP request contains details such as the browser and the computer making the request, such as the hostname, browser type, referer, and language.  
ii.  
Browser and system information.  
iii.  
First-party cookies.  
Website administrators who integrate the Google Analytics service can send instructions to Google for processing the data collected through Google Analytics. The website administrator can apply different settings, for example, regarding the data retention period. The Google Analytics function also allows website administrators to monitor and maintain the stability of their website, for example, keeping them informed of certain events such as a spike in audience or the fact that there is no traffic at all. Google Analytics also allows website administrators to measure and optimize the effectiveness of advertising campaigns conducted using other Google tools.  
Therefore, Google Analytics collects the user's HTTP query and information about the user's browser and operating system, among other elements. An HTTP request, for any page, contains details of the browser and device making the query, such as the domain name and browser information, its type, referer, and language. Google Analytics stores and reads cookies in the user's browser to evaluate the user's session and other information about the query.

1. On the qualification of the data subject to processing as personal data  
It can be asserted that the data collected under the Google Analytics function and transferred to the United States of America constitutes personal data. Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific factors relating to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  

It should be noted that online identifiers, such as IP addresses or information stored in cookies, can commonly be used to identify a user, especially when combined with other similar types of information. This is illustrated in Recital 30 of the GDPR, which states that the assignment of online identifiers such as IP addresses and cookie identifiers to natural persons or their devices can “leave traces that, particularly when combined...
25/35  
with unique identifiers and other information received by the servers, can be used to create profiles of individuals and identify them." In the particular case where the data controller claims not to have the ability to identify the user through the use (alone or combined with other data points) of such identifiers, it would be expected to disclose the specific means deployed to ensure the anonymity of the collected identifiers. Without such details, they cannot be considered anonymous.  
Therefore, it is necessary to examine to what extent the implementation of Google Analytics on a website allows the website administrator and Google to identify an interested party (a visitor to the website in question).  
When the claimant visited the website https://www.antares.es, the following data (via JavaScript code) was transmitted from the claimant's browser to the servers of Google LLC:  
- Cookies _ga and _gid  
- URL of the visited webpage (parameter dl) and title of the visited webpage (parameter dt)  
- IP  
- sr (screen resolution), among other parameters.  
- Data about the browser and operating system:  
- Unique identifier that identifies the website operator  
It should be noted that the CJEU has already stated that IP addresses are personal data (see case C-597-19, paragraph 102 and C-582/14, paragraph 49). The IP address does not lose its nature as personal data simply because the means of identification reside with third parties. Furthermore, the case in question is very different, as the IP address can be combined with other elements, as will be described below.  
Regarding unique identifiers, when a user visits the website https://www.antares.es, its cookie policy acknowledges the use of Google Analytics, which involves sending the values of the "_ga" and "_gid" cookies to Google LLC, as stated in the reference to Google Analytics cookies (page 134 of the file).

and “https://developers.google.com/analytics/devguides/collection/gajs/cookie-usage”, the cookies _ga and _gid are used to distinguish users and the parameter “sr” refers to screen resolution.  
Visitor identifiers are unique identifiers intended to differentiate individuals (where such differentiation was not possible before), and make individuals identifiable. These identifiers can also be combined with other information, such as the address of the visited website, metadata related to the browser and operating system, the time and data related to the website visit, and the IP address. This combination of information further differentiates individuals.

Appropriate safeguards: Standard data protection clauses  
Article 46, "Transfers subject to adequate safeguards," of the GDPR, establishes in its paragraph 1 that "In the absence of a decision pursuant to Article 45(3), the controller or processor may only transfer personal data to a third country or international organization if it has provided adequate safeguards and on condition that the data subjects have enforceable rights and effective legal remedies."  
Article 46, paragraph 2, of the GDPR provides that "The adequate safeguards referred to in paragraph 1 may be provided, without requiring any express authorization from a supervisory authority, by:  
(...)  
c) standard data protection clauses adopted by the Commission in accordance with the examination procedure referred to in Article 93(2).  
(...)."  
Google's standard contractual clauses for the transfer of personal data to the United States, updated on September 27, 2021, under the name "Google Ads & Measurement: Standard Contractual Clauses (Module 3: Processor-to-Processor)," which could be translated as: "Google Ads Data Processing Terms: Standard Contractual Clauses, Processor-to-Processor Standard Contractual Clauses" (hereinafter, SCC). These clauses comply with those published by the European Commission in Decision 2010/87/EU.  
In this context, it is noteworthy that standard contractual clauses are a transfer instrument in the sense of Chapter V of the Regulation and were not challenged as such by the CJEU in its judgment of July 16, 2020 (C-311/18). However, the CJEU considered that the contractual nature of these clauses implied that they could not be binding on the authorities of third countries. In particular, the CJEU stated that: "...Therefore, while there are situations in which, depending on the legislation and practices in force in the third country in question, the recipient of such a transfer is in a position to guarantee the

necessary protection of data solely on the basis of standard contractual clauses for data protection, there are other situations in which the content of such standard clauses may not constitute a sufficient means to ensure, in practice, the effective protection of personal data transferred to the third country in question. This is particularly the case when the legislation of that third country allows its public authorities to interfere with the rights of the data subjects to whom the data relates" (C-311/18, paragraph 126, emphasis added).  
No further analysis of the legal situation of the United States is necessary, as the CJEU has already provided it in its aforementioned judgment. Indeed, the CJEU found that regulatory surveillance programs such as Section 702 of FISA and E.O. 12333 in conjunction with PPD-28 do not meet the minimum requirements established by Union law regarding the principle of proportionality, so that surveillance programs based on these provisions cannot be considered limited to what is strictly necessary (C-311/18, paragraph 184). Furthermore, the CJEU found that the legal framework in question did not confer on data subjects rights that are actionable before the courts against U.S. authorities, from which it follows that these individuals do not have the right to effective judicial protection (C-311/18, paragraph 192).  
The CJEU's analysis is relevant in the present case, as Google LLC (as the importer of data to the U.S.) must be classified as a provider of electronic communications services within the meaning of subsection (b) of point 4 of Section 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code and, therefore, is subject to surveillance by U.S. intelligence services in accordance with subsection (a) of Section 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code ("FISA 702"). Therefore, Google LLC is obligated to provide personal data to the U.S. government when requested in accordance with subsection (a) of Section 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code (FISA 702).  
As can be seen in Google's Transparency Report, Google LLC is regularly subject to such access requests from U.S. intelligence services. The report can be accessed at:  
https://transparencyreport.google.com/user-data/us-national-security?hl=en  
The CJEU stated, on the one hand, that the EU-U.S. adequacy decision was invalid due to the access possibilities of U.S. intelligence services and, on the other hand, concluded that the use of standard contractual clauses cannot, on its own, guarantee a level of protection as required by Article 44 of the GDPR, since the guarantees they offer are not fulfilled when such access requests are made. In fact, the CJEU concluded the following:  
"It follows that the standard contractual clauses for data protection adopted by the Commission on the basis of Article 46(2)(c) of the GDPR are intended solely to provide contractual guarantees that apply uniformly across all third countries to the controllers and processors established in the European Union and, consequently, regardless of the level of protection guaranteed in each third country. To the extent that these standard contractual clauses for data protection cannot, by their very nature, provide guarantees beyond a contractual obligation to ensure compliance with..."

General observations on supplementary measures  
In its Recommendations 01/2020, dated June 18, 2021, (which can be consulted at the website https://edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-06/edpb_recommendations_202001vo.2.0_supplementarymeasurestransferstools_en.pdf, although the transcripts included in this document refer to its translation into Spanish) the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) clarified that when the assessment of the legislation or current practices of the third country may affect the effectiveness of the adequate safeguards of the transfer instruments on which the exporter relies, in the context of its specific transfer, as is the case here following the assessment by the CJEU, the exporter must suspend the transfer or implement appropriate supplementary measures. The EDPB notes in this regard that "Any supplementary measure can only be considered effective in the sense of the CJEU ruling 'Schrems II' to the extent that it addresses the specific deficiencies identified in its assessment of the legal situation in the third country. If, ultimately, it cannot guarantee a level of protection that is essentially equivalent, personal data must not be transferred." (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 75).  
The measures to complement the standard data protection clauses can be classified into three categories: contractual, technical, or organizational (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 74).  
Regarding contractual measures, the EDPB stated that: "In some situations, these measures may complement and reinforce the guarantees that the transfer instrument and the relevant law of the third country can provide when, considering the circumstances of the transfer, they do not meet all the necessary conditions to ensure a level of protection essentially equivalent to that guaranteed in the Union. Given the nature of contractual measures, which generally cannot bind the authorities of that third country when they are not part of the contract, these must be combined with other technical and organizational measures to provide the required level of data protection (...)." (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 99, emphasis added).  
As for organizational measures, the EDPB highlighted that: "… Selecting and applying one or more of these measures will not necessarily and systematically guarantee that your transfer meets the essential equivalence standard required by Union Law. Depending on the specific circumstances of the transfer and the assessment made regarding the law of the third country, organizational measures will be needed to complement contractual or technical measures, in order to ensure a level of protection of personal data that is essentially equivalent to that guaranteed in the Union" (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 128, emphasis added).

Complementary measures implemented by Google LLC  
Google LLC, as the recipient of user data from its Google Analytics services, adopted contractual, organizational, and technical measures to complement the SCC. In the document dated April 9, 2021, sent by Google LLC to the Austrian data protection authority, which it shared with the other authorities within the framework of the TF101 Working Group, Google LLC described the measures taken in detail.  
Considering the considerations of the CJEU and the EDPB, it must now be verified whether the complementary measures adopted by Google LLC were effective, meaning that they address the specific issue of the access possibilities of U.S. intelligence services.  
Regarding the “legal and organizational measures” adopted, it should be noted that neither the notification of users, even if such notification is admissible, nor the publication of a transparency report or a “public policy on the handling of governmental requests” actually prevent or reduce the access possibilities of U.S. intelligence services. Furthermore, it is unclear how the “careful review of each request” by Google LLC regarding its admissibility is effective as a complementary measure, considering that, according to the CJEU, admissible (legal) requests from U.S. intelligence services do not comply with the requirements of European Data Protection legislation.  
With respect to the “technical measures” adopted, it should be noted that it has not been clarified how the described measures, such as the protection of communications between Google services, the protection of data in transit between data centers, the protection of communications between users and websites, or “site security,” actually prevent or reduce the access possibilities of the services.

Regarding encryption technologies, such as in the case of "data at rest" in data centers, as specifically mentioned by Google LLC as a technical measure, it should be noted that Google LLC, as a data importer, has the obligation to grant access or deliver personal data imported under its control, including the cryptographic keys necessary for the data to be intelligible (see Recommendations 01/2020, point 81). In other words: as long as Google LLC has the ability to access individuals' data in clear text, such a technical measure cannot be considered effective in the present case.  
As Google LLC states that "to the extent that Google Analytics data for measurement transferred by website owners is personal data, it should be considered as pseudonymous," it must be taken into account that universal unique identifiers (UUIDs) do not fall within the definition of Article 4.5 of the GDPR. While pseudonymization can be a privacy-enhancing technique, unique identifiers have, as previously described, the specific intent of identifying users, not acting as a safeguard. Apart from this, it has also been previously described why the combination of unique identifiers with other elements (such as browser or device data and the IP address) and the possibility of linking such information to a Google account in any case allows for an individual to be identified.  
To the extent that Google LLC refers to an "optional technical measure" through an IP anonymization feature, it should first be noted that such a measure is, as its name indicates, optional and not applicable to all transfers. Furthermore, from Google's response, it is not clear whether this anonymization occurs before the transfer or if the full IP address is transmitted to the United States and only shortened after this transfer to the United States. Therefore, from a technical standpoint, there is potential access to the entire full IP address before it is shortened.  
Thus, the supplementary measures adopted, as presented by Google, are not effective to the extent that none of them address the specific issues in the present case, meaning that none of them prevent the possibilities of access by U.S. intelligence services nor render such access ineffective.

The European Data Protection Board (EDPB), in compliance with the objective of ensuring the consistent application of the General Data Protection Regulation, as attributed to it by Article 70 of the GDPR, issued Guidelines 2/2018 on the exceptions provided for in Article 49 of Regulation 2016/679. Regarding the interpretation of the assumption a) of Article 49 of the GDPR (section 2.1 of Guidelines 2/2018), the EDPB states that consent must be specific and informed about the possible risks, establishing the following: “this provision requires that data subjects are also informed of the specific risks arising from the fact that their data will be transferred to a country that does not provide an adequate level of protection and that no adequate safeguards are foreseen for the protection of the data.”  
In the present case, the users' consent for the storage of cookies during their visit to the website cannot be considered equivalent to having explicitly consented to the international transfer of data, as required by Article 49.1.a) of the GDPR; this consent must be obtained specifically, and for it to be valid, OCCIDENT should have previously informed the data subjects of the risks posed by the data transfers to the U.S., in light of the absence of an adequacy decision and appropriate safeguards in the sense of Article 49, paragraph 1 of the Regulation.  
Regarding the possible existence of a contractual relationship, this assumption is not present, considering that Recital 111 of the GDPR requires that international data transfers based on explicit consent must be “occasional and necessary in relation to a contract,” whereas in this case the transfers occur continuously and systematically and their necessity is not justified.

(...)  
c) the transfers of personal data to a recipient in a third country or an international organization pursuant to Articles 44 to 49;  
(...)”
