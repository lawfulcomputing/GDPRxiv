Classification and qualification of the infringement  
The infringement is classified in Article 83.5 of the GDPR, which considers the following as such:  
“5. Infringements of the following provisions shall be subject to administrative fines of up to 20,000,000 EUR or, in the case of an undertaking, an amount equivalent to up to 4% of the total annual global turnover of the preceding financial year, whichever is higher:

The LOPDGD, for the purposes of the statute of limitations for the infringement, qualifies in its Article 72.1 as a very serious infringement, with a statute of limitations of three years in this case, “b) The processing of personal data without any of the conditions for lawful processing established in Article 6 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”  
In response to the allegations presented by the accused entity, the following should be noted:  
Regarding the assertion that DIGI has not provided the alleged criminals with personal information of the claimant other than what they already had beforehand. Consequently, there has been no unauthorized processing of personal data.  
Indeed, the issuance of a duplicate is not sufficient to carry out banking operations in the name of the holders; certainly, to complete the fraud, it is necessary for a third party to “impersonate” the data holder before the financial entity.  
This, in principle, leads to processing outside the principle of lawfulness since a third party is processing data, as they have access to it, without any legal basis, in addition to the violation of other principles such as confidentiality.  
For this reason, this is a process where the diligence provided by the operators is fundamental to prevent this type of fraud and violations of the GDPR. Diligence that translates into the establishment of appropriate measures to ensure that data processing is compliant with the GDPR.  
Identical considerations apply to the actions of banking entities that provide payment services, in whose scope this type of fraud begins, as the third party has access to the credentials of the affected user and impersonates them.  

As these entities are responsible for processing their clients' data, they have the same obligations as those indicated so far for the operators regarding compliance with the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, as well as those derived from Royal Decree-Law 19/2018, of November 23, on payment services and other urgent financial measures.  
From the Established Facts, it is inferred that DIGI has provided a duplicate SIM card to a third party other than the legitimate holder of the mobile line, after a third party bypassed the existing security policy, which evidences a breach of the duty to protect customer information.  
Furthermore, DIGI claims that, “the store employee expressly states that they did carry out such verification, and their statement cannot be doubted without further argument than the result of the identity theft. Thus, there is a possibility that even following the protocol and having requested a copy of the ID, it could have been false. Moreover, the fact that a photocopy of that document was made would not have added greater security to the operation.”  
Well, it should be noted that it is not enough to say that the employee checked the ID without being able to substantiate this claim in any way; it is not acceptable to state that the Agency does not allow photocopying/scanning IDs, as Article 5.2 of the GDPR states that “The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and be able to demonstrate it (proactive responsibility),” since it is the controller, in compliance with their proactive responsibility obligations, who must implement the necessary technical and organizational measures, as expressed in Articles 24 and 25 of the GDPR.  
The principle of proactivity transfers to the data controller the obligation not only to observe the principles governing processing but also to be able to demonstrate such compliance.  
Article 5.2 of the GDPR is developed in Article 24 of the GDPR, which obliges the controller to adopt appropriate technical and organizational measures “to ensure and be able to demonstrate” that processing has been carried out in accordance with the GDPR.

Certainly, the principle of responsibility provided in Article 28 of the LRJSP states: "Only individuals and legal entities may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative infractions, as well as, when a law recognizes their capacity to act, the groups of affected parties, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous estates, that are found responsible for them on the grounds of intent or negligence."  
However, the manner of attributing responsibility to legal entities does not correspond to the forms of culpability that are attributable to human conduct, whether intentional or negligent. Thus, in the case of infractions committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present, it necessarily applies differently than it does with individuals.  
According to STC 246/1991, "(...) this distinct construction of the attribution of the authorship of the infraction to the legal entity arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the norms to which they are subject. Capacity to infringe and, therefore, direct reproachability that derives from the legal good protected by the norm that is infringed and the necessity for that protection to be truly effective and the risk that, consequently, the legal entity subject to compliance with that norm must assume" (in this sense, STS of November 24, 2011, Rec 258/2009).

The ruling made by the National Court in its SAN of May 5, 2021 (Rec. 1437/2020) regarding paragraph e) of Article 83.2 of the GDPR, concerning the commission of prior infringements:  
“On the other hand, it considers that the absence of a prior infringement should be regarded as a mitigating factor. As a mitigating factor, the absence of benefits is contrary to the spirit of Article 83.1 of the GDPR and to the principles governing the determination of the amount of the fine. If, following the commission of a GDPR infringement, it is classified as a mitigating factor that no benefits have been obtained, the deterrent purpose fulfilled by the sanction is partially nullified. Accepting DIGI's thesis in a case like the one at hand would imply introducing an artificial reduction in the sanction that should genuinely be imposed; the one that results from considering the circumstances of Article 83.2 GDPR that must indeed be evaluated.  
The Administrative Litigation Chamber of the National Court has warned that the fact that not all elements constituting a modifying circumstance of liability, which by its nature is aggravating, are present in a specific case cannot lead to the conclusion that such a circumstance is applicable as a mitigating factor. The ruling made by the National Court in its judgment of May 5, 2021 (Rec. 1437/2020) - even though that resolution deals with the circumstance of section e) of Article 83.2 of the GDPR, the commission of previous infringements - is extrapolable to the issue raised, the claim of the respondent that the "absence" of benefits be accepted as a mitigating factor, given that both the GDPR and the LOPDGDD refer only to "the benefits obtained":  
“On the other hand, it considers that the non-commission of a previous infringement should be appreciated as a mitigating factor. Well, Article 83.2 of the GDPR establishes that, among other things, the circumstance "e) any previous infringement committed by the controller or the processor" must be taken into account for the imposition of the administrative fine. This is an aggravating circumstance; the fact that the prerequisite for its application does not exist means that it cannot be taken into consideration, but it does not imply or allow, as the claimant intends, its application as a mitigating factor.”
