LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47 and 48.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Moreover, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection will be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, as long as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  

II  
Change of Legal Qualification of the Facts Subject to the Complaint  
Prior to analyzing the allegations presented, it is necessary to focus our attention on the outcome of the evidence-gathering phase, reflected in the proven facts.  
During this evidentiary phase, it has been established that the mobile phone number (…), which created the WhatsApp group “Equality Commission” on October 28, 2021, and added the complainant's mobile phone number and the mobile phone numbers of two other LISMARTSA employees, was a corporate mobile phone number.  
It has also been demonstrated that the complainant's mobile phone number and the mobile phone numbers of (…), which were included in that group, were their personal numbers.  
Consequently, it is noted that the facts subject to the complaint, which are the subject of this sanctioning file, perfectly fit the typification contemplated in Articles 5.1 f) and 32 of the GDPR. For this reason, a change in the legal qualification of these facts is warranted concerning the legal qualification made in the initiation agreement.  
Regarding whether it is appropriate to change the legal qualification of the facts subject to the complaint during the proposal phase, and the impact that such a change may have on the defense rights of the entity being complained against, it should be noted that nothing prevents this modification as long as, as is the case now, the facts on which the accusation is based remain unchanged.  
The Constitutional Court (hereinafter TC) has indicated that “the essential content of the constitutional right to be informed of the accusation refers to the punishable facts attributed to the accused” (STC 95/1995). (The underlining is from the AEPD).  
In contrast, and unlike what happens with the facts, the TC, in Judgment 145/1993, warns that the communication to the alleged infringer of the legal qualification and the potential sanction to be imposed does not integrate the essential content of the right to be informed of the accusation. So important is the knowledge of the constitutive facts of the administrative infringement that the TC has declared that the requirements of Article 24.2 of the CE are fundamentally satisfied with the mere communication of the alleged facts to enable defense against them (STC 2/1987 and 190/1987). In this line, the Supreme Court, in its Judgment of March 3, 2004, states that “the primary purpose of the initiation agreement is to inform about the alleged facts and not about the legal qualification, which will be handled by the resolution proposal.” (The underlining is ours).

- Article 76.2.c) of the LOPDGDD “The benefits obtained as a result of the commission of the infringement.” In this regard, LISMARTSA argues: “No benefit has been obtained from the infringement.”  
This criterion of graduation is established in the LOPDGDD in accordance with the provisions of Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR, according to which administrative fines will be imposed taking into account any “aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement,” understanding that avoiding a loss has the same nature for these purposes as obtaining benefits.  
In accordance with the provisions of Article 83.1 of the GDPR: “Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines in accordance with this article for the infringements of this Regulation indicated in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 are in each individual case effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.” Consequently, admitting the absence of benefits as a mitigating factor is not only contrary to the factual premises contemplated in Article 76.2.c), but also contrary to what is established in Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR and to the principles indicated.  
Thus, when assessing the absence of benefits as a circumstance that allows for a reduction in the amount of the fine to be imposed, the dissuasive effect of the fine would be nullified, to the extent that it diminishes the effect of the circumstances that effectively influence its quantification, providing the responsible party with a benefit that they have not earned. It would be an artificial reduction of the sanction that could lead to the understanding that infringing the regulation without obtaining benefits, financial or otherwise, will not produce a negative effect proportional to the severity of the infringing act.

- Article 76.2.d) of the LOPDGDD: "The possibility that the behavior of the affected party may have induced the commission of the infringement."  
In its written submissions, LISTMARSA states:

Again, the absence of minors or individuals belonging to a vulnerable group or at risk of exclusion affected by the actions of the responding party does not imply that the circumstance of Article 76.2.f) of the LOPDGDD should be applied as a circumstance that reduces the amount of the fine to be imposed.  
As we indicated regarding the circumstance related to the categories of affected data (Article 83.2.g) of the GDPR), the more categories of data that are affected or the more sensitive the data, the stricter the sanction should be; when the processing carried out by the infringer affects the rights of minors, the amount of the fine should be higher.  
The responding party concludes its written submissions regarding the initiation agreement by stating that a warning sanction would be more proportional to the specific case presented, "taking into account the measures adopted and the concurrent mitigating factors, as well as the delicate economic situation we find ourselves in."  
Article 58.2 of the GDPR provides:  
"2. Each supervisory authority shall have all of the following corrective powers:  
(...)  
b) to issue a warning to any controller or processor when the processing operations have infringed the provisions of this Regulation;  
(...)  
i) to impose an administrative fine in accordance with Article 83, in addition to or instead of the measures mentioned in this paragraph, depending on the circumstances of each particular case;"  
For its part, Article 83.2 of the GDPR states:  
"2. Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure for those provided for in Article 58, paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j)."  
Furthermore, the provisions of Article 83.1 of the GDPR must be respected:  
"1. Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines in accordance with this article for the infringements of this Regulation indicated in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 are effective, proportionate, and dissuasive in each individual case."  
Based on the provisions of these articles and considering the particular circumstances of the analyzed case, the AEPD has decided to impose an administrative fine.  
Finally, it should be noted that Recital 148 of the GDPR states:  
“(148) In order to reinforce the application of the rules of this Regulation, any infringement of it must be punished with sanctions, including administrative fines, in addition to appropriate measures imposed by the supervisory authority under this Regulation, or in substitution of these. In the case of a minor infringement, or if the fine that would likely be imposed constitutes a disproportionate burden for a natural person, a warning may be imposed instead of a fine. However, special attention must be paid to the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, its intentional character, the measures taken to mitigate the damages suffered, the degree of responsibility or any relevant previous infringements, the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, compliance with measures ordered against the controller or processor, adherence to codes of conduct, and any other aggravating or mitigating circumstances. (…)”  
From the content of this recital, it follows that in the case examined, a warning would not be appropriate, as it is not a minor infringement nor can it be appreciated that the fine constitutes a disproportionate burden for a natural person (the sanction is being imposed on a company).

The measures adopted are typical of a data controller acting based on the principle of proactive responsibility (Article 5.2 GDPR); likewise, as analyzed, the circumstances for graduation that would allow for a reduction in the amount of the fine to be imposed do not exist.

In accordance with the European Commission's Guidelines on the method of setting fines, there must be objective evidence that the imposition of the fine would irreparably jeopardize the economic viability of the affected company. Furthermore, the risks must be analyzed in a specific social and economic context.”  
For all the above reasons, all allegations made by the party being claimed against the initiation agreement are dismissed.

Data Breach  
Article 4, paragraph 12 of the GDPR broadly defines “personal data breach” (hereinafter security breach) as “a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration of, or unauthorized disclosure of, or access to personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed.”  
In the present case, there is a personal data breach under the circumstances mentioned above, categorized as a confidentiality breach, as the personal mobile phone number of the complainant and the personal mobile phone numbers of two others (…) were added to a WhatsApp group called “Equality Commission,” which had been previously created by the company using a corporate mobile phone. This circumstance has resulted in those mobile phone numbers being exposed and potentially known to the other members of the WhatsApp group.  
V  
Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR  
Article 5.1.f) “Principles relating to processing” of the GDPR states:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
(...)  
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, by means of appropriate technical or organizational measures (‘integrity and confidentiality’).”  
In the present case, it is noted that the personal mobile phone numbers, both of the complainant and of two others (…), (personal data) were improperly disclosed to the other members of the WhatsApp group called “Equality Commission,” which had been previously created by the company using a corporate mobile phone. This circumstance constitutes a violation of their confidentiality.  
In this regard, we must recall the definition of personal data contained in Article 4 of the GDPR: “… ‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person…”.  
Not to mention that, in Recital 26 of the same legal text, it is provided that “the principles of data protection should apply to all information concerning an identified or identifiable natural person.”

VII  
Article 32 GDPR  
Regarding the security of personal data, Article 32 of the GDPR, "Security of processing," establishes:  
"1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d) a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
2. In assessing the appropriateness of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the processing of data, in particular as a consequence of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data.  
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this article.  
4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data may only process such data on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law.”  
In this regard, Recital 83 of the GDPR states that “(83) In order to maintain security and prevent processing from infringing the provisions of this Regulation, the controller or the processor must assess the inherent risks of processing and implement measures to mitigate them, such as encryption. These measures must ensure an adequate level of security, including confidentiality, taking into account the state of the art and the cost of their implementation in relation to the risks and the nature of the personal data that must be protected. When assessing the risk in relation to data security, the risks arising from the processing of personal data must be taken into account, such as the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data, which may particularly cause physical, material, or immaterial damage.” (the underlining is ours)  
In the case examined, it cannot be appreciated that the data controller (LISMARTSA) has applied appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security adequate to the risk concerning the personal data it processed (personal mobile phone numbers of three employees), especially regarding the ability to ensure the confidentiality of such data.
