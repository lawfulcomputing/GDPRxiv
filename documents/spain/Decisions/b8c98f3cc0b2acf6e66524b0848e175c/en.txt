LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
The Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to resolve this appeal, in accordance with the provisions of Article 123 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter LPACAP) and Article 48.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD).  
II  
Response to the allegations presented  
First allegation: Nullity of the sanctioning resolution.  
The appellant claims that in the contested sanctioning resolution, the SECRETARY OF STATE FOR SECURITY is cited at the beginning with NIF S2816001H as responsible for the sanctioning conduct, while such responsibility should be attributed to the person holding the position of Director General of Coordination and Studies.  
They argue that, although the General Administration of the State has a single legal personality, its agencies have specific competencies and responsibilities defined by regulations. They assert that Royal Decree 207/2024 and the repealed Royal Decree 734/2020 assign specific functions to the Directorate General of Coordination and Studies, which include support and advice to the Secretary of State for Security in the coordination and supervision of the State Security Forces and Corps. In this regard, they indicate that the NIF S2816001H used in the resolution does not correspond to the Directorate General of Coordination and Studies.  
Similarly, they claim that, in the documentation of the processing and the activity register of the Ministry of the Interior, the person holding the position of Director General of Coordination and Studies is established as responsible for the processing.  
Consequently, they argue that the incorrect attribution of the infringing conduct to the Secretary of State for Security instead of to the Directorate General of Coordination and Studies is considered a cause of nullity in accordance with Article 47 of the LPACAP, subsections c) “those that have an impossible content” and g) “Any other that is expressly established in a provision with the rank of Law.”

On the other hand, the appellant's allegation that the resolution has an impossible content cannot be favorably accepted. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly indicated, defects of full nullity must be subject to a strict interpretation. The case of nullity due to impossible content refers to acts that are materially or physically impossible, that are totally and originally inadequate to the physical reality on which they fall, or that contain an internal contradiction in their terms. The Supreme Court has emphasized that this specific cause of full nullity must be assessed with utmost caution, avoiding an inappropriate extension of the legal assumption.  
In this regard, the appellant has not provided any argument regarding the reasons on which they consider that the resolution has impossible content. It has not been specified to what extent the resolution is materially or physically impossible to execute, nor has any internal contradiction been pointed out that would justify such nullity. Without adequate justification, the allegation lacks a solid foundation.  
It follows clearly from the above that in the present case, the appealed sanctioning resolution does not possess impossible content. Regarding the allegation that the infringement would be time-barred, this cannot be sustained either, due to the permanent nature of the imposed infringement, which consists of failing to carry out a DPIA despite being obliged to do so. In fact, it remains obliged to carry out a DPIA.  
In this regard, it is appropriate to mention the Judgment of the Supreme Court of November 4, 2013, which states the following:  
“In applying the statute of limitations, we must determine the starting date for the applicable two-year period, which, in accordance with Article 47.2 of the LOPD, will begin to be counted from the day the infringement was committed. This rule, similar to that established generally in Article 132 of the LRJPAC, applies in cases of completed instantaneous infringements, but requires some clarifications to determine when continued and permanent infringements should be considered committed.  
The continued infringement is referred to in Article 4.6, paragraph 2, of the Procedure Regulation for the Exercise of Sanctioning Power, approved by Royal Decree 1398/1993, of August 4 (RPEPS), which establishes that '... the performance of a plurality of actions or omissions that infringe the same or similar administrative provisions, executed according to a preconceived plan or taking advantage of the same opportunity, shall be sanctionable as a continued infringement.'  
Unlike the continued infringement, which requires a plurality of actions that infringe the same provision and thus constitutes a real concurrence of offenses, the permanent infringement does not require a concurrence of offenses but rather a single action of a lasting nature, whose unlawful content extends over time, as long as the active subject does not decide to cease their conduct.  
In the field of criminal law, the differentiation between the various types of infringements is significant for determining the initial date for calculating the statute of limitations, as indicated by Article 132.1 of the Penal Code, which, alongside the general rule that the limitation periods will be computed from the day the infringement was committed, establishes specific rules for cases of continued and permanent offenses, where the periods will be computed, respectively, from the day the last infringement was committed or from when the conduct ceased. These rules and criteria from Criminal Law have been accepted in the jurisprudence of this Chamber when calculating the limitation periods for infringements.  
In matters of data protection, such as the one at hand, this Chamber has distinguished, in judgments of March 7, 2006 (appeal 1728/2002) and November 20, 2007 (appeal 170/2003), between continued infringements, as defined in Article 4.6 of the aforementioned RPEPS, and permanent infringements, understood as 'those unlawful behaviors that persist over time and are not exhausted by a single act, determining the maintenance of the situation.'

Against this resolution, which concludes the administrative procedure in accordance with Article 48.6 of the LOPDGDD, and in accordance with the provisions of Article 123 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (LPACAP), the interested parties may file a contentious-administrative appeal before the Contentious-Administrative Chamber of the National Court, in accordance with the provisions of Article 25 and paragraph 5 of the fourth additional provision of Law 29/1998, of July 13, regulating the Contentious-Administrative Jurisdiction, within two months from the day following the notification of this act, as provided in Article 46.1 of the aforementioned Law.  
Finally, it is noted that in accordance with the provisions of Article 90.3 a) LPACAP, the firm resolution in the administrative procedure may be provisionally suspended if the interested party expresses their intention to file a contentious-administrative appeal. If this is the case, the interested party must formally communicate this fact by means of a written document addressed to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, submitting it through the Agency's Electronic Registry [https://sedeagpd.gob.es/sede-electronica-web/], or through any of the other registries provided for in Article 16.4 of the aforementioned LPACAP. They must also provide the Agency with documentation that proves the effective filing of the contentious-administrative appeal. If the Agency is not aware of the filing of the contentious-administrative appeal within two months from the day following the notification of this resolution, it would consider the provisional suspension to be concluded.
