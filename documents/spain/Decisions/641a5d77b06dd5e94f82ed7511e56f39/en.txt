Considering the claim filed on October 10, 2022, with this Agency by A.A.A. (hereinafter the claimant) against ALOKABIDE, S.A. (hereinafter the respondent), for not having duly attended to their right of access.  
After carrying out the procedural actions provided for in Title VIII of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD), the following FACTS have been established:  

FIRST: The claimant exercised their right of access against the respondent with NIF A01300706, and their request, as stated by the claimant, did not receive the legally established response. They provide various documents related to the claim made before this Agency and regarding the exercise of the right exercised. The claimant also requests the inspection reports, results, and photos taken at their home by the respondent.  

SECOND: In accordance with Article 65.4 of the LOPDGDD, which has established a mechanism prior to the admission of claims filed with the AEPD, consisting of transferring them to the Data Protection Delegates designated by the data controllers or processors, for the purposes provided for in Article 37 of the aforementioned regulation, or to them when they have not been designated, the claim was forwarded to the respondent entity for analysis and to respond to the claimant and this Agency within one month.  

THIRD: The result of the transfer procedure indicated in the previous Fact did not allow for the understanding that the claims of the claimant were satisfied. Consequently, on January 10, 2023, for the purposes provided for in Article 64.1 of the LOPDGDD, the Director of the Spanish Agency for the Protection of Data agreed to admit the claim for processing and informed the parties that the maximum period to resolve the present procedure, which is understood to have commenced through said admission agreement, will be six months.

THIRD: The rights of individuals regarding the protection of personal data are regulated in Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR and Articles 13 to 18 of the LOPDGDD. The rights of access, rectification, deletion, opposition, the right to restrict processing, and the right to data portability are contemplated. The formal aspects related to the exercise of these rights are established in Articles 12 of the GDPR and 12 of the LOPDGDD.  
Moreover, consideration is given to what is expressed in Recitals 59 and following of the GDPR.  
In accordance with the provisions of these regulations, the data controller must establish formulas and mechanisms to facilitate the exercise of rights by the data subject, which will be free of charge (without prejudice to the provisions of Articles 12.5 and 15.3 of the GDPR), and is obliged to respond to requests made no later than one month, unless it can demonstrate that it is unable to identify the data subject, and to express its reasons in case it does not intend to comply with such request. The burden of proof of compliance with the duty to respond to the request for the exercise of rights made by the affected party falls on the controller.  
The communication addressed to the data subject in connection with their request must be expressed in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible manner, using clear and simple language.  
Regarding the right of access to personal data, according to what is established in Article 13 of the LOPDGDD, when the exercise of the right refers to a large amount of data, the controller may request the affected party to specify the "data or processing activities to which the request refers." The right will be considered granted if the controller provides remote access to the data, being deemed that the request has been attended to (although the data subject may request the information referred to in the aspects provided in Article 15 of the GDPR).  
The exercise of this right may be considered repetitive on more than one occasion during a period of six months, unless there is a legitimate reason for it.  
On the other hand, the request will be considered excessive when the affected party chooses a means other than that offered which entails a disproportionate cost, which must be borne by the affected party.

In the case analyzed here, the claimant exercised their right of access through a form, expressly requesting as part of the right of access photographs, reports, and results regarding the property they are renting. The respondent, throughout the procedure, responds by providing the claimant with the routes they follow in their privacy policy in a generic manner, and individually sends the housing contract and some tax documents containing the claimant's data. Furthermore, the respondent, in their response to the claimant, states that: “Regarding what you included in your request to ‘Add the inspection reports, results, and photos taken in my home by Alokabide,’ we must indicate that this type of data is not personal in nature, and therefore, it is not appropriate to request it through the DPO since this channel is not suitable for the request for that information.” In the response to the AEPD, the report requested by the respondent is identified as “a pure technical report on some leaks that generated a series of events sent to the insurance of that property, which took care of the repairs.”  
However, the respondent indicates to the AEPD that they were not referring to the incident that occurred in the rented property but rather that what they requested “is for the photos and files generated by Alokabide regarding the contract renewals to be added, where a thorough inspection of the entire house is conducted, taking several photos of all the rooms, with my personal belongings inside, and which they tell me I must accept in order to renew my lease, without me knowing where those photos go, to whom they are sent, the time they are kept, or what is done with them afterwards, in addition to denying me information about the results of those inspections.”  
Therefore, in light of what was requested and what was provided, we must affirm that the right of access has not been adequately addressed.  
Firstly, the right of access has not been addressed as set forth in the aforementioned regulations, which requires not only confirmation of whether the personal data concerning the data subject is being processed and the information described in the

Article 15.1 of the GDPR states that the data subject must access the actual personal data, as this constitutes the core of this right. Access to personal data refers, therefore, not only to a general description of the data or a mere reference to the categories of personal data processed by the data controller, but to the specific and concrete personal data concerning the data subject that are being processed by the data controller.  
In the case examined and for this specific situation, although the data controller has provided certain documents, some of which contained personal data intrinsically linked to the document, such as the signature of the claimant in the signed rental contract, this does not exempt them from providing the entirety of personal data to the claimant in the terms previously specified.  
Secondly, the claimant has requested the inspection reports, results, and photographs taken at their home by the data controller for the purpose of proceeding with the renewals of the rental contract.  
The respondent claims that both the photographs and the reports would not fall within the right of access, asserting that these photographs and reports do not contain data of the claimant, which they consider not to be personal data, thereby denying access to such personal data.  
Well, the concept of personal data arises from Article 4.1 of the GDPR, which states that personal data is: “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  
The Opinion 4/2007 of the Article 29 Working Party on the concept of personal data already stated that the definition of personal data in Directive 95/46/EC “reflects the intention of the European legislator for a broad notion of personal data,” resulting in the GDPR maintaining the Directive's definition of personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person.”  
The definition reflects the legislator's intention to maintain a broad concept of “personal data,” which requires a wide interpretation that encompasses all information that can be linked to an identified person, or refers to an identifiable person, with the aim of protecting the fundamental freedoms and rights of natural persons, among others, particularly their right to privacy concerning the processing of personal data.

The interpretation of what constitutes personal data cannot, therefore, be interpreted restrictively, under penalty of contravening the provisions of the GDPR.  
This is based on the consideration that "the practical objective of the right of access is to allow individuals to have control over their own personal data," as stated in the Guidelines 1/2022 on the rights of data subjects – Right of Access by the European Data Protection Board, adopted on January 18, 2022.  
The aforementioned broadness in the definition of personal data is defined by:  
- "any information," including all data that provides information, regardless of the type of information, which must have a broad interpretation, encompassing subjective or objective information, including assessments, diagnoses, or opinions.  
- "about" a natural person, thus relating any type of information about a natural person. Here, it is essential to connect the purpose of the information with the "about" whom it concerns and the effects it may have for that person.  
- "identified" or "identifiable." Identified refers to any natural person who is distinguished within a group of people from all members of the group; identifiable is the natural person who, although not yet identified, can be identified.  
We will particularly focus on the second component of the definition "about," which determines the relationships, the links between the information and the natural person.  
Opinion 4/2007 of the Article 29 Working Party establishes that "Generally, information can be considered to be 'about' a person when it refers to them."  
It continues to clarify that "...it could be stated that to consider that the data is 'about' a person, there must be a 'content' element or a 'purpose' element or a 'result' element."  
The 'content' element is present in those cases where - according to what society generally and commonly understands by the word "about" - information is provided about a specific person, regardless of any purpose that the data controller or a third party may have, or the impact of that information on the data subject. Information is 'about' a person when it 'refers to' that person, which must be evaluated considering all the circumstances surrounding the case. For example, the results of a medical analysis clearly refer to the patient, or the information contained in a company's file under the name of a specific client clearly refers to them. (…)  
The presence of a 'purpose' element may also determine that the information is 'about' a specific person. It can be considered that this 'purpose' element exists when the data is used or is likely to be used, taking into account all the circumstances surrounding the specific case, with the

purpose of evaluating, treating in a certain way, or influencing the situation or behavior of a person.  
We are faced with a third category of "about" when there is an element of "result." Despite the absence of an element of "content" or "purpose," it can be considered that the data pertains "about" a specific person because, taking into account all the circumstances surrounding the specific case, it is likely that its use will affect the rights and interests of a particular person. It is sufficient that the person may be treated differently by others as a consequence of the processing of such data.  
These three elements (content, purpose, and result) should be considered as alternative and not cumulative conditions” (the underlining is ours).  
To the above, we must add that the aforementioned Guidelines 1/2022 provide that among the personal data that must be provided through the right of access are those consciously and actively provided by the data subject (such as responses to a questionnaire), or those data derived from other data (such as the country of residence derived from the postal code), or those inferred from other data, instead of being directly provided by the data subject (to assign a credit score or results from a health assessment or a personalization or recommendation process), among others.  
These Guidelines state that personal data includes the elements that have been used to reach a decision such as, for example, employee promotions, salary increases, or new job assignments. They further indicate that “…the right of access includes both inferred and derived data… the data subject must be provided not only with the personal data provided to the data controller for the purpose of conducting subsequent analysis or evaluation of this data but also with the result of such subsequent analysis or evaluation” (the underlining is ours).  
In the case examined and according to the data contained in the administrative file, the personal data collected by the data controller has been used to reassign the rented property to the claimant.  
According to the above, the photographs (of the interior of the property and with the belongings of the claimant), the report, and the result of it have been used to evaluate her condition to continue being a tenant of the property, to the extent that its use affects her rights and interests, since if the result of the evaluation is negative, it could hinder the renewal of the contract. And all this without prejudice to the fact that the right of access does not include copies of documents but access to personal data, understood as any information about an identified or identifiable natural person.  
Therefore, they are not mere photographs or random reports, but rather show particularities linked to the claimant; thus, in light of this specific task, they would be included in the right of access.
