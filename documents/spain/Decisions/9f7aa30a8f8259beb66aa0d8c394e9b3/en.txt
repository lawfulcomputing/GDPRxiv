Second.- That in Article 28.4 of the GDPR, the following is stated regarding the obligations of the Data Processor: “When a data processor engages another processor to carry out certain processing activities on behalf of the controller, the same data protection obligations as those stipulated in the contract or other legal act between the controller and the processor referred to in paragraph 3 shall be imposed on this other processor, by means of a contract or other legal act established in accordance with Union or Member State law, in particular the provision of sufficient guarantees to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures in such a way that the processing is in compliance with the provisions of this Regulation. If that other processor fails to comply with its data protection obligations, the initial processor shall remain fully liable to the controller for compliance with the obligations of the other processor.”  
Specifically, this last part of Article 28.4 GDPR refers to the fact that the data processor will be responsible for the processing carried out by its processors, in accordance with the fulfillment of the obligations of Article 28 of the GDPR.  
This translates to the fact that, in the event of non-compliance by the providers selected by the data processor, that is, as in the present case by QLIP, it must take direct responsibility for the actions and/or omissions of its providers, and not, on the contrary, CLIDOM.  
Third.- In relation to the attribution of responsibilities in this sanctioning procedure, it is important to note that Article 82 of the GDPR, titled “Right to compensation and liability,” states in its second paragraph that: “Any controller participating in the processing operation shall be liable for damages caused where that operation does not comply with the provisions of this Regulation. On this point, it should be noted that the Supreme Court has addressed this type of issue in its ruling of 10/22/2020 (RCA 4535/2019), which addresses the issue that must be pronounced upon by the Administrative Litigation Chamber of the Supreme Court, with the aim of forming jurisprudence,  
“focuses on determining whether the competent authority to resolve the sanctioning procedure (in this case, the Regulatory Supervision Chamber of the National Commission of Markets and Competition) can impose a sanction based on the modification or alteration of the legal qualification formulated by the Instructor of the sanctioning procedure, in those cases where the resolution proposal suggests the dismissal of the file and the archiving of the proceedings, considering that the facts do not constitute an administrative infringement. It also concerns establishing in which cases this action constitutes a violation of the accusatory principle and, therefore, of the right to defense and the right to be informed of the accusation, guaranteed by Article 24.2 of the Constitution, in light of the interpretation made by the Tri-

To this end, the Chamber, after analyzing the doctrine of the Constitutional Court regarding the accusatory principle and the doctrine derived from the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court on this matter, draws the following conclusions:  
“1st.- The imposition of a sanction more severe than that announced in the proposed resolution requires a new hearing if it arises from facts different from those contained in the proposal or if it is a consequence of a modification of the legal qualification of the same.  
2nd.- A more severe sanction cannot be imposed without prior hearing if it is a consequence of the rejection of modifying circumstances that had been taken into account in the proposed resolution. (Specifically, the aforementioned rulings of the Supreme Court of October 30, 2013 - appeal no. 2184/2012 - and May 21, 2014 - appeal no. 492/2013 - refer to a cause of mitigation of responsibility, regulated in Article 66 of Law 15/2007, of July 3, on the Defense of Competition, which was considered in the proposed resolution and rejected, without prior hearing, in the sanctioning resolution).  
3rd.- The jurisprudence is not uniform in determining whether a repetition of the hearing procedure is necessary when the sanctioning resolution assumes the facts as reported by the instructor in their proposal and does not change their legal qualification, deviating from the proposal only in the determination of the exact amount of the sanction within the range corresponding to that legal qualification. However, it seems that the most recent jurisprudence tends to require a new hearing procedure even in these cases.”  
Therefore, based on the aforementioned jurisprudence of this Chamber of the Supreme Court, as well as the doctrine of the Constitutional Court, we maintain that, in the case adjudicated in this appeal for cassation, it was not appropriate for the court of first instance to declare the nullity of the sanctions imposed for violation of the accusatory principle, since we reject that the National Commission of Markets and Competition violated the accusatory principle, as it should be understood to be protected under Article 24 of the Constitution, since we ascertain that in the processing of the sanctioning procedure the procedural guarantees derived from the aforementioned constitutional precept have been respected, as the principle of separation between the instructing body and the resolving body has been preserved, as well as the right of defense and the right to be informed of the accusation.

Let us remember that this article has as its antecedent Article 24 of the GDPR, which establishes that, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the GDPR.  
Furthermore, Recital 78 of the GDPR states that the protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data requires the adoption of appropriate technical and organizational measures in order to ensure compliance with the requirements of the GDPR and to be able to demonstrate such compliance with the GDPR. The data controller must adopt internal policies and implement measures that particularly comply with the principles of data protection by design and by default. It indicates, regarding the measures to be taken, that they could be, for example, minimizing the processing of personal data, pseudonymizing personal data as soon as possible, or providing transparency regarding the functions and processing of personal data, allowing data subjects to monitor the processing of their personal data.  
Therefore, based on all of this, the principle of data protection by design is the key that the data controller must follow to demonstrate effective compliance with the GDPR, as it is the data controller who must adopt internal policies and implement measures that particularly comply with the principles of data protection by design and by default. That is, it is the data controller who, from the moment they design and plan a potential processing of personal data, must determine all the elements that make up such processing, in order to effectively apply the principles of data protection, integrating everything necessary to comply with the provisions of the GDPR, with the aim of protecting the rights and freedoms of each and every data subject.  
Regarding the risks that may be present in the processing, the controller must carry out an exercise of analysis and detection of the same throughout the entire data processing cycle, with the ultimate goal of protecting the rights and freedoms of all data subjects, and not only when the processing actually occurs. This is expressed in the Guidelines 4/2019 of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) regarding Article 25 Data protection by design and by default, indicating in this regard:  
“35. In the process of making such decisions, the data controller must evaluate the appropriate measures and guarantees to effectively apply the principles and rights of the data subjects in the processing, and take into account elements such as risks, the state of the art, and the cost of implementation, as well as the nature, scope, context, and purposes. Taking into consideration the PDDD (data protection by design and by default) from the outset is crucial for the correct application of the principles and for the protection of the rights of the data subjects. The Privacy by Design Guide of the AEPD states that “Privacy by design (PbD) involves using a risk management and proactive responsibility approach to establish strategies that incorporate privacy protection throughout the entire lifecycle of the object (whether it is a system, hardware or software product, service, or process). Therefore, privacy by design, an obligation of the data controller that arises before the system is operational, is not a patch used to resolve an incident detected in the system.  
The risk-based approach refers directly and immediately to a preventive system aimed at visualizing, regarding personal data processing, the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Data protection by design is part of the compliance management system, which involves conceiving and planning the processing, verifying its compliance, and being able to demonstrate it, all framed within a process of continuous review and improvement.
