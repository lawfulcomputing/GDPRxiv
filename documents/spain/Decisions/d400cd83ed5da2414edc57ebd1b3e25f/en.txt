Precisely, the obligations imposed by the GDPR on data controllers are established with the aim of protecting the rights of the data subjects from the perspective of the risks present in the processing of personal data, identifying the risks, assessing them, evaluating them, and implementing technical and organizational measures of all kinds to mitigate or avoid the probability of materialization.  
For the same reason, it cannot be accepted as a mitigating factor that it is now generically offered, in the event that any of the affected parties has suffered any specific harm, to put measures in place to mitigate and repair them, since Article 83.2.c of the GDPR refers to taking into account “any measures taken by the controller or processor to alleviate the damages and harms suffered by the data subjects,” meaning certain and already adopted measures, and not possible future measures that could supposedly be adopted in the event that any of the affected parties suffered any harm.

The extremely limited dissemination of the video and the null benefits obtained  
DISPLAY claims, providing supporting documentation in this regard, that the video only had 385 views, which translates into no benefit, which should be considered as a mitigating factor under Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR.  
In this regard, it should be noted that, regarding this criterion, Article 76.2 of the LOPDGDD, in its letter c), includes among the criteria that must be weighed when determining the amount of the sanction “the benefits obtained as a result of the commission of the infringement” and not the absence of these benefits. The Judgment of the National Court, dated 05/05/2021, refers to the necessity for the “fact-based” premise contemplated in the regulation to apply a certain criterion of graduation, and, as stated, the absence of benefits is not among the circumstances regulated in the cited article.  
This criterion of graduation is established in the LOPDGDD in accordance with the provisions of Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR, according to which administrative fines will be imposed taking into account any “aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement,” understanding that avoiding a loss has the same nature for these purposes as obtaining benefits.  
If we add to this that the sanctions must be “effective, proportionate, and dissuasive in each individual case,” in accordance with the provisions of Article 83.1 of the GDPR, admitting the absence of benefits as a mitigating factor is not only contrary to the factual premises contemplated in Article 76.2.c), but also contrary to what is established in Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR and the principles indicated. Thus, valuing the absence of benefits as a mitigating factor would nullify the dissuasive effect of the fine, as it diminishes the effect of the circumstances that effectively impact its quantification, providing the responsible party with a benefit to which they are not entitled. It would be an artificial reduction of the sanction that could lead to the understanding that violating the regulation without obtaining benefits, financial or otherwise, would not produce a negative effect proportional to the seriousness of the infringing act. In any case, the administrative fines established in the GDPR, in accordance with what is provided in its Article 83.2, are imposed based on the circumstances of each individual case and, in the present case, it is not considered that the absence of benefits is an adequate and determining graduation factor to assess the seriousness of the infringing conduct.

3. Violation of the principle of typicity.  
Finally, it should be noted that DISPLAY has alleged a violation of the principle of typicity arguing, on the one hand, that it is not true that the personal data of 51 women victims of gender-based violence is displayed, but rather that of 40, as well as claiming that it does not agree with the circumstances that have been taken into account to aggravate the sanction.  
In this regard, it should be indicated that this is not related to the principle of typicity in sanctioning matters, as the events that occurred constitute a violation of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, the infringement of which is typified in Article 83.5 of the GDPR, regardless of the number of victims of gender-based violence affected.  
Therefore, the arguments put forth by DISPLAY to allege the supposed violation of the principle of typicity actually refer, on the one hand, to the discussion about the proven fact that the data of 51 women victims of gender-based violence have been affected, an argument that has been adequately addressed in the previous points of this Legal Basis and, on the other hand, to DISPLAY's belief that the circumstances considered as aggravating factors for the sanction are not applicable, allegations that have also been duly responded to and argued in this Legal Basis.  
For all the above reasons, the allegations made are dismissed.

B) Disproportion of the sanction in relation to the economic circumstances involved  
DISPLAY raises two issues here again:  
B.1. The very limited dissemination of the video and the null returns obtained  
DISPLAY argues, providing supporting documentation in this regard, that the video only had 385 views, which translates into no benefit, which should be considered as a mitigating factor under Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR.  
In response, it should be noted again that, regarding this criterion, Article 76.2 of the LOPDGDD, in its letter c), includes among the criteria that must be weighed when determining the amount of the sanction “the benefits obtained as a result of the commission of the infringement” and not the absence of these benefits. The Judgment of the National Court, dated 05/05/2021, refers to the necessity that the “budget” of fact contemplated in the norm must be present for a certain graduation criterion to be applied, and, as stated, the absence of benefits is not among the circumstances regulated in the cited article.  
This graduation criterion is established in the LOPDGDD in accordance with the provisions of Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR, according to which administrative fines will be imposed taking into account any “aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement,” understanding that avoiding a loss has the same nature for these purposes as obtaining benefits.  
If we add to this that the sanctions must be “in each individual case” effective, proportionate, and dissuasive, in accordance with the provisions of Article 83.1 of the GDPR, admitting the absence of benefits as a mitigating factor is not only contrary to the factual premises contemplated in Article 76.2.c), but also contrary to what is established in Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR and to the principles indicated. Thus, valuing the absence of benefits as a mitigating factor would nullify the dissuasive effect of the fine, as it diminishes the effect of the circumstances that effectively impact its quantification, providing the responsible party with a benefit to which they are not entitled. It would be an artificial reduction of the sanction that could lead to the understanding that violating the norm without obtaining benefits, financial or otherwise, would not produce a negative effect proportional to the seriousness of the infringing act. In any case, the administrative fines established in the GDPR, in accordance with what is established in its Article 83.2, are imposed based on the circumstances of each individual case and, in the present case, it is not considered that the absence of benefits is an adequate and determining graduation factor to assess the seriousness of the infringing conduct.

IV Right to Data Protection  
Individuals have the power of disposition over their personal data, as well as over its dissemination, and undoubtedly, the person whose personal data is disseminated in violation of the legal order deserves protection.  
Thus, the Constitutional Court ruling STC 292/2000, dated November 30, states that,  
“the content of the fundamental right to data protection consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that enables the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which data this third party may collect, and also allows the individual to know who holds that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose that possession or use.  
These powers of disposition and control over personal data, which are part of the content of the fundamental right to data protection, are legally specified in the authority to consent to the collection, obtaining, and access to personal data, its subsequent storage and processing, as well as its possible use or uses by a third party, whether the State or a private individual. And this right to consent to the knowledge and processing, whether computerized or not, of personal data requires, as indispensable complements, on the one hand, the authority to know at all times who has that personal data and what use they are subjecting it to, and, on the other hand, the power to oppose that possession and uses.”  
In this regard, and regardless of what the legal basis legitimizing the processing may be, every data controller must respect the principles of processing set forth in Article 5 of the GDPR. We will highlight Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, which establishes that:  
“1. Personal data shall be  
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’);”  
However, we are dealing with a fundamental right that is not absolute, since, in certain cases, the Fundamental Right to Data Protection may yield to the prevalence of other rights and freedoms that are also constitutionally recognized and protected, such as the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information, being weighed on a case-by-case basis.  
Nevertheless, in the present case, as will be explained, it must be considered that the processing carried out by the responding party within the framework of freedom of information has been excessive, as there is no prevailing public interest in the dissemination of the names and surnames and specific protective measures of 51 victims of gender-based violence, making them clearly identifiable. V Right to Information  
In the conflict between the Fundamental Rights to Freedom of Information and the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, although equal degrees of protection are recognized for both constitutional rights, the former is generally given precedence by our courts after evaluating and weighing all the elements at play.  
However, preponderance does not mean prevalence when, considering all the concurrent circumstances in a specific case, the limits established by regulations and jurisprudence are exceeded.  
In this regard, the Article 29 Working Party in its Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC, when examining the legal basis of legitimate interest under Article 7.1.f) of Directive 95/46/EC, fully transferable to the current Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR, includes the right to freedom of expression or information as one of the scenarios in which the issue of legitimate interest may arise, asserting that, “without prejudice to whether the interests of the data controller will ultimately prevail over the interests and rights of the data subjects when the balancing test is conducted.”

This is not, as in other jurisprudential cases examined, about giving precedence to one fundamental right over another, having to choose which one carries more weight in a specific case. The reconciliation of both rights is nothing new, as the European legislator mandates such reconciliation in Article 85 of the GDPR.

In the present case, the following aggravating circumstances are considered concurrent:  
- As aggravating factors:  
- The scope or purpose of the data processing operation, as well as the affected parties (Article 83.2.a) of the GDPR): It is considered that the nature of the infringement is very serious since it entails a loss of disposition and control over the personal data (name and surname) of individuals who have been victims of gender-based violence, and by disseminating such personal data, there is a real risk that such individuals may be recognized by third parties, with the serious harm and detriment that this would cause them. Furthermore, the number of affected individuals is relevant, amounting to 51.  
- The intentionality or negligence of the infringement by the claimed party (Article 83.2.b) of the GDPR): Although the Agency considers that there was no intentionality on the part of the media outlet, it concludes that it was negligent in not ensuring a procedure that guaranteed the protection of personal data in such sensitive circumstances, especially considering that it is an entity whose activity involves continuous processing of users' personal data.  
It is particularly important to recall at this point the Judgment of the National Court of October 17, 2007 (rec. 63/2006), which states: “…the Supreme Court has understood that there is negligence whenever a legal duty of care is disregarded, that is, when the infringer does not behave with the required diligence. And in assessing the degree of diligence, the professionalism or lack thereof of the subject must be especially weighed, and there is no doubt that, in the case now examined, when the activity of the appellant involves constant and abundant handling of personal data, it must be insisted upon the rigor and exquisite care to comply with the legal provisions in this regard.”  
- Any previous infringement committed by the responsible party or the processor (Article 83.2.e) of the GDPR): in the present case, the claimed party has been previously sanctioned by a firm resolution in the administrative route in the following sanctioning files:  
PS/00194/2022: publication on the same website (www.publico.es) of the audio of the statement before the judge of a victim of multiple rape, to illustrate the news regarding the holding of the trial, without distorting the victim's voice. The sanctioning resolution, dated May 27, 2022, which is firm in the administrative route, also determined the infringement of Article 5.1 c) of the GDPR precisely for understanding that, weighing the Fundamental Rights to Freedom of Information and to Data Protection, the claimed party has processed data that was excessive as it was not necessary for the purpose for which it was processed.  
PS/00555/2022: publication, on the same website (www.publico.es) of an expert report in which the name and surname of a minor appear, without obfuscation, to illustrate news related to a well-known and media-covered judicial proceeding involving one of their parents. The sanctioning resolution, dated January 10, 2023, which is firm in the administrative route, also determined the infringement of Article 5.1c) of the GDPR precisely for understanding that, weighing the Fundamental Rights to Freedom of Information and to Data Protection, the claimed party has processed data that was excessive as it was not necessary for the purpose for which it was processed.
