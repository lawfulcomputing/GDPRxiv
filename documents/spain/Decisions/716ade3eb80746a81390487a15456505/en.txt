3. The data protection regulations are aimed at ensuring the legality of the processing of personal data and not at ensuring effective judicial protection.  
The Court of Justice of the European Union (First Chamber), in its Judgment of 04/05/2023, Case 487/21, has declared that “Data subjects must have the right to access personal data collected concerning them and to exercise that right easily and at reasonable intervals, in order to know and verify the lawfulness of the processing.”  
Both the regulations and the actions of the AEPD must be directed towards ensuring the lawful processing of personal data.  
According to ECI, the claiming party did not exercise their rights to verify that a lawful and legal processing of their personal data was taking place, but rather the intended purpose was to obtain alleged evidence that could be used in a judicial proceeding for claiming damages due to injuries suffered from a fall.  
The agreement itself openly acknowledges this on page 16:  
“In relation to this issue, it should be considered that the ultimate purpose pursued by not deleting the images requested by the claimant, the holder of the data in question, is to obtain evidence of the causation of damages to her daughter, as a result of an accident that occurred in a center of the claimed party in which she was harmed by possible negligence of that entity.”  
While not denying the importance of the right to judicial protection as a fundamental right recognized by Article 24, this party understands that ensuring the effectiveness of that right corresponds to judges and courts, but not to the AEPD.  
Therefore, if one person denies another the possibility of obtaining usable evidence in a subsequent judicial process, that obstructive behavior must be assessed in the appropriate manner, but it must be evaluated in a judicial setting, without an administrative authority being responsible for sanctioning the prior potential deletion of evidence.

7. Regarding the unjustified application of aggravating factors.  
In the case at hand, the first aggravating factor applied has been that related to the "nature and severity of the infringement and the level of damages suffered."  
In this regard, the initiation agreement understands that the definitive deletion of the images limited the possibility of remedying the lack of attention to the right and harmed the claimant's ability to defend themselves.  
It should be noted that the alleged improper deletion of the images constitutes the infringing act, so the devaluation of the infringing type cannot be considered, in addition, as an aggravating circumstance.  
The second circumstance considered in the agreement as an aggravating factor is related to the "intentionality or negligence in the infringement." In this case, the agreement understands that there was no intentionality but "negligence" ("these circumstances demonstrate negligent conduct..." it states on page 21/26), but, nevertheless, it is negatively assessed as an aggravating factor, understanding that "a company that systematically and continuously processes data must exercise extreme care in fulfilling its obligations regarding data protection."  
This party understands that it is contradictory to assess the concurrence of negligence as an aggravating circumstance when the subjective element (negligence or intentionality) is something that must be present, by definition, in every infringement.  
Therefore, this circumstance may be assessed to grade the infringement to aggravate it when there has been "intentionality," which is the more serious conduct, or to mitigate it when what has occurred has been "negligence," which is the less serious conduct.  
In the case at hand, the agreement considers that the entity is a company that systematically and continuously processes data, so the degree of

The required diligence is greater. Well, this condition of being a large company can never be a condition per se that aggravates the infringement. However, once that circumstance is assessed and it is concluded that the conduct has been negligent and not intentional, that negligence (the lowest degree of the subjective element) cannot be considered as a circumstance that aggravates the infringement instead of mitigating it.  
The same conclusion can be drawn regarding the circumstance related to "the connection of the infringer's activity with the processing of personal data." As previously stated, the greater or lesser connection with data processing may be a parameter for measuring the required diligence but cannot be considered an aggravating factor in itself. Otherwise, an entity like ECI would inherently carry this aggravating circumstance regardless of the infringement committed, regardless of the diligence exercised in the specific case, and regardless of the diligence exercised in general in its mode of operation.  
It should not be forgotten that these types of entities, precisely because of their greater connection with data processing, have specific security measures, internal regulations, policies, and action protocols that other entities do not have.  
Finally, it is assessed as a negative circumstance "the categories of personal data affected by the infringement." The initiation agreement, despite recognizing that the affected data does not fall within the special categories of data, understands that, in this case, the affected data were "especially sensitive" "because it allows for the prompt identification of the data subjects and increases the risks to their privacy." Well, this risk (that the data allows for the prompt identification of the data subjects increasing the risk to their privacy) could make sense if the infringement had consisted of an improper transfer of data, but never in an infringement that consists, precisely, of the deletion of images which, by definition, prevents the risk of identification and the possible impact on privacy.

Right to restriction of processing. Right of access. Formal aspects related to the exercise of rights.  
The rights of individuals regarding the protection of personal data are regulated in Articles 15 to 22 of the GDPR and Articles 13 to 18 of the LOPDGDD. The rights of access, rectification, erasure, objection, right to restriction of processing, and right to data portability are contemplated.  
Regarding the right to restriction of processing, the GDPR stipulates in Article 18 the following:  
“1. The data subject shall have the right to obtain from the controller restriction of processing of personal data when one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject contests the accuracy of the personal data, for a period enabling the controller to verify the accuracy of the data;  
b) the processing is unlawful and the data subject opposes the erasure of the personal data and requests instead the restriction of their use;  
c) the controller no longer needs the personal data for the purposes of processing, but the data subject needs them for the establishment, exercise, or defense of legal claims;  
d) the data subject has objected to processing pursuant to Article 21(1), pending the verification of whether the legitimate grounds of the controller override those of the data subject.  
2. When the processing of personal data has been restricted under paragraph 1, such data may only be processed, except for storage, with the consent of the data subject or for the establishment, exercise, or defense of legal claims, or to protect the rights of another natural or legal person, or for reasons of substantial public interest of the Union or of a Member State.  
3. Any data subject who has obtained restriction of processing under paragraph 1 shall be informed by the controller before the restriction is lifted.”  
As the holder of their personal data, the affected data subject may exercise this right to restriction of processing before the controller when one of the specified conditions is met.

“1. Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines under this article for the infringements of this Regulation referred to in paragraphs 4, 9, and 6 are, in each individual case, effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.  
2. Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure to those contemplated in Article 58, paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j). In deciding on the imposition of an administrative fine and its amount in each individual case, the following shall be duly taken into account:  
a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing operation in question as well as the number of data subjects affected and the level of damage suffered;  
b) the intentionality or negligence in the infringement;  
c) any measures taken by the controller or processor to mitigate the damage suffered by the data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the controller or processor, taking into account the technical or organizational measures they have implemented pursuant to Articles 25 and 32;  
e) any previous infringement committed by the controller or processor;  
f) the degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority in order to remedy the infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects of the infringement;  
g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement;  
h) the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, in particular whether the controller or processor notified the infringement and, if so, to what extent;  
i) when the measures indicated in Article 58, paragraph 2, have previously been ordered against the controller or processor in relation to the same matter, compliance with such measures;  
j) adherence to codes of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to certification mechanisms approved under Article 42, and  
k) any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement.”

Imposition of Measures  
The infringements in the matter at hand may lead to the imposition on the responsible party of the obligation to adopt appropriate measures to adjust its actions to the regulations mentioned in this act, in accordance with the provisions of the aforementioned Article 58.2.d) of the GDPR, according to which each supervisory authority may "order the controller or processor to ensure that the processing operations comply with the provisions of this Regulation, where appropriate, in a specified manner and within a specified time frame…". The imposition of this measure is compatible with the sanction consisting of an administrative fine, as provided in Article 83.2 of the GDPR.  
In such a case, this Agency may require the responsible entity to, within the period determined, adjust its actions to the personal data protection regulations, to the extent expressed in the Legal Grounds of this agreement.  
In this act, the infringements committed and the facts that give rise to the violation of data protection regulations are established, from which it is clearly inferred what measures are to be adopted, without prejudice to the fact that the type of procedures, mechanisms, or specific instruments to implement them corresponds to the sanctioned party, as it is the data controller who fully knows its organization and must decide, based on proactive responsibility and a risk-based approach, how to comply with the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.  
However, in this case, regardless of the above, it is appropriate to require the entity ECI to, within a period of three months from the date of effectiveness of the final resolution concluding this procedure, establish mechanisms to ensure that requests for the exercise of rights are responded to in all cases, considering the retention period of the images, if
