The claim is directed against GENERAL LOGISTICS SYSTEMS SPAIN, S.A. with NIF A61441523 (hereinafter, the respondent or GLS).  
The reasons for the claim are as follows:

PROVEN FACTS  
FIRST: The two claimants each purchased a mobile terminal from XFERA, agreeing with that entity that these terminals would be sent to their home via a courier company; however, the claimants found that someone went to collect them at the courier company's headquarters instead of delivering them to their home.

LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
According to the provisions of Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states that: "The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, as long as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures."  
II  
It is necessary to point out that Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines: “personal data” as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  
On the other hand, verifying the identity for the delivery of the mobile terminal involves the processing of the personal data of its holder, as an identifiable natural person is one whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier (Article 4.1 of the GDPR).  
According to Article 4.7 of the GDPR, the “data controller” or “controller” is defined as the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing.  
In this case, it has been established that the claimants each acquired a terminal at a store of XFERA MÓVILES, which were sent by INTERBOX, an entity that has subcontracted the courier services with GLS (the entity being claimed), therefore it is this latter entity that is responsible for processing the data necessary for the management and delivery of the shipments to their recipients.

III  
Article 6.1 of the LOPDGDD establishes that “in accordance with the provisions of Article 4.11 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, consent of the data subject is understood as any manifestation of free, specific, informed, and unequivocal will by which the data subject accepts, either by means of a declaration or a clear affirmative action, the processing of personal data concerning them.”  
For its part, Article 6 of the GDPR establishes the following:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.  
The provisions of point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”  
IV  
In the present case, the two claimants have acquired a mobile device from XFERA, which is sent to them via a courier service, and the claimants find that someone has gone to collect them at the courier company's headquarters, instead of delivering them at home as they had agreed when making the purchase.  
We must take into account that the punishable facts in this case are that the conditions of the purchase contract were changed without having the legitimacy to do so.  
In its defense, GLS states that it has no contractual relationship with the claimants, but rather has a commercial contract with Interbox Technology, S.L.

The entity being claimed against asserts that it has acted in accordance with the obligations assumed towards Interbox and under the instructions received from Interbox, so as it does not have a contractual relationship with the Claimants, it could not modify the contractual conditions of a contract of which it is not a party.  
GLS therefore states that it has not modified the delivery location, but rather that what happened is that it received an external and legitimate request for a change of delivery location through the mechanisms enabled for this purpose.  
From the investigative actions carried out by this Agency, it has been confirmed, regarding Claimant 2, that the device was collected with an apparently false identification document, and regarding Claimant 1, it was only required for delivery that they indicate their correct name and ID number.  
The most significant aspect in this case is that both claimants requested home delivery, and despite this, the courier company delivered it to a store, providing as justification only a screenshot from their systems indicating that the recipient offered to go to the store.  
In this regard, it should be noted that, having designated home delivery as the method of receipt for the shipment, the modification of this method has increased the risk that the shipments may have been received by someone who is not directly the owner.  
Indeed, delivery to the recipient's home provides an additional guarantee that this possibility does not occur. This factor facilitated that the shipments may have been delivered to persons who did not correspond to the true recipients of the same.  
In fact, as indicated in the background, in the case of CLAIMANT 2, it has been subsequently verified that the shipment was supposedly collected by an impersonator of the claimant's identity, since they were able to collect it using a presumably false document, as it has the format of "Foreign Identification Document," when its numbering corresponds to that of the National Identity Document of CLAIMANT 2.  
Moreover, the photograph inserted in the document does not match the actual ID of that claimant (nor do the postal addresses of both documents match).  
Therefore, in accordance with the evidence available, it is considered that the entity being claimed against has modified the conditions of the claimants' contract without their consent, nor any other cause of legitimacy that allows for its processing, by requesting the change of the delivery location of the device, which constitutes a violation of Article 6 of the GDPR.
