II  
Preliminary Issues  
The voice of a person, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is a personal data as it makes the individual identifiable, and its protection is therefore the subject of the GDPR:  
“‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person;”  
The voice is a personal attribute unique and individual to each natural person, defined by its pitch, intensity, and timbre. Endowed with unique and distinctive traits that directly individualize it, associating it with a specific individual, it is shaped while speaking, allowing one to discern, through it, the age, sex, health status of the individual, their personality, culture, origin, hormonal, emotional, and psychological state. Elements of expression, idiolect, or intonation are also personal data considered in conjunction with the voice.  
Therefore, the report 139/2017 from the Legal Office of this Agency states that “the image, as well as the voice of a person, is personal data, just as any information that allows for the determination, directly or indirectly, of their identity (…)”  
In fact, the ruling of the National Court dated March 19, 2014 (rec. 176/2012) states that “the voice of a person constitutes personal data, as deduced from the definition provided by Article 3.a) of the LOPD, as <>, a matter that is not controversial.”  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines “processing” as: “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction.”  
The inclusion of a person's voice in journalistic publications, which identifies or makes an individual identifiable, constitutes processing of personal data and, therefore, the data controller carrying out such processing is obliged to comply with.

III  
Right to data protection  
This procedure is initiated because EUROPA PRESS published, on the website referred to in the facts, the audio of the statement before the judge of a victim of a multiple rape, to illustrate the news related to the holding of the trial in a case that was highly publicized. The victim's voice was clearly discernible as she recounted in graphic detail the multiple rape she suffered. All of this constitutes a processing of the victim's personal data.  
Individuals have the power to dispose of their personal data, including their voice, as well as its dissemination, and undoubtedly, the person whose personal data is disseminated in violation of the legal system deserves protection.  
Thus, the STC 292/2000, of November 30, states that "the content of the fundamental right to data protection consists of a power of disposal and control over personal data that empowers the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose that possession or use. These powers of disposal and control over personal data, which are part of the content of the fundamental right to data protection, are legally specified in the authority to consent to the collection, obtaining, and access to personal data, its subsequent storage and processing, as well as its possible use or uses by a third party, whether the State or a private individual. And that right to consent to the knowledge and processing, whether computerized or not, of personal data requires as indispensable complements, on the one hand, the authority to know at all times who has that personal data and what use they are subjecting it to, and, on the other hand, the power to oppose that possession and uses."  
In this regard, and regardless of what the legal basis legitimizing the processing may be, every data controller must respect the principles of processing set out in Article 5 of the GDPR. We will highlight Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR which states that:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
(...)  
c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’);”  
However, we are faced with a fundamental right that is not absolute, since in certain cases the Fundamental Right to Data Protection may yield to the prevalence of other rights and freedoms that are also constitutionally recognized and protected, such as, for example, the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information, weighing this case by case.  
However, in the present case, as we will explain, it must be considered that the processing carried out by EUROPA PRESS within the framework of freedom of information has been excessive, as there is no prevailing public interest in the dissemination of the victim's voice—without adding any value to the information by maintaining the victim's real voice (without distortion, for example)—under which pretext it seems that those data have been disclosed; a voice that, combined with the fact that it is a highly publicized case, makes the victim clearly identifiable. When weighing the conflicting interests and considering the concurrent circumstances of this case, namely, the particularly sensitive nature of the personal data and the intense impact on the victim's privacy, the interest of the holder of the right to protect her personal data and not to have it disseminated deserves greater protection than the purported public interest in its dissemination.  
IV  
Right to information  
In the conflict between the Fundamental Rights to Freedom of Information in relation to the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, even though equal degrees of protection are recognized for both constitutional rights, the former is usually granted precedence by our courts, after evaluating and weighing all the elements at play.  
However, precedence does not mean prevalence when, considering all the concurrent circumstances in a specific case, the limits set by regulations and jurisprudence are exceeded.  
In this sense, the Article 29 Working Party in its Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC, when examining the legal basis of legitimate interest under Article 7.1.f) of Directive 95/46/EC, fully transferable to the current art. 6.1.f) of the GDPR, includes the right to freedom of expression or information as one of the scenarios in which the issue of legitimate interest may arise, asserting that “without prejudice to whether the interests of the data controller will ultimately prevail over the interests and rights of the data subjects when the balancing test is carried out.”

VI  
Balance between the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information and the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data  
In the specific case examined, as we have indicated, EUROPA PRESS published, on the website referred to in the facts, the audio of the statement made before the judge by a victim of multiple rape, to illustrate the news of a highly publicized case. Thus, it is not a matter, as in other jurisprudentially examined cases, of giving precedence to one fundamental right over another, having to choose which one carries more weight in a specific case. Rather, it is about finding a balance between both in order to achieve the purpose of the former without distorting the latter. The reconciliation of both rights is nothing new, as the European legislator mandates such reconciliation in Article 85 of the GDPR.  
As we have seen previously, the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information is not unlimited, since the jurisprudential interpretation when confronting it with other rights and freedoms does not allow for it in every case and to the fullest extent, but rather, the prevalence that courts usually grant it can be limited by other fundamental rights that must also be respected. Thus, its limitation is observed when the personal data provided were unnecessary for the essence of the content of the information.  
We must consider the special circumstances present in the examined case. It concerns a very young woman who has suffered a multiple rape. In the published recording, she can be heard recounting, with great emotional weight, the sexual aggression suffered in all its rawness, (...).  
Furthermore, we cannot lose sight of the victim status of the woman whose voice, with all the nuances expressed, has been disseminated.  
Let us recall, for purely illustrative purposes, that Law 4/2015, of April 27, on the Statute of the Victim of Crime, as well as the recent Organic Law 10/2022, of September 6, on comprehensive guarantee of sexual freedom, provide for a special need for protection for victims of crimes against sexual freedom or sexual integrity. Additionally, the aforementioned Statute of the Victim of Crime also provides for special protection for victims of violent crimes. And in the examined case, both circumstances concur.  
In this case, the situation of the victim (who is not on the same level of equality as the accused) and what the dissemination of her voice with all its nuances implies must be considered, as well as the special protection that must be afforded to her.

VII  
Obligations of the data controller  
Every data controller has obligations regarding data protection, as prescribed in the GDPR and in the LOPDGDD, highlighting, in terms of our interest, proactive responsibility, Article 5.2 of the GDPR, risk assessment, and the implementation of appropriate security measures. Obligations that are even more relevant when, as in the case we are examining, it is particularly sensitive.  
Such obligations do not diminish because we are dealing with a data controller that is a media outlet.  
If we combine the dissemination of the victim's voice (with all its nuances), which makes them identifiable and recognizable by third parties, with the factual account they provide regarding the violation suffered, there is a very high and probable risk that they may suffer harm to their rights and freedoms. This has occurred in other cases of dissemination of personal data of victims of sexual assault. Moreover, with the dissemination of the victim's voice, they are being condemned again to be recognized by third parties, when it is neither a proportional nor necessary treatment in relation to the information purposes pursued.  
In the case examined, EUROPA PRESS immediately removed the recording of the hearing in which the victim's voice was disseminated at the request of the AEPD.

Article 85 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), under the heading "Termination in sanctioning procedures" provides as follows:  
“1. Once a sanctioning procedure has been initiated, if the offender acknowledges their responsibility, the procedure may be resolved with the imposition of the appropriate sanction.  
2. When the sanction is solely of a pecuniary nature or when it is possible to impose a pecuniary sanction and another of a non-pecuniary nature but the impropriety of the latter has been justified, voluntary payment by the presumed responsible party, at any time prior to the resolution, will imply the termination of the procedure, except regarding the restoration of the altered situation or the determination of compensation for damages caused by the commission of the infringement.  
3. In both cases, when the sanction is solely of a pecuniary nature, the competent authority to resolve the procedure shall apply reductions of at least 20% on the amount of the proposed sanction, which shall be cumulative. The aforementioned reductions must be specified in the notification of initiation of the procedure, and their effectiveness shall be conditioned upon the withdrawal or renunciation of any action or administrative appeal against the sanction. The percentage of reduction provided for in this section may be increased by regulation.”
