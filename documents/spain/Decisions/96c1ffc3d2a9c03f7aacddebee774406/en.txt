LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
The Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to resolve this appeal, in accordance with the provisions of Article 123 of the LPACAP and Article 48.1 of the LOPDGDD.  

II  
Response to the allegations presented  
In relation to the statements made by the appellant, which reproduce the allegations presented by the appellant during the processing of the sanctioning procedure, without adding any new arguments, it should be noted that they have already been analyzed and dismissed in Legal Grounds II to VII of the challenged Resolution, dated 21/03/2023, in which it is considered that it violated the provisions of Article 6 of the GDPR and sufficiently details the assessment of the evidence that has allowed for the determination of such violation and the scope granted to it, as well as the circumstances taken into account for the severity of the imposed sanction. In these Legal Grounds, the following is indicated:  
<<II  
The image is a personal data  
The physical image of a person, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is personal data and its protection, therefore, is subject to this Regulation. Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines the concept of “processing” of personal data.  
Images generated by a system of cameras or video cameras are personal data, so their processing is subject to data protection regulations.  
It is, therefore, pertinent to analyze whether the processing of personal data (image of natural persons) carried out through the reported video surveillance system is in accordance with the provisions of the GDPR.  

III  
Violation. Regulatory framework  
Article 6.1 of the GDPR establishes the circumstances that allow for the lawful processing of personal data.  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) the processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) the processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) the processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) the processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular where the data subject is a child.  
The provision in point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”  
The permanent implementation of a video camera system for security reasons has a legitimate basis in the LOPDGDD, whose explanatory memorandum states:  
“Alongside these cases, others are included, such as video surveillance… where the lawfulness of the processing arises from the existence of a public interest, as established in Article 6.1.e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”  
Regarding processing for video surveillance purposes, Article 22 of the LOPDGDD states that natural or legal persons, public or private, may carry out the processing of images through camera or video camera systems for the purpose of preserving the safety of persons and property, as well as their facilities.  
This same Article 22, in its section 8, provides that “The processing by the employer of data obtained through camera or video camera systems is subject to the provisions of Article 89 of this organic law.”  
This Article 89 of the LOPDGDD, referred to as the “Right to privacy against the use of video surveillance and sound recording devices in the workplace,” establishes the following:  
“1. Employers may process images obtained through camera systems or video cameras…”

Regarding the legitimacy for the implementation of video surveillance systems in the workplace, Royal Legislative Decree 1/1995, of 24/03, which approves the consolidated text of the Workers' Statute (LET), is taken into account, whose Article 20.3 states:  
“3. The employer may adopt the measures they deem most appropriate for surveillance and control to verify compliance by the worker with their obligations and labor duties, taking due consideration of their dignity and, where applicable, the real capacity of workers with disabilities.”  
The Constitutional Court, in Judgment 98/2000, of 10/04/2000, emphasizes in its legal foundation 6 that “The jurisprudence of this Court has repeatedly insisted on the full effectiveness of the fundamental rights of the worker within the framework of the labor relationship, as this cannot in any way imply the deprivation of such rights for those who serve in productive organizations, which are not foreign to constitutional principles and rights. However, the mere manifestation of the exercise of control power by the employer is not enough for the worker's right to be sacrificed. These business limitations must be indispensable and strictly necessary to satisfy a business interest deserving of protection and safeguarding, so that if there are other less aggressive and less affecting possibilities to satisfy that interest, the latter must be employed and not those that are more aggressive and affecting. Ultimately, it is about applying the principle of proportionality.”  
To verify whether a restrictive measure of a fundamental right passes the proportionality test, it is necessary to ascertain whether it meets the following three requirements or conditions:  
- Whether such a measure is capable of achieving the proposed objective (suitability test);  
- Whether it is necessary, in the sense that there is no other more moderate measure to achieve such purpose with equal effectiveness (necessity test);

If it is weighed or balanced, as long as it derives more benefits or advantages for the general interest than harms to other conflicting goods or values (proportionality judgment).  
Thus, among the admitted surveillance and control measures, the installation of security cameras is included; however, these systems must always adhere to the principle of proportionality, meaning that the use of video cameras must be proportional to the intended purpose, which is to guarantee security and compliance with labor obligations and duties.  
In short, even though Article 20.3 of the Workers' Statute empowers the employer to adopt the measures they deem most appropriate for surveillance and control to verify the worker's compliance with their labor obligations and duties, this adoption must necessarily take into account the specific rights of workers, respecting their rights to privacy and the fundamental right to data protection.  
In the terms of the aforementioned Article 89 of the LOPDPGDD, it is permitted for employers to process images obtained through camera or video camera systems for the exercise of the control functions of workers provided for in Article 20.3 of the Workers' Statute "as long as these functions are exercised within their legal framework and with the inherent limits thereof."  
In accordance with the above, employers must inform, in advance and in an express, clear, and concise manner, the workers or public employees and, where applicable, their representatives, about this measure.  
In conclusion, the processing must be adjusted and proportional to the purpose it is directed at. The relevance in the processing of data must occur both at the moment of data collection and in the subsequent processing carried out, so that the installed camera or video camera system cannot obtain images affecting the privacy of employees, making it disproportionate to capture images in private spaces, such as changing rooms, lockers, or rest areas for workers.  
This is expressly established in the aforementioned Article 89 of the LOPDGDD concerning the installation of video surveillance systems:  
“2. Under no circumstances shall the installation of sound recording systems or video surveillance in places designated for the rest or recreation of workers or public employees, such as changing rooms, restrooms, dining rooms, and similar places, be permitted.  
According to this article, surveillance in workplaces must not cover areas reserved for the private use of employees or that are not intended for the performance of work tasks (such as services, showers, changing rooms, or rest areas).  
IV  
Obligations regarding video surveillance  
In accordance with the above, the processing of images through a video surveillance system, to be compliant with current regulations, must meet the following requirements:  
1.- Individuals or legal entities, public or private, may establish a video surveillance system for the purpose of preserving the safety of persons and property, as well as their facilities.  
It must be assessed whether the intended purpose can be achieved in a less intrusive manner for the rights and freedoms of citizens. Personal data should only be processed if the purpose of the processing cannot be reasonably achieved by other means, considering Recital 39 of the GDPR.  
2.- The images obtained may not be used for a subsequent purpose incompatible with the one that motivated the installation of the video surveillance system.  
3.- The duty to inform the affected parties as provided in Articles 12 and 13 of the GDPR, and Article 22 of the LOPDGDD, must be fulfilled, in the terms already indicated.  
4.- Images of public roads cannot be captured, since the processing of images in public places, unless there is government authorization, can only be carried out by the Security Forces and Bodies.  
On some occasions, for the protection of private spaces, where cameras have been installed on facades or inside, it may be necessary to record a portion of the public road to ensure the purpose of security.  
That is to say, the cameras and video cameras installed for security purposes may not obtain images of the public road unless it is essential for that purpose, or it is impossible to avoid it due to their location. In such an extraordinary case, the cameras may only capture the minimum portion necessary to preserve the safety of persons and property, as well as their facilities.  
The installed cameras may not obtain images of private spaces belonging to third parties and/or public spaces without a duly justified cause, nor may they affect the privacy of passersby freely moving through the area.  
Therefore, the placement of cameras directed towards the private property of neighbors with the intention of intimidating them or affecting their private sphere without a justified cause is not permitted.  
Under no circumstances shall the use of surveillance practices extend beyond the environment subject to the installation and, in particular, shall not affect the surrounding public spaces, adjacent buildings, and vehicles other than those accessing the monitored space.  
Images may not be captured or recorded in spaces owned by third parties without the consent of their owners, or, where applicable, of the persons present in them.  
It is disproportionate to capture images in private spaces, such as changing rooms, lockers, or rest areas for workers.

It is not disputed in this case that the responding party is the owner and responsible for the reported surveillance system and, therefore, responsible for the data processing involved in the use of said system. Nor is it disputed that among the data processing activities carried out by the responding party is the collection and storage of personal data related to the image of employees captured within the previously mentioned area, specifically, the image of claiming parties 1 and 2.  
It is also proven in the proceedings that the installation of the surveillance system is carried out for security and labor control purposes. It is also noted that initially this system did not include the installation of any camera in the designated staff rest area.  
In collecting and using the images obtained in the staff rest area, the responding party does not take into account the limits set forth in Article 20.3 of the Workers' Statute (LET), which allows for the recording of images for the exercise of labor control functions when those functions respect the legal framework and the inherent limits thereof, such as respect for the dignity of the worker; nor does it consider the provisions of Article 89.2 of the LOPDGDD, which prohibits, in all cases, the installation of surveillance recording systems “in areas designated for the rest or recreation of workers…, such as changing rooms, restrooms, dining rooms, and similar places.”

It is concluded that the images that prompted the claims were captured in an area designated for the rest of the employees of the responding party.

It is important to highlight in this regard what was stated by the Superior Court of Justice of Catalonia, Social Chamber, in Judgment 2298/2022, dated April 12, Rec. 7352/2021, regarding a case of the installation of a surveillance camera in a warehouse that was used by the company's workers as a changing room with the knowledge of the person in charge:  
“On the other hand, in view of the proven facts, it is irrelevant whether the room where the cameras were installed was the warehouse or any other, since what is important is that it was used as a changing room by decision of the current appellant, which is why it falls within the factual assumption of the prohibition established in article 89.2 of Organic Law 3/2018. Furthermore, it is evident that having cameras installed in the workplace area designated for the workers' changing room constitutes a clear infringement of the fundamental right to privacy recognized in article 18.1 of the Spanish Constitution, even independently of the aforementioned article 89.2 of Organic Law 3/2018. And, of course, against all this, it is not relevant that the claimant could have known that the cameras were installed, as this circumstance does not affect her fundamental right to privacy.”  
And not only that. It is also understood that the capturing of these images for monitoring compliance by the claiming parties with their labor obligations and duties is disproportionate, as it has not been justified that the intended purpose could not have been achieved by other less intrusive means. It is taken into account that recording images in the indicated areas represents a greater intrusion into privacy.

“On the other hand, it must be appreciated as a mitigating factor the non-commission of a previous infringement. According to the aforementioned Article 83.2 of the GDPR, when deciding on the imposition of an administrative fine and its amount, “any previous infringement committed by the controller” must be taken into account. It is a regulatory provision that does not include the absence of previous infringements as a grading factor for the fine, which should be understood as a criterion close to recidivism, although broader.

in Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR, according to which administrative fines shall be imposed taking into account any “aggravating or mitigating factors applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement,” it is understood that avoiding a loss has the same nature for these purposes as obtaining benefits. It would be an artificial reduction of the sanction that could lead to the understanding that violating the norm without obtaining benefits, financial or otherwise, will not produce a negative effect proportional to the seriousness of the infringing act.  
In any case, the administrative fines established in the GDPR, in accordance with what is provided in its Article 83.2, are imposed based on the circumstances of each individual case and, in the present case, it is not considered that the absence of benefits is an adequate and determining factor to assess the seriousness of the infringing conduct. Only in the event that this absence of benefits is relevant to determine the degree of unlawfulness and culpability present in the specific infringing action can it be considered as a mitigating factor, in application of Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR, which refers to “any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case.”
