BACKGROUND  
FIRST: A.A.A. (hereinafter, the claiming party), represented by NOYB (European Centre for Digital Rights, Goldschlagstraße 172/4/3/2, 1140 Vienna, ZVR: 1354838270), filed a complaint with the Spanish Agency for Data Protection on August 21, 2020. The complaint is directed against FREEPIK COMPANY S.L. with NIF B93183366 (hereinafter, the responding party). The grounds for the complaint are as follows:  
- Through HTML code embedded in the website ***URL.1, personal data of the claiming party was collected on August 14, 2020, and transferred to the USA via the services of Facebook (including Facebook Connect) contracted by the portal's controller, FREEPIK COMPANY S.L.  
- The claiming party states that during their visit to the aforementioned website, which took place while connecting to the Facebook account associated with their email address, the responding party processed their personal data (at least, the IP address and "cookies"), and that some of this data has been transferred to Facebook Inc. in the USA.  
- The claiming party argues that the transfer of personal data to the USA by the responding party, whether through Facebook Ireland as an intermediary or directly to Facebook Inc., lacked a legal basis, since the CJEU had invalidated the decision on the "EU-US Privacy Shield" in ruling C-311/18 ("Schrems II"), and the responding party could no longer base the transfer of data to Facebook Inc. in the USA on an adequacy decision under Article 45 of the GDPR.  
- The responding party could not base the transfer of data on the standard contractual clauses provided for in Articles 46.2.c and 46.2.d of the GDPR, if the third country of destination did not ensure an adequate level of protection for the personal data transferred under those clauses, according to EU law. The CJEU had explicitly ruled that further transfers to companies covered by Article 50 of the United States Code (USC), such as Facebook, violated the relevant articles of Chapter 5 of the GDPR, since, under this regulation (50 USC § 1881a, or "FISA 702"), they were subject to US intelligence surveillance. As evidenced by the "Snowden Slides" and Facebook's own Transparency Report, this entity was actively providing personal data to the US government under that provision.  
- The responding party and Facebook had continued to rely on the "EU-US Privacy Shield" and the standard data protection clauses for the aforementioned data transfers.

3. FREEPIK as a potential "Relevant Entity"  
The conditions required in the Relevant Regulation in the previous sections greatly limit the scope of surveillance (especially, as we have seen, the requirement—as a prerequisite—that intelligence agencies are obliged to justify, based on facts, that not only the targets but also the information that is intended to be obtained with the access request, are subsumable within the objective scope of said Relevant Regulation).  
From this perspective, situations can be identified where the exporting organization itself represents an element of interest for Intelligence Agencies, due to its nature, members, or especially, due to its activity.  
In this sense, it is necessary to take into account, among others, elements such as:  
- Whether the activity of the exporter itself is strategic (interesting, relevant from the perspective of national security and U.S. intelligence services).  
- Whether the activity is classified as essential for the normal development of the daily, social, political, or economic activities of the country (so that its forced interruption would impact those, e.g.).  
- Whether services are provided with access to data to the Public Administration—at any of its levels—or to the public sector (which would allow access to data from a "Foreign Power").  
- Whether personal data of politicians, high-ranking officials of the administration, relevant figures in the private sector, and other individuals of interest are processed (data that would provide information and even the ability to exert pressure on them, as well as on the states or institutions they represent).  
- Whether the exporter develops technology with military or dual-use applications (since PPD-28 does not limit espionage actions on this type of technology).  

Assessment

It is also not subject to any regulations related to the imposition of protective measures due to its activity (such as strategic activities for national security, activities of general interest, essential or particularly relevant activities, management or maintenance of critical or strategic infrastructures, etc.).

a) The applicable U.S. regulations do not undermine the level of protection recognized for the data subject by the GDPR  
The respondent argues that Executive Order 12333 and Presidential Policy Directive 28 regulate and limit the intelligence-gathering capabilities of U.S. government agencies outside the U.S. It is emphasized that there is no obligation to cooperate with the government and that EO 12333 does not provide any mechanism to compel U.S. importing companies to cooperate with the government. Therefore, based on EO 12333, FREEPIK's providers in the U.S. are not obligated to cooperate with intelligence agencies to provide personal data of European users.  
Regarding the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA), the respondent claims that it has a very specific objective scope.  
First, the communications, content, and metadata that can be intercepted are only those materially related to activities linked to terrorism, hostile acts, weapons of mass destruction, clandestine espionage activities, or to national defense, or foreign affairs activities of the U.S.  
Second, the information request under Section 702 must include some type of identifier or "selector," such as an email address, and cannot be indiscriminate.  
Third, it is also necessary to justify the request with specific indications related to the person being investigated and the information expected to be obtained. A U.S. intelligence agency is required to provide objective and concrete indications, based on facts, to establish the likelihood that the target person possesses, has access to, or can communicate information related to foreign intelligence to foreign powers or foreign territories. Furthermore, intelligence agencies must also conduct an equally concrete and factually justified assessment regarding the nature of the information expected to be obtained with the required access.  
Fourth, it is explained that the Schrems II ruling refers to the version of FISA at the time the adequacy decision on the Privacy Shield was issued, back in 2016, and does not address subsequent modifications. One of the most relevant changes is that it has restricted the ability to request, based on Section 702, information based on keywords to be found in the content of communications (which was referred to with requests about "about") so that information can only be requested based on identifiers ("selector") of the person sending or receiving the communication (which is referred to as "from" and/or "to" requests). In this regard, the respondent argues that, in a general analysis, neither the personal data processed by them, nor the categories of data subjects, nor the purposes of processing include elements that could be considered sensitive or of special category. Section 702 of FISA and E.O. 12333 in conjunction with PPD-28 and annulled the aforementioned adequacy decision.  
This Agency understands that Meta Platforms, Inc. had the obligation to provide the authorities of the United States with personal data in accordance with Article 1881.a of the U.S. Code, should such a request be made. Regarding the adoption of adequate safeguards, the CJEU has explicitly ruled that further transfers to companies covered by Article 50 of the U.S. Code § 1881a not only violate the relevant articles of Chapter V of the GDPR but also Articles 7 and 8 of the CFR, as well as the essence of Article 47 of the CFR (see C-362/14 ("Schrems I"), para. 95). Therefore, any further transfer violated the fundamental right to privacy, data protection, and the right to effective judicial protection and to an impartial judge.  
This Agency understands that Meta Platforms Inc. qualifies as an electronic service provider within the meaning of 50 U.S. Code § 1881(b)(4) and, as such, was subject to U.S. intelligence surveillance under 50 U.S. Code § 1881a (“FISA 702”). IV  
Transfers of personal data to third countries  
Personal data  
Article 4 "Definitions" of the GDPR defines personal data as follows:  
“For the purposes of this Regulation, the following definitions shall apply:  
1) ‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person; (…)”  
In this case, the respondent – as the owner of the website – has implemented Facebook Business tools on its website ***URL.1. As a result of this implementation, at least the following information from the devices of visitors to the website (including that of the complainant) has been transmitted to the servers of Meta Platforms, Inc:  
- IP address;  
- User agent;  
- Mobile operating system and browser;  
- Information about the host server (the webpage that has the login functionality);  
- Date and time of the visit to the website;  
- Language of the content (the language of the audience targeted by the website);  
- Location (usually the country code referred to in the language field in the HTTP header);  
- HTTP referrer (URL of the page where the person is located);  
- Display port data (the screen resolution of the device used);  
- User identifier;  
- Cached access tokens;  
- Standard HTTP request headers that were not yet on the list;  
- Values stored in FB cookies.  
As a result of the implementation of Facebook Business Tools on ***URL.1, cookies were installed on the devices of visitors to the website (including that of the complainant), which contain a randomly generated unique value. This makes it possible to personalize the device of those who visited the website (including the complainant) and record the browsing activity of these visitors (among whom was the complainant) in order to show them appropriate personalized advertising.  
In any case, at least Meta Ireland had the option to link the data it received as a result of the implementation of Facebook Business Tools on ***URL.1 with the Facebook account of the visitors to the website (including that of the complainant).  
For example, the terms of use of Facebook Business Tools clearly indicate that Facebook Business Tools are used to exchange information with Facebook.  
It is not necessary for the respondent to establish a personal identification by itself, that is, to have all the information necessary for identification (see the judgments of the Court of Justice of the European Union of July 20, 2008, December 2017, C-434/16, paragraph 31, and of October 19, 2016, C-582/14, paragraph 43).  
This is in line with the Fashion ID ruling. The CJEU assumed that the inclusion of a "Like" button on a webpage – regardless of whether the button has been clicked or not – triggers the processing of personal data (judgment of July 29, 2018, C-40/17, paragraph 80). From the perspective of the CJEU, it is not necessary for the owner of a particular webpage to be able to establish a personal identifier.  
The rulings of the CJEU can be applied to the present case, given that according to the Terms of Use, the "Like" buttons and Facebook Login and Facebook Pixel are among the Facebook Business Tools.  
Therefore, these are personal data, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR.

Therefore, it is necessary to verify whether the transfers of personal data to the United States took place in accordance with the provisions of Chapter V of the GDPR.  
Article 44 "Transfers of personal data to third countries or international organizations" of the GDPR states:  
"Transfers of personal data that are being processed or are to be processed after their transfer to a third country or international organization may only take place if, subject to the other provisions of this Regulation, the controller and the processor comply with the conditions laid down in this chapter, including those relating to further transfers of personal data from the third country or international organization to another third country or another international organization. All provisions of this chapter shall apply in order to ensure that the level of protection of natural persons guaranteed by this Regulation is not undermined."  
That is to say, Chapter V of the GDPR provides various instruments to ensure a level of protection substantially equivalent to that guaranteed in the European Union, in accordance with Article 44 of the Regulation, namely:  
- adequacy decisions (Article 45);  
- appropriate safeguards (Article 46);  
In the absence of an equivalent level of protection, it establishes exceptions for specific situations (Article 49).  
i) Adequacy decision (Article 45 of the GDPR)  
At least until July 10, 2023, the responding party and Meta Platforms, Inc. relied on the EU-U.S. Adequacy Decision (“Privacy Shield”) of 2016 to carry out the aforementioned international transfers of personal data.  
However, the CJEU in its ruling of July 16, 2020, C-311/18, declared that such an adequacy decision did not guarantee an adequate level of protection for the

natural persons due to the relevant regulations of the United States and the implementation of official surveillance programs based, among others, on Section 702 of FISA and E.O. 12333 in conjunction with PPD-28, and annulled the aforementioned adequacy decision.  
This Agency understands that Meta Platforms, Inc must be classified as a provider of electronic communication services within the meaning of U.S. Code 50 § 1881(b)(4), and therefore was subject to surveillance by U.S. intelligence authorities, in accordance with U.S. Code 50 § 1881.a ("FISA 702").  
Therefore, Meta Platforms, Inc had the obligation to provide U.S. authorities with personal data in accordance with Article 1881.a of U.S. Code.  
ii) Adequate safeguards  
The respondent could not base the data transfer on the standard contractual clauses for data protection provided for in Article 46(2)(c) and (d) of the GDPR if the third country of destination did not guarantee adequate protection, under EU law, of the personal data transferred under those clauses (see paragraphs 134 and 135 of the Judgment). The CJEU has explicitly ruled that further transfers to companies covered by Article 50 of the U.S. Code § 1881a not only violate the relevant articles of Chapter V of the GDPR but also Articles 7 and 8 of the CFR, as well as the essence of Article 47 of the CFR (see C-362/14 ("Schrems I"), paragraph 95). Therefore, any further transfer violates the fundamental right to privacy, data protection, and the right to effective judicial protection and to an impartial judge.  
Meta Platforms Inc. is classified as a provider of electronic services within the meaning of 50 U.S. Code § 1881(b)(4) and as such is subject to U.S. intelligence surveillance under 50 U.S. Code § 1881a ("FISA 702").  
As evidenced by Facebook's own "Transparency Report" (see https://transparency.facebook.com/government-data-requests/country/US), Facebook is actively providing personal data to the U.S. government under 50 U.S. Code § 1881a.  
As a consequence, the respondent could not guarantee adequate protection of the personal data of those visiting its website (including, among others, the claimant) that were transferred to Meta Platforms Inc. Therefore, the respondent had a legal obligation to refrain from transferring the data of those visiting its website (including, among others, the claimant) - or any other personal data - to Meta Platforms Inc.
