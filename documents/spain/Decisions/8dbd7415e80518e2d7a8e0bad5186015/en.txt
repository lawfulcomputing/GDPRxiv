EIGHTH: Allegations of the defendant dated 01/05/2024.  
1) The defendant states that the timekeeping system referred to in the complaint was established in 2016, and attaches an invoice dated 14/12/2016 - “1 Facial identification terminal, and 1 Presence control software - PHUC Official Distributor.” At that time, the LOPDGDD and the GDPR were not applicable, distinguishing between the entry into force, twenty days after its publication in the DOUE, and its application, starting from 25/05/2018. “At the time the timekeeping system was implemented, that regulation was not applicable.”  
“In the previous applicable regulation, the LOPD 15/1999, biometric data were not classified as specially protected, nor was it required to carry out an EIA.”  
Furthermore, it adds that, once the GDPR came into force, the performance of such an impact assessment is not required, and inserts a link from the AEPD:  

(https://www.aepd.es/preguntas-frecuentes/2-rgpd/10-evaluacion-deimpacto/FAQ-0227-los-tratamientos-iniciados-antes-de-la-aplicacion-del-rgpddeben-somterse-a-una-evaluacion-deimpacto#:~:text=No%2C%20el%20mandato%20del%20RGPD,comience%20a%20ser%20de%20aplicaci%C3%B3n)  
“The principle of non-retroactivity of sanctioning provisions is violated, Article 9.3 of the CE, by sanctioning conduct with a regulation that was not in force at the time the facts occurred, also considering Article 40 of Law 40/2015 of 1/10, on the legal regime of the public sector, LRJSP regarding the application of the sanctioning provisions in force at the time the facts occurred.” The defendant cites as an example a ruling from the National Court dated 14/05/2021, of which no further details are provided other than the transcription of some paragraphs, coinciding in this regard with the Administrative Litigation Chamber, Section 1, Judgment of 14/05/2021, Rec. 115/2020:  
“Therefore, a regulation that was not applicable when the sanctioned facts occurred has been applied retroactively. We must add that the aforementioned regulation could have been applied if it had been more favorable - Article 26.2 of Law 40/2015, of October 1 - but as stated in the Judgment of the Supreme Court of October 30, 2009 - appeal no. 334/2006, F.J.6º - and, in the same sense, in the Judgments of said Court of November 12 and 26, 2020, resulting respectively from appeals numbers 4.039/2019 and 5.285/2019: “[...] the retroactive application of the more beneficial regulation must be made by determining which provision is more favorable, through the contrast between both, previous and subsequent, considered globally.... And such contrast does not exist in the contested resolutions, in which there is not even any mention of Organic Law 15/1999, of December 13, and in which the GDPR and Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, are applied as if it were the regulation that was in force when the sanctioned facts occurred, in April 2018.”  
The defendant understands that this judgment is applicable, because in 2016 neither the GDPR nor the LOPDGDD were applicable.

II Definition of biometric and personal data  
Biometric data is defined by Article 4.14 of the GDPR:  
“‘biometric data’: personal data obtained from a specific technical processing, relating to the physical, physiological or behavioral characteristics of a natural person that allow or confirm the unique identification of that person, such as facial images or fingerprint data;”  
It is worth noting the rest of the definitions contained in Article 4 of the GDPR:  
“1) ‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social identity of that natural person;  
2) ‘processing’: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, restriction, erasure or destruction;”

The concept of personal data (WP136), dated 20/06/2007, defines biometric data as:  
“biological properties, physiological characteristics, personality traits or tics, which are, at the same time, attributable to a single person and measurable, even if the models used in practice to technically measure them involve a certain degree of probability. Typical examples of biometric data include those provided by fingerprints, retinal patterns, facial structure, voices, but also hand geometry, venous structures, and even certain deeply ingrained skills or other behavioral characteristics (such as handwriting, pulse, a particular way of walking or speaking, etc.). A particularity of biometric data is that they can be considered both as content of information about a specific person (So-and-so has these fingerprints) and as an element to link information to a specific person (this object has been touched by someone who has these fingerprints, and these fingerprints correspond to So-and-so; therefore, So-and-so has touched this object). As such, they can serve as “identifiers.” Indeed, as they correspond to a unique person, biometric data can be used to identify that person. This dual nature is also present in the case of DNA data, which provides information about the human body and allows for the unequivocal identification of one, and only one, person.”  
It can be asserted that, from the biological identity unique to each person, automated technical instruments are used that will allow for the permanent and unambiguous identification of all individuals, as everyone possesses such biological identity (fingerprints, face, voice, iris, etc.).  
Biometric data irrevocably changes the relationship between the body and identity, as it makes the characteristics of the human body readable by machines and subject to repeated subsequent use.  
Biometric data has the particularity of being produced by the body itself and characterizes it definitively; they are data not about that person, but the data refers to the same person, in principle not modifiable by the will of the individual. Furthermore, because biometric data is unique to a person and perpetual, the user utilizes the same data across different systems.  
Biometric data can be processed and stored in various ways. Sometimes, the biometric information captured from a person is stored and processed in raw form, which allows for the recognition of the source from which it comes without special knowledge; for example, a photograph of a face, a photograph of a fingerprint, or a voice recording. Other times, the raw biometric information captured is processed in such a way that only certain characteristics or traits are extracted and saved as a biometric template.  
Biometric systems are closely linked to a person, as they can utilize a specific unique property of an individual for identification. Each individual has unique fingerprints that display specific characteristics that can be measured to determine whether a fingerprint corresponds with a registered sample. Biometric data has the particularity of being produced by the body itself and characterizes it definitively. Therefore, they are unique, permanent, or definitive over time, and the person cannot rid themselves of them; they can never be changed, not even with age, creating issues of liability in the event of compromise-loss or intrusion into the system. The definition of biometric data refers to "technical processing," without specifying, except to note that the purpose of such processing must be to identify a person.  
To be considered biometric data in the sense of the GDPR, the processing of unprocessed data, such as the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person, must involve a measurement of those characteristics. Therefore, from the concept, one must not lose sight of:  
- The nature of the data: data relating to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person;  
- The means and forms of processing: data "obtained from a specific technical processing"; which differentiates them, for example, from images of a person that appear in a surveillance system, which cannot be considered biometric data unless they have been technically processed in a specific manner to contribute to the unique identification of that person.  
They must be subject to "specific technical processing." According to WP80 of the GT29, adopted on 01/08/2003 (hereinafter, "WP 80 of GT 29"), the processing of these data is carried out through biometric systems that are: "applications of biometric technologies that allow for automatic identification and/or authentication/verification of a person. Authentication/verification applications are commonly used for various tasks in very different fields and under the responsibility of a wide range of different entities."  
The mention included in Article 4.14 of the GDPR as biometric data intended to "allow" can be understood as identification, while "confirm" refers to verification.  
For a better understanding, the concepts of authentication/verification and identification are described. The Opinion 3/2012 on the evolution of biometric technologies dated 27/04/2012 from Working Group 29 stated:  
“-Biometric identification: the identification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparing their biometric data (acquired at the time of identification) with a series of biometric templates stored in a database (i.e., a one-to-many matching process).  
-Biometric verification/authentication: the verification of an individual by a biometric system is normally the process of comparing their biometric data (acquired at the time of verification) with a single biometric template stored in a device (i.e., a one-to-one matching process).”  
With slight nuances, the concepts are mentioned in the “White Paper on Artificial Intelligence” by the European Commission, dated 19/02/2020, referring to facial images:  
“In relation to facial recognition, ‘identification’ is understood as the comparison of a person's facial image template with many other templates stored in a database to determine if their image is stored in it. ‘Authentication’ (or ‘verification’), on the other hand, usually refers to the search for matches.”

Therefore, this function is based on the comparison of two templates. It is also referred to as 1-to-1 verification.  
• The identification of a person in order to locate them among a group of individuals, within a specific area, in an image or in a database. In this case, the system must process each captured face to generate a biometric template and then check if it matches a known person in the system. Thus, this function is based on the comparison of a template with a database of templates or samples (reference database). It is also referred to as “one among many” identification. The "risk-based approach" is developed in the "Statement on the role of a risk-based approach in data protection legal frameworks WP218" of the Article 29 Working Party, WP218, and is not a novel concept within the framework of data protection.

In contrast, compliance risk management aims to provide the controller with a tool to verify the degree of compliance with the legally required obligations and provisions concerning a processing activity. Therefore, prior to the risk management process and as a sine qua non condition for undertaking a processing activity, it is necessary to systematize the verification of regulatory compliance throughout the entire lifecycle of the processing. The complexity of the risk management process must be adjusted not to the size of the entity, the availability of resources, the specialty or sector of the entity, but to the possible impact of the processing activity on the data subjects and to the inherent difficulty of the processing.
