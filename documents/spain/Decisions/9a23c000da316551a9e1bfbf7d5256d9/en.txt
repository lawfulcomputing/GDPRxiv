In this proposal for resolution, in response to the allegations made by the Ministry, the following response was provided:  
First. - Disagreement with the assessment of the facts  
In this section, the Ministry's allegations are consolidated, stating that, on the one hand, it does not agree with the assessment of the facts made by the complainant, and on the other, that personal data has not been affected and that, moreover, it is public information.  
In this regard, it should be noted that, despite the complainant referring to a plurality of recipients or any other subjective considerations, the present sanctioning procedure is being processed due to the communication of personal data to an unauthorized third party, that is, to a single person, thereby violating the duty of confidentiality of personal data.  
Regarding the assertion that the disclosed information does not cause serious harm to the complainant, it should be noted that the harm is inherent in the very violation of the confidentiality of the data, as this implies a loss of control and power of disposition over the same, constituting a violation of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data.  
It should be recalled that, in accordance with data protection regulations, personal data is subject to a duty of confidentiality and cannot be communicated to a third party unless there is consent from the data subject or any other circumstances that make the processing lawful, in accordance with Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
Regarding the Ministry's statement that the disclosed information does not contain personal data, it is indicated, on the contrary, that information about certain employment-related circumstances, such as cessation requests made by the complainant, their denial, as well as requests and denials of the necessary authorizations concerning the provision of jobs in the Public Administration, are personal data, as they relate to an identified person and are not public data, as understood by the Ministry, regardless of the fact that the final allocation of jobs must be published in the corresponding Official Bulletin (but not the authorizations, cessations, denials, processing errors, and other procedures and vicissitudes that form or may form part of the process).  
Second. - Legal qualification  
The Ministry argues in this section that the facts do not constitute a violation of Article 5.1.f) or Article 32 of the GDPR, as it was due to human error, classified as a mere material error regulated in Article 109.2 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations.  
In response, it should be noted that we are not dealing with a material error contained in an administrative act (which is susceptible to correction through another administrative act), but rather a violation of the confidentiality of personal data that was communicated to an unauthorized third party. Therefore, the error alleged by the Ministry does not lie in the content of the Official Letter (administrative act) sent, but in having sent it to another person and not to its intended recipient, thereby violating, as indicated, the confidentiality of the personal data contained in that Official Letter.  
For all the above reasons, the allegations made are dismissed.

Article 27 of Law 40/2015 of October 1 establishes the principle of typicity, which consists of the necessity for normative predetermination of infringing behaviors and the corresponding sanctions. The principle of typicity unfolds in the theoretical plane through the explicit formulation of the constitutive facts of the infringement and its repressive consequences in the legal norm. However, in practical terms, this previous requirement entails the impossibility of qualifying a behavior as an infringement and sanctioning it if the actions or omissions committed by a subject do not bear a perfect similarity to those designed in the legal types.  
It indicates that the actions of the Ministry cannot be subsumed under the type of Article 5.1.f of the GDPR, as the failure to adopt appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk of processing, in the terms required by Article 32 of the GDPR, when the Ministry contemplates a whole set of measures as described in the first allegation.  
It understands that there is no direct connection in terms of typicity and culpability between the breach of security measures and the violation of the duty of confidentiality. The Ministry implemented a security system that was breached due to a mere human error, which, as described in the written allegations regarding the initiation of the procedure, had never before occurred on the part of the official.  
It indicates that it scrupulously complies with these provisions. In this sense, there are two core elements that support this and allow for the facts not to be subsumed under the conduct of violating the duty of confidentiality:  
- The incident did not arise because a member of the Ministry failed to fulfill their confidentiality obligation and duty of secrecy by disseminating or disclosing information containing personal data without a legitimizing basis. Rather, it was simply due to a mere human error.  
- Nor is it because the systems lack security measures that would guarantee the confidentiality of the data, nor because its personnel do not have a commitment or duty of confidentiality.  
The Ministry points out that in our legal system, it is not permissible that, in a case such as the one at hand, the events that occurred can be considered constitutive of an infringement, based on an infringing type that does not fit the actions of the Ministry.  
For all the above reasons, there is no action or omission on our part that bears similarity to the failure to adopt appropriate technical measures to ensure an adequate level of security, and therefore our conduct cannot be subsumed as an infringement of Articles 5.1F and 32 of the GDPR.

II  
Preliminary Issues  
In the present case, according to the provisions of Article 4.1 of the GDPR, there is evidence of the processing of personal data, given that the Ministry carries out, among other processes, the collection, registration, conservation, use, access, and deletion of the following personal data of individuals, such as: name and surname, identification number, contact details, telephone number, email address, employment circumstances, etc.  
The Ministry carries out this activity in its capacity as the data controller, as it is the one who determines the purposes and means of such activity, by virtue of Article 4.7 of the GDPR.  
Article 4, paragraph 12 of the GDPR broadly defines "personal data security breaches" (hereinafter security breach) as "all breaches of security that lead to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or to the unauthorized communication or access to such data."  
In this case, there is a personal data security breach under the circumstances indicated above, categorized as a confidentiality breach, as a letter containing information about the claimant's employment circumstances was sent by email to a third party, which constitutes unauthorized communication of personal data.  
Within the principles of processing provided for in Article 5 of the GDPR, the integrity and confidentiality of personal data is guaranteed in paragraph 1.f) of Article 5 of the GDPR. For its part, the security of personal data is regulated in Articles 32, 33, and 34 of the GDPR, which govern the security of processing, the notification of a personal data security breach to the supervisory authority, and the communication to the data subject, respectively.

First.- Lack of culpability  
The Ministry argues that it has acted neither with intent nor with negligence in relation to the data protection regulations, as it insists that the breach of confidentiality resulted from a simple material error by a person induced by the concurrent circumstances of the case, since it occurred due to the simultaneous processing of two applications with numerous similarities. It concludes that a mere human error cannot lead to the attribution of sanctioning consequences, as it would constitute objective liability.  
Furthermore, the Ministry contends that there is no rational security measure that can mitigate human error, and therefore it is not appropriate to deduce that the security breach arises from the lack of adoption of adequate security measures, since the incident stems from a simple human mistake for which there are no reasonable protective measures commensurate with the risk, cost, and nature of the threat, as required by Article 32 of the GDPR, reiterating that there is no culpability, but rather a mere fortuitous event.  
It should be noted that the GDPR in the aforementioned Article 32 does not establish a list of security measures applicable according to the data being processed, but rather states that the controller and the processor shall implement technical and organizational measures that are appropriate to the risk posed by the processing, taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.  
Moreover, the security measures must be adequate and proportionate to the identified risk, indicating that the determination of technical and organizational measures should be made considering: pseudonymization and encryption, the ability to ensure confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience, the ability to restore availability and access to data after an incident, verification processes (not audits), evaluation, and assessment of the effectiveness of the measures.  
In any case, when assessing the adequacy of the level of security, particular attention will be paid to the risks presented by the processing of data, as a consequence of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data that could cause physical, material, or immaterial harm.  
In this same vein, Recital 83 of the GDPR states: “(83) In order to maintain security and prevent processing from infringing the provisions of this Regulation, the controller or the processor must assess the inherent risks of the processing and apply measures to mitigate them, such as encryption. Therefore, in the present case, the responsibility of the Ministry is determined by the security incident highlighted by the claimant, as it is responsible for making decisions aimed at effectively implementing the appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security adequate to the risk to guarantee the confidentiality of the data, restoring its availability and preventing access to it in the event of a physical or technical incident.  
Regarding the alleged lack of culpability, it is noted that the emergence of sanctioning responsibility presupposes the existence of culpability in any of its manifestations: intent, negligence, or slight negligence. In the field of Administrative Sanctioning Law, it is not possible to impose sanctions based on the objective responsibility of the alleged offender because administrative sanctions share the same nature as criminal ones, being one of the manifestations of the punitive power of the State and, as a requirement derived from the principles of legal certainty and criminal legality enshrined in Articles 9.3 and 25.1 of the Spanish Constitution (CE), it is essential that this element is present as a prerequisite for its imposition (STC 76/1999).  
The principle of culpability is required in the sanctioning procedure, but the principle of fault does not imply that only intentional actions can be sanctioned. The Supreme Court (STS April 16, 1991, and STS April 22, 1991) considers that from the culpability element it follows “that the action or omission, classified as an administratively sanctionable infringement, must be, in any case, attributable to its author, by intent or negligence, imprudence, or inexcusable ignorance.” The same Court reasons that “it is not enough...for exculpation against a typically unlawful behavior to invoke the absence of fault” but it is necessary “that the diligence required by the one claiming its non-existence has been employed.” (STS January 23, 1998).  
Furthermore, the National Court in matters of personal data protection has declared that “simple negligence or failure to comply with the duties imposed by law on those responsible for files or data processing to exercise due diligence is sufficient...” (SAN June 29, 2001).  
The aforementioned ruling connects the reproachability to a legal entity for a certain conduct with the fact that it has or has not provided effective protection to the legal good protected by the norm. Given the circumstances of the case, it is evident that such diligence has not been observed in relation to the sending of documentation to a third party containing personal data to which they could access without the authorization of its owner.  
Therefore, the conduct of the Ministry must be subject to reproach as a consequence of the breach of the regulations on personal data protection, both for the violation of the principle of confidentiality and for the breach or absence of adequate technical and organizational measures, by allowing access to the claimant's data without authorization.

IV  
Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR  
Article 5.1.f) “Principles relating to processing” of the GDPR establishes:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
(...)  
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, by means of appropriate technical or organizational measures (‘integrity and confidentiality’).”  
In this case, it is established that the personal data of the complainant were improperly exposed to a third party (C.C.C.), (...) as an Official Letter containing information about the complainant's work circumstances was sent to them by email (...).  
Although the complainant's name and surname do not appear directly, they are easily identifiable in that Official Letter as it mentions a nomination of theirs in the Official Bulletin of the City Council of Madrid. It was precisely C.C.C. who informed the complainant of the erroneous sending as they were able to identify them perfectly by that means.  
In this sense, Article 4 of the GDPR, in its paragraph 1), defines “personal data” as: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, particularly by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.  
Therefore, this constitutes an unauthorized communication of personal data to third parties.  
In light of the facts presented, it is considered appropriate to impose a sanction on the party being claimed for the violation of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR as defined in Article 83.5 of the GDPR.
