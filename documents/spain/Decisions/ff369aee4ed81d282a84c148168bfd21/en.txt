On August 12, 2022, the ETHICS DATA SOCIETY FOUNDATION with NIF G67267120 (hereinafter, the Foundation) submitted a document (registration number ***REFERENCE.8) to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection (AEPD) indicating a possible breach of personal data protection regulations in the operation of the “BOSCO information system” (hereinafter, BOSCO System) of the Ministry for Ecological Transition and the Demographic Challenge (hereinafter, MTERD, the Ministry, or the Ministerial Department).  
According to the Foundation, the BOSCO System is used by electric companies to determine whether a person meets the requirements to be a beneficiary of the social bonus. In this regard, it adds that, in its opinion, decisions are made automatically through the BOSCO System “as there is no qualified public employee who critically reviews the assessment of the System or requests additional data from the applicants,” failing to adequately comply with the obligations imposed by personal data protection regulations and the criteria established by the AEPD in the report “Technologies and Data Protection in Public Administrations.” It also states that “there is no evidence that periodic quality assurance controls of the systems exist to ensure that individuals receive fair and non-discriminatory treatment.”  
Additionally, the Foundation specifies the case in which “for a widow, the box for pensioners is checked” and the system “without analyzing the income, returns the message of ‘Inability to calculate’ without any public employee reviewing this response or requesting additional data from the applicants.”  
The Foundation references the document “Technologies and Data Protection in Public Administrations” from the AEPD, considering that the BOSCO System does not comply with the parameters established by data protection regulations, particularly the section “Artificial Intelligence,” which contains a subsection titled “Risks for the

“As a treatment can have an AI in continuous evolution and the data varies over time, there is a risk that its scope may drift in the accuracy of the inference made or that it may introduce biases in the results.  
[…] If the AI-based treatment makes decisions based solely on the automated processing of the exploited data, it is advisable for public bodies to analyze the risk arising from this decision-making method and adopt mechanisms for its analysis and management such as:  
- The establishment of ethics and data protection committees responsible for assessing the potential harms and benefits that a particular treatment may pose for the interested parties in particular and for society in general.  
- Establishing periodic quality assurance controls of their systems to ensure that individuals receive fair and non-discriminatory treatment.  
- Conducting audits to verify that the components used in automated decision-making systems function as intended.  
- To avoid fully delegating to the autonomy of the system and falling into conclusions that no one reviews and that may have a significant impact on the rights and freedoms of individuals, it is advisable to introduce guarantees of a subjective approach that explains a true connection between the data and the results.  
- Implementing mechanisms that allow the interested party to express their point of view and contest the decision, if applicable, with information about the agreed timelines for review and a designated contact point for consultation.  
- Even when it is deemed that there is an obligation for human oversight in processes involving automated decision-making, the risk must be assessed that there may not be a qualified human operator who can disregard the outcome of the decision made at a given moment. To this end, it is key to develop procedures for those situations identified as problematic based on the analysis of cases or incidents in which the treatment has not behaved as expected and where the “de facto” incorporation of a human supervisor who verifies the quality of the decision made must be opted for.

The MTERD identifies (EscritoMTERD) four stages in the application process:  
-  
“Stage I - Application. A consumer wishing to apply for the social bonus will submit the application form to the reference marketer (COR), along with the documentation indicated in the regulations.  
-  
Stage II – Verification. The reference marketer will enter the corresponding data of the consumer and, where applicable, the members of the household declared by the consumer into the BOSCO telematic application, which is available on the electronic headquarters of the Ministry for Ecological Transition and the Demographic Challenge, and the application will return the result of the verifications carried out to the reference marketer.  
-  
Stage III – Result. The reference marketer, based on the result displayed after verification, will inform the consumer whether they meet the requirements to be considered a vulnerable/severely vulnerable consumer and, in the event that they do meet the requirements, will indicate the date from which it will proceed to apply the bonus.  
-  
Stage IV – Complaint. In the event of discrepancies regarding both the accreditation of the requirements and the application of the social bonus, consumers may file a complaint with the corresponding local or regional consumer services, under the terms established by consumer protection regulations. If the corresponding complaint is upheld, the reference marketers must grant the social bonus under the terms indicated by the corresponding consumer services.

Human participation in the process  
Regarding human participation in the process, the MTERD states (EscritoMTERD) that once the reference marketer manually enters the application into the BOSCO application, it is executed automatically, providing the result back to the marketer. Regarding the claims stage, the MTERD states that it is the consumer services that process and resolve the procedures in accordance with their own regulations and the configuration of such procedures derived from decisions at the regional and local level.  
The MTERD concludes (EscritoMTERD) that “the making of a decision is not based solely on the automated processing of personal data,” so Article 22 of the GDPR would not apply.

FIRST:  
The social bond represents a discount on the price of electricity supplied to certain vulnerable consumers.  
In this regard, Article 6.1 of Royal Decree 897/2017, of October 6, which regulates the figure of the vulnerable consumer, the social bond, and other protective measures for domestic electricity consumers, provides:  
“Article 6. Social bond applicable to the vulnerable consumer.  
1. The vulnerable consumer may benefit, under the conditions established in this royal decree, from a discount on their bill called the social bond.” (the underlining is by the AEPD).  
Furthermore, the MTERD in the written allegations to the initiation agreement dated December 15, 2023, emphasizes:  
“The social bond has been defined as a public service obligation composed, according to the CJEU and the TS, of two differentiated but interrelated elements. The TS states that ‘following what was declared by the Court of Justice of the European Union - CJEU, Fifth Chamber of October 14, 2021, case C-683 (paragraph 40 of its legal basis) - that the public service obligation imposed by the social bond consists of two elements, on one hand, the discount on the price of electricity supplied to certain vulnerable consumers and, on the other hand, the financial contribution intended to cover the cost of that discount, which are indissociably linked.’ (…)”. The aforementioned CJEU ruling is provided as DOCUMENT 1.  
Thus, the public service obligation imposed by the social bond consists of two elements, on one hand, the discount on the price of electricity supplied to certain vulnerable consumers and, on the other hand, the financial contribution intended to cover the cost of that discount.” (the underlining is by the AEPD).

The MTERD highlights that the public service obligation imposed by the social bond consists of two elements:

The social bond was created in 2009. It is not a direct aid (like the thermal bond), nor is it a subsidy or a transfer of bonds. The social bond is a discount on the electricity bill that is imposed on certain companies, called reference marketers (the COR), so that they offer the regulated discount on the bill, in a determined amount, to a series of groups (second, third, and fourth proven facts).  
The beneficiary groups of the social bond have been modified over time, although they have always been referred to as vulnerable consumers (fifth proven fact):  
Initially, the discount was offered to certain groups without considering income level.  
The approval of Royal Decree-Law 7/2016, of December 23, resulted in significant changes to the social bond:  
- The definition of vulnerable consumers was modified, expanding it to include essential supplies (referring to vulnerability concerning income).  
- The financing scheme was modified.  
Therefore, as a consequence of this significant regulatory modification, the income criterion was included in the processing of the social bond (ninth proven fact).  
In the implementation of the management of the social bond, in accordance with the regulation contained in Royal Decree-Law 7/2016, of December 23, a series of safeguards were adopted:  
- The accreditation of special circumstances, which pertained to the conditions of victims of gender-based violence, terrorism, and disability, was done indirectly (the COR were only recipients of a certificate from the regional social services that accredited the existence of such circumstances). Seventeenth proven fact  
- The COR did not have direct access to the income data of consumers and members of the household unit (sixteenth proven fact).

3. In the case examined in this file, a chain of processing occurs:  
The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), in rulings such as the one issued in the case of the Belgian official journal, dated January 11, 2024, in case C-231/22, addressed and analyzed a scenario in which there was a "chain of processing," and not a "cumulative responsibility" or "joint responsibility."  
In the aforementioned ruling, the CJEU considered that the Belgian official journal was solely responsible for complying with the principles set out in Article 5 of the GDPR concerning the personal data processing operations it had to carry out under national law.  
Therefore, a chain of processing would consist of successive independent controllers, each of whom is responsible for the processing operations they carry out.  
In this same vein, the ruling of the Court of Justice of the European Union of July 29, 2019, in the Fashion ID case, C-40/17, ECLI:EU:2018:1039, establishes in its paragraph 74 that "(...) On the other hand, and without prejudice to any potential civil liability provided for in national law in this regard, such a natural or legal person cannot be considered responsible, in the sense of that provision, for the previous or subsequent operations in the processing chain regarding which it does not determine the purposes or means," highlighting once again that chains of processing would consist of successive independent controllers.

1. Regulation provided for in Article 22 of the GDPR:  
Article 22 of the GDPR, concerning “Automated individual decisions, including profiling,” establishes the following:  
1. Every data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her.  
2. Paragraph 1 shall not apply if the decision:  
   a) is necessary for the conclusion or performance of a contract between the data subject and a data controller;  
   b) is authorized by Union or Member State law applicable to the data controller and which also establishes appropriate measures to safeguard the rights and freedoms and the legitimate interests of the data subject; or  
   c) is based on the explicit consent of the data subject.  
3. In the cases referred to in paragraph 2, letters a) and c), the data controller shall implement appropriate measures to safeguard the rights and freedoms and the legitimate interests of the data subject, at a minimum the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view, and to contest the decision.  
4. Decisions referred to in paragraph 2 shall not be based on special categories of personal data as referred to in Article 9, paragraph 1, unless Article 9, paragraph 2, letter a) or g) applies, and appropriate measures have been taken to safeguard the rights and freedoms and the legitimate interests of the data subject.”  

2. Recital 71 of the GDPR:  
Furthermore, the GDPR, in its Recital 71, states:  
“The data subject should have the right not to be subject to a decision, which may include a measure, that evaluates personal aspects concerning him or her, and that is based solely on automated processing and produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her, such as the automatic denial of an online credit application or online hiring services in which there is no human intervention whatsoever. This type of processing includes profiling consisting of any form of automated processing of personal data that evaluates personal aspects concerning a natural person, in particular to analyze or predict aspects related to work performance, economic situation, health, personal preferences or interests, reliability or behavior, the situation or movements of the data subject, to the extent that it produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her. However, decisions based on such processing, including profiling, should be allowed if expressly authorized by Union or Member State law applicable to the data controller, even for the purposes of fraud control and prevention and tax evasion, carried out in accordance with the regulations, standards, and recommendations of the institutions of the Union or national supervisory bodies and to ensure the security and reliability of a service provided by the data controller, or necessary for the conclusion or performance of a contract between the data subject and a data controller, or in cases where the data subject has given his or her explicit consent. In any case, such processing must be subject to appropriate safeguards, including specific information to the data subject and the right to obtain human intervention, to express his or her point of view, to receive an explanation of the decision taken after such evaluation, and to contest the decision. Such a measure must not affect a minor. In order to ensure fair and transparent processing with respect to the data subject, taking into account the specific circumstances and context in which personal data are processed, the data controller must use appropriate mathematical or statistical procedures for profiling, apply appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure, in particular, that the factors introducing inaccuracies in personal data are corrected and the risk of error is minimized, secure personal data in a manner that takes into account the potential risks to the interests and rights of the data subject, and prevent, among other things, discriminatory effects on individuals on grounds of race or ethnic origin, political opinions, religion or beliefs, trade union membership, genetic condition or health status or sexual orientation, or processing that leads to measures producing such effects. 3.1. Cumulative Requirements:  
The aforementioned CJEU ruling of December 7, 2023 (case C-634/21) highlights that the applicability of Article 22.1 of the GDPR, relating to automated decisions, is subject to the concurrence of three cumulative requirements:  
“42. According to Article 22, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, every data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her.  
43. Therefore, the applicability of this provision is subject to three cumulative requirements, namely, first, there must be a ‘decision’; second, that decision must be ‘based solely on automated processing, including profiling’; and third, it must produce ‘legal effects [affecting the data subject]’ or significantly affect him or her in a similar manner.  
Consequently, all three cumulative requirements must be met:  
1. There must be a decision.  
2. That decision must be based solely on automated processing.  
3. It must produce legal effects affecting the data subject or significantly affect him or her in a similar manner.  

Regarding the first requirement (existence of a decision), the CJEU ruling of December 7, 2023 (case C-634/21) indicates:  
44. As for the first requirement concerning the existence of a decision, it should be noted that the concept of ‘decision’, in the sense of Article 22, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, is not defined in that Regulation. However, from the very wording of this provision, it is clear that this concept does not refer only to acts that produce legal effects affecting the data subject in question, but also to acts that significantly affect him or her in a similar manner.  
45. The broad scope of the concept of ‘decision’ is confirmed by Recital 71 of the GDPR, according to which a decision that involves the evaluation of personal aspects relating to a data subject, from which he or she has the right not to be subject, “may include a measure” that either produces “legal effects on him or her” or “significantly affects him or her in a similar manner.” According to this recital, the term ‘decision’ includes, by way of example, the automatic denial of an online credit application or online hiring services in which there is no human intervention whatsoever.  
46. The concept of ‘decision’ in the sense of Article 22, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, as noted by the Advocate General in paragraph 38 of his conclusions, may include various acts with the potential to affect the data subject in multiple ways, as it is broad enough to encompass the result of calculating a person's creditworthiness in the form of a probability value regarding their ability to meet future payment obligations.  
Therefore, the CJEU in its Judgment emphasizes that the term decision must be interpreted broadly, encompassing both acts that produce legal effects affecting the data subject and acts that significantly affect him or her in a similar manner.  

Regarding the second requirement (that decision must be based solely on automated processing), the CJEU ruling of December 7, 2023 (case C-634/21) highlights:  
47. As for the second requirement that the decision, in the sense of that Article 22, paragraph 1, must be ‘based solely on automated processing, including profiling’, as the Advocate General pointed out in paragraph 33 of his conclusions, it is undisputed that an activity such as that carried out by SCHUFA meets the definition of ‘profiling’ as set out in Article 4, point 4, of the GDPR and, therefore, this requirement is fulfilled in the present case, given that the wording of the first preliminary question expressly refers to the automated generation of a probability value based on personal data relating to a person and regarding their ability to satisfy a loan in the future.  

Regarding the third requirement (that decision must produce legal effects affecting the data subject or significantly affect him or her in a similar manner), the CJEU ruling of December 7, 2023 (case C-634/21) clarifies:  
48. As for the third requirement that the decision must produce ‘legal effects’ on the data subject or ‘[affect him or her]’...

Thus, according to the factual assessments of the referring jurisdiction, in the event that a consumer requests a loan from a bank, an insufficient value of probability will, in almost all cases, result in the bank denying the requested loan.

It must be carried out by an authorized and competent person to modify the decision. As part of the analysis, all relevant data must be taken into account.”

IX Unfulfilled obligation of Article 22 of the GDPR  
The regulation of Article 22 of the GDPR and Recital 71 of said Regulation must be interpreted, as indicated in the Guidelines on automated individual decision-making and profiling for the purposes of the Regulation, of the Article 29 Data Protection Working Party - WP251rev.01 - (hereinafter the Guidelines), that the term "right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing" of the provision does not mean that Article 22.1 applies only when invoked actively by the data subject, but rather establishes a general prohibition of decisions based solely on automated processing. Therefore, this prohibition applies whether the data subject takes action regarding the processing of their personal data or not, as highlighted in legal ground IV, section 3 (CJEU ruling of December 7, 2023, in case C-634/21).  
However, this generic prohibition has its exceptions, allowing the controller to make decisions based solely on automated processing, provided that one of the three scenarios set forth in section 2 of Article 22 of the GDPR is met, requiring, in these cases, the adoption of appropriate measures to safeguard the rights and freedoms and the legitimate interests of the data subject, which in some cases the GDPR specifies as, at a minimum, the right to obtain human intervention by the controller, to express their point of view, and to contest the decision.  
Article 22 concludes, in its section 4, with another added prohibition stating that decisions based solely on automated processing shall not be based on special categories of data, unless Article 9, section 2, letter a) or g) applies and appropriate measures have been taken to safeguard the rights and freedoms and the legitimate interests of the data subject.

1. Processing that involves profiling or assessing individuals, including the collection of data about the individual in multiple areas of their life (work performance, personality and behavior), covering various aspects of their personality or their habits.  
2. Processing that involves automated decision-making or significantly contributes to such decision-making, including any type of decision that prevents a data subject from exercising a right or accessing a good or service or being part of a contract.  
3. Processing that involves the systematic and comprehensive observation, monitoring, supervision, geolocation, or control of the data subject, including the collection of data and metadata through networks, applications, or in public access areas, as well as the processing of unique identifiers that allow the identification of users of information society services such as web services, interactive TV, mobile applications, etc.  
4. Processing that involves the use of special categories of data referred to in Article 9.1 of the GDPR, data related to criminal convictions or offenses referred to in Article 10 of the GDPR, or data that allows determining the financial situation or asset solvency or inferring information about individuals related to special categories of data.  
5. Processing that involves the use of biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person.  
6. Processing that involves the use of genetic data for any purpose.  
7. Processing that involves the use of data on a large scale. To determine whether processing can be considered large scale, the criteria established in the WP243 guidelines "Guidelines on Data Protection Officers (DPO)" of the Article 29 Working Party will be considered.  
8. Processing that involves the association, combination, or linking of records from databases of two or more processing activities with different purposes or by different controllers.  
9. Processing of data from vulnerable subjects or those at risk of social exclusion, including data from minors under 14 years of age, elderly individuals with some degree of disability, disabled persons, individuals accessing social services, and victims of gender-based violence, as well as their descendants and individuals under their guardianship and custody.” (the underlining is ours)
