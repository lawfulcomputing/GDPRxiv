RESOLUTION OF RECONSIDERATION APPEAL  
Examined the reconsideration appeal filed by GLOVOAPP23, S.A. (hereinafter, the appellant) against the resolution issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection dated March 7, 2023, and based on the following  

FACTS  
FIRST: On March 7, 2023, a resolution was issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection in file PS/00209/2022, whereby it was decided:  
- TO DIRECT GLOVOAPP23, S.A., with NIF A66362906, previously GLOVOAPP23, S.L., with NIF B66362906, a warning for an infringement of Article 13 of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.5 of the GDPR, for failing to adequately inform the delivery personnel that, by using the “excellence score” system, automated decisions were being made as regulated in Article 22 of the GDPR.  
- TO IMPOSE on GLOVOAPP23, S.A., with NIF A66362906, previously GLOVOAPP23, S.L., with NIF B66362906, a fine of €550,000 (five hundred fifty thousand euros), for the infringement of Articles 25 and 32 of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.4 of the GDPR, because the system used for managing access permissions to the delivery personnel's data allowed access to data not necessary for the users' work.  
This resolution, which was notified to the appellant on March 17, 2023, was issued following the processing of the corresponding sanctioning procedure, in accordance with the provisions of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), and subsidiarily under Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), regarding the processing of sanctioning procedures.  

SECOND: The appellant submitted on April 17, 2023, to this Spanish Agency for Data Protection, a reconsideration appeal, in which the proven facts of the resolution of the sanctioning procedure subject to this appeal are not contested, so they are considered proven facts of this reconsideration appeal resolution.

Secondly, the aforementioned Guidelines on automated individual decision-making and profiling for the purposes of Regulation 2016/679 state: “Decisions based solely on automated processing represent the ability to make decisions by technological means without the involvement of a human being.”  
That is to say, the making of an automated decision does not consist of a mere automated process, such as an automatic order dispatch system or goods receipt, but refers to the ability to make decisions by technological means. The use of technology to make decisions that affect individuals and that require the processing of personal data poses a greater risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, as the algorithm may make erroneous, biased, or unfair decisions. Therefore, the GDPR establishes the safeguards that must be adopted in the use of algorithms that make decisions due to the greater risk they entail, among which a duty of transparency is imposed on the data controller.

That is, the obligation to implement appropriate measures to ensure that only the personal data necessary for each specific purpose of processing is subject to processing applies not only to the accessibility of the data but also to the amount of personal data collected, the extent of its processing, and its retention period.  
And that, in particular, such measures must ensure that, by default, personal data is not accessible, without the intervention of the individual, to an indeterminate number of natural persons. This means that such measures must guarantee the aforementioned, but not only that.  
This Agency insists that when the GDPR refers to measures that ensure personal data is not accessible, without the intervention of the individual, to an indeterminate number of natural persons, it uses the phrase "in particular." This means that preventing indiscriminate access to the data of delivery personnel is one of the obligations referenced by the GDPR, but it is not the only one. In fact, the first obligation referenced in Article 25.2 of the GDPR is to implement appropriate measures to ensure that, by default, only personal data necessary for each specific purpose of processing is processed. In other words, that data that is not strictly necessary for each purpose for which it is processed is not processed.  
In the present case, data of delivery personnel from countries that did not need to be processed to track those delivery personnel was being processed due to a lack of foresight in designing appropriate measures to ensure compliance with the principle of data minimization, as well as the absence of appropriate measures to ensure that, by default, only necessary data is processed. Therefore, Article 25 of the GDPR is not complied with.  
The same can be said for Article 32 of the GDPR, since the technical and organizational measures applied did not guarantee an adequate level of security relative to the risk, given that the system allowed users access to data of delivery personnel that they did not need to process for the purpose for which it was used.

The guarantee of the private life of the person and of their reputation today has a positive dimension that exceeds the scope of the fundamental right to privacy (art. 18.1 CE), and translates into a right of control over data related to the person themselves. 6. The function of the fundamental right to privacy under art. 18.1 CE is to protect against any invasion that may occur in that area of personal and family life that the individual wishes to exclude from the knowledge of others and from the intrusions of third parties against their will (see, among others, STC 144/1999, of July 22, FJ 8). In contrast, the fundamental right to data protection aims to guarantee that individual a power of control over their personal data, its use and destination, with the purpose of preventing its unlawful and harmful trafficking to the dignity and rights of the affected party. Ultimately, the right to privacy allows for the exclusion of certain data about a person from the knowledge of others; for this reason, and as this Court has stated (SSTC 134/1999, of July 15, FJ 5; 144/1999, FJ 8; 98/2000, of April 10, FJ 5; 115/2000, of May 10, FJ 4), it means the power to safeguard one's private life from unwanted publicity. The right to data protection guarantees individuals a power of disposition over that data. This guarantee imposes on public authorities the prohibition of becoming sources of that information without the necessary safeguards; and also the duty to prevent risks that may arise from the improper access or disclosure of such information. However, that power of disposition over one's personal data is of no value if the affected party is unaware of what data is held by third parties, who holds it, and for what purpose.  
Hence the uniqueness of the right to data protection, since, on one hand, its object is broader than that of the right to privacy, as the fundamental right to data protection extends its guarantee not only to privacy in its constitutionally protected dimension under art. 18.1 CE, but also to what this Court has sometimes defined in broader terms as the sphere of personal goods that belong to the realm of private life, inextricably linked to the respect for personal dignity (STC 170/1987, of October 30, FJ 4), such as the right to honor, expressly mentioned in art. 18.4 CE, and likewise, in a very broad expression of art. 18.4 CE itself, to the full exercise of personal rights. The fundamental right to data protection expands the constitutional guarantee to those data that are relevant to or have an impact on the exercise of any personal rights, whether or not they are constitutional rights and whether or not they relate to honor, ideology, personal and family privacy, or any other constitutionally protected good.  
In this way, the object of protection of the fundamental right to data protection is not limited only to the intimate data of the individual, but to any type of personal data, whether intimate or not, whose knowledge or use by third parties may affect their rights, whether or not fundamental, because its object is not only individual privacy, which is covered by the protection granted by art. 18.1 CE, but personal data. Consequently, it also extends to those public personal data, which by virtue of being accessible to anyone, do not escape the power of disposition of the affected party because their right to data protection guarantees it. For this reason, the fact that the data is of a personal nature does not mean that only those related to the private or intimate life of the individual are protected, but rather...

These powers of disposition and control over personal data, which are part of the content of the fundamental right to data protection, are legally specified in the authority to consent to the collection, obtaining, and access to personal data, its subsequent storage and processing, as well as its possible use or uses, by a third party, whether the State or a private individual. And this right to consent to the knowledge and processing, whether computerized or not, of personal data requires as indispensable complements, on the one hand, the authority to know at all times who holds that personal data and what use they are subjecting it to, and, on the other hand, the power to oppose that possession and uses.

Therefore, the general rule that the suspension of processing deadlines is permissible does not mean that all possibilities for administrative action are prohibited while the state of alarm or its extensions are in effect. The regulation does not establish the paralysis of administrative activity or, more precisely, the paralysis of the activity of public sector entities (nor does it refer to the paralysis of the activity of the country’s judicial bodies). At no point does the aforementioned article of the GDPR state that these three issues are the only ones that must be considered to analyze the nature, severity, and duration of the infringement.

4.4. Existence of additional mitigating circumstances  
The appellant alleges that the following additional mitigating circumstances should be applied:  
I. The mitigating factor provided for in Article 83.2.e) of the GDPR, as there are no prior violations:  
The appellant argues that the lack of recidivism or reiteration was established as a "mitigating factor of special relevance" in the sanctioning resolution of the sanctioning procedure 120/2021, directed against MERCADONA, S.A.  
And that in application of the principle of equality in the application of the law, this same mitigating factor should be applied to GLOVO, as GLOVO has not been sanctioned previously for the acts subject to the Sanctioning Resolution.  
In this regard, this Agency wishes to point out that the fact of not having been sanctioned previously can only operate as an aggravating factor and in no case as a mitigating factor.  
In this sense, the National Court in its SAN of May 5, 2021 (Rec. 1437/2020) regarding section e) of Article 83.2 of the GDPR, concerning the commission of previous infringements, has stated that:  
"On the other hand, it considers that the non-commission of a previous infringement should be appreciated as a mitigating factor. Well, Article 83.2 of the GDPR establishes that the following circumstances must be taken into account for the imposition of the administrative fine, among others, the circumstance 'e) any previous infringement committed by the controller or the processor.' It is an aggravating circumstance; the fact that the prerequisite for its application does not occur means that it cannot be taken into consideration, but does not imply or allow, as the claimant intends, its application as a mitigating factor."  
For all the above reasons, this allegation is dismissed.

5.1. Existence of abuse of rights  
The appellant alleges that the AEPD opened a new sanctioning procedure (the current PS/00209/2022) after the previous procedure (PS/00020/2021) was declared expired, based on identical facts and legal grounds, which, in their opinion, has created a situation of evident legal uncertainty for Glovo from the very beginning.

In this regard, they add that it cannot be tolerated for an Administration to take advantage of the effects of its inactivity to argue that if it "sanctions again," it cannot be considered that the first time a sanction was imposed because that sanction "was null and void."  
Therefore, the appellant argues that a new procedure based on identical facts and legal grounds can only be qualified as a clear act of abuse of rights by this Agency, given that expiration, if it is a tool of guarantee and peace of mind for the citizen, would thus become a mere instrument of the alleged negligence of the officials; an instrument that ultimately falls on the shoulders of Glovo as the administered party, which would not only be subjected to the torture (and, by the way, also to the expenses) of a new file and the uncertainty it entails.  
In this regard, this Agency wishes to reiterate what has already been stated in the aforementioned Sanctioning Resolution subject to this appeal.  
In the present case, this Agency only declared the expiration of the proceedings on one occasion (procedure number PS/00020/2021) due to the lapse of the 9-month period established for its resolution under Article 64.2 of the LOPDGDD and initiated a new sanctioning procedure for infringements that were not time-barred, as provided for in Article 95.3 of the LPACAP:  
"Expiration will not, by itself, produce the time-bar of actions by the individual or the Administration, but expired procedures will not interrupt the time-bar period.  
In cases where it is possible to initiate a new procedure due to the absence of time-bar, the acts and procedures whose content would have remained the same had expiration not occurred may be incorporated into it. In any case, the new procedure must fulfill the procedures for allegations, proposal of evidence, and hearing of the interested party."  
Therefore, this is not a case in which several procedures are restarted after successive declarations of expiration, nor in which the existence of an abuse of rights by the Administration can be appreciated.
