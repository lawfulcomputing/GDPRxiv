LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of the GDPR, and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of the LOPDGDD, the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: <<The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, in so far as they do not contradict them, by the general rules on administrative procedures in a subsidiary manner.>>  
II  
Obligation breached  
Article 6.1 of the GDPR establishes that “in accordance with the provisions of Article 4.11 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, consent of the affected party means any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes by which they signify agreement, by a statement or by a clear affirmative action, to the processing of personal data concerning them.”  
On the other hand, Article 6.4 of the GDPR provides: “When the processing for a purpose other than that for which the personal data were collected is not based on the consent of the data subject or on Union or Member State law that constitutes a necessary and proportionate measure in a democratic society to safeguard the objectives set out in Article 23(1), the data controller, in order to determine whether the processing for another purpose is compatible with the purpose for which the personal data were initially collected, shall take into account, among other things:  
a) any relationship between the purposes for which the personal data were collected and the purposes of the intended further processing;  
b) the context in which the personal data were collected, in particular regarding the relationship between the data subjects and the data controller;  
c) the nature of the personal data, in particular when special categories of personal data are processed, in accordance with Article 9, or personal data relating to criminal convictions and offenses, in accordance with Article 10;  
d) the possible consequences for the data subjects of the intended further processing;
5/12  
e) the existence of adequate safeguards, which may include encryption or pseudonymization.”  
And Recital 50 of the aforementioned GDPR states: “(...) In order to determine whether the purpose of the further processing is compatible with the purpose of the initial collection of personal data, the data controller, having fulfilled all the requirements for the lawfulness of the original processing, must take into account, among other things, any relationship between these purposes and the purposes of the intended further processing, the context in which the data was collected, in particular the reasonable expectations of the data subject based on their relationship with the controller regarding its subsequent use, the nature of the personal data, the consequences for the data subjects of the intended further processing, and the existence of adequate safeguards both in the original processing operation and in the intended further processing operation. (…)”  
For its part, Article 5 of the GDPR establishes that personal data shall be:  
“a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (‘lawfulness, fairness, and transparency’);  
b) collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, further processing of personal data for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes shall not be considered incompatible with the initial purposes (‘purpose limitation’);  
c) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’);  
d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; all reasonable steps must be taken to ensure that personal data that are inaccurate with respect to the purposes for which they are processed are erased or rectified without delay (‘accuracy’);  
e) kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes of the processing of personal data; personal data may be stored for longer periods as long as they are processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes, in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, without prejudice to the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures required by this Regulation to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject (‘storage limitation’);  
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, through the application of appropriate technical or organizational measures (‘integrity and confidentiality’).  
The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and be able to demonstrate it (‘accountability’).”

III  
Classification and qualification of the infringement  
The infringement is classified under Article 83.5 of the GDPR, which considers the following:

The LOPDGD, for the purposes of the statute of limitations for the infringement, qualifies in its Article 72.1 as a very serious infringement, with a limitation period of three years in this case, “a) The processing of personal data in violation of the principles and guarantees established in Article 5 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.”

IV  
Allegations  
In response to the allegations presented by the defendant party, the following should be noted:  
Regarding the witness statements provided by the defendant party from Mr. C.C.C., with ID number ***NIF.2, and Ms. D.D.D., with ID number ***NIF.3, voluntary collaborators of (...) and Property Management (...), it is explained in these statements that they were witnesses to the fact that the claimant authorized the direct debit of the receipts accrued from the rental of the room.  
Well, concerning the evidentiary value of witness testimony, one can cite the SAN of March 14, 2013 (Rec.394/20111), which examined the witness statement provided by one of the claimants and declared that “we find ourselves in the administrative sanctioning sphere and in matters of data protection, so it is to this specific sphere that our analysis must be confined, being relevant for the purposes of this procedure to clarify whether it has been proven that the appealing entity has provided third parties with personal data of its clients and especially, regarding debts of the entity.” (The underlining is from the Agency)  
The Judgment assesses the evidence that the contested resolution had taken into consideration to prove the breach of the duty of confidentiality. Thus, one of the claimants had “provided the statement of his brother, in which he states that he has received calls from (...) at his home informing him that his brother had a debt with said entity,” and this evidence was deemed sufficient by the Agency to establish the existence of a breach of the duty of confidentiality; however, the National Court reaches the opposite conclusion and states that “the aforementioned statement provided by complainant 5 lacks consistency to undermine the presumption of innocence that protects (...) and to prove the breach of the duty of confidentiality attributed.”  
The reasoning offered by the Chamber of the National Court to undermine the evidentiary force attributed to the sworn statement of the claimant's brother was “that it is a very generic and imprecise statement. Thus, for example, it does not state in that statement what the phone number of the private residence was to which the declarant claims he was called, so it cannot be verified whether it appears in the notebook of (...), in which there is no record of contact with the brother. It also does not specify in the aforementioned statement the dates on which the calls were received, stating that he was called 'some time ago, months ago.'  
In the matter at hand, it must be concluded that the statements provided as evidence of the existence of consent do not have the necessary validity to prove the existence of validly given consent, in the terms set forth in Article 4.11 of the GDPR, that is to say, that it was free, unambiguous, specific, and informed consent, especially considering the power imbalance existing in the employment relationships between employees and employer.  
In the present case, it is established that the defendant party used the bank account number provided by the claimant when formalizing the employment contract.

On January 4, 2021, with the entity (...), of which the claimed party is the president, to set up a direct debit for the collection of the lease contract for a room signed on November 17, 2020, between both parties.  
Well, the claimed party uses this data for a purpose different from that for which it was provided, which is none other than the collection of the salary by the claimant.  
These facts are evidenced by the claimant providing the document that she delivered to (...) for the deposit of her salary payroll, which she received as a worker of the (...). On the other hand, the claimed party has used the bank account number provided by (...), to create a direct debit without a legitimate cause for doing so.  
Therefore, there are indications of the processing of personal data of the claimant that is incompatible with the purposes for which such data was collected.  
In short, the claimed party has not justified the compatibility of the purposes nor that it has the consent of the claimant to process the data for this new purpose.  

Therefore, the claimed party is charged with committing an infringement for violating Article 5.1.b) of the GDPR, which regulates the principle of purpose limitation, establishing that personal data shall be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes, and shall not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes; it is also established that the data controller has a proactive responsibility to demonstrate compliance.  
In summary, the personal data of the claimant was collected to fulfill specific purposes arising from the employment relationship she maintained with the (...), which in turn is a legal entity distinct from the claimed party, who is a natural person, resulting in an incompatible use with the initially authorized purposes.  
Furthermore, Article 7 of the GDPR, relating to "Conditions for Consent," establishes in its paragraph 1 that:  
“When processing is based on the consent of the data subject, the controller shall be able to demonstrate that the data subject has consented to the processing of their personal data.”  
Likewise, applicable case law has repeatedly indicated that when the data subject denies consent for the processing of their data, the burden of proof lies with the party asserting its existence, and the data controller must collect and retain the necessary documentation to prove the consent of the data subject. This is supported by the Judgment of the National Court of May 31, 2006 (Rec. 539/2004): “On the other hand, it is the responsibility of the data controller (see, among others, the judgment of this Chamber of October 25, 2002, Rec. 185/2001) to ensure that the person from whom consent is requested actually gives it, and that this person giving consent is indeed the owner of those personal data, and must retain proof of compliance with the obligation available to the Administration, which is responsible for ensuring compliance with the Law.”  
Consequently, in accordance with the evidence presented, the aforementioned facts constitute a violation of the provisions of Article 5.1.b) of the GDPR.

“1. Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines under this article for the infringements of this Regulation indicated in paragraphs 4, 9, and 6 are, in each individual case, effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.”  
“2. Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure to those provided for in Article 58, paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j). a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing operation in question, as well as the number of data subjects affected and the level of damage suffered;  
b) the intentionality or negligence in the infringement;
