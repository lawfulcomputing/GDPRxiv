After examining the allegations of the responding party, this Agency considers that when an email address, even if it is a company email account, can be linked to an identified or identifiable natural person, it is undoubtedly a personal data. This means that it is necessary to obtain the unequivocal consent of the affected person or that this consent may be considered exempt under some of the exceptions of the aforementioned law.  
The fact that there may have been a professional relationship in the past due to the position held by the now-complaining party in their previous job, or that this person continues to maintain contact with part of the staff of the responding company through various communication channels as pointed out by the responding party, is not a reason to understand that there is consent on their part to receive commercial communications from the responding party.  
For all the above reasons, the allegations presented are DISMISSED.  
III  
Currently, “spam” refers to all types of unsolicited communication conducted electronically. Thus, “spam” is understood to mean any unsolicited message that typically aims to offer, market, or try to arouse interest regarding a product, service, or company. Although it can be done through various means, the most commonly used is email.  
This conduct is particularly serious when carried out en masse. The sending of commercial messages without prior consent is prohibited by Spanish legislation.  
The low cost of sending emails via the Internet or through mobile telephony (SMS and MMS), its possible anonymity, the speed with which it reaches recipients, and the possibilities it offers regarding the volume of transmissions have allowed this practice to be carried out abusively and indiscriminately.  
Law 34/2002, of July 11, on information society services and electronic commerce (hereinafter, LSSI), in its article 21.1, expressly prohibits “the sending of advertising or promotional communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means that have not been previously requested or expressly authorized by the recipients.” That is to say, commercial communications aimed at the direct or indirect promotion of the goods or services of a company, organization, or person engaged in commercial, industrial, artisanal, or professional activity are disallowed without the express consent of the recipient, although this prohibition finds its exception in paragraph 2 of the aforementioned article 21, which authorizes the sending of commercial communications when “there is a prior contractual relationship, provided that the provider has lawfully obtained the contact details of the recipient.”

and will use them to send commercial communications regarding products or services of their own company that are similar to those initially contracted with the client.” Thus, the sending of unsolicited commercial communications, outside the exceptional case of Article 21.2 of the LSSI, may constitute a minor or serious infringement of the LSSI.  
In accordance with these regulations, consent, in addition to being prior, specific, and unequivocal, must be informed. This information must be complete and accurate regarding the sector of activity from which one may receive advertising, with a warning about the right to refuse or withdraw consent. This information, thus configured, must be taken as a necessary prerequisite to grant validity to the expression of will of the affected party.  
IV  
As already noted, the LSSI prohibits unsolicited or expressly authorized commercial communications, based on a concept of commercial communication that is qualified as an information society service and is defined in its Annex as follows:  
“f) ‘Commercial communication’: any form of communication aimed at the promotion, directly or indirectly, of the image or of the goods or services of a company, organization, or person engaged in a commercial, industrial, artisanal, or professional activity.  
For the purposes of this Law, the data that allows direct access to the activity of a person, company, or organization, such as the domain name or email address, will not be considered commercial communication, nor will communications related to the goods, services, or image offered when they are produced by a third party and without economic consideration.”  
The concept of commercial communication, according to the definition included in Annex f), first paragraph of the LSSI, encompasses all forms of communications intended to promote, directly or indirectly, goods, services, or the image of a company, organization, or person with a commercial, industrial, artisanal, or professional activity.  
On the other hand, the LSSI in its Annex a) defines “Information Society Service” as “any service normally provided for remuneration, at a distance, by electronic means, and at the individual request of the recipient.”  
The concept of information society service also includes services not remunerated by their recipients, insofar as they constitute an economic activity for the service provider.  
The aforementioned section adds that the sending of commercial communications is an information society service, as long as it represents an economic activity.

According to section d) of the aforementioned Annex, the recipient is the “natural or legal person who uses, whether or not for professional reasons, an information society service.”  
From the above, it follows that when the commercial communication does not meet the requirements required by the concept of Information Society Services, it loses its character as a commercial communication. In this sense, the second paragraph of Annex f) of the LSSI indicates two cases that will not be considered commercial communication for the purposes of this Law. On the one hand, the data that allows direct access to the activity of a person, company, or organization, such as the domain name or email address, and, on the other hand, communications related to the goods, services, or image offered when they are produced by a third party and without economic consideration.  
V  
Title VII of the LSSI under the heading “Infringements and Sanctions” contains the sanctioning regime applicable in the event that any of the infringements contained in the infringement table included therein occur.  
Specifically, Article 37 specifies that “providers of information society services are subject to the sanctioning regime established in this Title when this Law is applicable to them.”  
The definition of service provider is contained in section c) of the Annex of the aforementioned regulation, which considers as such the “natural or legal person who provides an information society service.”

VI  
The known facts constitute an infringement, attributable to the responding party, for violation of Article 21.1 of the LSSI, which states the following:  
“1. The sending of advertising or promotional communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means that have not been previously requested or expressly authorized by the recipients is prohibited.”  
In light of the above and considering that the complainant states that they have no prior contractual relationship or consent regarding this matter with the responding party to receive communications, it is understood that a violation of the cited article has occurred due to the receipt of commercial communications (spam) in which legitimate interest does not apply, applying the LSSI and not 6.1.f) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679.  
VII  
The infringement is classified as minor under Article 38.4.d) of the LSSI, which considers as such: “The sending of commercial communications by email or other equivalent electronic communication means when the requirements established in Article 21 are not met and does not constitute a serious infringement.”  
This infringement is sanctioned with a fine of €2,000 (TWO THOUSAND EUROS), in accordance with Article 39.1.c) of the LSSI.  
“1. For the commission of the infringements set forth in the previous article, the following sanctions shall be imposed:  
(...)  
c) For the commission of minor infringements, a fine of up to 30,000 euros.”  
In view of the above, the following

Article 85 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter LPACAP), under the heading "Termination in sanctioning procedures" provides as follows:  
“1. Once a sanctioning procedure has been initiated, if the offender acknowledges their responsibility, the procedure may be resolved with the imposition of the appropriate sanction.
