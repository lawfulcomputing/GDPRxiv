RESOLUTION OF SANCTIONING PROCEDURE  
From the procedure conducted by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  

BACKGROUND  
FIRST: The data protection authority of Italy (Garante per la Protezione dei Dati Personali) conducted an investigation into the company Foodinho SRL, in which it was discovered that the activities involved in the delivery of food or other items by the couriers (“riders”) with the help of a specific technical platform (used by Foodinho SRL and owned by GLOVOAPP23, S.L.) entailed the processing of a wide range of personal data: geographical location (in real time); monitoring of each step of the delivery through a mobile application downloaded on the courier's mobile; systematic retention of data on communications exchanged with the platform operator via emails, chats, and phone calls; delivery time (both estimated and actual); routes followed; compliance with orders; ratings of the couriers by customers or vendors; reputation scores.  
Additionally, during the aforementioned investigation, there were indications that the data of the couriers making deliveries in Italy could be viewed by operators located in other countries outside of Italy.  

SECOND: Through the “Internal Market Information System” (hereinafter IMI System), regulated by Regulation (EU) No. 1024/2012 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of October 25, 2012 (IMI Regulation), which aims to promote cross-border administrative cooperation, mutual assistance among Member States, and the exchange of information, a notification was sent to this Agency on September 19, 2019, in order to identify the main supervisory authority to investigate the processing activities carried out on the couriers (“riders”) of the Italian company Foodinho SRL, which is engaged in the transport and delivery of food and other goods through a computer platform owned by the company based in Spain GLOVOAPP23, S.A., with Tax Identification Number A66362906, previously GLOVOAPP23, S.L., with Tax Identification Number B66362906 (hereinafter, GLOVOAPP), which is the parent company of the business group of which Foodinho SRL and other companies based in other states within and outside the European Economic Area are subsidiaries.  
For the reasons stated, this Agency was requested to conduct an investigation regarding the data processing of the couriers carried out by GLOVOAPP23, S.L. and its subsidiaries to verify compliance with Article 5.1.a) (lawfulness, fairness, and transparency) in relation to Article 13 (information to be provided to the data subject) and Article 88 (processing in the employment context); Article 5.1.c), 5.1.e), and 5.2 (data minimization, limitation of storage period, and proactive responsibility, respectively); and Article 22 (automated individual decision-making);

FORTY-FIRST: In the “DOCUMENT 19 - Detailed information on the operation of the Jarvis module, used in order assignment,” which accompanies the allegations presented by GLOVOAPP to the initiation agreement of the sanctioning procedure PS/00020/2021, it is indicated:  
“Hi, this is Jarvis  
May 5th, 2020 • 5 min read  
What do we understand by an algorithm?  
An algorithm is a set of defined, non-ambiguous instructions or rules that, applied systematically to appropriate entry data, solve problems automatically in a finite number of pre-set steps.  
At Glovo, the algorithm used to assign orders to couriers is called Jarvis.  
Why is it called Jarvis?  
Jarvis is an artificial intelligence robot that helps the main character in the film Iron Man. It’s extremely intelligent and always gives good advice. When we developed the first version of this algorithm, it was given this name because it fitted well with what it was going to do: help the agents that were previously manually assigning orders to couriers.  
What exactly does the Glovo algorithm do?  
Jarvis is a process carried out every few seconds that looks at all the new orders in the city, all the available couriers, and calculates the optimum courier-order assignment.  
Why are algorithms used by companies? What value do they add?  
The use of the algorithm mainly allows for processes to be automated and made more efficient. If you imagine a switchboard situation, this would be the process it would follow for a similar activity to Glovo’s: receive a call with a client order, identify the business where the product will be picked up, identify the courier who’s closest to both the business and the end client, call the business to let them know about the order, call the courier to let them know the pickup point, call the user to confirm their order and track the order in case of any problems.  
Technology allows the Glovo algorithm to take charge of this whole process and do it efficiently because it calculates which courier will have the shortest journey between point A and point B objectively and immediately. That means that journeys are more efficient and there can be more orders per hour, less waiting time, etc.

What are the areas of knowledge that the algorithm is based on?  
The algorithm is mainly based on two areas of knowledge. The scientific community is currently very interested in both areas, and we always try and stay up to date with new discoveries to see if they could help with the algorithm’s performance.  

What prediction times does the algorithm work with?  
The algorithm is operating 24 hours a day for all the cities that Glovo operates in. In each city, the algorithm runs every few seconds, noting the orders and couriers that are available at that moment.  

How does this algorithm affect the 3 pillars of Glovo: courier, user, business?  
This algorithm is vital for the satisfaction of the 3 pillars of Glovo, because it directly impacts every one of them.  
From the point of view of the courier, a good assignment algorithm allows them to deliver more orders per hour, and so increase their income. The algorithm means they avoid having to follow inefficient routes (very long, a lot of waiting, etc.). It also considers dangerous areas of the city, protests, or inclement weather.  
For the user, the algorithm has a decisive influence on the total delivery time, which is one of the biggest factors in satisfaction. It helps avoid their order spending too long at the store or restaurant, makes sure the courier’s route is direct, etc.  
For businesses, the algorithm should mean that they avoid having a backlog of prepared orders nor waiting couriers. A mistake with the algorithm might mean that prepared orders end up waiting for a courier coming to pick them up. They get cold, and often that the restaurant has to prepare them again, whilst also leading to a poor user experience.  

Does it determine the slots a courier can choose?  
No, the algorithm doesn’t play any role in couriers’ collaboration slots. Roughly one week in advance, all couriers have the chance to choose their slots on the calendar: this is the point when they can decide how many slots they want or hope to do.  

Does the algorithm determine how much the couriers earn?  
Jarvis, as an algorithm, isn’t able to determine anything. It needs the input of a human being. The structure of couriers’ earnings is determined by the operations team. It works like a taxi: there’s a base rate, to which is added an amount for every km covered, and for waiting time. Jarvis simply calculates things based on each journey.  

How does Jarvis choose a certain courier? Is it predetermined?  
Jarvis establishes the optimum courier based on the distance between the pickup point, the delivery point, and their current location. Its main aim is for this journey to be as efficient as possible (short, calculated times). If the courier rejects the order, the procedure starts all over again.

The aforementioned Guidelines state: "Decisions based solely on automated processing represent the ability to make decisions by technological means without human involvement."  
That is to say, the making of an automated decision does not consist of a mere automated process, such as an automatic order dispatch system or goods receipt, but refers to the ability to make decisions by technological means. The use of technology to make decisions that affect individuals and that require the processing of personal data poses a greater risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, as the algorithm may make erroneous, biased, or unfair decisions. Therefore, the GDPR establishes the safeguards that must be adopted in the use of algorithms that make decisions due to the greater risk they entail, among which the controller is imposed a duty of transparency.  
In point IV of the aforementioned Guidelines, it states:  
"Article 22, paragraph 1, refers to decisions 'based solely' on automated processing. This means that there is no human involvement in the decision-making process.  
Example  
An automated process produces what is, in reality, a recommendation regarding the data subject. If a human reviews and takes into account other factors to make the final decision, that decision will not be 'based solely' on automated processing.  
The data controller cannot overlook the provisions of Article 22 by inventing human involvement. For example, if someone routinely applies automatically generated profiles to individuals without this having any real influence on the outcome, this would still be a decision based solely on automated processing.  
To be considered as human involvement, the data controller must ensure that any oversight of the decision is meaningful, rather than merely a symbolic gesture. It must be carried out by an authorized and competent person to modify the decision. As part of the analysis, all relevant data must be taken into account.  
As part of the DPIA, the data controller must identify and record the degree of human involvement in the decision-making process and at what point this occurs."  
In the present case, setting aside considerations about which ordering criterion is the fairest for the couriers, this Agency understands that it is the system that made the decision about the order in which certain couriers were allowed to access a specific time slot for reservation, regardless of the fact that it was GLOVOAPP as the data controller who introduced the necessary parameters into the System for it to make such a decision.  
The decision about the order in which couriers were allowed to access time slots was made by the application, without any human intervention of any kind.  
Human intervention only occurred in those cases where couriers made claims, but if there was no claim, there was no human intervention that modified that decision nor any oversight of such a decision.

As previously indicated, proactive responsibility involves the implementation of a compliance and management model for the GDPR that determines the widespread fulfillment of obligations regarding data protection. It includes the establishment, maintenance, updating, and control of data protection policies within an organization—understood as the set of guidelines governing the actions of an organization, practices, procedures, and tools—from privacy by design and by default, which ensure compliance with the GDPR, prevent the materialization of risks, and allow for the demonstration of compliance.  
It pivots on risk management. As established in Report 0064/2020 from the Legal Department of the AEPD, the metamorphosis of a system that has transitioned from being reactive to becoming proactive is shown, since “at the present time, it must be taken into account that the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in addressing the regulation of the right to personal data protection, which is now based on the principle of ‘accountability’ or ‘proactive responsibility’ as repeatedly pointed out by the AEPD (Report 17/2019, among many others) and is reflected in the Explanatory Memorandum of the LOPDGDD: ‘the greatest novelty presented by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 is the evolution from a model fundamentally based on compliance control to one that rests on the principle of active responsibility, which requires a prior assessment by the data controller or processor of the risk that may be generated by the processing of personal data in order to adopt the necessary measures based on that assessment.’”  
It requires a conscious, committed, active, and diligent attitude. Consciousness implies the knowledge of one’s organization by the data controller and how it is affected by data protection and the inherent risks of personal data processing; commitment involves the willingness to comply and to truly take responsibility for the implementation of data protection policies within the organization; the active attitude is related to proactivity, effectiveness, efficiency, and operability; and diligence is the care, zeal, and dedication put into compliance.  
GLOVO should have taken into account the applicable measures to its access system aimed at complying with the principle of minimization during the design process, which it does not appear to have done, a circumstance that constitutes a violation of Article 25.1 of the GDPR.  
On the other hand, to comply with the principle of data protection by default, the data controller must not collect more data than is necessary, nor carry out a processing of the collected data that is broader than necessary for its purposes, nor retain the data for longer than necessary. The basic requirement is that data protection is integrated into processing by default, as stated in Guidelines 4/2019 regarding Article 25 Data Protection by Design and by Default.

2.- On the infringement of Article 13 of the GDPR for failing to inform about the existence of automated individual decisions  
GLOVOAPP considers that to address this matter, it is necessary to insist, once again, on the difference between an "automated decision" and an "automated execution of a decision." It emphasizes that what exists are only automated processes within the framework of the "Excellence Score" scoring system, but in no case does Glovo make automated individual decisions that need to be reported to the couriers.  
To support this, it cites the definition of the verb "decide" from the Dictionary of the Spanish Language of the Royal Spanish Academy (RAE) and that from the Oxford English Dictionary.  
It is not the purpose of this file nor is it the function of this Agency to conduct a semantic analysis of the verb "decide." However, regarding GLOVOAPP's assertion, some clarifications can be made.  
In this case, it was, in GLOVOAPP's words, a matter of "a problem affecting the couriers themselves: the excess of these during certain time slots, in relation to the number of available orders."  
In response, GLOVOAPP decided to "establish the possibility of setting a maximum limit of couriers per time slot, a limit that could be determined, among other things, based on statistics of previous orders and time slots." And to "determine which couriers could access the selection of time slots within the aforementioned maximum limit in advance, Glovo's product development team decided to use the Excellence Score system as an objective means for such early access."  
As has been evidenced in the present procedure, the "excellence score" is the score assigned to couriers based on, among other things, the following data:  
a. the number of orders made during high-demand time slots in the last 28 days,  
b. the feedback from stores in the last 50 orders, which can be positive, negative, or not rated,  
c. the feedback from users in the last 50 orders, which can be positive, negative, or not rated,  
d. rejected or reassigned orders in the last 50 orders, although, in Spain, reassignment does not penalize,  
e. order history since the beginning of the collaboration.  
C/ Jorge Juan, 6  
www.aepd.es  
28001 – Madrid  
sedeagpd.gob.es
101/135  
This score is calculated automatically, without any human intervention, based on the aforementioned parameters. While it is true that the values used to calculate the excellence score could be modified manually, this would only occur in the event that a delivery person files a complaint, typically through billing.  
Returning to the matter at hand, in order to decide which delivery persons would work during a specific time slot, the procedure followed by GLOVOAPP was as follows:  
1) The maximum number of delivery persons needed for a specific time slot on a given day was calculated (automatically).  
2) All registered delivery persons in a city or operational area of the app were selected (automatically).  
3) These delivery persons were ranked (automatically) according to their Excellence Score (also calculated automatically).  
4) Twice a week (on Mondays and Thursdays), a notification was sent (automatically) to each delivery person informing them that the selection of time slots for certain days of the week was now available (on Mondays, they could reserve time slots for Thursday, Friday, Saturday, and Sunday; and on Thursdays, they could reserve time slots for the following week's Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday), following a descending order based on the previous ranking.  
Of course, at each of these stages, there could be human intervention to correct specific issues (and upon prior complaint), but the reality was that the entire process was carried out automatically, meaning that the decision regarding which delivery persons accessed or did not access a specific time slot and in what order was an automated decision.  
Moreover, this automated decision had legal effects on the interested parties (in this case, the delivery persons) since the possibility of accessing or not accessing a specific time slot directly affected their income.  
Therefore, in the present case, this Agency considers that GLOVOAPP was making automated decisions as referred to in Article 22 of the GDPR, and that this situation should have been communicated to the delivery persons in those terms, and GLOVOAPP violated the provisions of Article 13 of the GDPR by failing to do so.  
For all the above reasons, this allegation is dismissed.

Article 13 of the GDPR establishes the obligation to inform the data subject about the existence of automated decisions and, at least in such cases, to provide significant information about the logic applied, as well as the importance of the anticipated consequences of such processing for the data subject.  
In this case, within the corporate purpose of GLOVOAPP is the management and exploitation of the technological platform, which is a channel of offer involving three parties: end customers, delivery personnel (referred to as "riders"), and local stores.  
This platform had a scoring system for the delivery personnel (referred to as "excellence score"), which was formed through a series of variables, such as the number of orders made during peak demand time slots, the rating given to the delivery personnel by customers and vendors, or the number of rejected or reassigned orders, among others.  
This "excellence score" system would be used exclusively in cases where, during a specific time slot, more delivery personnel had signed up than were needed, to favor the delivery personnel with a better "excellence score." According to GLOVOAPP's own statements, the "excellence score" would limit the possibility of accessing a specific one-hour time slot, but would not affect the rest of the hours of that day.  
That is to say, in the event that more delivery personnel signed up than necessary for a specific time slot, it would be the "excellence score" system that would decide which delivery personnel could work in that slot and which could not.

However, this Agency considers that GLOVOAPP has indeed been carrying out automated individual decisions to order and prioritize the access of delivery personnel to the reservation of a specific time slot, based on the assessment of the "excellence score."  
Although the aforementioned "Privacy Policy for Mandatarios" stated that no automated decisions were made and that all parameters to be considered were generated manually, the reality is that the "excellence score" system was the one making decisions regarding which delivery personnel would work in a specific time slot in case there were more delivery personnel than necessary, and this system made such decisions automatically, without human intervention.  
Given that, in such cases, a delivery personnel could end up without work in a specific time slot, it can be concluded that the use of the "excellence score" system produced legal effects on the data subjects and that GLOVOAPP, through the use of the "excellence score" system, via its technological platform, was making, when necessary, automated decisions as mentioned in Article 22 of the GDPR.  
None of these aspects were communicated by GLOVOAPP to the delivery personnel; rather, the opposite occurred, as only a generic mention was made in the contract regarding the possibility of being evaluated by end users and store vendors, and in the "Privacy Policy for Mandatarios," it was stated that no automated decisions were made, when it has been established that they indeed were.  
However, despite what was stated in the contract signed with the delivery personnel and in the privacy policy for mandatarios, it is true that GLOVOAPP provided information during the pre-contractual phase detailing how the application worked, how the "excellence score" was formed, for what it was used, and also provided advice on how to make the most of it. And it is true that Article 13 of the GDPR does not specify in which documents such information must be provided and does not require the provision of information that is already in the possession of the data subjects.  
In this case, GLOVOAPP made all this information available to the delivery personnel in a tutorial that was mandatory to work as a delivery personnel, and progress in such a course was monitored, and an evaluation test was also conducted to check the knowledge of the delivery personnel.  
However, it remains true that both in the contract and in the privacy policy, GLOVOAPP explicitly stated that no automated decisions were being made, when it was indeed making them.

In this regard, the ruling SAN of October 17, 2007 (rec. 63/2006) is very illustrative, which indicates that “…the Supreme Court has understood that there is negligence whenever a legal duty of care is neglected, that is, when the offender does not behave with the required diligence. And in assessing the degree of diligence, the professionalism or lack thereof of the subject must be especially weighed, and there is no doubt that, in the case now examined, when the activity of the appellant involves constant and abundant handling of personal data, it must be insisted upon the rigor and exquisite care to comply with the legal provisions in this regard."  
As mitigating factors:  
- The technological application used by GLOVOAPP was modified, implementing the system called “city group permissions” and strictly limiting access to platform profiles by country; allowing access solely and exclusively by country and by functions when such access is required for the performance of specific work tasks and depending on the position, thus rendering the “EU User Access” system ineffective and replacing it with country-by-country access and not EU – non-EU, at least from May 18, 2020 (section b).  
It is also considered appropriate to adjust the sanction to be imposed according to the following criteria established in section 3 of Article 76 “Sanctions and corrective measures” of the LOPDGDD:  
- The link between the infringer's activity and the processing of personal data (section b).  
The balance of the circumstances contemplated in Article 83.2 of the GDPR and 76.2 of the LOPDGDD, regarding the infringement committed by violating the provisions of Articles 32 and 25 of the GDPR, allows for the imposition of a sanction of €550,000 (five hundred fifty thousand euros).
