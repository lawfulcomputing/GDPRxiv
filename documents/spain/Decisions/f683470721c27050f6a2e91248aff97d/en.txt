The complainant states that the SCHOOL created an email account ***EMAIL.1 for his daughter, a minor under 14 years of age, without obtaining the consent of the guardians for the creation of said email account or for working with a Classroom profile. On November 20, 2022, he became aware that someone had impersonated his daughter and was posing as her by sending emails from the account ***EMAIL.1 to different people, as well as accessing the content of her profile in Classroom and writing in her name.

The complainant asserts that the SCHOOL has not notified the security breach nor has it given any importance to the matter.

The complainant points out that the privacy policy of the respondent does not contain the contact details of the DPO. However, if the DPO is searched on the website www.aepd.es, the contact for the DPO is ***EMAIL.2, but this email does not exist.

II  
Preliminary Issues  
Article 4 of the GDPR, under the heading "Definitions," provides the following:  
“1) ‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person;  
Consequently, it is evident that a person's email address, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is personal data and its protection is therefore subject to this Regulation.  
In Article 4.2 of the GDPR, the concept of "processing" of personal data is defined.  
2) ‘processing’: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of making available, alignment or combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”  
In subsection seven of the same article of the GDPR, it is stated:  
7) ‘data controller’ or ‘controller’: the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; where the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of processing, the controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided by the law of the Union or of the Member States.”  
In this regard, it should be noted that the SCHOOL is the data controller of the personal data in the specific case at hand, the email address of the claimant's daughter, as it is the entity that determines the purposes and means of processing the personal data.

Article 32 of the GDPR  
Article 32 “Security of processing” of the GDPR establishes:

“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying probability and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d) a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
2. In assessing the appropriateness of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the processing of data, in particular as a result of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data.  
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this article.  
4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data only processes such data on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law.”  
Thus, this provision requires the adoption of security measures, which may be of a technical or organizational nature, and which must be appropriate to the risk of the processing of personal data carried out. Conversely, this provision would be violated if such measures were not adopted, which, appropriate to the risk and in general, would guarantee the security of the processing.  
The obligation to adopt all technical and organizational measures imposed by Article 32 of the GDPR on the data controller is a manifestation of the two essential pillars of the GDPR: proactive responsibility enshrined in Article 5.2 and developed in Article 24.1 of the GDPR and the risk-based approach. Thus, Article 32, while establishing a set of measures that could be considered minimal to be adopted by the controller, cannot in any case be understood as a numerus clausus since these must address the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of probability and severity affecting personal data.  
As can be seen, there has been an evolution from a reactive system to a proactive one, based on prevention and not on responding to a specific incident that warranted it.

must be corrected, and it must operate throughout the entire lifecycle of personal data.  
In this regard, Recital 74 of the GDPR states (the emphasis is from the AEPD):  
“74 The responsibility of the data controller for any processing of personal data carried out by themselves or on their behalf must be established. In particular, the controller must be obliged to implement appropriate and effective measures and must be able to demonstrate compliance of the processing activities with this Regulation, including the effectiveness of the measures. Such measures must take into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.”  
For its part, the Supreme Court ruling of February 15, 2022 (Rec. 7359/2020) indicates that “It is not enough to design the necessary technical and organizational means; their correct implementation and appropriate use are also necessary, so that they will also be liable for the lack of diligence in their use, understood as reasonable diligence considering the circumstances of the case.”  
Furthermore, the CJEU in its ruling of December 14, 2023, in case C-340/21 has stated regarding “whether the principle of responsibility of the data controller, as set out in Article 5, paragraph 2, of the GDPR and developed in Article 24 of the same, should be interpreted to mean that... the data controller bears the burden of proof of the appropriateness of the security measures it has adopted in accordance with Article 32 of the aforementioned Regulation,” concluding that “From the wording of Articles 5, paragraph 2, 24, paragraph 1, and 32, paragraph 1, of the GDPR, it is unequivocally clear that the burden of proof that personal data is processed in a manner that ensures adequate security, in the sense of Articles 5, paragraph 1, letter f), and 32 of that Regulation, rests with the data controller in question [see, by analogy, the judgments of May 4, 2023, Bundesrepublik Deutschland (Judicial electronic mailbox), C-60/22, EU: C:2023:373, paragraphs 52 and 53, and of July 4, 2023, Meta Platforms and others (General terms of service of a social network), C-252/21, EU:C:2023:537, paragraph 95].” (paragraph 52)  
This CJEU ruling also indicates the following:  
“[the adoption of security measures regarding the protection of personal data] is not fulfilled by the adoption of any measure, as they must be necessary to guarantee those objectives set by the provision, and of course, it is not enough to have formal approval of the security measures, as it is required that they be established and effectively implemented. We have consequently considered that a result obligation is imposed, consisting of adopting the necessary measures to prevent data from being lost, misplaced, or ending up in the hands of third parties. Ultimately, every data controller (or processor) is, by legal provision, a debtor of security regarding data, and must ensure that such measures or mechanisms are effectively implemented in practice.”
