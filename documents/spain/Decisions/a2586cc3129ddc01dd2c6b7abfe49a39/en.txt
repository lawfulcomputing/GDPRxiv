RESOLUTION OF RECONSIDERATION APPEAL  
Examined the reconsideration appeal filed by A.A.A. (hereinafter, the appellant) against the resolution issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection dated May 4, 2023, and based on the following  

FACTS  
FIRST: On May 4, 2023, a resolution was issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection in file EXP202208325, by virtue of which a fine of 300 euros (THREE HUNDRED euros) was imposed on A.A.A. for an infringement of Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.5 of the GDPR.  
This resolution, which was notified to the appellant on May 12, 2023, was issued following the processing of the corresponding sanctioning procedure, in accordance with the provisions of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (LOPDGDD), and subsidiarily under Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), regarding the processing of sanctioning procedures.  

SECOND: As proven facts of the aforementioned sanctioning procedure, PS/00610/2022, the following was recorded:  
“FIRST: It is recorded, according to documentation provided by the complainant, that the respondent has installed four surveillance cameras in their home that, due to their location and orientation, are capable of capturing images of the complainant's home.”  

THIRD: The appellant submitted a reconsideration appeal on May 31, 2023, to this Spanish Agency for Data Protection, basing it fundamentally on:  
“That a resolution has been notified to them in File 202208325, dated May 4, 2023, by which a sanction of 300 Euros is imposed on this party due to the alleged infringement of Article 5.1 c) of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.5 of the same regulation, also ordering the removal of the cameras installed by this party or their reorientation so that in the viewing of the images observed, common areas of the home, the adjoining home, and public thoroughfare are not captured.

ALLEGATIONS:  
FIRST.- The Administrative File referenced in the heading of this document arises from the complaint filed by B.B.B. regarding a video surveillance system installed by this party at ***ADDRESS.1.  
Accepting the claims of B.B.B., the Spanish Agency for Data Protection considers it proven that the video surveillance system in question, which consists of 4 cameras, is capable of capturing images of the claimant's residence.  
As a result, a fine of 300 Euros is imposed on the appellant for allegedly violating Article 5.1 c) of the GDPR, with the facts being subsumed under the infringement typified in Article 83.5 of the same regulation.  
SECOND.- It is inferred in the resolution that this party, upon receiving the transfer of the complaint, did not present any allegations. It is stated that the notification occurred on August 4, 2022.  
However, this party has not been aware of such notification. Nor has the transfer been sent to them along with the resolution that is now being appealed.  
We understand that in this way, the right to defense and the right to be informed of the accusation recognized in Article 24.2 of the Spanish Constitution (CE) have been violated. As a consequence of the Administration's actions, this party was indeed unable to express their disagreement with the complaint raised against them.  
In this regard, we bring to attention the Constitutional Court ruling STC 84/2022, of June 27, in which the Constitutional Court clarified that if the activity carried out by the administration prevents the appellant from being aware of the act and, therefore, from defending themselves, a clear violation of the right to defense and the right to be informed of the accusation recognized in Article 24.2 CE is generated.  
The Chamber states that: “…we consider that the activity carried out by the administration has not respected the right to defense and the right to be informed of the accusation recognized in Article 24.2 CE. Blaming the claimant for not being aware of the designated electronic address, the content of the request, and the other communications made electronically, based on the way they traced the letter “v” when writing their email address in the declaration made on December 16, 2016, implies attributing to this circumstance manifestly disproportionate consequences, given the adverse effects produced in the sanctioning procedure. In the opinion of this court, the fact that they wrote that letter without total calligraphic precision does not constitute a situation comparable to cases where, according to our doctrine, the reported injury is to be attributed to the conduct of the affected party due to their inexperience, negligence, or carelessness. It is worth noting that, in practice, it is not uncommon for the handwriting of certain letters not to conform exactly to their orthodox format, in addition to the relevance of the subjective valuation component that leads a third party to interpret which letter has actually been transcribed. Aside from the above, in the present case, it should be noted that the first letters of the correct email address —javn— coincide with the initials of the claimant's full name and both surnames, a fact that the administration was aware of.

In light of the aforementioned provisions, it is clear that the lack of notification and the consequent omission of the hearing procedure violates the rights of the interested party in the proceedings, and the resolution must be considered null and void by law.  
On this matter, the Supreme Court has already ruled in its judgment STS of May 18, 2020, stating that the omission of the hearing procedure in the sanctioning context is of such relevance that it leads to the absolute nullity of the actions taken. Indeed, not only is the right to defense of the interested party violated, but the legally established procedure is also disregarded.  
In this regard, it is important to emphasize that by virtue of Article 47 of the LPAC, the following are null and void by law:  
“1. The acts of the Public Administrations are null and void in the following cases:  
a) Those that violate rights and freedoms that are subject to constitutional protection.  
[…]  
e) Those issued disregarding totally and absolutely the legally established procedure or the norms that contain the essential rules for the formation of the will of collegiate bodies.”  
In a subsidiary manner, it should be highlighted that Article 48 of Law 39/2015 defines as voidable acts those that incur any infringement of the legal order, including abuse of power. We therefore understand that the resolution is null and void by law or, subsidiarily, voidable, and must be revoked and rendered ineffective.  
THIRD.- In any case, in a subsidiary manner in case the previous argument is dismissed. It is necessary to make some clarifications regarding the substance of the resolution that is now being challenged.

“c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’).”

a) the basic principles for processing, including the conditions for consent under Articles 5, 6, 7, and 9;”

FOURTH.- It is worth recalling that the sanctioning power of the Administration constitutes a manifestation of the State's ius puniendi recognized in Article 25 of the Spanish Constitution.  
It is peacefully accepted by Doctrine and Jurisprudence the necessity, repeatedly proclaimed by the Constitutional Court, to apply to such power the guiding principles and guarantees of Criminal Law.  
Thus, the constitutional Jurisprudence has reiterated the application of the principles and guarantees derived from Article 25 of the Spanish Constitution applicable to criminal proceedings, specifically, legality, typicity, non-retroactivity, culpability, proportionality, and non bis in idem.  
In the same way, the full application of the rights and guarantees of Article 24 of the Spanish Constitution has been declared, especially the right to the presumption of innocence and the prohibition of defenselessness. One of the cardinal principles of contemporary Criminal Law -both substantive and procedural- is the one that proclaims the presumption of innocence of any person accused of an infringement until their guilt has been legally declared. This principle, incorporated in a preferred position in Article 24 of the Spanish Constitution, produces an immediate procedural consequence that consists of shifting the burden of proof to the accuser and, in the case of the sanctioning power, to the Administration since, as the Constitutional Court has repeatedly pointed out, although Article 24 of the Spanish Constitution does not expressly refer to the exercise of the sanctioning power of the Administration, the procedural guarantees established in that provision are applicable in sanctioning Administrative Procedures.  
This implies, of course, that in order to impose a sanction, it will be necessary to substantiate the factual assumption to which the legal consequence that the Administration intends to impose is linked. Therefore, since there is no fact that can be classified as an infringement, there can be no sanction, and this procedure should be archived.  
It is emphasized that the Administration does not have a license to decide ad nutum, as its actions are closely connected to the public interest that must be satisfied.  
The prohibition of arbitrariness by public authorities is one of the supra-principles of law enshrined in Article 9.3 of our Constitution and which, in the administrative sphere, is regulated in Article 37 of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on Common Administrative Procedure, which states:  
“1. Administrative resolutions of a particular nature may not violate what is established in a general provision, even if they originate from an organ of equal or higher hierarchy than that which issued the general provision.

to the Administration. In what is now of interest, it has not been demonstrated that the video surveillance system installed by the appellant constitutes an undue intrusion into the privacy of the claimant, being adequate and in accordance with current legislation.  
In this regard, this Agency recalls, as has already been indicated, that the physical image of a person, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is a personal data and its protection is therefore subject to this Regulation. Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines the concept of "processing" of personal data.  
“‘processing’: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of access, comparison or interconnection, restriction, erasure or destruction;”  
The images generated by a system of cameras or video cameras are personal data, so their processing is subject to data protection regulations.  
In turn, Article 24 of the GDPR states:  
"Taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and be able to demonstrate that the processing is carried out in accordance with this Regulation. Such measures shall be reviewed and updated when necessary."  
*(the underlining corresponds to the AEPD)*  
In the present case, the appellant has not demonstrated, either during the instruction of the sanctioning procedure, during which no allegations were presented nor evidence proposed, nor in the documentation submitted with the appeal, that the field of vision of the installed cameras affects solely and exclusively their own dwelling, which means that the claim presented to this Agency by the claimant cannot be considered unfounded, nor can it be considered that the principle of presumption of innocence is being violated, given that, according to the aforementioned Article 24 of the GDPR, it has not been demonstrated that the data processing carried out by the cameras for which they are responsible is in accordance with that Regulation.  
Apart from the fact that the respondent has not succeeded in proving that the processing was not excessive, there is sufficient evidence in the file from the outset, namely the images provided by the claimant, which in our judgment substantiate the excess in processing by the appellant.
