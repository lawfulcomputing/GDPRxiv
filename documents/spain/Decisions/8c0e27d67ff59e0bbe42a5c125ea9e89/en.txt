In the event that this Agency understands that Vodafone has indeed violated Article 6.1 of the GDPR, no sanction should be imposed on my client since Vodafone has not acted culpably, and therefore, no sanction should be imposed. Article 28.1 of Law 40/2015, of October 1, on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector, regulates the principle of culpability, under which it is necessary for the alleged offender to be responsible for the infringement committed. Thus, said article provides that: "Only individuals and legal entities, as well as, when a Law recognizes them the capacity to act, groups of affected parties, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous assets, that are found responsible for the same by way of intent or negligence, may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative infringement."  
Therefore, in the event that the Agency understands that Vodafone's conduct constitutes the infringement indicated in the Initiation Agreement, it is clear that there is no culpability in Vodafone's conduct, neither by way of intent nor by way of negligence.  
Thus, taking into account the special nature of sanctioning administrative law that determines the impossibility of imposing sanctions without considering culpability and diligence, or the errors that may have led to the breach of a legal obligation, this party maintains the impropriety of imposing any sanction.

On the other hand, the principle of proactivity implies transferring to the data controller the obligation not only to comply with the regulations but also to be able to demonstrate compliance. It is stated that “it must be considered that the ordinary duty of cooperation is mandatory and, therefore, must be considered neutral (and not a mitigating factor).” This is confirmed in the same Guidelines of the EDPB on the application and imposition of administrative fines for the purposes of Regulation 2016/679, adopted on October 3, 2017, which asserts that “That said, it would not be appropriate to additionally take into account the cooperation required by law; for example, in any case, the entity is required to allow the supervisory authority access to the facilities to conduct audits or inspections.”  
Regarding the application of Article 76.2.c) of the LOPDGDD, in connection with Article 83.2.k), regarding the non-existence of benefits obtained, it should be noted that such circumstance can only operate as an aggravating factor and in no case as a mitigating circumstance. Article 83.2.k) of the GDPR refers to “any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as financial benefits obtained or losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement.” And Article 76.2.c) of the LOPDGDD states that “2. In accordance with the provisions of Article 83.2.k) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following may also be taken into account: [...] c) The benefits obtained as a result of the commission of the infringement.” Both provisions mention as a factor that may be considered in the assessment of the sanction the “benefits” obtained, but not the “absence” of these, which is what Vodafone claims.  
Furthermore, according to Article 83.1 of the GDPR, the imposition of fines is governed by the following principles: they must be individualized for each particular case, be effective, proportionate, and dissuasive. The admission that the absence of benefits operates as a mitigating factor is contrary to the spirit of Article 83.1 of the GDPR and to the principles governing the determination of the amount of the fine. If, as a result of the commission of a GDPR infringement, it is classified as a mitigating factor that no benefits have existed, the dissuasive purpose fulfilled through the sanction is partially nullified. Accepting Vodafone's thesis in a case like the one at hand would imply introducing an artificial reduction in the sanction that truly should be imposed; the one that results from considering the circumstances of Article 83.2 GDPR that must indeed be assessed.  
The Administrative Litigation Chamber of the National Court has warned that the fact that in a specific case not all the elements that make up a modifying circumstance of liability, which by its nature has an aggravating character, are present, cannot lead to the conclusion that such circumstance is applicable as a mitigating factor.
