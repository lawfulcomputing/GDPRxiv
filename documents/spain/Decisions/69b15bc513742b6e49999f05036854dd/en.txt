Certainly, the principle of responsibility provided in Article 28 of the LRJSP states: “Only individuals and legal entities may be sanctioned for acts constituting an administrative infraction, as well as, when a law recognizes their capacity to act, the groups of affected individuals, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous estates, that are found responsible for them due to intent or negligence.”  
However, the way responsibility is attributed to legal entities does not correspond to the forms of intentional or negligent culpability that are attributable to human conduct. Thus, in the case of infractions committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present, it necessarily applies differently than it does with individuals.  
According to STC 246/1991, “(...) this different construction of the attribution of the authorship of the infraction to the legal entity arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the rules to which they are subject. Capacity for infringement and, therefore, direct reproachability that derives from the legal good protected by the norm that is infringed and the necessity for that protection to be genuinely effective and the risk that, consequently, the legal entity subject to compliance with that norm must assume” (in this sense, STS of November 24, 2011, Rec 258/2009).  
To the above, it should be added, following the ruling of January 23, 1998, partially transcribed in the SSTS of October 9, 2009, Rec 5285/2005, and October 23, 2010, Rec 1067/2006, that “although the culpability of the conduct must also be proven, it should be considered in terms of the assumption of the corresponding burden, that ordinarily the volitional and cognitive elements necessary to appreciate it form part of the proven typical conduct, and that their exclusion requires evidence of the absence of such elements, or in its normative aspect, that the diligence required by the one claiming its non-existence has been exercised; in summary, the mere invocation of the absence of fault is not sufficient for exculpation against typically unlawful behavior.”

III  
Unfulfilled Obligation  
Well, the defendant is accused of committing an infringement for violating Article 6 of the GDPR, "Lawfulness of processing," which states in its paragraph 1 the cases in which the processing of third-party data is considered lawful:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child. The provision in point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”  
The issuance of a duplicate SIM card necessarily involves the processing of personal data, as it is carried out on the line and the identity of the holder. Such processing must comply with a legal basis that legitimizes it, as required by the aforementioned Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
In this case, the issuance of the duplicate SIM at the request of a third party without the intervention or consent of the holder constitutes a lack of valid legal basis for such data processing. Although DIGI claims to follow its own protocols, this is not sufficient to justify the lawfulness of the processing under the GDPR, as such protocols do not replace the need for the existence of a legitimate basis.

The identity verification procedure described by DIGI, which is based solely on the matching of certain data in its database, does not adequately guarantee compliance with the GDPR in that:  
- It does not demonstrate the effective involvement of the data subject in the process.  
- The mere matching of data does not ensure consent or any other legal basis under Article 6.1 that legitimizes the processing.  
Therefore, the issuance of the duplicate SIM under these conditions constitutes processing of personal data without a legal basis and, consequently, an infringement of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, since it has been carried out without complying with any of the legitimizing bases for such processing. The responsibility does not lie in the failure to comply with an internal protocol of DIGI, but in the absence of a valid legal basis in accordance with the provisions of the GDPR.  
In accordance with the evidence available, it is estimated that the conduct of the defendant violates Article 6.1 of the GDPR, which constitutes an infringement classified under Article 83.5.a) of the aforementioned Regulation 2016/679.  
In this regard, Recital 40 of the GDPR states:  
“(40) In order for processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on another legitimate basis established by law, whether in this Regulation or under another law of the Union or of the Member States to which this Regulation relates, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject or the necessity of performing a contract to which the data subject is a party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.”  
For the reasons stated, it is considered that DIGI has violated Article 6.1 of the GDPR.

Article 6.1 establishes that the processing of personal data is only lawful if at least one of the conditions set forth in the aforementioned article is met. In this case, the violation of the principle of legality is manifested in the improper processing of the personal data of the defendant, who was not the actual requester of the duplicate card.  
This type of procedure requires adequately verifying the identity of the requester, and for this, it is necessary to establish measures that ensure that correct identification. The establishment of these measures or protocols, as well as their monitoring and attention by the responsible entity, serves to assess the conduct and degree of diligence employed by it, but these are not the determining facts of the infringement.

Certainly, the principle of responsibility provided in Article 28 of the LRJSP states: “Only individuals and legal entities may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative infractions, as well as, when a law recognizes their capacity to act, affected groups, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous estates, that are deemed responsible for them due to intent or negligence.”  
However, the manner of attributing responsibility to legal entities does not correspond to the forms of culpability that are attributable to human conduct. Thus, in the case of infractions committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present, it necessarily applies differently than it does with individuals.  
According to STC 246/1991, of December 19, Rec 1274/1988 “(...) this distinct construction of the attribution of authorship of the infraction to the legal entity arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the rules to which they are subject.  
The capacity to infringe and, therefore, the direct reproachability that derives from the legal good protected by the rule that is infringed and the necessity for such protection to be truly effective and the risk that, consequently, the legal entity subject to compliance with that rule must assume” (in this sense, STS of November 24, 2011, Rec 258/2009).  
To the above, it should be added, following the judgment of January 23, 1998, partially transcribed in the STS of October 9, 2009, Rec 5285/2005, and of October 23, 2010, Rec 1067/2006, that “although the culpability of the conduct must also be subject to proof, it should be considered in relation to the assumption of the corresponding burden, that ordinarily the volitional and cognitive elements necessary to appreciate it are part of the proven typical conduct, and that their exclusion requires evidence of the absence of such elements, or in its normative aspect, that due diligence has been exercised by the one who claims its non-existence; in short, the mere invocation of the absence of fault is not sufficient for exculpation against typically unlawful behavior.”
