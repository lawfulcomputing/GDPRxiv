express possibility of delegation by which DEH intervenes in the process of issuing certificates.  
This delegation is twofold: the Certification Authority delegates some of its powers to DEH for the purpose of verifying the identity of applicants so that it acts as the Registration Authority, and DEH, as expressly permitted by its agreements with the Certification Authorities it works with, in turn delegates part of these powers to the so-called In-Person Verification Points (PVP).  
This double delegation allows applicants for digital certificates, distributed throughout the national territory, to obtain such certificates without the need to travel to the headquarters of the Certification Authority, which is the entity that will issue the requested certificate.  
Additional precision: the process that will be described below does not apply to all issuances of digital certificates. Some of them are issued through the video identification process, regulated by Order ETD/465/2021, of May 6, which regulates remote identification methods by video for the issuance of qualified electronic certificates, in which DEH does not intervene as the Registration Authority, because the very nature of the video identification system does not make it necessary.  
However, the process of issuing digital certificates, in most cases, follows the requirements generally imposed by Article 7.1 of Law 6/2020, of November 11, regulating certain aspects of electronic trust services:  
- The PVP receives the request for the issuance of a digital certificate from the interested party and must verify the identity of the applicant in person by presenting their ID card or another valid document proving their identity that is in force.  
- The PVP fills out and signs a document, called IDT, through which it conveys this request to DEH and states that it has verified the identity of the applicant in person, sending it to DEH along with a copy of the applicant's ID card (or another valid identification document that is in force).  
- Upon receiving this IDT, DEH, acting as the Registration Authority of the Certification Authority, checks the validity of the received documentation.  
- Once this verification is completed and passed, DEH sends the request to the Certification Authority for the issuance of the digital certificate to proceed.  
- The Certification Authority, after conducting its own checks regarding the received request, proceeds to issue the digital certificate and provides the applicant with a link so that, only once, the applicant can download the obtained certificate.  
The process just described is formalized through three distinct documents:  
- The IDT signed by the PVP.

II  
Unfulfilled Obligation  
The responding party is accused of committing two violations for breaching Articles 6.1 and 32 of the GDPR.  
Article 6 of the GDPR, under the heading "Lawfulness of processing," details in its paragraph 1 the circumstances under which data processing is considered lawful:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if it meets at least one of the following conditions:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the implementation of pre-contractual measures at the request of the data subject;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.  
The provision in point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”  
In turn, Article 6.1 of the LOPDGDD states, regarding the processing of personal data based on the consent of the affected party, that: “1. In accordance with the provisions of Article 4.11 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, consent of the affected party is understood as any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of their wishes by which they signify agreement, either by a statement or a clear affirmative action, to the processing of personal data concerning them (...).”  
For its part, the security of processing, as set out in Article 32 of the GDPR, establishes that:  
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the varying risks of likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data in a timely manner in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d) a process for regularly testing, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
2. In assessing the appropriateness of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the processing of data, in particular as a result of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized disclosure of or access to such data.  
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this Article.  
4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data is only able to process such data on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law.”  
Recital 74 of the GDPR states:  
“The controller shall be responsible for any processing of personal data carried out by them or on their behalf. In particular, the controller shall be obliged to implement appropriate and effective measures and to be able to demonstrate compliance of processing activities with this Regulation, including the effectiveness of the measures. Such measures shall take into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.”  
It should be noted that the GDPR in the cited provision does not establish a list of security measures that are applicable according to the data being processed, but rather establishes that the controller and the processor shall implement technical and organizational measures that are appropriate to the risk posed by the processing, taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, and the risks of likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects.

It is noteworthy that in the Clarification Order of the AN (Contentious Chamber) dated 22/12/2015, complementing the SAN of 19/10/2015 (Rec. 423/2014), it is indicated that: "The doctrine established repeatedly by the Supreme Court, Second Chamber, regarding the issue of provoked crime is applicable in this respect, considering that the principles governing criminal proceedings are applicable to the sanctioning procedure for administrative infractions."  
Thus, the ruling of the Supreme Court dated October 5, 2004, and in relation to those others mentioned therein, establishes the doctrine that, in the crime provoked by the agents themselves, there is actually no crime but merely the appearance of one, although it is different when the criminal conduct is being committed, generally in a continuous manner, and the agents only provide evidence of the existence of a pre-existing crime.  
The actions of the private detective will always be framed in those cases where the aim is to discover already existing situations. When there are well-founded suspicions of the commission of the reprehensible act, the services of the detective will be sought to ascertain the facts and obtain the necessary evidence for its submission to the judicial process.  
Consequently, the same limits and requirements that operate in the criminal sphere for the figure of the provocateur must be taken into account by private detectives in their actions.  
The objective or purpose of the investigator is precisely to discover already existing illicit situations. Therefore, it is not intended to provoke the commission of the punishable act, but to uncover that which is already occurring. Thus, the conduct of the provoked subject should indeed be deserving of reproach when the actions of the intervening third party have been carried out in order to uncover a transgression that is known to have been committed or is in the process of being committed.  
Numerous jurisprudences have accepted the actions of the private detective when they have been limited to interacting impartially, without any maneuver that would guide the conduct of the investigated party towards those interested actions, and without influencing their intention or subjective state. the Judgment of the Superior Court of Justice of Aragon, STSJ AR 606/2018, of May 16, in which the TSJ declares that the simulation by the detective to pose as a client of the investigated party who was conducting concurrent activities on his own account “Was not an induced proof. The detective did not provoke an unlawful act by the plaintiff, but was merely another user of his medical services, advertised on the internet and that he had been providing previously, limiting himself to verifying how the actor was medically attending to the patients who requested him in the aforementioned public consultation. His intervention allowed for the evidence that the plaintiff was violating the non-competition agreement that prohibited the plaintiff from providing aesthetic surgery services to other individuals or legal entities during the term of his contract with the defendant company.” Therefore, this judgment highlights the objective nature of the detective's intervention, who did not provoke the unlawful act of the investigated party, but simply limited himself to obtaining evidence of such practices.  

Another example can be found in the judgment of the Provincial Court of Madrid, SAP Madrid 655/2016, of November 29, in which a software manufacturing company hires the services of a private detective to demonstrate the sale of unlicensed software in a computer sales store. The private detective simulates being a customer who purchases a computer, and it is the store's own seller who offers to install the program without the relevant exploitation license. The court confirms that we are not dealing with a provoked crime, as the author does not generate the will to commit the prohibited act, but rather it is about obtaining evidence of the commission of a crime by a subject who already had the intent to commit a crime and who had already been engaging in such conduct prior to the investigator's intervention.  

This same criterion is applied by the TSJ Castilla-La Mancha in its STSJ CLM 1437/2019, of June 3, 2019, in which it denies that the detective's action constituted a “deception,” and applies the doctrine established by the TS regarding provocation: “Regarding the fact that on one occasion the worker was willing to provide a repair service at the request of one of the detectives, we cannot endorse the instance's criterion of denying any validity to such an event by labeling the investigative action as ‘deception.’ On the contrary, no reproach can be made to such action, even applying to the case due to its evident similarity the criminal doctrine of the provoked crime, according to which the consequences of the act should only be dismissed if they were provoked by the investigating agent in such a way that without their intervention the fact would not have occurred.”  

The TSJ concludes that the detective's action did not provoke the commission of the illicit act, but simply limited itself to verifying its execution: “Applying the previous doctrine to the case at hand, it turns out that the detective did not provoke any conduct that the worker would not have performed otherwise, but simply posed a demand for services that the interested party himself offered on the internet as a professional and had been performing in the terms already described. Nothing was provoked; rather, a simple verification action was carried out, to which the interested party did not state that he was not engaged in such activity, but rather that he ‘did not know when he could come by,’ adjusting the appointment after a new call.”

The detective's role has simply served to bring to light, without any intimidation, and with the aim of demonstrating a previously existing behavior; we would not be facing an inadmissible situation regarding individual free determination that affects the validity of the evidence.  
Equating the case of the detective to that of the provocateur, and following the same doctrine established by the Supreme Court, there would be no reproach against the actions of Private Detectives when they operate with the aim of discovering breaches that have already been committed - or are in the process of being committed - because in such cases the detective does not seek to provoke the commission of the sanctionable act, but rather to expose it and obtain evidence of an activity about which there are already well-founded suspicions.  
In the present case, the intervention of the detective is decisive for the commission of the administrative infraction (it provokes it), and not only for its verification (it does not result from the detective's actions that it is pre-existing). Therefore, the doctrine of provocation is applicable, and consequently, the procedure should be archived in relation to Article 6.1 of the GDPR due to the lack of an infringing act as a result of provocation, illicit obtaining of evidence, etc. This would be considered an "irrelevant" action from the perspective of sanctioning administrative law.
