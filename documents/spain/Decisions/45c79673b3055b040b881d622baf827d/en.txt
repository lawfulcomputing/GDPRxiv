RESOLUTION OF SANCTIONING PROCEDURE  
From the procedure instructed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  

BACKGROUND  
FIRST: A.A.A. (hereinafter, the claimant) filed a complaint with the Spanish Agency for Data Protection on February 4, 2024. The complaint is directed against INSTITUTO VALENCIANO DE SERVICIOS SOCIALES (IVASS) with NIF Q4601042G (hereinafter, the respondent/IVASS). The reasons for the complaint are as follows:  
The claimant states the following: “At IVASS, fingerprints are taken for clocking in, and I submitted a request to have my fingerprint removed as it is a biometric data, and I preferred to sign in on paper with a pen. I presented the request to the on-duty manager and the center's management, and they denied it orally, with no written response.”  
Along with the notification, a photograph of a form titled “internal regime note” from IVASS, signed by the claimant, dated 13-12-23, is provided, in which the suppression of his fingerprint is requested and a change to a paper and pen clocking system or any other means of attendance control that does not involve the processing of biometric data.  

SECOND: In accordance with Article 65.4 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter LOPDGDD), on February 23, 2024, this complaint was forwarded to the Vice-Presidency and Department of Equality and Inclusive Policies of the Generalitat Valenciana, to which IVASS is attached, according to the evidence obtained by the General Subdirectorate of Data Inspection, for analysis and to inform this Agency within one month of the actions taken to comply with the requirements set forth in data protection regulations.  
The transfer, which was carried out in accordance with the rules established in Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), was acknowledged on 27/02/2024, as evidenced by the receipt in the file.  

THIRD: On March 4, 2024, a first written response from the respondent, IVASS, was received by this Agency, in which it is stated, in summary, the following:  
- IVASS does not have a system for monitoring the working hours of employees that stores biometric data, but rather a numerical code obtained from the fingerprint taken from the employee, which is not stored.  
- Following the recommendations issued by a report from ***COMPANY.1, dated January 19, 2024, regarding the use of biometrics after the publication of the new Guide from the Spanish Agency for Data Protection on the processing of attendance control through biometric systems (attached as Document 1), the respondent contracted with ***COMPANY.2 (hereinafter, ***COMPANY.2) for the operation of the clocking control application, which allows the identification of IVASS staff through fingerprinting.  
- According to the informational note from the ***PLATFORM.1 platform, which is attached as Document 2, provided to employees from the application, the following is stated:  
“***COMPANY.2 terminals do not store an image of employees' fingerprints, only a multi-digit number generated from the fingerprint. It is completely impossible to create a photograph or retrieve an employee's fingerprint from that number. Practically, this means that each time you place your fingerprint on the reader, the reader generates a number. The program then compares whether this number corresponds to an employee or not – just as it would with a card. Please note that this number is not any confidential information, nor can it be used to obtain the fingerprint.”  
- Therefore, the request made by the claimant on December 13, 2023, for the deletion of his fingerprint from the computer system used for clocking in cannot be accommodated.  
The respondent did not attach documents 1 and 2 mentioned in its written response.

LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence and procedure  
In accordance with the powers granted by Article 58.2 of the GDPR and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and the Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Presidency of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states that: "The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, in so far as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures."  
II  
Preliminary issues regarding the processing of biometric data  
2.1. Definition and characteristics of biometric data.  
The GDPR defines in Article 4.14 biometric data as “personal data obtained through a specific technical processing, related to the physical, physiological or behavioral characteristics of a natural person which allow or confirm the unique identification of that person, such as facial images or fingerprint data.”  
Any biometric system for controlling working hours must first register the identity of the user in the system by capturing a series of biometric parameters. The aim is to process those parameters to identify the person each time they enter and exit through the access point. Processing that starts from an image of the fingerprint, which is converted into what is called a biometric template or fingerprint vector, which is stored in the system.  
Biometric data processing systems are based on collecting and processing personal data related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of natural persons, among which the neural characteristics of these may be included, through devices or sensors, creating biometric templates (also referred to as signatures or patterns) that enable the identification, tracking, or profiling of such individuals.  
As the report of the DPD of the GVA dated March 11, 2024, submitted to this procedure rightly points out, the respondent starts from a misinterpretation of the content of the informative note of the application used for clocking in, since the fact that biometric systems are translated into a numerical code does not imply that biometric data is not being stored or processed.  
The processing of data carried out through biometric systems was explained in Convention 108+ for the protection of individuals with regard to the automated processing of personal data approved by the Council of Europe, which since January 1981 has stated in its paragraph 60 the following, as noted in the Guide on presence control treatments using biometric systems published in November 2023:  
“A biometric data contained in a system is stored in the form of a biometric template or pattern. A biometric template is a representation of a human biometric characteristic, such as a face or a fingerprint, in such a way that it can be efficiently and effectively interpreted by a machine for a specific purpose or purposes. The biometric template is not intended to be interpreted by a person, like a photograph, but is intended to be processed in an automated process, that is, to be efficiently and effectively interpretable by a machine. This form of storage would allow for the singularization of an individual and the execution of actions automatically, profiling or inferring information about a subject such as attitudes or behavior patterns, etc.  
In the case of identification or authentication operations, for a biometric template to be effective, it is necessary that the templates generated from two distinct individuals are clearly distinguishable. In that case, the template acts as a unique identifier of the person. The fact that, from a biometric template, for example, of facial recognition, it does not...

Consider just some of their special characteristics and think about the significant impact they produce when these biometric data are compromised, compared to other methods (signature and completion of sheets, or clocking in with magnetic cards):

- Each individual has unique fingerprints that show specific characteristics that can be measured to determine if a fingerprint matches a registered sample. Therefore, they are unique, permanent, or definitive over time, and the person cannot free themselves from them; they can never be changed, not even with age, so the damage caused in the event of compromise, loss, or intrusion into the system is irreparable in this case. Unlike a password, in the event of loss, the data of our fingerprint or face cannot be changed.

- Furthermore, because biometric data are inherent to a person and perpetual, the user can use the same data across different systems, which poses an additional risk.

- While traditional authentication methods such as passwords require a 100% character-by-character match to allow the user to access, for example, an account or application (deterministic methods), biometric methods are referred to as "probabilistic," because they are based on the probability that the user attempting to access a particular device or application is the same person as the registered user. We can measure the performance of a biometric system based on three main characteristics. These are: false rejection rate (FRR), false acceptance rate (FAR), and equal error rate (ERR). The false rejection rate represents the probability of detection errors by a biometric system, meaning that it cannot recognize a user whose biometric characteristics are already in the database. In the event of a rejection, the person must verify their identity again. From a security and protection perspective, this rate does not necessarily mean that it is a negative outcome. Each biometric method, whether facial reading, fingerprint, palm print, iris, etc., has different values for different rates based on which a system rejects or accepts entries.

2.2. Biometric templates as special category personal data and high risk.  
According to the definition provided by Article 4.14 of the GDPR, biometric data processed by these systems is considered personal data as long as the purpose of the processing is the identification or authentication of a person, as defined in Article 4.1 of the GDPR, which defines personal data as:  
“1. Personal data: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social identity of that natural person.”  
It should be noted that the approval of the GDPR has brought about a paradigm shift in the field of personal data protection aimed at further ensuring citizens' control over their personal data, establishing high protection standards adapted to the digital environment in which we live. In accordance with the Principle of Proactive Responsibility, which inspires the new regulation, the new GDPR emphasizes that the data controller must seriously assess the risks of the processing they wish to establish concerning the rights and freedoms of the data subjects (always prior to initiating any processing, and continuously if they decide to proceed), opting for a risk analysis approach by design and by default, in order to identify them, determine the likelihood of their occurrence and their impact, and foresee measures and guarantees that eliminate or, at least, mitigate the identified risks, preventing their occurrence. Likewise, certain obligations must be fulfilled, and specific principles established by the regulation must be respected.  
Thus, whenever personal data of any kind is processed, the data controller must comply with the principles and obligations set forth in the data protection regulation for all types of personal data.  
However, when the personal data to be processed is biometric (fingerprints, facial recognition, etc.), it must also be considered that—unlike what was the case under the regime prior to the GDPR—this type of data is classified as special category personal data under Article 9, the processing of which is prohibited, unless one of the exceptions provided for in Article 9.2 of the GDPR applies. This does not exempt the necessity of having a lawful basis as provided in Article 6 of the same, among many other requirements and principles that must be complied with by anyone who decides to opt for this type of processing.  
According to Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the processing of biometric data is prohibited when it is: “biometric data processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person.” While Recital 51 of the GDPR includes both identification and authentication procedures: “since they are only included in the definition of biometric data when the fact that they are processed by specific technical means allows for the unique identification or authentication of a natural person. In this regard, it is important to emphasize that the qualification as special category data necessarily implies the observance of special caution when determining whether it is possible to carry out processing of such data.  
Among other things, and in addition to the existence of an exception that allows for the lifting of the prohibition in Article 9.1 of the GDPR, that there is a lawful basis under Article 6 of the GDPR, and that the principles of the GDPR are met, the entity intending to implement biometric data systems (in this case, IVASS) should have previously analyzed the concurrence of the necessary criteria of necessity, suitability, and proportionality of the processing.  
That is, anyone intending to establish processing of personal data of this nature must, first and foremost, ensure that they surpass what has been termed in jurisprudence as “the triple test of proportionality,” particularly considering whether the processing of biometric data is suitable, proportional, and above all, necessary. If there are other non-biometric systems that allow achieving the same purpose of effectively identifying-verifying the identity of individuals, it will not be necessary to initiate biometric processing, and therefore, the implementation of this system will be considered contrary to the GDPR. This assessment must be the starting point of their analysis, as only if these methods surpass the aforementioned triple test will compliance with other requirements or guarantees be required.  
Moreover, in addition to being special category data, the processing of biometric data is considered high risk according to the provisions of paragraph 4 of Article 35, which states that “The supervisory authority shall establish and publish a list of the types of processing operations that require an impact assessment regarding data protection in accordance with paragraph 1,” given that it is included among the processing types listed that require an impact assessment regarding data protection, as made public by the AEPD in development of the provision contemplated in the fourth paragraph of the aforementioned Article 35.  
There is no doubt about the high-risk nature of this data, considering that biometric data meets the criteria corresponding to numbers 4, 5, and 10 of that document (those involving the use of special categories of data; the use of biometric data; and those involving the use of new technologies or an innovative use of established technologies). Therefore, the processing of biometric data can never be initiated without having developed and surpassed a valid DPIA prior to the processing.  
Thus, since the entry into force of the GDPR, when processing is of “high risk,” as is the case with biometric time-tracking methods, it is mandatory to always conduct an impact assessment (DPIA), in accordance with the provisions of Article 35.1 of the GDPR, and this DPIA must be prior to the commencement of processing, but must also be conducted continuously. It will not be sufficient to merely conduct it; it must be considered valid because it meets the requirements set forth in the aforementioned article, particularly that it contains at least the information specified in Article 35.7 of the GDPR.

2.3. IVASS as the data processing operations manager for biometric data of public employees using the fingerprint timekeeping system.  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines "processing of personal data" as:  
“any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, restriction, erasure or destruction.”  
If the processing consists of the daily recording of working hours through fingerprint recognition, it will necessarily require a varied set of operations by the controller that involve the processing of biometric personal data according to Article 4.2 of the GDPR. These may include: the collection or capture of the employee's fingerprint, its processing to convert it into a biometric vector or template, its registration, storage, the identification-authentication of the employee in the timekeeping process, the recording of time and other possible data, its processing to determine excess or missing hours, its conservation, erasure, restriction, etc. All of these will be operations of processing biometric personal data (biometric template) of a special category and high risk.

Unfulfilled obligation.  
Art. 35 Data Protection Impact Assessment  
6.1. On the necessity to conduct and surpass a DPIA.  
As previously stated, before implementing or continuing with a data processing platform based on such intrusive technology as the program “***PLATFORM.1” that the respondent contracted with ***COMPANY.2 (now ***COMPANY.3) to enable the clocking of its personnel's working hours, it is also necessary to audit its operation beforehand, not in isolation but within the framework of the specific processing in which it will be used (in this case, daily recording of working hours for IVASS employees).  
The data protection impact assessment, DPIA, has appeared since the entry into force of the GDPR as a new tool required of controllers of “high-risk” processing to ensure compliance with this aspect of processing, as established in Article 35, paragraph 1 of the GDPR:  
“When it is likely that a type of processing, in particular if it uses new technologies, by its nature, scope, context, or purposes, entails a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller of the processing...

will carry out, prior to the processing, an assessment of the impact of the processing operations on the protection of personal data…”  
As already indicated, the processing of biometric data has been included in the List of processing operations considered to be of high risk by the AEPD, pursuant to the provisions of Article 35.4 of the GDPR, so we must start from the premise that the processing of biometric data implemented by IVASS should have been preceded by a valid impact assessment, which included at least the sections provided for in Article 35.7 of the GDPR. This implies that it is not enough to carry out a DPIA; it must be passed in order to comply with the GDPR.  
This assessment will be carried out prior to the commencement of processing, but it should be understood as a continuous or periodic assessment, in the sense established by Article 35.11 of the GDPR, which states: “Where necessary, the controller shall examine whether the processing is in accordance with the data protection impact assessment, at least when there is a change in the risk posed by the processing operations.” Based on this, it is understood that, if the assertion made by IVASS that the processing began in 2010 when the platform was contracted is confirmed, the processing of biometric data carried out through it must have complied with the requirements set forth in the previous personal data protection regulations, and, above all, it must have been reviewed with the entry into force of the GDPR in May 2018, conducting and passing the corresponding DPIA from then on.  
A DPIA must meet the minimum requirements or content outlined in Article 35.7 of the GDPR, which states:  
“The assessment shall include at least:  
a) a systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes of the processing, including, where applicable, the legitimate interests pursued by the controller;  
b) an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations in relation to their purpose;  
c) an assessment of the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects referred to in paragraph 1, and  
d) the measures envisaged to address the risks, including safeguards, security measures, and mechanisms to ensure the protection of personal data, and to demonstrate compliance with this Regulation, taking into account the rights and legitimate interests of data subjects and other affected persons.”  
In short, passing a DPIA requires that the controller of a high-risk processing operation documents in writing that it surpasses the assessment of suitability, necessity, and proportionality of the processing, and that it manages the specific risks of the processing from the design stage, with the practical application of measures aimed at them in such a way as to ensure an acceptable risk threshold throughout the entire lifecycle of the processing, as established in Article 35 of the GDPR.  
Furthermore, it obliges prior consultation with the supervisory authority in cases where the controller has not taken measures to mitigate the risk in accordance with Article 36 of the GDPR.

6.2. Regarding the requirement that the processing of biometric data is necessary, suitable, and proportional.  
Not having conducted and completed the mandatory DPIA in this case means, among other things, that IVASS acknowledges that it has been processing biometric data of its employees since it implemented the time tracking control system through fingerprint detection, and that it has continued with the same until now without having previously conducted the assessment of its necessity, suitability, and proportionality as required by Articles 5.1.c) and 35.4.b) of the GDPR. Thus, Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR includes the so-called "Principle of data minimization," which states the following:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
a) adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’).”  
It is noteworthy that, especially since the entry into force of the GDPR, respect for this principle must be the starting point for the initiation of any processing, and the controller must consider whether this processing will truly be necessary, suitable, and proportional before starting or continuing with it in this case. Thus, the DPIA must contain, in addition to other aspects, “b) an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations concerning their purpose,” in accordance with Article 35.7. b) of the GDPR.

Unfulfilled obligation. On the prohibition of processing special category data under Article 9 of the GDPR  
As previously mentioned, since the entry into force of the GDPR, according to Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the processing of biometric data is prohibited when they are: “biometric data intended to uniquely identify a natural person,” as a general rule, so another requirement for this type of biometric data processing is that prior to commencing the processing, the data controller must also verify and demonstrate that one of the exceptions provided for in Article 9.2 of the GDPR or other specific legislation applies, which enables them to carry out such processing for the specific purpose in question (in this case, the registration and control of the working hours of IVASS employees).  
It should be noted that, while the processing is generally prohibited, any exception to this prohibition must be interpreted restrictively. This is deduced from recitals 51 and 52 of the GDPR.

Once consulted, the DPD did not hesitate to issue two reports on March 5 and 11, 2024, where it examined the implemented system, determining that it is a biometric processing, that the respondent had misinterpreted the documentation obtained from the data processor that developed ***PLATAFORMA.1, and that it was necessary to comply with the obligation to carry out and surpass a prior DPIA, as well as to demonstrate the existence of a lawful basis, exception, and the rest of the principles and obligations provided for in the data protection regulations.  
On the other hand, although in its allegations to the proposed resolution the respondent already refers to the possible designation of its own DPD, to whom it may have consulted on these matters, it still fails to prove that it has designated one, that it has consulted them about the new system that is pending tender, or, if applicable, that the DPD of the GVA is participating in it.  
Consequently, it is considered that the known facts would constitute an infringement of Article 38.1 of the GDPR, which assigns the data controller the duty to ensure that the DPD participates adequately and in a timely manner in all matters relating to the protection of personal data.  
XI  
Typification and qualification of the infringement of Article 38 of the GDPR.  
The violation of Article 38 of the GDPR is typified in Article 83.4.a) of the aforementioned GDPR in the following terms:  
“4. Infringements of the following provisions shall be sanctioned, in accordance with paragraph 2, with administrative fines of up to 10,000,000 EUR or, in the case of an enterprise, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 2% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount:  
a) the obligations of the controller and the processor under Articles 8, 11, 25 to 39, 42, and 43.”  
For its part, the LOPDGDD in its Article 73, for the purposes of prescription, qualifies as “Serious Infringements:  
According to what is established in Article 83.4 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following are considered serious and shall prescribe after two years:  
w) Failing to enable the effective participation of the data protection officer in all matters relating to the protection of personal data, failing to support them, or interfering with the performance of their duties.”
