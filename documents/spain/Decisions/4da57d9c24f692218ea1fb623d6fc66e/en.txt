RESOLUTION OF RECOURSE FOR RECONSIDERATION  
Examined the recourse for reconsideration filed by ORANGE ESPAGNE, S.A.U. (hereinafter, the appellant) against the resolution issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection dated January 30, 2023, and based on the following:  
FACTS  
FIRST: On January 30, 2023, a resolution was issued by the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection in file EXP202203638, by virtue of which ORANGE ESPAGNE, S.A.U. was imposed a fine of 70,000 euros (seventy thousand euros) for an infringement of Article 6.1 of the GDPR, classified under Article 83.5 of the GDPR.  
This resolution, which was notified to the appellant on January 31, 2023, was issued following the processing of the corresponding sanctioning procedure, in accordance with the provisions of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (LOPDGDD), and subsidiarily under Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations (hereinafter, LPACAP), regarding the processing of sanctioning procedures.  
SECOND: As proven facts of the aforementioned sanctioning procedure, PS/00255/2022, the following was recorded:  
FIRST: The complainant states that on February 24, 2022, one of their two mobile phones lost service. After contacting Orange, they were informed that a duplicate of their SIM card had been made, a request that the complainant did not make, so they went to a physical store and were provided with the details related to that duplicate, with the location being Barcelona, despite the complainant residing in Madrid.  
SECOND: The complainant has provided a copy of the complaint filed with the Civil Guard in the locality of ***LOCALIDAD.1 (Madrid) regarding these events and a document related to the request for a duplicate of the SIM card. Point of Sale of ISP ***LOCALIDAD.2, with no signature from the complainant.  
THIRD: Orange states that on February 24 and 25, 2022, a request for a duplicate SIM (specifically an e-SIM) associated with the Point of Sale of ISP ***LOCALIDAD.2 was made through Siebel 8 (internal activation system for Orange mobile cards requested from stores). The Fraud department proceeded to categorize the contract as irregular and to cancel the duplicate e-SIM.  
FOURTH: Orange acknowledged in its response letter dated October 18, 2022, to this Agency that the incident was due to a specific human error by the Agent at the Orange Point of Sale who, due to the insistence and knowledge of the criminals of the company’s ‘jargon’ - believing they were dealing with a colleague - managed to have the Agent reveal their credentials, violating all protocols and instructions communicated regarding their confidentiality.  
FIFTH: These events have been reported by Orange, with the complaint recorded under number ***DILIGENCIAS.1 at the Court of First Instance and Instruction No. X of ***LOCALIDAD.3. Additionally, in collaboration with the authorities, Orange has proceeded to trace the IMEI of the device from which the fraudulent eSIM duplicate was made, identifying it and including it in an internal BlackList, so that it cannot be used again.  
THIRD: The appellant submitted a recourse for reconsideration to this Spanish Agency for Data Protection on February 27, 2023.  
As the basis for the recourse, the appellant reiterates the arguments presented throughout the procedure, stating that in the present procedure, it has not been proven in any way that the third party had access to the banking data, nor to other personal data associated with the device.  
They add that, although Orange establishes security measures and subjects them to continuous review and improvement, it is essential that individuals, as holders of their personal data, exercise due diligence in safeguarding them, without making them available to third parties or neglecting them, allowing third parties to obtain their personal information, thus facilitating impersonation.  
On the other hand, they argue that Orange does not participate in the process of identifying a user before their bank, but rather it is the bank that determines how it wants to carry out this verification, so responsibility cannot be transferred to the telephone operators.  
Regarding the inadmissibility of objective liability, they point out that the AEPD limits its argumentation to the result and request that a resolution be issued indicating the filing of the procedure. Alternatively, they request the procedure to be concluded with a warning and, as a last resort, that the sanction be moderated or adjusted.

<<II  
Typification and qualification of the infringement  
Article 4 of the GDPR, under the heading "Definitions," provides the following:  
“1) ‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person;  
2) ‘processing’: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination, or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”  
7) ‘data controller’ or ‘controller’: the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; where the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of processing, the controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided by the law of the Union or of the Member States.”  
ORANGE is the data controller for the data processing referred to in the aforementioned background, since according to the definition in Article 4.7 of the GDPR, it is the one that determines the purposes and means of the processing carried out for the purposes indicated in its Privacy Policy.  
Furthermore, the issuance of a duplicate SIM card involves the processing of personal data of its holder, as any person whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by means of an identifier (Article 4.1) of the GDPR, is considered an identifiable natural person.  
In this regard, it is important to clarify that the SIM card is inserted within the mobile terminal. It is a smart card, made of plastic and of small dimensions, which contains a chip that stores the service key of the subscriber used to identify themselves to the network, that is, the mobile phone line number of the customer MSISDN (Mobile Station Integrated Services Digital Network), as well as the personal identification number of the subscriber IMSI (International Mobile Subscriber Identity), but it can also provide other types of data such as information about the telephone directory or call and message logs.

The SIM card can be inserted into more than one mobile terminal, provided that it is either unlocked or from the same company.  
In Spain, since 2007, through the Single Additional Provision of Law 25/2007, of October 18, on the retention of data related to electronic communications and public communications networks, it is required that the holders of all SIM cards, whether prepaid or contract, are duly identified and registered. This is important because the identification of the subscriber will be essential to activate the SIM card, which means that when obtaining a duplicate of this card, the person requesting it must also identify themselves and their identity must match that of the holder.  
In summary, both the data processed to issue a duplicate SIM card and the SIM card (Subscriber Identity Module) that unequivocally identifies the subscriber on the network are personal data, and their processing must be subject to data protection regulations.  
The defendant is charged with committing an infringement for violating Article 6 of the GDPR, "Lawfulness of processing," which states in its paragraph 1 the circumstances under which the processing of third-party data is considered lawful:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following applies:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person;  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child. The provisions of point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”

III  
Unfulfilled Obligation  
In response to the allegations presented by the defendant entity, the following should be noted:  
From the Established Facts, it is deduced that ORANGE has provided a duplicate SIM card to a third party other than the legitimate holder of the mobile line, after a third party bypassed the existing security policy, which evidences a failure to protect customer information.  
Denying the occurrence of negligent conduct by ORANGE would be equivalent to acknowledging that its behavior—by action or omission—has been diligent. Obviously, we do not share this perspective of the facts, as the lack of due diligence has been proven. The SAN of October 17, 2007 (rec. 63/2006) is very illustrative; starting from the premise that these are entities whose activity involves continuous processing of customer data, it indicates that “...the Supreme Court has understood that there is imprudence whenever a legal duty of care is neglected, that is, when the infringer does not behave with the required diligence. And in assessing the degree of diligence, the professionalism or lack thereof of the subject must be especially weighed, and there is no doubt that, in the case now examined, when the activity of the appellant involves constant and abundant handling of personal data, it must be insisted upon the rigor and exquisite care to comply with the legal provisions in this regard.”  
It is evidenced in the file that adequate security has not been guaranteed in the processing of personal data, given the outcome produced by the identity theft. That is to say, a third party has managed to access the personal data of the line holder without the security measures that ORANGE claims exist being able to prevent it. Thus, we are faced with the occurrence of typical, unlawful, and culpable conduct.  
In short, the operator's rigor in monitoring who is the holder of the SIM card or the person authorized by them requesting the duplicate should meet strict requirements. It is not that the information referred to is not contained in the SIM card, but rather that if, in the process of issuing a duplicate SIM card, the identity of the applicant is not adequately verified, the operator would be facilitating identity theft.

ORANGE cites in its defense a series of resolutions issued by the AEPD, stating that the present case is analogous to those recorded in the procedures EXP202104010; EXP202104011 and EXP202105686 against ORANGE, also for cases of "Sim Swapping" fraud, which were archived by the AEPD.  
In this regard, it is important to highlight that these procedures aimed to analyze the processes followed to manage SIM change requests by ORANGE, identifying any vulnerabilities that may exist in the operational procedures implemented, to detect the causes for which these cases may be occurring, as well as to find points of non-compliance, improvement, or adjustment, to determine responsibilities, reduce risks, and enhance the security in the processing of personal data of the affected individuals. The facts claimed in the cited procedures refer to the same operational data protection procedure that has been investigated and sanctioned by the AEPD through a resolution dated 10/11/2021 in the context of the sanctioning procedure PS/00022/2021, processed against the respondent party, which is charged with the violation of Article 5.1f).  
In the present sanctioning procedure, the penalty is imposed because ORANGE provided a duplicate of the claimant's SIM card to a third party, without their consent and without verifying the identity of that third party, and for this reason, Article 6.1 of the GDPR is invoked.  
Regarding ORANGE's responsibility, it should be indicated that, in general, ORANGE processes its customers' data under the provisions of Article 6.1 b) of the GDPR, as it is considered necessary for the execution of a contract in which the data subject is a party or for the application at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures. In other cases, it bases the lawfulness of the processing on the grounds provided in Articles 6.1.a), c), e), and f) of the GDPR.  
The Constitutional Court stated in its Judgment 94/1998, of May 4, that we are dealing with a fundamental right to data protection that guarantees individuals control over their data, any personal data, and over its use and destination, to prevent the illicit trafficking of such data or harm to the dignity and rights of the affected individuals; thus, the right to data protection is configured as a citizen's power to oppose the use of certain personal data for purposes other than those that justified its acquisition.  
In turn, in Judgment 292/2000, of November 30, it considers it an autonomous and independent right that consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that empowers the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose that possession or use.  
Regarding ORANGE's conduct, it is considered to fall under the title of fault. As a custodian of personal data on a large scale, therefore, accustomed or specifically dedicated to the management of personal data of the

It is Recital 74 of the GDPR that states: “The responsibility of the controller for any processing of personal data carried out by them or on their behalf must be established. Likewise, Recital 79 states: The protection of the rights and freedoms of data subjects, as well as the responsibility of controllers and processors, also with regard to the supervision by the supervisory authorities and the measures adopted by them, requires a clear allocation of responsibilities under this Regulation, including cases where a controller determines the purposes and means of processing jointly with other controllers, or where processing is carried out on behalf of a controller.”

The computer system and the intervening technologies must be adequate to prevent identity impersonation and must be properly configured.  
This Agency does not share ORANGE's assertions regarding the circumstances that have been established.  
It is true that there are protocols to prevent identity impersonation in these processes; that they have been communicated to those involved in the processing; that improvements have been introduced after certain vulnerabilities were identified; that there are penalties for non-compliance. However, we do not agree that these protocols or internal procedures can be considered adequate as they are susceptible to improvement. Identification and authentication mechanisms must be reinforced with technical and organizational measures that are particularly appropriate to prevent impersonations.  
Regarding due diligence, it is acknowledged that ORANGE has acted diligently in minimizing the impact on potential affected parties by implementing new security measures to prevent the recurrence of similar incidents in the future.  
Certainly, the principle of responsibility provided for in Article 28 of the LRJSP states: “Only individuals and legal entities, as well as, when a law recognizes them as capable of acting, groups of affected parties, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous estates, that are responsible for the same due to intent or negligence, may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative infractions.”  
However, the manner of attributing responsibility to legal entities does not correspond to the forms of culpability that are intentional or negligent.

attributable to human conduct. Thus, in the case of infringements committed by legal entities, although the element of culpability must be present, it necessarily applies in a different manner than it does with natural persons.  
According to STC 246/1991 " (...) this distinct construction of the attribution of the authorship of the infringement to the legal person arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the norms to which they are subject. The capacity to infringe and, therefore, direct reproachability derives from the legal good protected by the norm that is infringed and the necessity for such protection to be truly effective and the risk that, consequently, the legal person must assume, which is subject to compliance with that norm" (in this sense, STS of November 24, 2011, Rec 258/2009).  
To the above, it should be added, following the judgment of January 23, 1998, partially transcribed in the SSTS of October 9, 2009, Rec 5285/2005, and October 23, 2010, Rec 1067/2006, that "although the culpability of the conduct must also be subject to proof, it should be considered in terms of the assumption of the corresponding burden, that ordinarily the volitional and cognitive elements necessary to appreciate it form part of the proven typical conduct, and that their exclusion requires evidence of the absence of such elements, or in its normative aspect, that due diligence has been exercised by the party claiming its non-existence; in summary, the mere invocation of the absence of fault is not sufficient for exculpation against a typically unlawful behavior."  
Therefore, the lack of culpability is dismissed. The ultimate responsibility for the processing remains attributed to the responsible party, who is the one that determines the existence of the processing and its purpose. Let us remember that, generally, operators process their customers' data under the provisions of Article 6.1 b) of the GDPR, as it is considered necessary processing for the execution of a contract in which the data subject is a party (…). In this regard, ORANGE has a network of sales representatives, points of sale, and authorized distributors through a distribution contract to offer ORANGE services. Among these services offered from their points of sale is the issuance of duplicates of SIM cards corresponding to a mobile phone line.  
In the present case, it is established that Orange provided a duplicate of the claimant's SIM card to a third party, without their consent and without verifying the identity of said third party, who accessed information contained in the mobile phone, such as banking data, passwords, email address, and other personal data associated with the device. Thus, the defendant did not verify the identity of the person who requested the duplicate of the SIM card, nor did it take the necessary precautions to prevent these events from occurring.  
Based on the above, in the analyzed case, the diligence employed by the defendant to identify the person who requested a duplicate of the SIM card is called into question.

“(40) For processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on another legitimate basis established in accordance with law, whether in this Regulation or under another Union or Member State law to which this Regulation applies, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject or the necessity of performing a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract.”  
IV  
Sanction  
The determination of the sanction to be imposed in this case requires observing the provisions of Articles 83.1 and 2 of the GDPR, which respectively state the following:  
“1. Each supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition of administrative fines pursuant to this article for the infringements of this Regulation referred to in paragraphs 4, 9, and 6 are, in each individual case, effective, proportionate, and dissuasive.”  
“2. Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual case, as an additional or substitute measure to those provided for in Article 58, paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j). In deciding to impose an administrative fine and its amount in each individual case, the following shall be duly taken into account:  
a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing operation in question, as well as the number of data subjects affected and the level of damage and detriment suffered;  
b) the intentionality or negligence in the infringement;  
c) any measures taken by the controller or processor to mitigate the damage and detriment suffered by the data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the controller or processor, taking into account the technical or organizational measures they have implemented pursuant to Articles 25 and 32;  
e) any previous infringements committed by the controller or processor;  
f) the degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority in order to remedy the infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects of the infringement;  
g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement;  
h) the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement, in particular whether the controller or processor notified the infringement and, if so, to what extent;  
i) when the measures referred to in Article 58, paragraph 2, have previously been ordered against the controller or processor in relation to the same matter, compliance with those measures;  
j) adherence to codes of conduct under Article 40 or to approved certification mechanisms under Article 42, and  
k) any other aggravating or mitigating factor applicable to the circumstances of the case, such as the financial benefits obtained or the losses avoided, directly or indirectly, through the infringement.”  
Within this section, the LOPDGDD provides in its Article 76, titled “Sanctions and corrective measures”:  
“1. The sanctions provided for in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 of Article 83 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 shall be applied taking into account the criteria for graduation established in paragraph 2 of the aforementioned article.  
2. In accordance with the provisions of Article 83.2.k) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following may also be taken into account:  
a) The continued nature of the infringement.  
b) The connection of the infringer's activity with the processing of personal data.  
c) The benefits obtained as a result of the commission of the infringement.  
d) The possibility that the conduct of the affected party may have induced the commission of the infringement.  
e) The existence of a merger by absorption after the commission of the infringement, which cannot be attributed to the absorbing entity.  
f) The impact on the rights of minors.  
g) Having, when not mandatory, a data protection officer.

- The evident link between the business activity of the respondent and the processing of personal data of clients or third parties (Article 83.2.k of the GDPR in relation to Article 76.2.b of the LOPDGDD).  
The Judgment of the National Court of 17/10/2007 (rec. 63/2006), which, regarding entities whose activity involves continuous processing of client data, indicates that “…the Supreme Court has understood that there is negligence whenever a legal duty of care is neglected, that is, when the offender does not behave with the required diligence. As mitigating factors:  
The respondent blocked the line as soon as it became aware of the facts (art. 83.2 c).  
It is appropriate to adjust the sanction to be imposed on the respondent and set it at the amount of €70,000 for the alleged infringement of Article 6.1) classified in Article 83.5.a) of the aforementioned GDPR.
