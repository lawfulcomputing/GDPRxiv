LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, and 64.3 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on Personal Data Protection and guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states that: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection will be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development and, as long as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  
II  
On the data controller  
The complaint that gives rise to this procedure was directed against the pharmacy located at ***ADDRESS. However, the owner of the pharmacy is the company “FARMACIA COLLBLANCH, CB,” a community of goods constituted by two partners, Ms. C.C.C. and the respondent, both pharmacists.

Article 4 of the GDPR, paragraph 7, defines the “data controller” as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; […]”.  
The C.B. is not a legal entity, and therefore cannot hold rights and obligations. Broadly speaking, it is an association of natural persons to facilitate the management and administration of a business project or a common asset without its nature being altered by the fact that the State Agency for Tax Administration (AEAT) assigns a tax identification number to the C.B.  

In the statements made by the respondent in their response to the transfer from the General Subdirectorate of Inspection, they state that they are a co-owner, along with Ms. C.C.C., of the company “***EMPRESA.1.” and also acknowledge that it was they who attended to the claimant when they visited the pharmacy and who responded to the comment that the claimant published on Google Maps about the pharmacy. In their response, the respondent informed about the name of the medication and the dosage prescribed in the medical prescription that the claimant presented to them.  
Thus, the respondent, co-owner of the pharmacy, is the one who has decided on the purposes and means of the processing of personal data that has given rise to the present procedure and, consequently, is considered the data controller.  
III  
On the existence of personal data processing  
The respondent categorically denied in their response to the transfer of the claim that they had processed personal data of the claimant, neither when the claimant visited the pharmacy nor subsequently when they responded to their comment on Google Maps.  
They also insist that they did not process any health data, since they never stated that the claimant was the recipient of the medication, but merely said that the claimant had attempted to acquire the medication.  
The legal definition of personal data and processing is set out in Article 4 of the GDPR, paragraphs 1 and 2, which provide:

“1) ‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  
“2) ‘Processing’: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.” (Emphasis added)  
In defense of their claim that they did not carry out personal data processing, the respondent argued in their response to the transfer: “I do not mention the name of the person who came to the pharmacy, nor the name in favor of whom the prescription was issued.”  
Despite the respondent's denial of admitting that through the comments they published on Google Maps, in which they informed about the name of the medication and the dosage prescribed in the prescription presented by the claimant, they carried out personal data processing, it should be emphasized, on the one hand, that according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, personal data is “any information” concerning an “identified or identifiable” natural person and, on the other hand, that the respondent published their comment on a social network in response to a previous comment from a natural person who identified themselves on that social network by their name, a surname, and their photograph.  
Indeed, the respondent stated in the document they sent to the Inspection Subdirectorate of this Agency: “[...] I saw that there had been a misclassification of the pharmacy on Google Maps [...] what struck me most was identifying in the small photo next to the review the image of the person who had come the day before.” (Emphasis added)  
It should also be added that the comment the respondent published on the social network included, on two occasions, the name and dosage of the medication prescribed in the medical prescription that the claimant presented when they visited their pharmacy on February 1, 2023.  
By publishing on the social network Google Maps the name and dosage of the prescribed medication, the respondent carried out a processing – a “use” – of personal data of the claimant, who was identified on the social network through various elements, such as their name, surname, and photograph.

V  
Breach of Article 9 of the GDPR  
1. Processing of health data.  
The respondent is held responsible for having carried out processing of specially protected data - health data - that violates Article 9.1 of the GDPR.  
According to Article 4.15 of the GDPR, health data is defined as “personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status.”  
It has been established that the respondent publicly disclosed on the social network Google Maps - in response to a comment made by the claimant on that social network regarding the pharmacy - the name of the medication and the dosage prescribed in the prescription presented when the claimant visited their establishment.  
The respondent denies having processed data of this special category and argues that it is not possible to assert that the claimant was the recipient of the medication prescribed in the prescription shown when they visited the pharmacy on February 1, 2023.

Regardless of the fact that the claimant seems to have acknowledged through the social network, in their response to the pharmacist's comment, that the medication was indeed for them - stating that “according to data protection regulations, a pharmacy cannot publish that I am using a certain medication” - we must add that, in the event that the recipient of the medication prescribed in the prescription was not the claimant, but someone in their environment, that person “could be identified” (“identifiable person”). This is because, following the events, given that access to the social network Google Maps and the comments included therein linked to establishments, in this case to the pharmacy, is free, and since the claimant's identity was identifiable on the social network through their photograph, as well as by their first and last name, a public connection has been created and disseminated between the claimant and the medication in question.  
2. General prohibition on processing health data and exceptions.  
Article 9.1 of the GDPR generally prohibits the processing of special category data, including those related to health. However, paragraph 2 of the provision introduces ten exceptions, ten scenarios in which the prohibition on processing may be lifted if any of them apply.  
Regarding the scope of the exceptions set out in Article 9.2 of the GDPR, we must point out that, in most of them, their application requires the presence of two elements:  
i. That the processing is “necessary” in relation to its specific purpose. This element is included in the description of all the circumstances of Article 9.2 GDPR, except for those contemplated in paragraphs a), d), and e).  
Well, the term “necessity” has its own and independent meaning in Community Law. The Court of Justice of the European Union considers it to be an “autonomous concept of Community Law” (CJEU Judgment of 16/12/2008, case C-524/2006, paragraph 52) and the European Court of Human Rights states that the “adjective necessary is not synonymous with ‘indispensable’ nor does it have the flexibility of the expressions ‘admissible,’ ‘ordinary,’ ‘useful,’ ‘reasonable,’ or ‘desirable’” (paragraph 97 of the ECtHR Judgment of 25/03/1983).  
ii. That the processing, for the specific purpose referred to in each of the scenarios of Article 9.2 of the GDPR, is expressly authorized by a provision of internal or Union law. This requirement is included in all scenarios of the

Article 9.2 of the GDPR with the exception of the circumstances set forth in paragraphs c), d), e), and f).  
After examining the exceptions of Article 9.2 of the GDPR that lift the general prohibition of Article 9.1 of the GDPR, we conclude that, in the case examined, none of those contemplated by the provision apply.  
- The consent of the claimant is obviously not present, paragraph a) of Article 9.2 of the GDPR.  
- The processing of health data that has been carried out was also not necessary for the respondent to comply with obligations or exercise specific rights, nor is there a legal norm in domestic law or EU law that supports the processing we are analyzing (paragraph b).  
- Likewise, it was not necessary for the protection of vital interests, either of the data subject or of third parties, to include in the response to a comment on a social network the data relating to the name and dosage of the medication (paragraph c).  
- The exceptions in paragraphs e) - relating to data that the data subject has manifestly made public - and f) - the processing necessary for the formulation or exercise of claims - cannot apply either.  
- The general prohibition on processing health data cannot be lifted by paragraph g), as it refers to processing necessary for reasons of "essential public interest" provided for in a norm of domestic law or EU law, which also establishes adequate measures for the protection of the rights of the data subject.  
- The circumstance set forth in paragraph h) is also inapplicable, as it again requires, in addition to a specific purpose (among others, preventive medicine), the necessity of the processing, the enabling of the processing carried out here through a norm of domestic law or EU law, and that it has been carried out by a professional subject to the obligation of professional secrecy.  
- The circumstance in paragraph j) is not applicable, as it refers to processing carried out for specific purposes that are not relevant here (for public interest archiving, scientific or historical research purposes, or statistical purposes) and requires that the processing be supported by a norm of domestic law or EU law and that the processing authorized by the norm be proportional to the objective pursued, essentially respecting the right to data protection and establishing adequate measures to protect the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject.  
Finally, we will make a particular mention of the circumstance provided for in paragraph i) of Article 9.2: “i) the processing is necessary for reasons of public interest in the field of public health, such as the protection against serious cross-border threats to health, or to ensure high levels of quality and safety of healthcare and of medicines or medical products, based on Union law or the laws of the Member States that establish adequate and specific measures to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subject, in particular professional secrecy.” (The underlining is ours)  
The reason for analyzing the circumstance of Article 9.2.i) of the GDPR is that the respondent has claimed that they acted “in the interest of public health.” Thus, they have stated:  
“Therefore, although it is true that I should have refrained from that comment, I must say that I thought I had no other means to hinder the irregular circulation of a medication - thus, I acted in the interest of public health - given my inability to file a complaint with the police, which is what I would have done if I could have confirmed the irregularity of the prescription, which I could not do as Mr. [the claimant] took it in that manner.” (The underlining is ours)  
Regarding this issue - the alleged application of the circumstance in paragraph i) of Article 9.2 of the GDPR to the case examined - we must indicate that there is no norm of domestic law or EU law that enables the respondent for the processing of health data as they have carried out.  
It is also added that another of the elements that make up the exception described in paragraph i) of Article 9.2 GDPR, to which we refer, is also lacking: on the occasion of the processing carried out, the respondent did not adopt any measures that guaranteed the right to the protection of the personal data of the affected party; on the contrary, in this case, the respondent would have violated the duty of professional secrecy that pertains to them.

1. All processing of personal data must respect the principles governing the fundamental right to data protection established in Article 5 of the GDPR, including the principle of data minimization (Article 5.1.c of the GDPR).  
The respondent is held responsible for a violation of the principle of "data minimization" according to which "Personal data shall be: […] c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed" (Article 5.1.c).  
It is evidenced in the file that the respondent responded to a comment about the pharmacy that the claimant made through the social network Google Maps, providing the name of the medication and the dosage prescribed by the medical prescription presented when the claimant visited the pharmacy.  
Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR states that the data processed must be "adequate and relevant" and "limited to what is necessary" in connection with "the purposes" that the processing operation pursues.  
The suitability of processing certain personal data or the necessity of processing referred to by the data minimization principle is inevitably connected with the purpose or purposes that the data controller seeks to satisfy with their processing operation. It is also necessary to take into account that the GDPR requires that the purpose of the processing be legitimate, that is, compliant with the legal framework (Article 5.1.b).  
Based on these premises, it is appropriate to examine the context in which the personal data concerning the complaint at hand were collected and disseminated by the respondent.  
The respondent, by reading the medical prescription presented by the claimant, had access, with a legitimate purpose - to dispense the medication - to the name and dosage of the medication prescribed therein. This, in turn, obliged him, according to sectoral regulations, to verify that the presented prescription met the regulatory requirements.  
At this point, we must recall that the respondent, by virtue of being a pharmacist, is bound by professional secrecy. And that R.D. 1718/2010 "on medical prescriptions and dispensing orders" establishes in its Article 19, section 1, under the heading "Data protection in medical prescriptions and hospital dispensing orders," that "In the procedures to which medical prescriptions are subjected […] the confidentiality of medical and pharmaceutical assistance, the personal and family privacy of citizens, and the protection of their personal data must be guaranteed, in accordance with the specific applicable regulations." (The emphasis is ours)  
On the other hand, the context in which the respondent publicly discloses the name and dosage of the medication requested by the claimant is that of a social network through which individuals who freely choose to participate make evaluations about establishments.  
Thus, the respondent, in order to respond through the social network Google Maps to a negative comment from the claimant about the pharmacy and its staff, carries out processing of data to which he had access in the exercise of his professional activity and regarding which he has an obligation to maintain secrecy.  
The explanation initially put forth by the respondent to justify his behavior, that he acted with a public interest purpose in defense of public health, cannot prosper either, as he sought to prevent the circulation of a prescription that, in his opinion, showed signs of not being legitimate, although in his second submission - allegations to the initiation agreement - he acknowledges that it was a "mistake" to "attempt to prevent the circulation of an apparently illegitimate prescription by such means."  
In this regard, Article 15, "Actions of the pharmacist in the dispensing of prescriptions," paragraph 2, of R.D. 1718/2010, establishes:  
"4. The pharmacist, when reasonable doubts arise about the authenticity or validity of the presented medical prescription, shall not dispense the medications requested by patients or users, unless he can verify the legitimacy of the prescription; otherwise, he shall inform the competent health administration to determine the existence of possible administrative or criminal infringements." (The emphasis is ours)  
In summary, the processing that the respondent made of the data relating to the name and dosage of the medication prescribed in the prescription - to which he had access by virtue of his professional activity - that he published on the social network in which he freely and voluntarily decided to participate was neither pertinent nor adequate to fulfill that purpose.
