LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence and procedure  
In accordance with the powers granted by Article 58.2 of the GDPR and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: "The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection will be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, insofar as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures."  
II  
Biometric data as special category personal data  
2.1. Definition and characteristics of biometric data.  
Biometric data processing systems are based on collecting and processing personal data related to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of individuals, which may include their neural characteristics, through devices or sensors, creating biometric templates (also referred to as signatures or patterns) that enable the identification, tracking, or profiling of such individuals.  
The GDPR defines biometric data in Article 4.14 as “personal data obtained through a specific technical processing, relating to the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person which allow or confirm the unique identification of that person, such as facial images or fingerprint data.”  
Any biometric system for recording working hours must first register the identity of the user in the system through the capture of...

a series of biometric parameters (in this case, the fingerprints of the workers at the residence). The aim is to process these parameters to identify the person each time they enter and exit through the access point.  
The decision to implement a new method of monitoring the working hours of employees that involves the processing of biometric data, such as those based on fingerprint detection, as is the case here (or facial recognition in other instances), is not a trivial decision. This type of processing relies on technology that can be truly intrusive and requires a calm ethical and legal debate before deciding on its implementation—even if it is still in the testing phase—since collecting the fingerprints of staff can have very adverse effects on the fundamental values and human integrity of these employees that do not occur with other attendance control methods. Consider just some of its special characteristics and think about the significant impact they have when these biometric data are compromised, compared to other methods (signature and completion of sheets, or clocking in with magnetic cards):  
- Each individual has unique fingerprints that exhibit specific characteristics that can be measured to determine whether a fingerprint matches a registered sample. Therefore, they are unique, permanent, or definitive over time, and a person cannot free themselves from them; they can never be changed, not even with age, so the damage caused in the event of compromise, loss, or intrusion into the system is irreparable in this case. Unlike a password, in the event of loss, our fingerprint or facial data cannot be changed.  
- Furthermore, because biometric data are unique to a person and perpetual, the user can use the same data across different systems, which poses an additional risk.  
- While traditional authentication methods such as passwords require a 100% character-by-character match to allow the user to access, for example, an account or application (deterministic methods), biometric methods are referred to as “probabilistic,” because they are based on the probability that the user attempting to access a particular device or application is the same person as the registered user. We can measure the performance of a biometric system based on three main characteristics. These are: false rejection rate (FRR), false acceptance rate (FAR), and equal error rate (ERR). The false rejection rate represents the probability of detection errors by a biometric system, meaning that it cannot recognize a user whose biometric characteristics are already in the database. In the event of a rejection, the person must verify their identity again. From a security and protection perspective, this rate does not necessarily mean that it is a negative outcome. Each biometric method, whether facial recognition, fingerprint reading, palm print, iris, etc., has different values for different rates based on which a system rejects or accepts inputs. Error rates, as stated by the City Council, were high in this case.

in the sense provided in Article 4.1 of the GDPR, which defines personal data as:  
“1. Personal data: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, particularly by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  
In this case, there is no doubt that biometric data of a personal nature is being processed, since the purpose of the implemented system is to determine the identity, directly or indirectly, of the employee of the residence who enters or exits the workplace and to verify the time and date of the beginning and end of their working day. Since the process assigns an identifier (the biometric template obtained by collecting the fingerprint samples of the data subjects) that allows for the individualization of a person and distinguishes them from others through “specific elements of their physical, physiological, genetic, mental identity.”  
It should be noted that the approval of the GDPR has represented a paradigm shift in the field of personal data protection, aiming to further guarantee citizens control over their personal data by establishing high protection standards adapted to the digital environment in which we live. In accordance with the Principle of Proactive Responsibility, which inspires the new regulation, the new GDPR emphasizes that the data controller must seriously assess the risks of the processing they wish to establish concerning the rights and freedoms of the data subjects (always prior to initiating any processing, and continuously if they decide to do so), opting for a risk analysis approach from the design and by default, in order to identify them, determine the likelihood of materialization and their impact, and foresee measures and guarantees that eliminate or, at least, mitigate the detected risks, preventing their materialization. Likewise, certain obligations must be fulfilled, and specific principles established by the regulation must be respected.  
Thus, whenever personal data of any kind is processed, the controller must comply with the principles and obligations set forth in the data protection regulation for all types of personal data.  
However, when the personal data to be processed is biometric (fingerprints, facial recognition, etc.), it must also be considered that - unlike what occurred under the regime prior to the GDPR - this type of data is considered special category personal data under Article 9, the processing of which is generally prohibited unless one of the exceptions provided for in Article 9.2 of the GDPR applies. This does not exempt the necessity for a lawful basis to always exist as provided in Article 6 of the same, among many other requirements and principles that must be complied with by anyone who decides to opt for this type of processing.  
According to Article 9.1 of the GDPR, the processing of biometric data is prohibited when it is: “biometric data processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person.” While Recital 51 of the GDPR includes both identification and authentication procedures: “since they are only included in the definition of biometric data when the fact of...

Data treated with specific technical means that allow for the unequivocal identification or authentication of a natural person. Such personal data must not be processed unless their processing is permitted in specific situations contemplated in this Regulation.  
In this regard, it is important to emphasize that the qualification as special category data necessarily implies the observance of special caution when determining whether it is possible to carry out processing of this nature. Among other things, and in addition to the existence of an exception that allows for the lifting of the prohibition in Article 9.1 of the GDPR, there must be a lawful basis under Article 6 of the GDPR, and the principles of the GDPR must be fulfilled. The entity intending to implement biometric data systems, in this case, the City Council, should have previously analyzed the concurrence of the necessary criteria of necessity, suitability, and proportionality of the processing.  
That is, anyone intending to establish processing of personal data of this nature must, first and foremost, ensure that they surpass what has been termed in jurisprudence as "the triple test of proportionality," particularly considering whether the processing of biometric data is suitable, proportional, and above all, necessary. If there are other non-biometric systems that allow achieving the same purpose of identifying-verifying the identity of individuals effectively, it will not be necessary to initiate biometric processing, and therefore, the implementation of this system will be considered contrary to the GDPR. This assessment must be the starting point of their analysis, as only if these methods surpass the aforementioned triple test will compliance with other requirements or guarantees be required.  
An assessment that the City Council did not carry out before initiating the processing on July 1, 2021 (which is when it should have been done), nor during or at the end of the processing, as it claims to have finally withdrawn the system on December 15, 2022, for technical reasons, without considering whether what actually occurs is that there are other alternative methods that allow for the unequivocal verification of the identity of workers entering and leaving the workplace for the purpose of monitoring working hours, which are not as intrusive as fingerprint detection and are equally suitable for fulfilling the legal function of monitoring working hours.  
Moreover, in addition to being special category data, the processing of biometric data is considered high risk according to the provisions of paragraph 4 of Article 35, which states that "...The supervisory authority shall establish and publish a list of the types of processing operations that require a data protection impact assessment in accordance with paragraph 1...", given that it is included among the processing types listed that require a data protection impact assessment, as published by the AEPD in development of the provision contemplated in the fourth paragraph of the aforementioned Article 35.  
There is no doubt about the high-risk nature of this data, considering that biometric data meets the criteria corresponding to numbers 4, 5, and 10 of that document (those involving the use of special categories of data; the use of biometric data; and those involving the use of new technologies or an innovative use of established technologies). Therefore, the processing of biometric data can never be initiated without having developed and surpassed a valid DPIA prior to the processing.

When a processing activity is of "high risk," as is the case with biometric timekeeping methods, it is mandatory to always conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), in accordance with the provisions of Article 35.1 of the GDPR. This DPIA must be carried out prior to the commencement of the processing and must also be conducted continuously. It will not be sufficient to merely perform it; it must be considered valid, as it meets the requirements set forth in the aforementioned article, particularly that it contains at least the information specified in Article 35.7 of the GDPR.  
It is established that the City Council did not conduct a DPIA in this case. 2.3. The City Council of Fuentepelayo as the data controller for the processing of biometric data of the staff at the Residence.  
Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines "processing of personal data" as:  
“any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination or any other form of enabling access, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction;”  
Biometric characteristics are subjected to a technical processing through which a person is recognized via a chronological process contained in all biometric data processing: capturing or recording data followed by its storage or processing, and the phase of comparison or matching, data retention, as well as subsequent erasure, restriction, etc.  
If the processing consists of the daily recording of working hours, it requires a varied set of operations that necessitate the processing of biometric personal data, which may include: the identification of the employee, the collection or capture of their fingerprint (whether raw or through a vector), its registration, storage, the identification-authentication of this in the timekeeping process, the recording of time and other possible data, its processing to determine excess or missing hours, its retention, erasure, restriction, etc.  
Although the City Council insists that the biometric system has been removed, it is evident that there has been prior processing of biometric data that did not comply with the legally established requirements. Some of these processing operations must have necessarily taken place during the period when the fingerprint system was implemented, regardless of whether technical problems occurred, or whether the data (neither fingerprints nor entry and exit records, etc.) were archived after its removal, or whether a biometric vector was obtained when collecting the fingerprint, since it is acknowledged and stated that the fingerprints were collected and the system was used as a mandatory timekeeping system, as indicated in the informational posters placed by the management of the residence.

III.  
On the necessity of conducting a prior and adequate impact assessment for the processing.  
As previously stated, before implementing a data processing project based on such intrusive technology, it is also necessary to audit its operation beforehand, not in isolation but within the framework of the specific processing in which it will be employed (in this case, daily recording of the working hours of City Council employees).  
The data protection impact assessment (DPIA) then appears as the tool required by the GDPR to ensure compliance with this aspect of processing, as established in Article 35, paragraph 1 of the GDPR,  
“When a type of processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, in particular if it uses new technologies, by its nature, scope, context, or purposes, the controller shall, prior to the processing, carry out an assessment of the impact of the processing operations on the protection of personal data…”

As already indicated, the processing of biometric data has been classified as high risk by the AEPD, pursuant to the provisions of Article 35.4 of the GDPR, so we must start from the premise that the processing of biometric data implemented by the City Council must have been preceded by a valid impact assessment, which included at least the sections provided for in Article 35.7 of the GDPR. This implies that it is not enough to carry out a DPIA; it must be surpassed to comply with the GDPR.  
This assessment will be carried out prior to the commencement of processing, but it must be understood as a continuous or periodic assessment, in the sense established by Article 35.11 of the GDPR, which states: “Where necessary, the controller shall review whether the processing is in accordance with the data protection impact assessment, at least when there is a change in the risk posed by the processing operations.”  
A DPIA must meet the minimum requirements or content outlined in Article 35.7 of the GDPR, which states:  
“The assessment shall include at least:  
a) a systematic description of the envisaged processing operations and the purposes of the processing, including, where applicable, the legitimate interest pursued by the controller;  
b) an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing operations in relation to their purpose;  
c) an assessment of the risks to the rights and freedoms of data subjects referred to in paragraph 1; and  
d) the measures envisaged to address the risks, including safeguards, security measures, and mechanisms to ensure the protection of personal data, and to demonstrate compliance with this Regulation, taking into account the rights and legitimate interests of data subjects and other affected persons.”  
In short, surpassing a DPIA requires that the controller of high-risk processing documents in writing that they exceed the assessment of suitability, necessity, and proportionality of the processing, and that they manage the specific risks of the processing from the design stage, with the practical application of measures aimed at them in such a way as to ensure an acceptable risk threshold throughout the entire lifecycle of the processing, as established in Article 35 of the GDPR.  
Furthermore, it requires prior consultation with the supervisory authority in cases where the controller has not taken measures to mitigate the risk in accordance with Article 36 of the GDPR.

As already anticipated, what determines the need to carry out a DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment) is not related to the legal effects that the biometric fingerprint system will produce in the control of working hours, so it is irrelevant whether this system was primary or complementary to fulfill this function, or whether it was in a testing phase, which has not been substantiated by the City Council.  
It is also irrelevant that the installed system had alleged technical failures and was removed for this reason. Technical failures that are neither detailed nor substantiated by the City Council, which does not provide any certificate or technical report to justify them, nor specifies at what phase of the technical procedure of the system they occurred, nor therefore, to which operations of the processing they were allegedly affecting (fingerprint registration, storage, processing, identity verification, etc.). However, in any case, they are entirely irrelevant because the obligation to prepare a DPIA falls on any responsible party that intends to carry out high-risk processing, prior to doing so, in order to analyze beforehand whether the purpose it intends to achieve with biometric methods is truly necessary, proportional, and appropriate, after analyzing all concurrent risks and whether the same purpose can be achieved using less intrusive methods.  
What is truly relevant is that it has been substantiated that the system was installed and functioning (well or poorly) for almost a year and a half, which involved the collection of biometric data, which necessarily had to be stored during that time and used to perform identification at each entry and exit, even if technical defects occurred. Therefore, there was high-risk data processing by the responsible entity, without a prior DPIA being carried out, which was mandatory under Article 35 of the GDPR, thus the City Council incurred in a presumed infringement of the aforementioned provision.  
IV  
Occurrence of an exception under Article 9 of the GDPR, and legitimizing basis under Article 6 of the GDPR.  
Another of the additional requirements for this type of processing will therefore be that prior to initiating the processing, the responsible party must also verify and substantiate that one of the exceptions provided for in Article 9.2 of the GDPR or other specific legislation applies.  
It should be noted that, while the processing is generally prohibited, any exception to this prohibition must be subject to restrictive interpretation. As deduced from recitals 51 and 52 of the GDPR.  
In this context, the exceptions that could possibly allow for the lifting of the general prohibition on processing biometric data aimed at identifying-verifying the identity of natural persons are those provided for in Article 9.2 of the GDPR, with the following literal wording, which must be interpreted restrictively, always in favor of protecting the rights and freedoms of citizens in case of doubt:

Asked about the circumstance of Article 9.2 of the GDPR that would allow the City Council to process these data, it states that it relies on paragraph b), which refers to the processing being necessary for compliance with a legal obligation. Although this is not specified, it can be understood to refer to the obligation to monitor compliance with working hours by public employees and the corresponding obligation of the staff to submit to such control established by the administration. Specifically, it pertains to the obligation to monitor the working hours of the labor personnel of the municipal-owned residence.  
Therefore, since the exception for the processing of biometric data for compliance with a legal obligation provided for in Spanish law does not apply, and no other exception provided for in Article 9.2 of the GDPR has been demonstrated, it is considered that the biometric processing carried out by the City Council has also incurred an administrative infringement of Article 9 of the GDPR.  
Furthermore, it should be clarified that it is not questioned that the City Council acts with a lawful basis provided for in Article 6.1.b), since it concerns labor employees with whom there must be a contractual relationship, so it can initially be considered that the processing of their personal data "is necessary for the execution of a contract in which the data subject is a party or for the application at the request of the data subject of pre-contractual measures." Therefore, an infringement of Article 6 of the GDPR cannot initially be attributed.  
However, the lawful basis of Article 6 of the GDPR may legitimize the City Council to process non-biometric personal data, which allows for the recording and control of attendance through other traditional methods (signed sheets, cards, etc.), but in no case does it legitimize the initiation of biometric processing unless one of the exceptions of Article 9.2 of the GDPR applies.  
In the present case, it should be clarified that the exception of Article 9.2.b) of the GDPR cannot be accepted, which refers to: "when the processing is necessary for compliance with obligations and the exercise of specific rights of the data controller or the data subject in the field of labor law and social security and protection, to the extent authorized by the law of the Union of the Member States."  
As this Agency has already stated in the Guide on attendance control treatments using biometric systems, in the case of the Spanish state, this reference must be understood as requiring a legal reserve, for the purposes provided for in Article 53 of the Constitution. According to the doctrine of the Constitutional Court, in rulings such as STC 76/2019, of May 22, or STC 292/2000, of November 30, the legal reserve implies that there must be a norm with the rank of law that expressly enables the limitation of the fundamental right in question.  
Applying this doctrine to cases of limitation of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data, and to the present case, the application of the exception of Article 9.2.b) of the GDPR to process biometric data of employees to identify-authenticate the identity of a person for the purpose of monitoring compliance with their working hours would require that there be a norm with the rank of law that expressly enables such control to be carried out through methods that involve the processing of biometric data.

In this regard, the cited Guide concludes that: “the current Spanish legal regulations do not contain any sufficiently specific authorization to consider the processing of biometric data for the purpose of time control of the workday to be enabled, neither for labor personnel, since Articles 20.3 and 34.9 of the Consolidated Text of the Workers' Statute (ET) do not contain such authorization, nor for personnel subject to an administrative legal relationship, as the provision related to compliance with working hours and schedules referred to in Article 54.2 of the Consolidated Text approving the Basic Statute of Public Employees (TREBEP) does not constitute a necessary enabling provision.”

V  
On the information duties of Article 13 of the GDPR  
Finally, it should be noted that one of the obligations of the data controller for any processing of personal data is to comply with the information duties to the data subjects as provided in Articles 12 to 14 of the GDPR.  
According to the provisions of Article 12.1 of the GDPR, it is based on a Principle of “Transparency of information, communication, and modalities for exercising the rights of the data subject”:  
“1. The data controller shall take appropriate measures to provide the data subject with all information indicated in Articles 13 and 14, as well as any communication in accordance with Articles 15 to 22 and 34 related to the processing, in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language, in particular any information specifically addressed to a child. The information shall be provided in writing or by other means, including, where appropriate, by electronic means. When requested by the data subject, the information may be provided verbally, provided that the identity of the data subject is demonstrated by other means.”

Classification of infractions and qualification for the purposes of prescription.  
As has been stated in the Legal Grounds III to VI of this resolution, it is considered that the City Council of Fuentepelayo has committed the following infractions of the current regulations on data protection:  

6. 1. Infraction of Article 35 of the GDPR  
As stated in Legal Ground III, in accordance with the proven facts in this procedure, it is considered that the facts presented violate the provisions established in Article 35 of the GDPR, which constitutes the commission of an administrative infraction classified in Article 83.4.a) of the GDPR, which indicates that:  
“Infractions of the following provisions shall be sanctioned, in accordance with paragraph 2, with administrative fines of up to 10,000,000 EUR or, in the case of a company, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 2% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount:  
a) The obligations of the controller and the processor under Articles 8, 11, 25 to 39, 42, and 43.”  
For the purposes of prescription, the LOPDGDD establishes in its Article 73.t) that: “Based on what is established in Article 83.4 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following infractions are considered serious and shall prescribe after two years: infractions that constitute a substantial violation of the articles mentioned therein and, in particular, the following:  
t) The processing of personal data without having carried out the impact assessment of the processing operations on the protection of personal data in cases where it is required.”  

6. 2. Infraction of Article 9 of the GDPR.  
As stated in Legal Ground IV, in accordance with the proven facts in this procedure, it is considered that the facts presented violate the provisions established in Article 9 of the GDPR, which constitutes the commission of an administrative infraction classified in Article 83.5 of the GDPR, which provides as follows:  
“Infractions of the following provisions shall be sanctioned, in accordance with paragraph 2, with administrative fines of up to 20,000,000 EUR or, in the case of a company, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 4% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount:  
a) The basic principles for processing, including the conditions for consent under Articles 5, 6, 7, and 9.”  
For the purposes of prescription, the LOPDGDD establishes in its Article 72.e):  
“Based on what is established in Article 83.5 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the following infractions are considered very serious and shall prescribe after three years: infractions that constitute a substantial violation of the articles mentioned therein and, in particular, the following:  
e) The processing of personal data of the categories referred to in Article 9 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, without any of the circumstances provided for in that provision and in Article 9 of this organic law.”  

6. 3. Infraction of Article 13 of the GDPR.  
As stated in Legal Ground V, in accordance with the proven facts in this procedure, it is considered that the facts presented violate the provisions established in Article 13 of the GDPR, which constitutes the commission of an administrative infraction classified in Article 83.5 of the GDPR, which provides as follows:  
“Infractions of the following provisions shall be sanctioned, in accordance with paragraph 2, with administrative fines of up to 20,000,000 EUR or, in the case of a company, an amount equivalent to a maximum of 4% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount:
