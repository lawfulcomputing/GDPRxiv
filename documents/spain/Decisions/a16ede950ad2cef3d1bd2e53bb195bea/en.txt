IV  
Obligation Unfulfilled  
The inclusion of a person's image or a video containing their image and voice in journalistic publications, under conditions that identify or make them identifiable (without applying any technique that prevents their identification), in this case, additionally along with their name, constitutes the processing of personal data. Therefore, the entity that carries out this processing is obliged to comply with the principles and obligations regarding data protection that are established for the data controller in the GDPR and in the LOPDGDD.  
Such principles and obligations do not diminish simply because we are dealing with a data controller that is a media outlet.  
Moreover, the responsible entity is especially obliged to consider that the dissemination through a media outlet of the image and voice of the claimant and their name, enabling their identification by third parties, implies a greater risk for the data subject, making it highly likely that they may suffer harm to their rights and freedoms. Therefore, it must take extreme precautions to ensure that it cannot be concluded that such data processing is not proportional or necessary in relation to the information purposes pursued.  
Thus, in general terms, it must take into account the principle of proactive responsibility, established in Article 5.2 of the GDPR, the assessment of risks, and the implementation of appropriate security measures. Obligations that are even more relevant when the risk is particularly sensitive.  
In this regard, and regardless of what the legal basis legitimizing the processing may be, every data controller must respect the principles of processing set out in Article 5 of the GDPR. Regarding the present case, it is worth highlighting the principle of "data minimization" set out in Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR, which states the following:  
“1. Personal data shall be  
c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’);”  
It is understood that the processing of personal data must be limited to what is necessary to achieve the objective, that is, when the purpose cannot be reasonably achieved without the processing of such personal data.  
This is related to the principle of purpose limitation in Article 5.1.b) of the GDPR, and only leads to precise results if the specific purpose is well defined by the data controller and is linked to the proportionality and necessity of such processing.

In the specific case examined, as we have indicated, the entity EL ESPAÑOL published, on the website referred to in the facts, the image of the claimant to illustrate a news story about social networks, in which her name is also mentioned, inserting links that led to the video that is the subject of the claim that has motivated the present sanctioning procedure.

This assessment made by the resolution does not impose on a media outlet requirements that do not equally apply to another entity with the same degree of professionalism, belonging to any other sector of activity. In any case, as already indicated in the contested resolution, “it is not the AEPD, as EL ESPAÑOL states, that demands this special responsibility, but rather it is the applicable regulation itself that evaluates circumstances such as risk or professionalism, as already pointed out, the context or the state of the art, among others.”  

On the other hand, EL ESPAÑOL defends in its appeal the relevance of the data processed in light of the public figure status of the interested party and the necessity of using and disseminating their image and voice, as well as the accessory nature of the images that illustrated the published information.  
EL ESPAÑOL asserts that the status of “anonymous individual” of the interested party is lost with the viralization of the video that had already occurred when their news was published on the website “elespañol.com,” referring to the concept of “emergent relevance.”  
The AEPD does not agree that the viralization of the video in question causes the interested party to lose their status as a private and anonymous person and turns them into a person of public relevance. In any case, even if that were the case, the publication of said video is not justified without subjecting it to a process of anonymization of the personal data of the interested party, as the images shown strictly belong to the realm of their privacy. It is already known that for the publication of images, it is not enough for the interested party to be a person of public relevance; it is also necessary that the image depicts an act related to the public activity of the person or related to a public event.  
EL ESPAÑOL also insists on pointing out that it is not understood how the sanctioning resolution maintains that it is not possible to prove the participation of the claiming party in the viralization of the video. However, there is no evidence in the proceedings that proves the intention of said claiming party in the dissemination of the video, which was uploaded to a social network by a third party without any indication that the claiming party participated in this act.  
Furthermore, even if the claiming party had disseminated it on their own social media profiles, this dissemination does not legitimize subsequent use by a media outlet without obtaining the prior consent of the interested party for that use of their personal data, disclosing it in a different manner, as stated by the Supreme Court in Judgment No. 91/2017, dated 15/02/2017.

In the Judgment of the Supreme Court 593/2022, of July 28, regarding the publication by a media outlet of a video uploaded to YouTube with the consent of the actor, cited by the appellant, the issue concerning the role played by social networks on the internet and their impact on fundamental rights is addressed. It cites the STC 27/2020, of February 24, as follows:  
“The aforementioned STC 27/2020, of February 24, refers to this new landscape, with respect to platforms such as Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, or Tuenti. From its reading, the following constitutional doctrine can be summarized:  
(i) Users of social networks continue to hold fundamental rights, and their content remains the same as in the analog era.  
(ii) The fact that private data circulates through social networks on the Internet does not mean that what is private has become public, since the digital environment is not comparable to the concept of "public place" referred to in Organic Law 1/1982, nor can it be asserted that citizens of the digital society have lost or renounced the rights protected in Article 18 of the Spanish Constitution (CE).  
(iii) The constitutional recognition of the fundamental rights included in Article 18 CE entails the individual's power to control the data that circulates on the social network and that concerns them.  
(iv) Unless there is unequivocal authorization for the capture, reproduction, or publication of the image by its owner, the interference with the fundamental right to one's own image must necessarily be justified by the preponderant public interest in having access to it and in disclosing it.  
(v) The holder of the fundamental right must authorize the specific act of using their image and the purposes for which it is granted. Consent given, for example, for the capture of the image does not extend to other subsequent acts, such as its publication or dissemination. Similarly, it must be understood that the authorization of a specific publication does not extend to others, whether they have the same or different purpose than the original. Nor does the permission for use granted to a specific person extend to other potential recipients. Ultimately, it must be understood that consent initially given for a specific occasion or for a specific purpose cannot be deemed indefinite and binding.”  

This Agency also does not dispute the dissemination of the video along with the published news. It is simply a matter of requiring the establishment of adequate guarantees for the protection of personal data subjected to processing through the use of said audiovisual content, with its publication; guarantees that, in this case, do not undermine the informative interest of the news. In this way, the AEPD exercises the powers that the regulation attributes to it as the control authority in the field of personal data protection, which in no way implies the performance of any type of control over the content of the information published by media outlets.
