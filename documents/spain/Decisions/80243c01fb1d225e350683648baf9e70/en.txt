3. The claimant states that personal data would be transferred to the United States through the services of Google Analytics and Google Ads.  
In this section, we consider it important to point out again that within the cookie management government project, periodic scans may classify non-strictly necessary cookies into a category that does not correspond to them, but this does not imply that they are being installed. Therefore, those cookies from Google Analytics and Google Ads are only installed with consent once the user, after reading the privacy policy and the cookie policy, decides to accept them.  
Consequently, only those cookies that have been consented to are sent to Google Analytics after an anonymization process, where the IP anonymization function applies IP masking so that Google Analytics uses only a part of a collected IP address, instead of the full address.  
The anonymization method is performed on the variable "aip," which, if it has the value "1" in the call, means that anonymization is active. Here it is documented:

For those cookies that are part of the migration to version 4 of Google Analytics, it is not necessary to anonymize IPs, as these are neither recorded nor stored. In this version, IP addresses are used at the time of collection to determine location information (country, city, latitude, and longitude of the city) and are then discarded before the data is recorded in data centers or servers.  

Regarding Google Ads, when using the tags provided by Google Analytics, as they are anonymized, they are under the same protection. The cookies _gac and _gcl store information about campaigns to provide Google Ads with a more reliable way to measure customer interactions with their business. Analytics records campaign information in the cookies _gac and _gcl when a user opens a page on their website through a URL that uses Google Ads automatic tagging.

In this context, it is important to highlight that the standard contractual clauses are a transfer instrument in the sense of Chapter V of the Regulation and were not challenged as such by the CJEU in its judgment of July 16, 2020 (C-311/18). However, the CJEU considered that the contractual nature of these clauses implied that they could not be binding on the authorities of third countries. In particular, the CJEU stated that: “...Therefore, while there are situations in which, depending on the legislation and practices in force in the third country in question, the recipient of such transfer is in a position to ensure the necessary protection of the data solely on the basis of standard contractual clauses for data protection, there are others in which the content of such standard clauses may not constitute a sufficient means to ensure, in practice, the effective protection of the personal data transferred to the third country in question. This is particularly the case when the legislation of that third country allows its public authorities to interfere with the rights of the data subjects to whom the data relates” (C-311/18, paragraph 126, emphasis added).  
No further analysis of the legal situation in the United States is necessary, as the CJEU has already provided it in its aforementioned judgment. Indeed, the CJEU considered that regulatory surveillance programs such as Section 702 of FISA and E.O. 12333 in conjunction with PPD-28 do not meet the minimum requirements established by Union Law regarding the principle of proportionality, so that surveillance programs based on these provisions cannot be considered limited to what is strictly necessary (C-311/18, paragraph 184). Furthermore, the CJEU found that the legal framework in question did not confer on data subjects rights that could be enforced in court against U.S. authorities, from which it follows that these individuals do not have the right to effective judicial protection (C-311/18, paragraph 192).  
The CJEU's analysis is relevant in the present matter, as Google LLC (as the importer of data to the U.S.) must be classified as a provider of electronic communications services in the sense of paragraph (b) of point 4 of Article 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code and, therefore, is subject to surveillance by U.S. intelligence services in accordance with paragraph (a) of Article 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code (“FISA 702”). Therefore, Google LLC is obliged to provide personal data to the U.S. government when requested in accordance with paragraph (a) of Article 1881 of Title 50 of the United States Code (FISA 702).

General observations on supplementary measures  
In its Recommendations 01/2020, dated June 18, 2021, (which can be consulted on the website ***URL.15, although the transcripts included in this document refer to its translation into Spanish) the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) clarified that when the assessment of the legislation or practices in force in the third country may affect the effectiveness of the adequate safeguards of the transfer instruments on which the exporter relies, in the context of its specific transfer, as is the case here following the CJEU's assessment, the exporter must suspend the transfer or implement appropriate supplementary measures. The EDPB notes in this regard that “Any supplementary measure can only be considered effective in the sense of the CJEU ruling ‘Schrems II’ to the extent that it addresses the specific deficiencies identified in its assessment of the legal situation in the third country. If, ultimately, it cannot guarantee a level of protection essentially equivalent, it must not transfer the personal data.” (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 75).  
Measures to supplement standard data protection clauses can be classified into three categories: contractual, technical, or organizational (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 74).

Exceptions provided in Chapter V of the Regulation  
Article 49, "Exceptions for specific situations," of the GDPR states:  
"1. In the absence of an adequacy decision in accordance with Article 45, paragraph 3, or of appropriate safeguards in accordance with Article 46, including binding corporate rules, a transfer or a set of transfers of personal data to a third country or international organization may only take place if one of the following conditions is met:  
a) the data subject has explicitly consented to the proposed transfer, after being informed of the possible risks to him or her of such transfers due to the absence of an adequacy decision and appropriate safeguards;  
b) the transfer is necessary for the performance of a contract between the data subject and the data controller or for the implementation of pre-contractual measures taken at the request of the data subject;  
(…)”  
The European Data Protection Board (EDPB), in compliance with the objective of ensuring the consistent application of the General Data Protection Regulation, as assigned to it by Article 70 of the GDPR, issued Guidelines 2/2018 on the exceptions contemplated in Article 49 of Regulation 2016/679. Regarding the interpretation of the assumption a) of Article 49 of the GDPR (paragraph 2.1 of Guidelines 2/2018), the EDPB indicates that consent must be specific and informed about the possible risks, stating the following: “this provision requires that data subjects also be informed of the specific risks arising from the fact that their data will be transferred to a country that does not provide an adequate level of protection and that no appropriate safeguards are foreseen for the protection of the data.”  
In the present case, user consent for the storage of cookies during their visit to the website cannot be considered equivalent to having explicitly consented to the international transfer of data, as required by Article 49.1.a) of the GDPR; this consent must be obtained specifically, and for it to be valid, MAPFRE must have previously informed the data subjects of the risks posed by the data transfers to the U.S., in the absence of an adequacy decision and appropriate safeguards in the sense of Article 49, paragraph 1 of the Regulation.

Current legal framework  
On July 10, 2023, the Commission approved the Implementing Decision (EU) 2023/1795 regarding the adequacy of the level of protection of personal data under the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework in accordance with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council, so that international transfers made after this date may be covered by the aforementioned Decision.  
On the website https://www.dataprivacyframework.gov/s/, it can be verified that Google LLC has certified its adherence to the principles of the Data Privacy Framework until September 13, 2024, due to the need to renew this certification annually. Therefore, currently, international data transfers to Google LLC in the U.S. are covered by the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework.  
However, it must be concluded that, on the date when the events subject to the complaint occurred, MAPFRE cannot invoke any of the tools provided for in Chapter V of the GDPR to justify the international transfers of personal data of visitors to its website, particularly unique identifiers, IP addresses, browser data, and metadata, to Google LLC in the United States, with the full application of the doctrine established by the Court of Justice of the European Union in the judgment of the Schrems II case, which invalidated the decision on the "EU-U.S. Privacy Shield."  
Consequently, in accordance with the evidence available in this resolution of the warning procedure, it is considered that the known facts constitute an infringement attributable to MAPFRE for violation of Article 44, “General principle for transfers,” of the GDPR, which states that transfers of personal data that are subject to processing or will be processed after their transfer to a third country or international organization may only take place if, subject to the other provisions of the GDPR, the controller and the processor comply with the conditions established in this chapter, including those relating to further transfers of personal data from the third country or international organization to another third country or another international organization.
