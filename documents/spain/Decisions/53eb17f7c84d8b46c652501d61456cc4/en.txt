**Procedure for Changing Energy Supplier**  
Naturgy expresses (DocumentNaturgy#1) that the parties involved in the energy supplier change procedure are three (in addition to the customer user):  
- “Incoming supplier: This is the new supplier that requests the distributor to change the supplier in its favor.  
- Outgoing supplier: The supplier that stops supplying the consumer once the process is completed and the supplier change is effective.  
- Distributor: This is the owner of the distribution network where the supply point is generally located, the entity that bills the access fees, the one responsible for the information system of the supply points, and the one in charge of reading at the consumption points. The distributor, therefore, processes the requests for supplier changes.”  

Naturgy describes the energy supplier change procedure in the following paragraphs of DocumentNaturgy#1:  
“The process begins with the customer signing the supply contract with the new supplier (incoming). Subsequently, the incoming supplier will send a change request to the distributor through the Transport Distribution Communication System (hereinafter, SCTD). The distributor performs the format and content validations defined for each process and, consequently, evaluates whether to accept or reject its processing under criteria also defined and included in the regulations governing this communication system. If the request is accepted, it activates the change and informs both the incoming and outgoing suppliers of this change.  
All exchanges of information between distributors and suppliers are carried out using the format defined by the National Commission for Markets and Competition (hereinafter CNMC) for each process. The format defines the flow of communications or messages exchanged by the involved agents, namely, distributors and suppliers, the established deadlines for sending messages, the cases of concurrency with other processes, the reasons for rejection and incidents, as well as the electronic communication files that must be used to transmit each message.  
Thus, in the case of changing suppliers, there are two different formats:  
1. One for changing suppliers without modifications to the access contract, called:  
   a. C1 in the electricity sector  
   […]  
2. Another for changing suppliers with modifications to the access contract (e.g., change of contract holder, change of ATR, etc.), called:  
   a. C2 in the electricity sector  
   […]  
As indicated, all requests for supplier changes, with or without contractual modification, are processed through the SCTD system of each distributor, which manages both the exchange of messages defined for each process and the execution, activation, and communication of the same, according to the information exchange file formats between distributors and suppliers established by the CNMC in the resolutions corresponding to Files INF/DE/152/15 and INF/DE/011/19.”  

The CNMC document “RESOLUTION APPROVING THE FORMATS OF THE INFORMATION EXCHANGE FILES BETWEEN DISTRIBUTORS AND SUPPLIERS OF ELECTRICITY AND NATURAL GAS” - hereinafter the Resolution - (see Diligence References) includes information about the file formats C1 (Change of supplier without modifications to the access contract) and C2 (Change of supplier with modifications to the access contract). In both cases, it defines the concepts of “format validations” and “content validations” that the distributor must perform for the request to be accepted:  
- “Format validations: Formal checks carried out by the distributor on the change request of a supply point to verify that the XML messaging (eXtensible Markup Language) used corresponds to the definition given in the basic structure of the XSD (XML Schema Definition, a schema language used to describe the structure and restrictions of the contents of XML documents). These format validations will be performed on the total content of the XML message regardless of the mandatory or optional nature of the fields contained in the message. If an XML message does not pass the format validation, it does not enter the distributor's systems.”  
- “Content validations: Checks performed by the distributor on the content of the supplier change request to verify that the specific requirements of the process and common to all distributors are met (e.g., minimum mandatory content of the request). They are also referred to as cross validations.”  
- “Accepted request: C1 request that passes the distributor's format and content validation filters, for which the distributor assumes a commitment to activation.”

- "Request rejected: C1 request that passes the distributor's format validation filter but does not pass the content validation."  
- "Request accepted: C2 request that passes the distributor's format and content validation filters, for which the distributor assumes a commitment to activation subject to the technical limitations of the process."

III  
On the data controller  
The GDPR defines in Article 4.7) the figure of the “data controller” as “the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; if the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of the processing, the data controller or the specific criteria for its appointment may be established by the law of the Union or of the Member States.”  
The Guidelines 7/2020 of the European Data Protection Board, adopted on 07/07/2021 (version 2.0), clarify that the concept of data controller is a “functional” concept, as its purpose is to assign responsibilities based on the actual role played by each party in the processing carried out.  
The Guidelines (section 12) state that “the legal status of ‘data controller’ […] of the participants should be established in principle based on their specific activities in a given situation and not based on the formal designation of a participant as ‘data controller’ […] This implies that the assignment of the role of data controller […] should normally derive from an analysis of the facts or circumstances of the case and, consequently, is not negotiable.” They add (section 13) that the concept of data controller is an “autonomous” concept as it must be interpreted fundamentally according to Union law on data protection and “should not be affected by other concepts —with which it sometimes collides or overlaps— from other areas of law.”  
Based on what is stated by the Guidelines 7/2020 of the EDPB, we must emphasize that in the process of changing electricity suppliers with or without a change of holder, both the incoming supplier and the distributor act as controllers regarding the processing of personal data they carry out in compliance with the functions assigned to them by sector regulations.  
Thus, when the supplier collects the data of its client, particularly regarding the CUPS data that now concerns us, and transmits it to the distributor connected to a

5. The requirement for sanctioning responsibility necessitates the concurrence of the subjective element of the infringement, an essential requirement since the principle of culpability governs our sanctioning Administrative Law, which prevents the imposition of sanctions based on the objective responsibility of the alleged infringer.  
As the Constitutional Court (T.C.) has repeatedly confirmed, the presence of the subjective element or culpability in a broad sense is a condition for the emergence of sanctioning responsibility. Furthermore, it clarifies that the direct responsibility of legal entities, accepted in our sanctioning Administrative Law, does not mean that the subjective element of fault has been eliminated in the case of administrative infringements committed by legal entities, but simply that this principle must necessarily be applied differently than it is with respect to natural persons.  
We refer to STC 246/1991, of 19/12/1991, which in its Legal Foundation 2 states:  
“[…], regarding fault, this Court has declared that, indeed, the Spanish Constitution undoubtedly enshrines the principle of culpability as a basic structural principle of Criminal Law […]. This principle of culpability also governs in matters of administrative infringements, since to the extent that the sanction for such infringement is one of the manifestations of the State's ius puniendi, an objective or faultless liability regime is inadmissible in our legal system (STC 76/1990). Even this Court has qualified the principle of personal responsibility for one's own acts - the principle of the personality of the penalty or sanction - as ‘correct’ (STC 219/1988).  
All of this, however, does not prevent our Administrative Law from admitting the direct responsibility of legal entities, thus recognizing their capacity to infringe. This does not mean, in any way, that in the case of administrative infringements committed by legal entities the subjective element of fault has been eliminated, but simply that this principle must necessarily be applied differently than it is with respect to natural persons. This distinct construction of the imputability of the authorship of the infringement to the legal entity arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which these subjects respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the norms to which they are subject.”  
Law 40/2015 on the Legal Regime of the Public Sector provides in Article 28, under the heading "Responsibility":  
“1. Only natural and legal persons, as well as, when a Law recognizes them the capacity to act, groups of affected parties, unions, and entities without legal personality and independent or autonomous assets, that are responsible for the same by way of intent or fault, may be sanctioned for acts constituting administrative infringement.” (The underlining is from the AEPD)
