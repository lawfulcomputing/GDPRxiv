From the procedure instructed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following:  
BACKGROUND  
FIRST: A.A.A. (hereinafter, the complainant), represented by NOYB (European Centre for Digital Rights, Goldschlagstraße 172/4/3/2, 1140 Vienna, ZVR: 1354838270), filed a complaint with the Spanish Agency for Data Protection on August 20, 2020. The complaint is directed against VACACIONES EDREAMS, S.L. (hereinafter, EDREAMS) with NIF B61965778 (hereinafter, EDREAMS). The grounds for the complaint are as follows:  
On 14/08/2020 at 4:44:00 AM, the complainant visited the website ***URL.1 while connected to the Google account associated with the address ***EMAIL.1 belonging to the complainant. The complainant had logged into their Google account associated with their email inbox. Through HTML code embedded in the webpage “www.edreams.es,” personal data (at least, the IP address and "cookies") of the complainant were collected and transferred to Google LLC, 1600 Amphitheatre Parkway, Mountain View, CA 94043, USA, through the Google Analytics and Google Ads services contracted by the portal operator, EDREAMS.  
The complainant argues that the transfer of personal data to the USA by the operator through its data processor lacks legal basis, as the CJEU has invalidated the decision on the "EU-US Privacy Shield" in ruling C-311/18 ("Schrems II"), and the operator can no longer base the transfer of data to Google in the USA on an adequacy decision under Article 45 of the GDPR.  
It also asserts that the operator cannot base the transfer of data on the standard contractual clauses provided for in Articles 46.2.c and 46.2.d of the GDPR if the third country of destination does not guarantee an adequate level of protection for the personal data transferred under those clauses, according to EU law.  
Furthermore, it indicates that the CJEU has explicitly ruled that subsequent transfers to companies covered by Article 50 of the United States Code (USC), such as Google, violate the relevant provisions of Chapter 5 of the GDPR, since, under this regulation (50 USC Par. 1881, or "FISA 702"), they are subject to US intelligence surveillance. The complainant understands that, as evidenced by the "Snowden Slides" and Google's own Transparency Report, this entity is actively providing personal data to the US government under that provision.  
The complainant asserts that the operator and Google have continued to rely on the "EU-US Privacy Shield" and the standard data protection clauses for the aforementioned data transfers.  
And requests that the complaint be fully investigated and that it be established:  
1. What personal data was transferred from the operator to Google LLC in the USA or to any other third country and international organization.  
2. On what transfer mechanism under Article 44 and subsequent articles of the GDPR the operator based this data transfer.  
3. Whether the provisions of the Google Analytics Terms of Service and the new Data Processing Terms for Google Ads in their current version and effective from 31/08/2020 comply with the requirements of Article 28 GDPR regarding the transfer of personal data to third countries.  
4. That an immediate prohibition or suspension of any data flow to Google LLC in the USA be imposed and that the return of such data to the EU/EEA or to another country that offers adequate protection under Articles 58(2)(d), (f), and (j) GDPR be ordered.  
5. That an administrative fine be imposed against the Operator and Google.

In order to limit itself to what is strictly necessary for the provision of the service, the CNIL emphasizes that these trackers must:  
- Have a purpose strictly limited to the exclusive measurement of the audience of the site or application (performance measurement, detection of navigation problems, optimization of technical performance or ergonomics, estimation of the power of the necessary servers, analysis of the consulted content), solely on behalf of the publisher;

- Serve solely to produce anonymous statistical data;  
- Not lead to a matching of the data with other processing operations nor to the transmission of the data to third parties.

- Users of the application will be informed about these trackers, for example, through the privacy policy of the site or mobile application;

Furthermore, Google employs strong technical measures (such as encryption, which is described below) to protect against interception in transit, including attempts at surveillance by government authorities around the world.  
Encryption is a process that takes readable data as input (often referred to as plaintext) and transforms it into an output (often called ciphertext) that prevents the reading of the plaintext. If the data is encrypted, it will be unreadable to a third party without the encryption key, and it cannot be meaningfully accessed by a government agency in the United States or another country unless it goes through formal access channels to obtain the unencrypted text. Encryption is, therefore, an effective means to prevent any ‘covert’ or unauthorized access to customer data.  
Google employs strong technical measures to safeguard personal data and to protect it against interception during transit. Google Analytics by default uses the HTTP Strict Transport Security (HSTS) protocol, which instructs browsers that support HTTP over SSL (HTTPS) to use that encryption protocol for all communications between end users, websites, and Google Analytics servers. This encryption prevents intruders from passively listening to communications between websites and users. More information about Google’s use of HTTPS is available.  

Google protects service-to-service communications at the application layer through a mutual authentication and transport encryption system developed by Google called Application Layer Transport Security (ALTS). The concept of ALTS is similar to that of mutual authentication TLS, but it has been designed and optimized to meet the needs of Google’s data center environments. ALTS authenticates communication between Google services and helps

to protect data in transit. AL TS is a highly reliable and trusted system that provides authentication and security for Google’s Remote Procedure Call (RPC) communications. AL TS requires minimal participation from Google’s own services. When Google services communicate with each other, they do not need to explicitly configure anything to ensure the protection of data transmission; this is the protection of user data by design and by default.  
AL TS ensures that the integrity of Google traffic is protected and encrypted as necessary. After a handshake protocol between the client and the server is completed and the client and server negotiate the necessary shared cryptographic secrets to encrypt and authenticate network traffic, AL TS secures RPC (Remote Procedure Call) traffic by enforcing integrity and optional encryption using the negotiated shared secrets. Google supports multiple protocols to ensure integrity, for example, AES-GMAC (Advanced Encryption Standard) with 128-bit keys. Whenever traffic leaves a physical boundary controlled by or on behalf of Google, for example, in transit over a WAN (Wide Area Network) between data centers, all protocols are automatically updated to provide encryption as well as integrity guarantees.  
HTTPS encryption helps keep users' browsing secure by securely connecting their browser or application with the websites they visit. HTTPS relies on encryption technology -SSL or TLS- to secure these connections. This encryption prevents intruders from passively eavesdropping on communications between websites and users. LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence and applicable regulations  
In accordance with the provisions of Articles 58.2 and 60 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 and 68.2 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Data Protection Agency is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Data Protection Agency shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development and, in so far as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  
II  
Preliminary issues  
In this case, according to the provisions of Articles 4.1 and 4.2 of the GDPR, there is evidence of the processing of personal data, since EDREAMS collects and processes, through the Google Analytics service, among others, the following personal data of natural persons: unique user identifiers (_ga and _gid), the IP address, as well as other data associated with the browser and the browsing itself, among other processes.  
EDREAMS carries out this activity in its capacity as data controller, as it determines the purposes and means of such activity, pursuant to Article 4.7 of the GDPR. Furthermore, it is a cross-border processing, given that EDREAMS is established in Spain, although it provides services to other countries in the European Union.  
In response to the information request made by this Agency on December 10, 2020, EDREAMS communicated that it had decided to integrate the functionality of Google Analytics into its website and that the statistics obtained through Google Analytics affected individuals from several member states of the European Union. Therefore, the processing activity resulting from the integration of the Google Analytics functionality into its website fits the definition of cross-border processing in the sense of Article 4.23.b) of the GDPR.  
The GDPR provides, in its Article 56.1, for cases of cross-border processing, as foreseen in its Article 4.23), regarding the competence of the lead supervisory authority, that, without prejudice to the provisions of Article 55, the supervisory authority of the main establishment or the only establishment of the controller or processor shall be competent to act as the lead supervisory authority for the cross-border processing carried out by such controller or processor in accordance with the procedure established in Article 60. In the case examined, as stated, EDREAMS has its only establishment in Spain, so the Spanish Data Protection Agency is competent to act as the lead supervisory authority.  
On its part, Article 44 of the GDPR regulates the transfer of personal data to third countries.

It should be noted that online identifiers, such as IP addresses or information stored in cookies, can commonly be used to identify a user, especially when combined with other types of similar information. This is illustrated in Recital 30 of the GDPR, which states that the assignment of online identifiers such as IP addresses or cookie identifiers to natural persons or their devices can "leave traces which, in particular when combined with unique identifiers and other information received by servers, can be used to create profiles of natural persons and identify them." In the particular case where the data controller claims not to have the ability to identify the user through the use (alone or in combination with other data points) of such identifiers, it would be expected to disclose the specific means deployed to ensure the anonymity of the collected identifiers. If the opposite were decided, the scope of the right to data protection, guaranteed by Article 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, would be undermined, as it would allow companies to specifically select individuals along with personal information (for example, when visiting a specific website) while simultaneously denying them any right to protection against such singling out. Such a restrictive opinion that would undermine the level of protection for individuals is also not in line with the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter, CJEU), which has repeatedly ruled that the scope of the GDPR must be understood very broadly (see, for example, judgment C-439/19, paragraph 61).  
Google LLC claims that it has "no intention" of using online identifiers to identify the claimant (or other individuals) and that it in fact "does not do this," it should be noted that Article 4(1) of the GDPR does not require an entity to have a specific intention to identify a person. According to the clear wording of Article 4(1) of the GDPR, the term "personal data" is fulfilled when an entity can (has the possibility to) do so.  
A more restrictive interpretation of Article 4(1) of the GDPR would be contrary to the jurisprudence of the CJEU; therefore, the definition of "personal data" is applicable to the exposed data. In this case, the claimant had logged into their Google account at the time the website www.edreams.es was visited. As can be seen in Google LLC's statement of April 9, 2021, the implementation of Google Analytics on a website allows Google to receive information from a specific user who has visited that website.  
In the context of using Google Analytics, and depending on some settings in the Google account configuration, Google is informed that a user connected to a Google account has visited a particular website. Therefore, personal data related to this account is collected.  
Consequently, it must be considered that the data in question are personal data within the meaning of Article 4.1 of the GDPR.

a.  
Decisions of adequacy  
In its judgment in case C-311/18 ("Schrems II"), dated July 16, 2020, the CJEU invalidated the Commission's Implementing Decision (EU) 2016/1250 of July 12, 2016, pursuant to Directive 95/46/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council, regarding the adequacy of the protection provided by the "EU-US Privacy Shield," without maintaining its effects.  
In the absence of an applicable adequacy decision in the present case, the data transfer in question cannot be based on the provisions of Article 45.3 of the GDPR.  
b.  
Appropriate safeguards: Standard data protection clauses  
Article 46, "Transfers subject to appropriate safeguards," of the GDPR, establishes in its paragraph 1 that "In the absence of a decision under Article 45(3), the controller or processor may only transfer personal data to a third country or international organization if it has provided appropriate safeguards and on condition that data subjects have enforceable rights and effective legal remedies."  
Article 46, paragraph 2, of the GDPR provides that "The appropriate safeguards referred to in paragraph 1 may be provided, without requiring any express authorization from a supervisory authority, by:  
(...)  
c) standard data protection clauses adopted by the Commission in accordance with the examination procedure referred to in Article 93(2).  
(...)."  
In the present case, EDREAMS and Google LLC have signed standard contractual clauses for the transfer of personal data to the United States, updated on August 12, 2020, titled "Google Ads Data Processing Terms: Model Contract Clauses, Standard Contractual Clauses for Processors," which could be translated as: "Google Ads Data Processing Terms: Model of Contractual Clauses, Standard Contractual Clauses for Processors" (SCC). These clauses comply with those published by the European Commission in Decision 2010/87/EU.  
In this context, it is noteworthy that standard contractual clauses are a transfer instrument in the sense of Chapter V of the Regulation and were not challenged as such by the CJEU in its judgment of July 16, 2020 (C-311/18). However, the CJEU considered that the contractual nature of these clauses implied that they could not be binding on the authorities of third countries. In particular, the CJEU stated that: "...Therefore, while there are situations in which, depending on the legislation and practices in the third country of...

i.  
General observations on complementary measures  
In its Recommendations 01/2020, dated June 18, 2021 (which can be consulted on the website ***URL.83, although the transcripts included in this document refer to its translation into Spanish), the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) clarified that when the assessment of the legislation or current practices of the third country may affect the effectiveness of the adequate safeguards of the transfer instruments on which the exporter relies, in the context of its specific transfer, as is the case here following the assessment by the CJEU, the exporter must suspend the transfer or implement appropriate complementary measures. The EDPB notes in this regard that "Any complementary measure can only be considered effective in the sense of the CJEU ruling 'Schrems II' to the extent that it addresses the specific deficiencies identified in its assessment of the legal situation in the third country. If, ultimately, it cannot ensure a level of protection that is essentially equivalent, it must not transfer personal data." (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 75).  
The measures to complement the standard data protection clauses can be classified into three categories: contractual, technical, or organizational (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 74).  
Regarding contractual measures, the EDPB stated that: "In some situations, these measures may complement and reinforce the guarantees that the transfer instrument and the relevant law of the third country can provide when, taking into account the circumstances of the transfer, they do not meet all the necessary conditions to ensure a level of protection that is essentially equivalent to that guaranteed in the Union. Given the nature of contractual measures, which generally cannot bind the authorities of that third country when they are not part of the contract, these must be combined with other technical and organizational measures to provide the required level of data protection (...)." (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 99).  
As for organizational measures, the EDPB highlighted that: "… Selecting and applying one or more of these measures will not necessarily and systematically guarantee that your transfer complies with the essential equivalence standard required by Union law. Depending on the specific circumstances of the transfer and the assessment carried out on the law of the third country, organizational measures will be needed to complement contractual or technical measures, in order to ensure a level of protection of personal data that is essentially equivalent to that guaranteed in the Union" (see Recommendations 01/2020, paragraph 128).

ii.  
Complementary measures implemented by Google LLC  
Google LLC, as a contractual partner of EDREAMS and data recipient, adopted contractual, organizational, and technical measures to complement the SCC. In the document dated April 9, 2021, sent to the Austrian data protection authority, which it shared with the other authorities within the framework of Working Group TF101, Google LLC described the measures adopted in detail.  
Taking into account the considerations of the CJEU and the EDPB, it must now be verified whether the complementary measures adopted by Google LLC are effective, meaning that they address the specific issue of the access possibilities of U.S. intelligence services.  
Regarding the “legal and organizational measures” adopted, it should be noted that neither user notification, even if such notification is admissible, nor the publication of a transparency report or a “public policy on the handling of government requests” actually prevents or reduces the access possibilities of U.S. intelligence services. Furthermore, it is unclear how the “careful review of each request” by Google LLC regarding its admissibility is effective as a complementary measure, considering that, according to the CJEU, admissible (legal) requests from U.S. intelligence services do not comply with the requirements of European Data Protection legislation.  
With respect to the “technical measures” adopted, it should be noted that neither Google LLC nor EDREAMS has clarified how the described measures, such as the protection of communications between Google services, the protection of data in transit between data centers, the protection of communications between users and websites, or “site security,” actually prevent or reduce the...

possibilities of access by U.S. intelligence services based on the legal framework of the United States.  
Regarding encryption technologies, as in the case of "data at rest" in data centers, as specifically mentioned by Google LLC as a technical measure, it should be noted that Google LLC, as a data importer, has the obligation to grant access or deliver personal data imported under its control, including the cryptographic keys necessary for the data to be intelligible (see Recommendations 01/2020, point 81). In other words: as long as Google LLC has the ability to access individuals' data in clear text, this technical measure cannot be considered effective in the present case.  
As Google LLC states that "to the extent that Google Analytics data for measurement transferred by website owners is personal data, it should be considered pseudonymous," it should be noted that universal unique identifiers (UUIDs) do not fall within the definition of Article 4.5 of the GDPR. While pseudonymization can be a privacy-enhancing technique, unique identifiers have, as previously described, the specific intent of identifying users, not acting as a safeguard. Apart from this, it has also been previously described why the combination of unique identifiers with other elements (such as browser or device data and IP address) and the possibility of linking such information to a Google account in any case allows for an individual to be identified.  
To the extent that Google LLC refers to an "optional technical measure" through an IP anonymization feature, it should be noted, first of all, that this measure is, as its name indicates, optional and not applicable to all transfers. Furthermore, from Google's response, it is not clear whether this anonymization occurs before the transfer or if the full IP address is transmitted to the United States and only shortened after this transfer to the United States. Therefore, from a technical standpoint, there is potential access to the entire full IP address before it is shortened.  
Thus, the supplementary measures adopted, as presented by Google, are not effective in that none of them address the specific issues in the present case, meaning that none of them prevent the possibilities of access by U.S. intelligence services nor render such accesses ineffective.
