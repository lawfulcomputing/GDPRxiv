3. Regarding the alleged non-compliance with data protection obligations by design:  
CaixaBank asserts that it has not violated Article 25 of the GDPR.  
a. Scope of the principle of data protection by design:  
The banking entity refers to Guidelines 4/2019 of the European Data Protection Board, reproducing part of them to conclude:  
“(...) the EDPB conceives Privacy by Design as a holistic process, which encompasses the evaluation of each and every aspect of the processing, in order to ensure compliance with the principles and guarantees established in Article 5 of the GDPR. Thus, only in the event that the supervisory authorities could appreciate that a data controller has not carried out that process in its entirety, or has only developed it as a mere formality, without applying the process to the specific processing of personal data under analysis, could non-compliance with the provisions of Article 25 be appreciated.  
That is to say, the obligation of compliance with Article 25.1 of the GDPR is not conceived by the EDPB as an obligation of result, in the sense that it could be considered that the results derived from its execution are compliant with what is established in the GDPR, but rather as an obligation of analysis, so that if a data controller has adequately carried out the same, even if the result could be erroneous in the opinion of the supervisory authority, there would not be a violation of the principle of Privacy by Design, but, where applicable, of one of the principles established in Article 5 of the GDPR or of the norms in which those principles are specified.  
To put it graphically, and as has been pointed out previously, it is possible that from the analysis of the processing carried out by a controller, a scope of compliance with the duty of transparency may arise that is subsequently not considered sufficient by the supervisory authority, understanding that the information provided to the data subjects is not adequate. However, this would not imply that the entity in question had not fulfilled its obligation to adopt the necessary measures to comply with the principle of transparency, but rather that this principle would not be considered fully respected by the AEPD. That is to say, the result of that appreciation would be a violation of Articles 5.1 a), 13, and 14 of the GDPR, but not a violation of its Article 25. There would be no grounds to sanction the lack of privacy by design, but rather the alleged non-compliance with the principle or the norm in which it is specified.  
And this circumstance is what occurs in the present case: my client has fully analyzed and evaluated all the measures that should be adopted to ensure compliance with the principles established in Article 5 of the GDPR.

In relation to the allegations that have just been reproduced, it is necessary to bring to attention the difference between the violation of Article 5.1(f) and Article 32 of the GDPR, the different classifications in separate sections of Article 83 of the GDPR, and the different qualification of both for the purposes of prescription in the LOPDGDD.  
Article 5.1(f) of the GDPR is violated when there is a loss of confidentiality or integrity of personal data, which may or may not occur due to the absence or deficiency of security measures.  
This principle merely determines the channel through which the maintenance of confidentiality and integrity can be achieved when it explicitly states "through the application of appropriate technical and organizational measures," which are not strictly security measures.  
There are multiple technical or organizational measures, which are not security-related, that the data controller can implement as a means to ensure this principle.  
However, Article 32 of the GDPR encompasses the obligation to implement appropriate technical and organizational security measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk. Security measures. Only security measures.  
Moreover, its objective is to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, whereas in the case of Article 5.1(f) of the GDPR, confidentiality and integrity must be guaranteed. As can be observed, the two articles pursue different aims, although they may be related.

The GDPR is a directly applicable European regulation in the Member States, which contains a new, complete, and global system aimed at ensuring the protection of personal data uniformly throughout the European Union.  
In relation, specifically and also, to the sanctioning regime provided therein, its provisions apply immediately, directly, and integrally, foreseeing a complete and gapless system that must be understood, interpreted, and integrated absolutely, completely, and integrally, thus leaving its ultimate purpose intact, which is the effective and real guarantee of the fundamental right to the Protection of Personal Data. First of all, we must point out that this judgment is issued under the regulations prior to the GDPR, in which, according to the system provided in the LOPD and in the RLOPD, security measures were perfectly standardized. The principle of privacy by design is a demonstration of the shift from reactivity to proactivity and a direct manifestation of the risk-based approach imposed by the GDPR. Thus, regarding the risks that may be present in the processing for the rights and freedoms of data subjects, the data controller shall carry out an analysis and detection of risks throughout the entire data processing cycle, with the primary and ultimate purpose of protecting the rights and freedoms of data subjects, and not only when the processing actually takes place. In response to the proposed imposition of measures set forth in the proposal for resolution, the respondent expresses surprise at the requirement contained in the legal reasoning of the proposal for resolution, as the AEPD does not indicate why its adoption is necessary to adjust its processing activities to the provisions of the GDPR; the respondent reiterates that with the (...) it is sufficient to avoid the outcome analyzed by the AEPD, reducing the risk to zero for the rights and freedoms of the data subjects; it also claims that it has (...). It alleges that (...). In summary, it cannot deduce what the corrective measures are, which leaves it defenseless.  
On this particular matter, a series of issues must be indicated.  
The first is that the AEPD has provided since the initiation agreement, through the proposal for resolution, the possibility of imposing measures. And this is in consideration of the facts recorded in the initiation agreement and the proven facts stated in the proposal for resolution that determined the necessity for the respondent to adjust its processing to the provisions of the GDPR.  
And all this by virtue of the corrective powers granted to the AEPD as the supervisory authority in Article 58.2 of the GDPR.  
In fact, in this case, measures have been specifically imposed with the proposal for resolution, a procedural moment in which the proven facts have been established that clearly determine what the breaches of the GDPR are that the respondent has incurred, following a phase of proof, composed of three testing practices.

No surprise, therefore, can represent for the respondent what has been warned and indicated to them from the beginning. Throughout the entire process followed in the AEPD - from the filing of the complaint, through the transfer, the preliminary investigative actions, and the sanctioning procedure - the respondent has addressed some issues related to their non-compliance due to the transfer, requirement, or evidence gathering by the AEPD, reactively and not proactively, but not everything and not completely, in light of the text of the resolution.  
Second, the administrative resolution has not only determined in detail and with precision the background and the proven facts from which the infringements attributed to CaixaBank are concluded, but it has also undermined the presumption of innocence of the entity, perfectly demonstrating and justifying what the non-compliances with the GDPR by the respondent are.  
In contrast, the respondent limits themselves to denying their non-compliance, without providing sufficient elements to demonstrate that they have complied with the GDPR.  
An undeniable indication that they are not correctly applying, for example, privacy by design, is evident in the allegations now examined when they reiterate that the (…).  
The respondent makes this absolute assertion without any evidence to support it, (…), in addition to what such an assertion implies in terms of ignorance of data protection regulations, which considers that risks are in continuous evolution, making it necessary to adopt the necessary measures against such changing risks.  
Moreover, what is asserted is not true, since the (…), without any risk being identified or evaluated. It also results that such an assertion represents an evident contradiction with their own subsequent actions, given the changes made in their procedure when the AEPD itself warns them in the initiation agreement of the (…). If the risk to the rights and freedoms of the interested parties was zero after the (…), they will have to explain the reasons for the subsequent modifications.  
Third, the text of the resolution establishes and proves what the constitutive facts of the infringement have been that determine the need for compliance with data protection regulations, from which it is clearly inferred what measures need to be adopted, as these measures must aim to resolve the non-compliances detected by the AEPD, complying with the GDPR.  
Thus, for example, and without any intention of being exhaustive, from a simple reading of the administrative resolution, it is observed that, regarding the specific personal data breach suffered by the complainant due to the lack of availability of their personal data and evidenced by the AEPD, the respondent has ultimately made available to the client the personal data concerning them in full regarding the operation carried out on (…).  
However, regarding the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures of all kinds derived from the application of privacy by design, among other issues, the determination and establishment of those they claim to have implemented have not been approached from the risks to the rights and freedoms of the interested parties (but rather from the risks to the organization), nor is the system managed proactively, in such a way that compliance with privacy has not been achieved.

Therefore, the measures that the responding party claims to have strictly implemented regarding data protection are not sufficient, and the responding party must carry out all necessary actions to correctly apply privacy by design within the framework of compliance management as provided by the GDPR, in accordance with its proactive responsibility.  
In summary, each and every one of the GDPR violations by the responding party is clearly outlined in the proposed resolution and in the resolution itself, from which the measures to be adopted can be inferred.  
All of this is without prejudice to the fact that the specific types of procedures, mechanisms, or instruments for implementing them correspond to the sanctioned party, as it is the data controller who fully understands its organization and must decide, based on proactive responsibility and a risk-based approach, how to comply with the GDPR and the LOPDGDD.  
In any case, the responding party has a DPO who, in compliance with the functions assigned by Article 39 of the GDPR, must inform and advise it, as well as supervise compliance with the GDPR, also assisting in implementing the necessary corrective measures.  
Therefore, the alleged defenselessness is not considered to exist.  
In fact, this Agency does not believe that we are facing a problem of defenselessness, nor a lack of detailed motivation on the part of the AEPD regarding the infringements committed and the corresponding corrective measures imposed, nor even a difficulty in understanding the proposed resolution. Rather, such an allegation of defenselessness responds more to a simple defense strategy of the responding party: it seeks to justify its defenselessness based on the claim that it has complied with the GDPR and, therefore, no longer knows what else it needs to do.  
However, this is not the case, as evidenced and substantiated throughout this administrative resolution.  
CaixaBank has violated the GDPR; it has not adopted, throughout the sanctioning procedure, all necessary measures to comply with current legislation, and it is up to the Agency, in accordance with the powers conferred upon it, to impose corrective measures so that the treatments of the data controller comply with the GDPR and protect the rights and freedoms of all individuals whose personal data is processed, with the aim of preventing the risks from materializing again in the future.
