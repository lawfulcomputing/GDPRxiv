III  
Preliminary Issues  
1. Applicable Sectoral Regulations  
1.1 Pharmacies  
Pharmacies are, according to Article 86.6 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, which approves the consolidated text of the Law on Guarantees and Rational Use of Medicines and Health Products, private healthcare establishments of public interest.  
Their regulation is found, among other applicable norms, in Law 14/1986, of April 25, General Health Law; Law 16/1997, of April 25, on the Regulation of Pharmacy Office Services; Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, which approves the consolidated text of the Law on Guarantees and Rational Use of Medicines and Health Products; as well as in the applicable regional legislation such as, in the case of Catalonia, Law 31/1991, of December 13, on Pharmaceutical Regulation of Catalonia.  
Both Law 14/1986, of April 25, and Law 16/1997, of April 25, state: “the pharmacist owner of the same, assisted, where appropriate, by assistants or auxiliaries, must provide the following basic services to the population:  
1. The acquisition, custody, conservation, and dispensing of medicines and health products.  
2. The monitoring, control, and custody of the dispensed medical prescriptions (…)”.  
For its part, Article 3 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, is more emphatic in stating that the custody, conservation, and dispensing of medicines for human use corresponds exclusively to:  
“a) Pharmacies open to the public, legally authorized.  
b) The pharmacy services of hospitals, health centers, and primary care structures of the National Health System for their application within such institutions or for medicines that require particular monitoring, supervision, and control by the multidisciplinary health care team, in accordance with the qualification granted by the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products for such medicines (…)”.  
Pharmacies are obliged, according to Article 86.3 of Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, to “dispense medicines requested by both individuals and the National Health System under the established regulatory conditions.” However, the principle of free choice of pharmacy must be respected. Thus, this regulation establishes as a serious infringement in its Article 111 b):  
“26th. Restricting the user's freedom in choosing the pharmacy through any act or omission.”  
It is also classified as a serious infringement in the same article:  
“22nd. Defrauding the National Health System or its beneficiaries in relation to the billing and collection of official prescriptions.”  

It is also emphasized that, according to the aforementioned Royal Legislative Decree 1/2015, of July 24, it constitutes a very serious infringement according to Article 111 c):  
“23rd. Conducting, by pharmacies, activities of distribution of medicines to other pharmacies, authorized distribution entities, or other entities, centers, or individuals without authorization to do so.”

For its part, Law 16/1997, of April 25, enshrines the figure of the pharmacist as an essential and indispensable element for the functioning of a pharmacy by determining in its Article 5:  
“1. The presence and professional action of a pharmacist is an inexcusable condition and requirement for the public dispensing of medications. The collaboration of assistants or auxiliaries does not excuse the action of the pharmacist in the pharmacy while it remains open to the public, nor does it exclude their professional responsibility.  
2. The Autonomous Communities may regulate the minimum number of assistant pharmacists, in addition to the owner, who must provide services in pharmacies to ensure adequate professional assistance to users. This regulation must take into account, among other factors, the volume and type of activity of the pharmacies and the schedule of services.  
3. Without prejudice to the action of the assistant, the owner pharmacist shall be responsible for ensuring service to users.”  
Likewise, Article 103 of Law 14/1986, of April 25, General Health Law states:  
“1. The custody, conservation, and dispensing of medications shall correspond to:  
a) Legally authorized pharmacies.  
b) The pharmacy services of hospitals, Health Centers, and Primary Care structures of the National Health System for their application within such institutions or for those requiring particular monitoring, supervision, and control by the multidisciplinary health care team.  
2. Pharmacies open to the public are considered health establishments for the purposes provided in Title IV of this Law.  
3. Pharmacies shall be subject to health planning in the terms established by the special legislation on medications and pharmacies.  
4. Only pharmacists may be owners and holders of pharmacies open to the public.”  
Consequently, the owner of the pharmacy is the one who is responsible for the actions carried out by the pharmacy and to whom responsibilities may be demanded.

In its Article 9, it states:  
“1. 2. After the unequivocal identification of the patient, and where applicable, of the person to whom they delegate, the pharmacist may only access, from the equipment installed in the pharmacy, with the requirements and conditions established in the following section, the necessary data for a correct informed dispensing and follow-up of the treatment and shall dispense exclusively, from among the pending prescriptions, those requested by the patient.

3. Responsibility for processing in the field of personal data protection  
Article 4.1) of the GDPR defines “personal data” as: “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social identity of that natural person.”  
Article 4.2) of the GDPR defines “processing” as: “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or on sets of personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure or destruction.”  
Article 4.7) of the GDPR defines the “data controller” or “controller” as: “the natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; where the law of the Union or of the Member States determines the purposes and means of processing, the controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided for by the law of the Union or of the Member States.” In turn, Article 4.8) of the GDPR defines the “processor” or “processor” as the natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the controller.  
For its part, the Guidelines 07/2020 of the European Data Protection Board on the concepts of “data controller” and “data processor” (hereinafter, Guidelines 07/2020) emphasize that the concepts of data controller and data processor are functional concepts, and it is necessary to establish them based on their specific activities in the analyzed case and not based on the formal designation that may appear in the contract:  
C/ Jorge Juan, 6

28001 – Madrid  
sedeaepd.gob.es
18/50  
“12. The concepts of ‘data controller’ and ‘data processor’ are functional concepts: their objective is to assign responsibilities based on the actual role of each party. This implies that the legal status of ‘data controller’ or ‘data processor’ of the participants must be established primarily based on their specific activities in a given situation and not based on the formal designation of a participant as ‘data controller’ or ‘data processor’ (e.g., in a contract). This implies that the assignment of the role of controller or processor should normally derive from an analysis of the facts or circumstances of the case and, consequently, is not negotiable.” (underlined by the AEPD).  
In short, the identification of the controller must be carried out according to material criteria in accordance with the real conditions and circumstances of each processing operation; it cannot be understood as a mere formal acknowledgment of what is stated, for example, in a contract signed between parties. Thus, there will be occasions when the participants in a chain of processing operations have been respectively identified as controller and processor, and yet, based on who determines the purpose of the processing and the essential means thereof, the role of controller corresponds to someone who was not formally identified as such.  
In addition to what has been stated so far, it should be noted that the aforementioned Guidelines of the EDPB highlight:  
“20. (…) The data controller is the one who decides on certain essential aspects of the data processing. The responsibility for processing can be established in the regulations or inferred from an analysis of the facts or circumstances of the case. It is necessary to focus on the specific processing activities in question and understand who determines them. To this end, the following questions must first be examined: ‘Why is the processing taking place?’ and ‘Who has decided that the processing should be carried out for a specific purpose?’.” (underlined by the AEPD).  
Furthermore, the aforementioned EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 advocate for a broad interpretation of the concept of data controller in order to promote effective and comprehensive protection of data subjects. In this regard, they provide:  
“14. Since the ultimate objective of assigning the role of data controller is to ensure proactive responsibility and effective and comprehensive protection of personal data, the concept of ‘controller’ should be interpreted broadly enough to promote, as far as possible, effective and comprehensive protection of data subjects, in order to ensure the full effectiveness of Union law on data protection, avoid gaps, and prevent possible circumventions of the regulations, without this implying a reduction of the powers of the data processor.” (underlined by the AEPD).
