FIRST: On August 3, 2024, and August 6, 2024, Ms. A.A.A., with ID No. ***NIF.1 (claimant 1) and Mr. B.B.B., with ID No. ***NIF.2 (claimant 2), filed separate complaints with the Spanish Agency for Data Protection. The reasons for the complaint are as follows: claimant 1 indicates that the respondent company has published that she is a complainant in a workplace harassment process, disclosing her full name along with the word "complainant."  
On April 15, 2024, the Works Council requested the company to open a workplace harassment protocol due to a series of events, with the claimant expanding on the facts concerning her through an email from her personal address to the instructing committee, with which she conducted several interviews.  
The claimant reports that the company opened a harassment protocol on May 6, 2024, with 5 complainants and 10 respondents.  
On July 31, 2024, the company sent an email to the Works Council informing that it considered the instruction concluded, attaching the resolution to each of the complainants, totaling 5, thereby revealing the identity of each complainant, where their names, surnames, and job positions are exposed, including that of the claimant.  
The claimant states that it is the Works Council that forwards the email with the resolution to her.  
At the same time, the company sent the same resolutions (identifying complainants and respondents) to the entire list of individuals, making them clearly identifiable (a total of 15 people).  
The claimant points out that the entire workplace knows she is one of the complainants and is aware of the respondents, a fact that led one of the respondents to post a kiss emoji and the phrase "Thanks for the complaint" in a work WhatsApp group on the same day the resolution was known.  
The claimant indicates that on that same day she suffered an anxiety attack, resulting in medical leave.  
The claimant states that in another WhatsApp group, a third party informs that an email has been sent exposing who the complainants and respondents are, thus failing to maintain the supposed confidentiality of the complainant's information.  
The claimant asserts that she did not authorize the disclosure of her identity.  
She provides:

III  
Preliminary Issues  
Article 4.1) of the GDPR defines “personal data” as: “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.”  
Article 4.2) of the GDPR defines “processing” as: “any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction.”  
Article 4.7) of the GDPR defines the “data controller” or “controller” as: “the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing; where the purposes and means of such processing are determined by Union or Member State law, the controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided for by Union or Member State law.” Likewise, Article 4.8) of the GDPR defines the “processor” or “processor” as the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the controller.  
In the present case, in accordance with the provisions of Articles 4.1 and 4.2 of the GDPR, there is evidence of the processing of personal data, since the party
5/15  
The respondent carries out, among other treatments, the collection and storage of personal data of individuals: name, surname, job position, among others.  
The respondent conducts this activity in its capacity as the data controller, as it is the one that determines the purposes and means of such activity, by virtue of Article 4.7 of the GDPR.  

IV  
                           Unfulfilled obligation: infringement of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR  
The facts revealed in the complaint materialize in that during a workplace harassment procedure where the complainant is the whistleblower, the respondent has sent the closure resolutions of the procedure in such a way that both the whistleblowers and the accused have had access to each other's identities, which could violate the regulations on the protection of personal data.  
Article 5 of the GDPR, "Principles relating to processing," establishes in its paragraph 1, letter f) that:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
(...)  
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, by applying appropriate technical or organizational measures (‘integrity and confidentiality’).”  
(...)  
The documentation in the file provides clear indications that the respondent violated Article 5 of the GDPR, principles relating to processing, by allowing access to personal data, thereby breaching their confidentiality, so that the parties involved in the procedure, both whistleblowers and accused, have had access to each other's identities.  
In this case, the facts complained of stem from the communication on 03/05/2024, to the members of the Investigative Commission in harassment processes, of the harassment complaint, in order to proceed with the actions deemed appropriate, resulting from the "Protocol for the prevention and action against psychological harassment and discriminatory harassment in the company."  
The complaint identified those responsible for alleged behaviors that could be considered as potentially constituting harassment under the terms established in the aforementioned protocol, which is why the Investigative Commission has carried out the corresponding inquiries to clarify the complaint.  
Once the procedure was initiated and after the investigations carried out, the Investigative Commission issued a report concluding that the complaint could not be classified as workplace harassment. The aforementioned report contains the identifying data of the whistleblowers and the accused.  
The complainant in their document dated 03/08/2024 stated that “On Wednesday, July 31, 2024, the company sent an email to the email of the Works Council (attached as Document No. 1) concluding the investigation, attaching the resolution to each of the whistleblowers, 5 in total, thus revealing the identity of each of the whistleblowers, where their names and surnames and their job positions and those accused, including mine, are exposed. It is the email from the Works Council itself that forwards me the email with my resolution. At the same time, the company sends the same resolutions (identifying whistleblowers and accused) to the entire list of people, making them clearly identifiable. A total of 15 people.”  
The respondent in its response dated 13/09/2024 has acknowledged regarding the incident that “While it is true that Servisa includes in its internal guidelines the protection of confidentiality in the processing of this type of files, the reality surrounding the present procedure is that there was not even the mere possibility of an unknown identity for the reasons, moreover, evident, already pointed out.”  
Therefore, it follows from the processing of the workplace harassment procedure in the company that the respondent sends the resolution of the procedure in such a way that all the whistleblowers and the accused have had access to each other's identities, compromising their identity and the confidentiality of their data.  
In accordance with the available indications, it is considered that the facts complained of could constitute an infringement attributable to the respondent, for violation of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR.  

V  
Qualification of the infringement of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR and qualification for the purposes of prescription  
In accordance with the evidence available and without prejudice to what results from the instruction, it is considered that the respondent did not adequately guarantee the confidentiality of personal data.  
The infringement attributed to the entity is classified under Article 83.5 a) of the GDPR, which considers that the infringement of “the basic principles for processing, including the conditions for consent under Articles 5, 6, 7, and 9” is subject to sanction, according to paragraph 5 of the aforementioned Article 83 of the cited Regulation, “with administrative fines of up to €20,000,000 or, in the case of an undertaking, an amount equivalent to 4% of the total annual global turnover of the previous financial year, opting for the higher amount.”  
The LOPDGDD in its Article 71, Infringements, states that: “Acts and behaviors referred to in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6 constitute infringements.”

In accordance with the provisions of Article 50 of the LOPDGDD, this Resolution will be made public once it has been notified to the interested parties.  
Against this resolution, which concludes the administrative pathway as provided by Article 114.1.c) of Law 39/2015, of October 1, on the Common Administrative Procedure of Public Administrations, the interested parties may file a contentious-administrative appeal before the Contentious-Administrative Chamber of the National Court, in accordance with the provisions of Article 25 and Section 5 of the fourth additional provision of Law 29/1998, of July 13, regulating the Contentious-Administrative Jurisdiction, within a period of two months from the day following the notification of this act, as provided in Article 46.1 of the aforementioned Law.  
However, in accordance with the provisions of Article 90.3.a) of the LPACAP, the final resolution in the administrative pathway may be provisionally suspended if the interested party expresses their intention to file a contentious-administrative appeal. If this is the case, the interested party must formally communicate this fact by means of a written document addressed to the Spanish Agency for Data Protection, submitting it through the Agency's Electronic Registry [https://sedeagpd.gob.es/sede-electronica-web/], or through any of the other registries provided for in Article 16.4 of the aforementioned Law 39/2015, of October 1. They must also provide the Agency with documentation that proves the effective filing of the contentious-administrative appeal. If the Agency is not aware of the filing of the contentious-administrative appeal within two months from the day following the notification of this resolution, it will consider the provisional suspension to be concluded.
