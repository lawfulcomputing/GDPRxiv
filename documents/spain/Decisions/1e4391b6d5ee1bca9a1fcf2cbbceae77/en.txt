CONCLUSIONS  
1. The complainant received an email that was addressed to 64 WALLBOX customers. The rest of the customers were in blind copy, so they could not see their email addresses. This has led to the knowledge of the following personal data by WALLBOX customers:  
- If one of those 64 customers replied to all recipients of the email, the complainant would receive an email from that customer in which the email address of that customer appeared as the sender and the pseudonym of that email address (which, in many cases, coincides with the name and surname of the email address owner). According to statements from the complainant, this occurred with 4 customers.  
- The 64 customers to whom the email was addressed received within the email (in the “CC” field of the email header) the email address of the complainant and their pseudonym.  
2. The complainant indicates that they have informed the 4 customers from whom they received emails that this situation has occurred.  
3. WALLBOX has conducted an assessment of the necessity to notify the breach to the AEPD and the necessity of communicating the breach to the affected individuals following the former “Guide for the Management and Notification of Security Breaches” from 2018 prepared by the AEPD and ISMS Forum Spain. WALLBOX provides evidence that it has conducted this assessment following Annex III of this guide, resulting in the conclusion that neither notification to the AEPD nor communication to the affected individuals was necessary.

Competence and applicable regulations  
In accordance with the provisions of Articles 58.2 and 60 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, regarding the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and the free movement of such data (GDPR), and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 and 68.2 of Organic Law 3/2018, of December 5, on the Protection of Personal Data and the guarantee of digital rights (hereinafter, LOPDGDD), the Director of the Spanish Agency for the Protection of Data is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for the Protection of Data shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, this organic law, the regulatory provisions issued for its development, and, in so far as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  

II  
Preliminary issues  
In the present case, in accordance with the provisions of Articles 4.1 and 4.2 of the GDPR, there is evidence of the processing of personal data, since WALLBOX collects and retains, among others, the following personal data of natural persons: name and surname and email address, among other processing activities. WALLBOX carries out this activity in its capacity as data controller, as it is the one who determines the purposes and means of such activity, pursuant to Article 4.7 of the GDPR. Moreover, this is a cross-border processing, given that WALLBOX has its main establishment in Spain, although it provides services to other countries in the European Union.  
The GDPR provides, in its Article 56.1, for cases of cross-border processing, as foreseen in its Article 4.23), regarding the competence of the lead supervisory authority, that, without prejudice to the provisions of Article 55, the supervisory authority of the main establishment or the only establishment of the controller or processor shall be competent to act as the lead supervisory authority for the cross-border processing carried out by such controller or processor in accordance with the procedure established in Article 60. In the case examined, as stated, WALLBOX has its main establishment in Spain, so the Spanish Agency for the Protection of Data is competent to act as the lead supervisory authority.  
Article 4, paragraph 12 of the GDPR broadly defines “personal data breaches” (hereinafter, security breach) as “all breaches of security that lead to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or unauthorized communication or access to such data.”  
In this case, there is a personal data breach under the circumstances mentioned above, categorized as a confidentiality breach, as an email was sent without concealing the email address of the complaining party.  
Within the principles of processing provided for in Article 5 of the GDPR, the integrity and confidentiality of personal data is guaranteed in paragraph 1.f) of Article 5 of the GDPR. On the other hand, the security of personal data is regulated in Articles 32, 33, and 34 of the GDPR, which regulate the security of processing, the notification of a personal data breach to the supervisory authority, and the communication to the data subject, respectively.  

III  
Principle of integrity and confidentiality  
Article 5.1.f) “Principles relating to processing” of the GDPR establishes:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
(...)  
f) processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, by applying appropriate technical or organizational measures (‘integrity and confidentiality’).”  
In this case, it is noted that the personal data of the complaining party, contained in WALLBOX's database, were improperly exposed to third parties by sending an email without concealing their address. Furthermore, the complaining party was able to access the names and surnames and email addresses of 4 data subjects who responded to the first message in question using the reply-all option.  
As evidenced in the file, an email was manually sent to 65 people in response to a generic information request from the data subjects, and the email address of the complaining party was included in the CC field and not BCC, making it accessible to those other 64 people. Additionally, 4 of these individuals responded to the complaining party, allowing them to access their names and surnames and email addresses.  
In accordance with the evidence available in this initiation agreement of the sanctioning procedure, and without prejudice to what may result from the instruction, it is considered that the known facts could constitute an infringement attributable to WALLBOX for violation of Article 5.1.f) of the GDPR.
