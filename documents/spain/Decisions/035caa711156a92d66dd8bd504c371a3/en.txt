RESOLUTION OF SANCTIONING PROCEDURE  
From the procedure instructed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  

BACKGROUND  
FIRST:  
On September 26, 2022, the Division of Technological Innovation of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection was notified of a security breach of personal data reported by TELEFONICA DE ESPAÑA SAU with NIF A82018474 (hereinafter, Telefónica) as the data controller.  
The received document informs the following:  
- Start date of the breach: September 16, 2022.  
- Date of detection that the breach affects personal data: September 23, 2022.  
- Resolution date: September 20, 2022.  
- The data is not encrypted.  
- Summary of the incident: “(…)”  
- Approximate number of individuals whose personal data is processed, referring exclusively to the processing regarding which the personal data breach occurred: 6,000,000  
- Number of individuals whose data has been affected by the breach: 1,021,253  
- Categories of affected data: Contact data, access credentials or identification (username and/or password).  
- As a consequence of the incident, confidentiality has been compromised.  
- The authorities have not been informed.  
- Communication to the affected individuals is pending decision.  

SECOND:  
As a consequence of the known facts, on September 30, 2022, the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection ordered the General Subdirectorate of Data Inspection (SGID) to carry out the appropriate preliminary investigations in order to determine a possible violation of data protection regulations.  

THIRD:  
After analyzing the notification of the personal data breach and by virtue of the functions and powers of this Agency referred to in Article 47 of Organic Law 3/2018 as well as Article 58 of Regulation (EU), on September 30, 2022, Telefónica was ordered to communicate the personal data breach to the affected individuals in accordance with Article 34 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter GDPR), without undue delay, so that, once they are aware of it, they can take the measures they deem appropriate to avoid any risks that may affect them.  
Against this Resolution, Telefónica filed a request for reconsideration on October 13, 2022, arguing that a complete forensic analysis was not available and that the data affected by the incident is considered non-identifiable to a third party outside Telefónica, which was dismissed by Resolution of October 20, 2022.

- It is stated that the breach was resolved on September 20, 2022.  
- It is stated that on September 23, 2022, it was detected that the breach had affected personal data.  
- It is indicated that, in total, a total of 1,407,257 individuals have been affected, all of whom reside in Spain.

III On the nature of the data affected by the breach  
Article 4 of the GDPR defines in its paragraph 1) "personal data" as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ("the data subject"); an identifiable natural person is considered to be any person whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that person.  
It follows that the concept of personal data requires the concurrence of a double element: on the one hand, the existence of information or data, and on the other, that such data can be linked to an identified or identifiable natural person.  
The concept of identifiable person is also addressed in Recital 26 of the GDPR, which states the following:  
(26) The principles of data protection should apply to all information concerning an identified or identifiable natural person. Pseudonymized personal data, which could be attributed to a natural person by the use of additional information, should be considered information about an identifiable natural person. To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means reasonably likely to be used by the data controller or any other person to identify the natural person, directly or indirectly, should be taken into account. To determine whether there is a reasonable likelihood that means will be used to identify a natural person, all objective factors, such as the costs and time required, should be considered.

www.aepd.es  
28001 – Madrid  
sedeagpd.gob.es
66/128  
for identification, taking into account both the technology available at the time of processing and technological advancements. Therefore, the principles of data protection should not apply to anonymous information, that is, information that does not relate to an identified or identifiable natural person, nor to data that has been rendered anonymous in such a way that the data subject is no longer identifiable, or ceases to be so. Consequently, this Regulation does not affect the processing of such anonymous information, including for statistical or research purposes.  
Thus, this concept of personal data cannot be broader. The National Court in its ruling of 08/03/2002 has indicated that for personal data (as opposed to dissociated data) to exist, it is not essential for there to be a full match between the data and a specific person; it is sufficient that such identification can be made without disproportionate effort.  
With the current wording of the GDPR, it is automatically inferred that a landline phone number is personal data concerning the holder of the line - it is an identifier - which is sufficient to make them identifiable.  
Opinion 4/2007 on the concept of personal data from the Article 29 Working Party, adopted on June 20, 2007, determines regarding what is identifiable that “a natural person is ‘identifiable’ when, even if they have not yet been identified, it is possible to do so (which is the meaning of the suffix ‘ble’). (…) Identification is normally achieved through specific data that we can call ‘identifiers’ and that have a privileged and very close relationship with a particular person…”.  
In this sense, in the case of the landline phone number, it should be noted the existence of subscriber directories, provided for in Article 72 of Law 11/2022, of June 28, General Telecommunications Law (hereinafter, LGTel), and developed through Royal Decree 424/2005 of April 15, which approves the Regulation on conditions for the provision of electronic communications services, universal service, and user protection (in force as long as it does not oppose the LGTel in accordance with the provisions of the sole repealing provision, section c).  
Thus, Article 30.4 of the aforementioned RD 424/2005 indicates that “Without prejudice to the provisions of Article 67, regarding the data relating to each subscriber, at least the following information must be included: a) Name and surname or business name. b) Subscriber number or numbers. c) Postal address of the residence, except for floor, letter, and staircase. d) Specific terminal that they wish to declare, if applicable. …”  
And it continues to indicate in its section 6 that “…The data will be listed in alphabetical order of the first surname or business name. After the first surname, the second will be fully reflected, followed by a comma, the first name or their initials. Associated with each number, the subscriber's address will also appear, without specification of floor or letter, and, if applicable, an identifier of the type of terminal (normal telephone, fax, ISDN, videoconference, mobile phone, text telephone for deaf people, among others) that the subscriber has expressed a desire to have included in such a way that allows for the record of the content of the request and the identity of the requester.”

From all of this, it can be inferred that a fixed telephone number is indeed associated with other identifying data of a natural person.  
In this regard, and merely for illustrative purposes, it is appropriate to recall Opinion 5/2000 on the use of public telephone directories for reverse or multi-criteria search services (Reverse Directories) by the Article 29 Working Party, adopted on April 13, 2000, which raised the ease of obtaining other personal data from the telephone number of the line holder.  
Thus, identification does not have to be direct or complete; an indirect identification that makes a person identifiable in any way is sufficient.  
Lastly, it turns out that for a person to be identifiable, it is not necessary for all the information that allows identifying the interested party to be held by a single person.  
In the case of a fixed telephone number, if a third party unrelated to the data controller obtains it, it will be considered personal data since it is possible to singularize the person, even if the information does not refer to an identified person— it does allow identifying the line holder, in the sense outlined in Recital 26 of the GDPR:  
(26) The principles of data protection must apply to all information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Pseudonymized personal data, which could be attributed to a natural person through the use of additional information, must be considered information about an identifiable natural person. To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means, such as singularization, that the data controller or any other person could reasonably use to identify the natural person directly or indirectly must be taken into account. To determine whether there is a reasonable likelihood that means will be used to identify a natural person, all objective factors, such as the costs and time required for identification, must be considered, taking into account both the technology available at the time of processing and technological advancements. Therefore, the principles of data protection should not apply to anonymous information, that is, information that does not relate to an identified or identifiable natural person, nor to data that has been rendered anonymous in such a way that the interested party is no longer identifiable, or ceases to be so. The principle of integrity and confidentiality imposes on the data controller the obligation to guarantee the integrity and confidentiality of personal data by preventing the alteration of personal data, its loss, unauthorized or unlawful processing or access through appropriate technical and organizational measures. The existence of this fundamental right is not possible if confidentiality, integrity, and availability of personal data are not guaranteed.  
Hence, the integrity and confidentiality of personal data are considered essential to prevent data subjects from suffering negative effects. Therefore, they must be processed in a manner that ensures adequate integrity and confidentiality of

personal data, especially to prevent unauthorized access, processing, or use of such data.  
Ultimately, it is the data controller who has the obligation to integrate the necessary guarantees into the processing, in order to comply with and be able to demonstrate compliance, in accordance with the principle of proactive responsibility, while respecting the fundamental right to data protection.  
In this regard, it should be noted that the confidentiality of personal data is regulated in Article 5 of the GDPR, being, therefore, one of the principles related to processing. The principles related to processing are, on one hand, the starting point and the closing clause of the legal framework for data protection, constituting true guiding rules of the system with intense expansive force; on the other hand, having a high level of specificity, they are mandatory norms that can be infringed.  
Therefore, the principle of confidentiality must inform all aspects, rights, and obligations involved in the processing of personal data. This is so because the breach of confidentiality leads to the loss of control over the data for its holders, which can pose serious risks to their rights and freedoms.  
Article 5.1(f) of the GDPR establishes a clear obligation of compliance consisting of preventing unauthorized or unlawful processing by implementing appropriate security measures. Consequently, data controllers must be able to guarantee the confidentiality and integrity of personal data to prevent a third party from accessing data that does not belong to them, as it is precisely their responsibility to process personal data in accordance with the GDPR and LOPDGDD. For this reason, the diligence exercised by them is fundamental to avoid such access.

IX Article 32 of the GDPR  
Article 32 "Security of processing" of the GDPR states:  
“1. Taking into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, which may include, among others:  
a) the pseudonymization and encryption of personal data;  
b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability, and resilience of processing systems and services;  
c) the ability to restore the availability and access to personal data quickly in the event of a physical or technical incident;  
d) a process for regularly verifying, assessing, and evaluating the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing.  
2. In assessing the appropriateness of the level of security, particular consideration shall be given to the risks presented by the processing of data, in particular as a result of the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data.  
3. Adherence to an approved code of conduct pursuant to Article 40 or to an approved certification mechanism pursuant to Article 42 may serve as an element to demonstrate compliance with the requirements set out in paragraph 1 of this Article.  
4. The controller and the processor shall take measures to ensure that any person acting under the authority of the controller or the processor and having access to personal data may only process such data on instructions from the controller, unless required to do so by Union or Member State law.”

Article 32 does not establish static security measures; rather, it is the responsibility of the data controller to determine the necessary security measures to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of personal data. Therefore, the same data processing may involve different security measures depending on the specific circumstances in which such data processing takes place.  
In line with these provisions, Recital 75 of the GDPR states: “The risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, of varying severity and probability, may arise from data processing that could cause physical, material, or immaterial harm, particularly in cases where processing may lead to discrimination, identity theft or fraud, financial losses, damage to reputation, loss of confidentiality of data subject to professional secrecy, unauthorized reversal of pseudonymization, or any other significant economic or social harm; in cases where data subjects are deprived of their rights and freedoms or prevented from exercising control over their personal data; in cases where the processed personal data reveal ethnic or racial origin, political opinions, religion or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, data concerning health, or data concerning sexual life, or criminal convictions and related security measures; in cases where personal aspects are evaluated, in particular the analysis or prediction of aspects related to work performance, economic situation, health, personal preferences or interests, reliability or behavior, situation or movements, in order to create or use personal profiles; in cases where personal data of vulnerable individuals, particularly children, are processed; or in cases where processing involves a large amount of personal data and affects a large number of data subjects.” (the underlining is ours)  
Furthermore, Recital 83 of the GDPR states: “In order to maintain security and prevent processing from infringing the provisions of this Regulation, the controller or the processor must assess the inherent risks of processing and implement measures to mitigate them, such as encryption. These measures must ensure an adequate level of security, including confidentiality, taking into account the state of the art and the cost of their implementation in relation to the risks and the nature of the personal data that must be protected. When assessing the risk in relation to data security, the risks arising from the processing of personal data must be taken into account, such as the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, or alteration of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or the unauthorized communication or access to such data, which may in particular cause physical, material, or immaterial harm.” (the underlining is ours)  
Ultimately, the first step in determining security measures will be the risk assessment. Once assessed, it will be necessary to determine the security measures aimed at reducing or eliminating the risks associated with data processing.

Data security requires the application of appropriate technical or organizational measures in the processing of personal data to protect such data against access, use, modification, dissemination, loss, destruction, or accidental, unauthorized, or unlawful damage. In this sense, security measures are key to guaranteeing the fundamental right to data protection. The existence of the fundamental right to the protection of personal data is not possible if confidentiality, integrity, and availability of such data cannot be guaranteed. It should not be forgotten that, in accordance with Article 32.1 of the cited GDPR, the technical and organizational measures to be applied to ensure an adequate level of security in relation to the risk must take into account the state of the art, the costs of implementation, the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the variable probability and severity risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. The approach must be focused on the risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons and not on the risks that the processing entails for the organization, centering it, as in the examined case, solely on information security measures.  
Therefore, Telefónica, when assessing risks and determining the appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure an adequate level of security in relation to the risk, is obliged to take into account the specific activity that its business entails, which involves continuously and on a large scale processing personal data (numerous data to be collected, processed, stored, etc.); the type of data processed: fixed telephone number, technical data and configuration of wifi network access equipment, etc.; the context: use of an application accessible through a web portal on the internet, that is, in a non-isolated environment, which entails risks arising from the very interconnectivity that the network implies, which must be addressed in a specialized manner.  
Therefore, due to the activity in which it is engaged, Telefónica is obliged to carry out a highly specialized analysis of the risks and the implementation of appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure an adequate level of security in relation to the risk of its activity for the rights and freedoms of individuals.  
In the present case, as previously noted, Telefónica suffered a cyberattack on its application ***APP.1 through an internet portal, causing a security breach consisting of a breach of confidentiality due to access to personal data of its customers contained in said applications.  
The application ***APP.1 is an application of Telefónica that contains information, including personal data of its customers, which is used for customer support and maintenance purposes by Telefónica's technical teams (diagnosis and treatment of faults in connectivity equipment: router, TV decoder, wifi amplifier, etc.), which, as noted, can be accessed by technicians through a portal published on the Internet.
