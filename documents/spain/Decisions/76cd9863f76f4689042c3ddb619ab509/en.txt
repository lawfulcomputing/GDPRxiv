In response to this allegation, it must be stated that the image of a person is a piece of personal data that can, by itself, make the person to whom it belongs identifiable.  
Indeed, the image perfectly fits the definition of what constitutes personal data under Article 4.1 of the GDPR:  
“‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person;”  
As highlighted in the initiation agreement, the image is a personal attribute unique and individual to each natural person, defined by their height, build, manner of sitting, dressing, etc. Endowed with unique and distinctive features that directly individualize them, associating them with a specific individual, it allows one to know, through it, the age range, sex, skin or hair color, their mannerisms through their way of sitting, their style of dressing, etc., which facilitates the identification of the individual.  
In this regard, Report 139/2017 from the Legal Department of this Agency states that “the image (...) is personal data, just as any information that allows for the determination, directly or indirectly, of their identity (...)”  
As determined in Opinion 4/2007 of the Article 29 Working Party on the concept of personal data, “a natural person can be considered ‘identified’ when, within a group of people, they are ‘distinguished’ from all other members of the group.”  
That is to say, it is possible to singularize that natural person.  
And it is clear that the image of any person can be identified at least by those who are part of their closest circle of acquaintances (family, colleagues from work or social activities, etc).

Analysis of the photograph in which the image of the complainant appears and the jurisprudence referenced by the initiation agreement and the respondent in their written submissions:  
Next, the photograph in which the complainant appears will be examined in order to assess whether he was identifiable.  
In the image subject to the complaint, Mr. A.A.A. is indeed located on the left, sitting on a sofa behind a glass wall.  
The vinyl that decorates the glass wall does not completely cover his face, as DIGIMAN claims, but it does hide a significant part of it. However, a large part of his forehead and hair, the entire right ear, part of the right temple of dark glasses, as well as part of his beard can be seen.  
Despite the colored squares of the vinyl on the glass wall that cover part of his body, the clothing he is wearing, the way he is sitting (the body posture), and his silhouette are perfectly identifiable, with the exception of his feet.  
In relation to this image, it is necessary to refer to Judgment No. 784/2004, of Section 1 of the Civil Chamber of the Supreme Court, dated July 12, 2004, part of whose content is reproduced in legal basis IX of this resolution.  
Despite DIGIMAN's assertion in the written submission to the proposed resolution that the Supreme Court ruling analyzes a case that has nothing to do with the one subject to this sanctioning file, this appreciation is not shared.  
In that ruling, the Supreme Court examined whether a woman, whose photograph had appeared on the cover of a provincial newspaper, was identifiable or not. The Provincial Court had determined that she was not identifiable, following this reasoning:  
“(…) as can be seen in the aforementioned photograph, the ‘face’ of the person appearing naked is not ‘visible’; on the other hand, the ‘silhouette’ does not offer any special, singular, specific signs that, in normal coexistence and public citizen relations, would allow its attribution to a specific and determined person (…)”  
The Supreme Court, for its part, considered that the conclusion reached by the Provincial Court (that the woman whose image appeared in the published photograph was not identifiable) had to be deemed illogical and arbitrary, given that the witnesses who testified in the instance identified that woman in the photograph.  
Therefore, the Supreme Court considers the image of a natural person appearing in a photograph where their face is not visible but their silhouette is to be identifiable.  
In the case analyzed in this sanctioning file, the image of the complainant published by DIGIMAN provides abundant information that allows acquaintances of the complainant to identify him: sex, skin and hair color, hairstyle, physical build, sitting posture, clothing style, age range, as well as part of his face and silhouette.  
In Opinion 4/2007 on the concept of personal data, when referring to anonymous data, it is indicated:  
“For the purposes of the Directive, ‘anonymous data’ can be defined as any information relating to a natural person that does not allow their identification by the data controller or by any other person, taking into account all means that may reasonably be used by the data controller or by any other person to identify that person.  
(…)  
Once again, the decision on whether the data allows for the identification of a person and whether the information can be considered anonymous or not depends on the specific circumstances of each case, so a case-by-case analysis must be carried out (…)”

DIGIMAN claims that the image of the claimant was anonymized  
DIGIMAN claims that the image of the claimant was anonymized.  
The Opinion 4/2007 on the concept of personal data, prepared by the Article 29 Working Group, highlights:  
“Anonymous data  
For the purposes of the Directive, ‘anonymous data’ can be defined as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person that does not allow for their identification by the data controller or by any other person, taking into account all means that may reasonably be used by the data controller or by any other person to identify that person. ‘Anonymized data’ will therefore be the anonymous data that previously referred to an identifiable person, whose identification is no longer possible. Recital 26 also refers to this concept when it states that ‘the principles of protection shall not apply to those data made anonymous in such a way that the data subject can no longer be identified.’” (the underlining is ours).  
In this case, DIGIMAN has not taken any action aimed at anonymizing the image of the claimant. It claims that the image is anonymized simply because the decorative vinyl on the glass covers part of the face and body of the claimant.  
Consequently, the allegation that the image of the claimant was anonymized is dismissed.

VIII  
Right to data protection  
In this case, the claimant bases his claim on the use by the responding party of a photograph that reproduces his image sitting on a sofa. This photograph would have been taken by the responding party to illustrate the work they were doing. The image does not allow for a complete view of the person, but part of them is visible. This, combined with the fact that he is linked to the responding party, where he worked, which is additional information, could make that person identifiable in this specific case. All of this constitutes a processing of personal data of the claimant.  
Although initially, according to the claimant, he gave his verbal authorization to the responding party, allowing publication on websites, catalogs, social networks, web platforms, etc., of images where he appeared in order for the company to promote its services and facilities. After being dismissed, he exercised the right to deletion in 2019, 2020, and 2022. From the content of these writings, it follows that the responding party no longer had the claimant's consent for the processing of his image.  
Consequently, it is considered that the processing of data carried out by the responding party of the claimant, consisting of publishing the image subject to this procedure on its YouTube channel, has been carried out without a legitimate basis as outlined in Article 6 of the GDPR.  
Individuals have the power of disposition over their personal data, including their image, as well as over its dissemination, resulting, without a doubt, in deserving protection the person whose personal data is disseminated in violation of the legal system.  
Thus, the STC 292/2000, of November 30, states that “the content of the fundamental right to data protection consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that empowers the individual to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose that possession or use. These powers of disposition and control over personal data, which are part of the content of the fundamental right to data protection, are legally specified in the authority to consent to the collection, obtaining, and access to personal data, its subsequent storage and processing, as well as its possible use or uses, by a third party, whether the State or a private individual. And this right to consent to the knowledge and processing, whether computerized or not, of personal data requires as indispensable complements, on the one hand, the authority to know at all times who has that personal data and what use they are subjecting it to, and, on the other hand, the power to oppose that possession and uses.”  
Article 6.1 of the GDPR establishes the circumstances that allow for the lawful processing of personal data, in the following sense:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if it meets at least one of the following conditions:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of his or her personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is party or in order to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or of another natural person.  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.

The provisions of paragraph f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to the processing carried out by public authorities in the exercise of their functions."  
On this issue of the lawfulness of the processing, Recital 40 of the aforementioned GDPR also emphasizes that "For processing to be lawful, personal data must be processed with the consent of the data subject or on some other legitimate basis established by law, whether in this Regulation or under another law of the Union or of the Member States to which this Regulation refers, including the necessity of complying with a legal obligation applicable to the data controller or the necessity of performing a contract to which the data subject is a party or for the purpose of taking steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract."
