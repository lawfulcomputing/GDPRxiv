From the procedure instructed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection and based on the following  

BACKGROUND  
FIRST: On 22/11/2021, a complaint was received by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection (AEPD), forwarded by the Catalan Data Protection Authority, filed by A.A.A. (hereinafter, the complainant). The complaint is directed against INMOBILIARIA MESLLOC, S.L., with NIF B61043162 (hereinafter, the respondent or the real estate agency MESLLOC).  
The complaint is based on the fact that the real estate agency MESLLOC, which mediated in the lease contract of a dwelling entered into by the complainant and his partner, provided third-party companies with the personal data of both - name, two surnames, and NIF - without their authorization and without being authorized to make such communication. The communication was made during the settlement of the deposited security deposit. The complainant's and his partner's data were processed by third-party companies to issue invoices in both their names for a repair and a cleaning service in the dwelling.  
The complainant states in his complaint:  
"On 30/08, the delivery of keys and the termination of the lease contract for the apartment located at ***ADDRESS.1, owned by a private individual but with the mediation of the Meslloc properties, is made effective. On 17/09, following a few exchanges of emails (authorizing them to change the door lock and to charge us the difference in the water bill), I receive an email with a PDF file notifying the return of the security deposit made at the beginning. Within this file, the deduction of expenses is also notified: €108.9 for the lock, €34.64 for water, and €175.45 for cleaning. We had not been informed of the existence of this last invoice, nor of its cost, but instead, in the PDF file they send us, my name and ID number and the name and ID number of my partner appear as clients, when we have not authorized the use of this data at any time. Therefore, I consider that the current data protection regulations have been violated by using our name and official documentation (ID) to issue an invoice without our authorization and consent. […]” (The underlining is ours)

In this regard, they specify that the "Claimants had given their express consent to such communications and that, furthermore, such processing takes place within the framework of the compliance or execution of the existing lease contract between the Parties," a contract that allowed that real estate agency to "request and demand, if applicable, compliance with the obligations of the claimants."  
The next two headings (second and third) are dedicated to defending the concurrence of each of the invoked legal bases, but prior to that, the summoned party makes these clarifications:

In the third allegation of its writing - under the title “The claimants validly provided consent for the processing” - the respondent argues that the processing carried out was covered by the legal basis of Article 6.1.a, GDPR. It begins by stating that “the GDPR requires that consent be unequivocal. Applicable provisions  
Article 5 of the GDPR, “Principles relating to processing,” establishes:  
“1. Personal data shall be:  
a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (<<lawfulness, fairness, and transparency>>)  
[...]  
2. The data controller shall be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 and be able to demonstrate it (<<accountability>>)”.  
Article 6 of the GDPR, under the heading “Lawfulness of processing,” determines in its paragraph 1 the circumstances under which data processing is considered lawful:  
“1. Processing shall be lawful only if at least one of the following conditions is met:  
a) the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes;  
b) processing is necessary for the performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party or to take steps at the request of the data subject prior to entering into a contract;  
c) processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;  
d) processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data subject or another natural person.  
e) processing is necessary for the performance of a task carried out in the public interest or in the exercise of official authority vested in the data controller;  
f) processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data, in particular when the data subject is a child.  
The provision in point f) of the first paragraph shall not apply to processing carried out by public authorities in the performance of their tasks.”  
Article 4 of the GDPR, “Definitions,” provides that, for the purposes of this Regulation, “2) <<processing>>: any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment, combination, restriction, erasure, or destruction;”.

VI  
Violation of the principle of lawfulness  
1. The GDPR requires that any processing of personal data complies with the principles established in its Article 5, including that of lawfulness (Article 5.1.a). For a specific processing operation to be lawful, it must be based on at least one of the legal bases listed in Article 6.1 of the GDPR. Among the processing bases included in Article 6.1 of the GDPR, its paragraphs a) and b) refer, respectively, to the consent of the data subject for the processing of their data for one or more specific purposes and to the processing that is necessary for the performance of a contract in which the data subject is a party or for the application at their request of pre-contractual measures.  
The responding party has claimed, both in its response to the information request from the Inspection Sub-Directorate and in its arguments regarding the initiation agreement and the resolution proposal, that the processing carried out was lawful and based on the circumstances of paragraphs a) and b) of Article 6.1 of the GDPR.  
However, the disputed processing is not covered by any of the lawful grounds provided for in Article 6.1 of the GDPR, nor by those described in paragraphs a) and b).  
2. In this sense, the STS of 23/01/1998 affirmed that “although the culpability of the conduct must also be proven, it should be considered in relation to the assumption of the corresponding burden that ordinarily the volitional and cognitive elements necessary to appreciate it are part of the proven typical conduct, and that their exclusion requires evidence of the absence of such elements, or in its normative aspect, that the diligence required by the one claiming its non-existence has been employed; in summary, the mere invocation of the absence of fault is not sufficient for exculpation against typically unlawful behavior.” (The emphasis is ours)  
The attribution of responsibility to legal entities is also unique, so that, in the case of infringements committed by legal entities - as is the matter at hand - although the element of culpability must be present, it necessarily applies differently than it does with natural persons. The SAN of 30/05/2015 (Rec. 163/2014) has highlighted the differences that exist between the attribution of responsibility to a natural person and to a legal person and connects the “blameworthiness” of a certain conduct to a “legal person” with the circumstance that it “provided or did not provide effective protection to the legal good protected by the norm.”  
Therefore, according to the criterion of the STS of 23/01/1998 regarding the proof of culpability, in this case of lack of diligence, “its exclusion requires that the absence of such elements be evidenced, or in its normative aspect, that the diligence required by the one claiming its non-existence has been employed;” Connecting this consideration with the obligation that the GDPR imposes on the data controller to be in a position to demonstrate compliance with the principles of Article 5.1 (the lawfulness principle as it pertains here) and considering the results of the evidence requested from the responding party, it is undeniable that they omitted the appropriate diligence regarding the processing of the data of the tenants being examined here. This lack of diligence must be classified as serious.

In the course of its own activity, the respondent needs to habitually process personal data, and this fact impacts the diligence that is required in complying with the principles governing the processing of data.
