Procedure for changing energy supplier  
Naturgy states (EscritoNaturgy#1) that the parties involved in the procedure for changing the electricity supplier are three (in addition to the customer user):  
- "Incoming supplier: This is the new supplier that requests the distributor to change the supplier in its favor.  
- Outgoing supplier: The supplier that ceases to supply the consumer once the process has been completed and the change of supplier is effective.  
- Distributor: This is the owner of the distribution network where the supply point is generally located, the entity that bills the access tariffs, the one responsible for the information system of the supply points, and the one in charge of the reading at the consumption points. The distributor, therefore, processes the requests for changing the supplier."  
Naturgy describes the procedure for changing the electricity supplier in the following paragraphs of EscritoNaturgy#1:  
"The process begins with the customer's signing of the supply contract with the new supplier (incoming). Next, the incoming supplier will send a change request to the distributor through the Transport Distribution Communication System (hereinafter, SCTD). The distributor performs the format and content validations defined for each process and, consequently, assesses whether to accept or reject its processing under criteria also defined and included in the regulations governing this communication system. If the request is accepted, it activates the change and informs both the incoming and outgoing suppliers of this change."

All information exchanges between distributors and marketers are conducted using the format defined by the National Commission on Markets and Competition (hereinafter CNMC) for each process. The format defines the flow of communications or messages exchanged by the involved agents, namely, distributors and marketers, the established deadlines for sending messages, the assumptions of concurrency with other processes, the reasons for rejection and incidents, as well as the electronic communication files that must be used to transmit each message.  
Thus, in the case of changing marketers, there are two distinct formats:  
1. One for changing marketers without modifications to the access contract, referred to as:  
   a. C1 in the electricity sector  
   […]  
2. Another for changing marketers with modifications to the access contract (e.g., change of contract holder, change of ATR, etc.), referred to as:  
   a. C2 in the electricity sector  
   […]  
As indicated, all requests for changing marketers, with or without contractual modification, are processed through the SCTD system of each distributor, in which both the exchange of messages defined for each process and the execution, activation, and communication of the same are managed, according to the information exchange file formats between distributors and marketers established by the CNMC in the resolutions corresponding to Files INF/DE/152/15 and INF/DE/011/19.  
The CNMC document “RESOLUTION APPROVING THE FORMATS OF THE INFORMATION EXCHANGE FILES BETWEEN DISTRIBUTORS AND MARKETERS OF ELECTRICITY AND NATURAL GAS” - hereinafter the Resolution - (see Diligence References) includes information on the file formats C1 (Change of marketer without modifications to the access contract) and C2 (Change of marketer with modifications to the access contract). In both cases, it defines the concepts of “format validations” and “content validations” that the distributor must carry out for the request to be accepted:  
- “Format validations: Formal checks carried out by the distributor (…) of a supply point in order to verify that the XML messaging (eXtensible Markup Language) used corresponds to the definition provided in the basic structure of the XSD (XML Schema Definition, a schema language used to describe the structure and constraints of the contents of XML documents). These format validations will be performed on the total content of the XML message regardless of the mandatory or optional nature of the fields contained in the message. If an XML message does not pass the format validation, it does not enter the distributor's systems.”

2. The position of IBERCLI regarding the nature of the CUPS when it is processed, in the context of a change of supplier, by the incoming supplier.  
Starting from the ruling of the Supreme Court (STS), Third Chamber, dated 12/07/2019 (Rec. Casación 4980/2018) and from the ruling of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) dated 19/10/2016 (Case C-582/2014-Breyer), and taking into consideration the third and fourth paragraphs of Article 7.2 of Royal Decree 1435/2002, it essentially argues that if the only way through which an incoming supplier can access the information that allows it to identify the holder of the CUPS is expressly prohibited by a regulation (Article 7.2, third paragraph, of RD 1435/02), in such a way that the means that would reasonably allow it to know the identity of the holder of the CUPS is evidently unlawful, the direct application of the doctrine upheld by the CJEU and by the Supreme Court leads to the conclusion that the CUPS, in terms of the processing carried out by the incoming supplier, cannot be considered personal data since IBERCLI cannot identify its holder nor is it identifiable for that supplier. Article 4.1 of the GDPR defines personal data as “any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more specific elements of the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural or social identity of that natural person.”  
Recital 26 of the GDPR states:  
“[…] To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means, such as singling out, that may reasonably be used by the data controller or by any other person to identify the natural person, directly or indirectly, must be taken into account.”  
In light of Recital 26 of the GDPR, a natural person is “identifiable” when they can be singled out through means that objectively allow for their differentiation from other persons.

The Article 29 Working Party (hereinafter, WP29) in its Opinion 4/2007, dated June 20, (WP 136) "on the concept of personal data," which analyzes the definition of personal data in Article 2 of Directive 95/46/EC, the considerations of which are fully applicable to the interpretation of Article 4.1 of the GDPR, stated regarding the information referring to an "identifiable" natural person:  
"Generally speaking, a natural person can be considered 'identified' when, within a group of people, they are 'distinguished' from all other members of the group. Consequently, a natural person is 'identifiable' when, although they have not yet been identified, it is possible to do so (which is the meaning of the suffix 'ble'). Thus, this second alternative is, in practice, the sufficient condition to consider that the information falls within the scope of application of the third component.  
Identification is usually achieved through specific data that we can call 'identifiers' and that have a privileged and very close relationship with a particular person.  
[...]  
The Directive mentions these 'identifiers' in the definition of 'personal data' in Article 2 when it establishes that 'any person whose identity can be determined, directly or indirectly, in particular by means of an identification number or one or several specific elements, characteristic of their physical, physiological, mental, economic, cultural or social identity, shall be regarded as identifiable.'  
Referring to the requirement that the person be 'directly or indirectly identifiable,' the WP29 Opinion 4/2007 states on page 14:  
"On our part, when we talk about 'indirectly' identified or identifiable persons, we are generally referring to the phenomenon of 'unique combinations,' whether they are small or large. In cases where, at first glance, the available identifiers do not allow for the singularization of a specific person, that person may still be 'identifiable' because that information, combined with other data (whether the data controller is aware of them or not), will allow for distinguishing that person from others."  
"At this point, it is worth noting that, while identification through name and surname is practically the most common, that information may not be necessary in all cases to identify a person. This may occur when other 'identifiers' are used to singularize someone.  
[...] Also on the Internet, traffic control tools easily allow for identifying the behavior of a machine and, therefore, that of the user behind it." (The underlining is ours)  
And referring to "Means of identification," on page 16, the Opinion states:  
"Recital 26 of the Directive pays particular attention to the term 'identifiable' by stating that 'to determine whether a person is identifiable, there are...'"

that considers the set of means that may be reasonably used by the data controller or by any other person, to identify that person." This means that the mere and hypothetical possibility of singling out an individual is not sufficient to consider the person as "identifiable." If, taking into account "the set of means that may be reasonably used by the data controller or by any other person," there is no such possibility or it is insignificant, the person should not be considered as "identifiable" and the information should not be classified as "personal data."

In light of Recital 26 of the GDPR and Opinion 4/2007 of the Article 29 Working Party, it follows that the electricity user is identifiable through the so-called CUPS (Código Universal de Punto de Suministro) electric code, an alphanumeric code that is exclusive to the supply point of which a specific user is the holder.  
The CUPS identifies the supply point and, therefore, objectively designates and distinguishes an electricity user, who is the holder of that supply point, from other users. It is possible, through the CUPS data - by which its holder is objectively differentiated - to attribute legal consequences without the need to know their name and surname.  
That is to say, the CUPS is a piece of personal data as it uniquely identifies its holder without the need for their name and surname to be known, as it is a unique identifier. The CUPS is personal data because it allows for the identification of its holder on its own, just like an ID number or a bank account number.  
In short, this Agency reiterates that the CUPS data processed by IBERCLI does indeed have the nature of personal data. In accordance with the GDPR, the data controller shall be obliged to adopt at all times reasonable measures to ensure that the personal data it collects or processes in any other way are accurate.  
The activity or measures that the controller must deploy or adopt in order to ensure the accuracy of the data cannot translate into disproportionate demands, but reasonable measures are required, which makes it necessary to assess the purpose and context of the processing. In this sense, while Recital 39 of the GDPR refers to “reasonable measures to ensure that inaccurate personal data are rectified or erased,” Article 5.1(d) of the GDPR connects the accuracy of the data with the purposes for which they are processed.  
The National Court stated in its Judgment of 27/02/2008 (Appeal 210/2007) that “The principle of truthfulness or accuracy is of great relevance, as it is not only necessary for the data to be collected for processing according to a series of criteria (principle of proportionality) and that they are used for purposes compatible with those that motivated the collection (principle of purpose), but it also requires that those who collect and process personal data guarantee and protect that the information being processed is not inaccurate and is kept up to date. The breach or violation of the principle of truthfulness can have significant consequences for the affected individual.” (The underlining is ours)  
Furthermore, Article 5.2 of the GDPR incorporates the principle of proactive responsibility, under which the data controller will be responsible for compliance with the provisions of paragraph 1 (for the purposes currently of interest, in paragraph d) and capable of demonstrating such compliance. The principle of proactivity transfers to the data controller the obligation not only to observe the principles governing the processing but also to be able to demonstrate such compliance.

Sanction agreed to be imposed on IBERCLI  
1. The corrective powers attributed to the AEPD as the supervisory authority are  
related in Article 58.2 of the GDPR, paragraphs a) to j), among which the provision  
includes the power to impose an administrative fine in accordance with Article 83 of  
the GDPR (Article 58.2.i).  
This Agency, in light of the actions taken, agrees to sanction IBERCLI for the infringement  
of Article 5.1.d) of the GDPR, principle of accuracy, with an administrative fine.  
Article 83 of the GDPR, “General conditions for the imposition of administrative fines,”  
establishes in its paragraph 1 that the supervisory authority shall ensure that the imposition  
of fines for the infringements of this Regulation indicated in paragraphs 4, 5, and 6,  
complies in each individual case with the principles of effectiveness, proportionality, and  
deterrent effect.  
The principle of proportionality entails a correlation between the infringement and the  
sanction, prohibiting unnecessary or excessive measures, so that it must be suitable to  
achieve the purposes that justify it. Article 83.2 of the GDPR determines the technique to  
be followed to achieve that adequacy between the sanction and the infringement committed  
and provides a list of criteria or factors that will be taken into account to grade the sanction.  
Paragraph 2 of Article 83 of the GDPR states:  
“Administrative fines shall be imposed, depending on the circumstances of each individual  
case, as an additional or substitute measure for the measures provided for in Article 58,  
paragraph 2, letters a) to h) and j). In deciding the imposition of an administrative fine  
and its amount in each individual case, the following shall be duly taken into account:  
a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the infringement, taking into account the  
nature, scope, or purpose of the processing operation in question, as well as the number  
of affected data subjects and the level of damage suffered;  
b) the intentionality or negligence in the infringement;  
c) any measures taken by the controller or processor to mitigate the damage suffered  
by the data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the controller or processor, taking into account the  
technical or organizational measures they have implemented pursuant to Articles 25 and 32;  
e) any previous infringement committed by the controller or processor;  
f) the degree of cooperation with the supervisory authority in order to remedy the  
infringement and mitigate the possible adverse effects of the infringement;  
g) the categories of personal data affected by the infringement;  
h) the manner in which the supervisory authority became aware of the infringement,  
in particular whether the controller or processor notified the infringement and, if so, to  
what extent;  
i) when the measures indicated in Article 58, paragraph 2, have been previously ordered  
against the controller or processor in relation to the same matter, compliance with such  
measures;  
j) adherence to codes of conduct under Article 40 or to certification mechanisms approved  
under Article 42, and

The circumstances considered in the proposed resolution as aggravating factors are expressly included in Article 83.2 of the GDPR, so this Agency has limited itself to applying the aforementioned Regulation. (The underlining is ours)  
In relation to this issue, we must clarify that the element of culpability, in a broad sense, is an indispensable condition for the emergence of sanctioning liability, as objective liability is prohibited in our legal system. It should also be added that according to the Order of the Constitutional Court 193/2007, of March 26, the imputability of the authorship of the infringement to the legal entity arises from the very nature of the legal fiction to which legal entities respond. They lack the volitional element in the strict sense, but not the capacity to infringe the norms to which they are subject. This capacity for infringement derives from the legal good protected by the norm that is infringed and from the necessity that such protection be truly effective. Having established the above, as has been exposed when examining the infringement of the principle of accuracy, the lack of diligence demonstrated by the responding entity to comply with that principle has been absolute.

The concurrence, as aggravating factors, of the circumstances described in Article 83.2 of the GDPR, paragraphs a), b), e), and k) is noted.
