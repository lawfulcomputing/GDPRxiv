LEGAL GROUNDS  
I  
Competence  
In accordance with the powers granted to each supervisory authority by Article 58.2 of the GDPR and as established in Articles 47, 48.1, 64.2, and 68.1 of the LOPDGDD, the Director of the Spanish Agency for Data Protection is competent to initiate and resolve this procedure.  
Furthermore, Article 63.2 of the LOPDGDD states that: “The procedures processed by the Spanish Agency for Data Protection shall be governed by the provisions of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, by this organic law, by the regulatory provisions issued in its development, and, insofar as they do not contradict them, subsidiarily by the general rules on administrative procedures.”  
II  
The image as personal data  
The physical image of a person, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is personal data as it makes the person identifiable, and its protection, therefore, is the subject of said GDPR:  
“‘personal data’: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (‘the data subject’); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier, such as by
4/18  
for example, a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or several elements specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, psychological, economic, cultural, or social identity of that person;”  
The report 139/2017 from the Legal Office of this Agency states that “the image as well as the voice of a person is personal data, just as any information that allows determining, directly or indirectly, their identity will be.”  
The physical image of a person, according to Article 4.1 of the GDPR, is personal data and its protection, therefore, is subject to this Regulation.  
Thus, the processing of a person's image is subject to data protection regulations. Article 4.2 of the GDPR defines the concept of “processing” of personal data.  
“any operation or set of operations performed on personal data or sets of personal data, whether by automated means or not, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or modification, extraction, consultation, use, communication by transmission, dissemination, or any other form of enabling access, comparison or interconnection, limitation, erasure, or destruction.”  
The inclusion of a person's image in journalistic publications, which identifies or makes them identifiable, constitutes processing of personal data and, therefore, the entity that carries out such processing is obliged to comply with the obligations established for the data controller in the GDPR and in the LOPDGDD.  
III  
Right to data protection  
This procedure is initiated because the respondent published, on the website “lavozdeasturias.es,” a news article about the dissemination of videos on social media in which the text referred to the name of the complainant (“***LABEL.1”) and was illustrated with a photograph of the complainant, taken from a video that was published and widely disseminated through the social network “Twitter.” The image of the complainant was clearly visible in that photograph.  
Individuals have the power of disposition over their personal data, including their image, as well as over its dissemination, resulting, without a doubt, in the person whose personal data is disseminated in violation of the legal order being deserving of protection.  
Thus, the STC 292/2000, of November 30, states that “the content of the fundamental right to data protection consists of a power of disposition and control over personal data that empowers the person to decide which of those data to provide to a third party, whether the State or a private individual, or which this third party may collect, and that also allows the individual to know who possesses that personal data and for what purpose, being able to oppose that possession or use. These powers of disposition and control over personal data, which constitute part of the content of the fundamental right to data protection, are legally specified in the authority to consent to the collection, obtaining, and access to personal data, its subsequent storage and processing, as well as its possible use or uses, by a third party, whether the State or a private individual. And this right to consent to the knowledge and processing, whether computerized or not, of personal data requires as indispensable complements, on the one hand, the authority to know at all times who has that personal data and what use they are subjecting it to, and, on the other hand, the power to oppose that possession and uses.”

In this regard, and regardless of the legal basis legitimizing the processing, every data controller must respect the principles of processing set forth in Article 5 of the GDPR. We will highlight Article 5.1.c) of the GDPR which establishes that:  
“1. Personal data shall be  
c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (‘data minimization’);”  
However, we are faced with a fundamental right that is not absolute, as the Fundamental Right to Data Protection may yield to the prevalence of other rights and freedoms that are also constitutionally recognized and protected, such as, for example, the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information, weighing this on a case-by-case basis.  
Nevertheless, in the present case, as we will explain, it must be considered that the processing carried out by the responding party within the framework of freedom of information has been excessive, as there is no prevailing public interest in the dissemination of the image of the claimant, along with their name, which does not add any value to the information by maintaining such an image (without blurring the image, for example), under the pretext that such data has been disclosed; an image that clearly identifies the person, even more so if the name is specified. When weighing the conflicting interests and considering the concurrent circumstances of this case, namely, the particularly sensitive nature of the personal data and the impact on the privacy of the person (the claimant), the interest of the data subject in protecting their personal data and preventing its dissemination deserves greater protection than the purported public interest in its dissemination.  
IV  
Right to Information  
In the conflict between the Fundamental Rights to Freedom of Information in relation to the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, even though equal protection is recognized for both constitutional rights, the former is usually granted precedence by our courts after evaluating and weighing all the elements at play.  
However, preponderance does not mean prevalence when, considering all the concurrent circumstances in a specific case, the limits set by regulations and jurisprudence are exceeded.  
In this sense, the Article 29 Working Party in its Opinion 06/2014 on the concept of legitimate interest of the data controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC, when examining the legal basis of legitimate interest in Article 7.1.f) of Directive 95/46/EC, fully transferable to the current art. 6.1.f) of the GDPR, includes the right to freedom of expression or information as one of the scenarios in which the issue of legitimate interest may arise, asserting that “without prejudice to whether the interests of the data controller will ultimately prevail over the interests and rights of the data subjects when the balancing test is carried out.”

Thus, it is not a matter, as in other jurisprudential cases examined, of granting precedence to one fundamental right over another, having to choose which one carries more weight in a specific case. Rather, it is about finding a balance between both in order to achieve the purpose of the first without undermining the second. The reconciliation of both rights is nothing new, since the European legislator mandates such reconciliation in Article 85 of the GDPR.  
As we have seen previously, the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information is not unlimited, since the jurisprudential interpretation when confronting it with other rights and freedoms does not allow for its unrestricted application in all cases, but rather, the precedence that courts tend to grant it may be limited by other fundamental rights that must also be respected. This limitation is observed when the personal data provided was unnecessary for the essence of the content of the information. In this case, the image of the claimant and their name, without constraining the provision of information, must be made compatible with the principle of data minimization, applicable to the form, the medium in which the information is provided and disseminated due to the immediate impact on the personal data and the identification of the person.  
Precisely because the public interest in the news is not denied, in this specific case, it is not a matter of diminishing the Fundamental Right to Freedom of Information due to the precedence of the Fundamental Right to the Protection of Personal Data, but rather of making them fully compatible so that both are absolutely guaranteed. That is, the freedom of information of the media is not called into question, but rather the balancing with the right to data protection based on the proportionality and necessity of publishing the specific personal data of the image or the name of the person. Such a situation could have been resolved by using technical procedures to prevent the recognition of the image, both security measures, applied depending on the case in a regular manner by the media, and by not indicating the name of the claimant.  
Furthermore, we must emphasize that the claimant is an anonymous person and our Constitutional Court, in all STC 58/2018 of June 4, states that public authorities, public officials, and public figures or those engaged in activities that involve public notoriety "voluntarily accept the risk that their subjective personal rights may be affected by criticism, opinions, or adverse revelations and, therefore, the right to information reaches, in relation to them, its maximum level of legitimizing effectiveness, insofar as their life and moral conduct participate in the general interest with a greater intensity than that of private individuals who, without a vocation for public projection, are circumstantially involved in matters of public significance, to whom, consequently, a higher scope of privacy must be recognized, which prevents granting general significance to facts or behaviors that would have it if referred to public figures."  
The CJEU (Second Chamber) ruling of February 14, 2019, in case C 345/17, Sergejs Buivids, mentions various criteria for weighing between the right to respect for privacy and the right to freedom of expression, among which are "the contribution to a debate of general interest, the notoriety of the affected person, the subject of the report, the previous behavior of the interested party, the content, the form and the repercussions of the publication, the manner and circumstances in which the information was obtained and its veracity (see, in this sense, the judgment of the ECtHR of June 27, 2017, Satakunnan Markkinapörssi Oy and Satamedia Oy v. In the present case, (i) we are neither dealing with a public figure, in the sense that such relevance is sufficient to understand that it entails, ex lege, a dispossession of their fundamental right to the protection of their personal data, and (ii) if we are faced with “publicly relevant” facts, in the sense that they are revealed as “necessary” for the exposition of ideas or opinions of public interest, that necessity does not extend to providing data that identifies the claimant.  
Therefore, and as expressed by the Supreme Court in its ruling (civil) 697/2019, dated December 19, 2019, the formation of a free public opinion does not require, nor justify, affecting the fundamental right to one’s own image [in this case, the protection of personal data] with such gravity and in a manner that does not maintain the necessary connection with the identification of the person subject to the information.  
VII  
Obligations regarding data protection  
Every data controller has obligations regarding data protection, as prescribed in the GDPR and the LOPDGDD, highlighting, in terms of our interest, proactive responsibility, Article 5.2 of the GDPR, risk assessment, and the implementation of appropriate security measures. Obligations that are even more relevant when the risk is particularly sensitive.  
Such obligations do not diminish simply because we are dealing with a data controller that is a media outlet.  
Considering that the dissemination of the image of the claimant and their name, which makes them identifiable and recognizable by third parties, is carried out through a media outlet, there is a very high risk and it is very likely that they may suffer harm to their rights and freedoms. This is particularly true when it is not a proportional or necessary processing in relation to the information purposes pursued.

Competence
