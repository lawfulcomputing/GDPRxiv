The Data Protection Authority decides on the data protection complaint [Note from the editor: referring to: complaint, appeal against a decision, or official complaint] of the Federal Ministry of the Interior (complainant) dated October 27, 2021, against the decision of the Data Protection Authority dated October 5, 2021, GZ D124.33435, 2021-0.658.855 (challenged decision), as follows:

The ruling of the challenged decision is amended to read as follows:  

1. The complaint of the complainant is upheld, and it is determined that the respondent has violated the complainant's right to information by failing to deliver the information requested on October 19, 2020, via email to the complainant.  
2. The respondent is ordered to comply with the complainant's request for information within a period of two weeks by delivering the information to their electronic delivery address.

**D. From a legal perspective, it follows that:**  
1. In the present case, the 3rd main section (in §§ 36 – 61) of the DSG applies, with which Directive (EU) 2016/680 on the protection of natural persons in the processing of personal data by competent authorities for the purpose of preventing, investigating, detecting or...
**Prosecution of Criminal Offenses (hereinafter: Data Protection Policy for the Justice and Interior Sector)**  
has been implemented, which was not disputed by the parties involved in the proceedings.  
According to § 44 DSG, every affected person has the right to obtain information from the controller about whether their personal data is being processed. In the affirmative case, the affected person has the right to receive the information in accordance with § 44 para. 1 no. 1 to 7 DSG.  
The Data Protection Policy for the Justice and Interior Sector further stipulates in its Art. 12 para. 1 last sentence: “The transmission of information occurs in any suitable form, which also includes electronic transmission. In principle, the controller transmits the information in the same form in which the request was received.”  
Art. 12 para. 1 last sentence of the Data Protection Policy for the Justice and Interior Sector has been implemented as follows in § 42 para. 4 last sentence DSG: “If the affected person submits the request electronically, they should be informed electronically if possible, unless they indicate otherwise.”  
Additionally, it should be noted that Art. 12 para. 3 last sentence GDPR - although this provision is not applicable here - has the same wording as § 42 para. 4 last sentence DSG.  
Now, if a request for information is made - as in the present case - the controller must make all necessary efforts to ensure that the information provided actually reaches the affected person.  
2. In this context, the complainant believes it was necessary to deliver the information to their own hands. The complainant justifies this by stating that they are an authority and therefore § 21 AVG applies to them, which stipulates that deliveries must be made according to the ZustG. Furthermore, § 22 AVG also applies to them, the first sentence of which states that a written copy with proof of delivery must be delivered if there are important reasons for this. The relevant second sentence of § 22 AVG states that in the presence of particularly important reasons or when it is legally required, delivery must be made to the recipient's own hands. The complainant stated in the proceedings D124.3435 before the Data Protection Authority that information from the BMI regularly contains criminally relevant data that must be handled with care. The complainant therefore assumes that a “particularly important reason” in the sense of the second sentence of § 22 AVG is present, which is why such information must be delivered by post to their own hands (i.e., by means of a registered letter) in accordance with § 21 ZustG (“Delivery to Own Hands”). If the complainant wishes to deliver electronically instead of by post, they must comply with Section 3 of the ZustG (“Electronic Delivery”). § 28 para. 4 ZustG further stipulates that electronic deliveries with proof of delivery are only permissible through delivery systems in accordance with § 28 para. 3 no. 1 and 3, as well as in the case of § 37a second sentence ZustG. That an electronically provided information of the complainant in accordance with § 44 DSG only with
Delivery Confirmation (and not by simple email) must be made, as it follows that the data to be provided are criminally relevant data that must be handled with care. This certainly constitutes an important reason in the sense of the first sentence of § 22 AVG, which obliges authorities to deliver with delivery confirmation. Now, according to § 37a second sentence ZustG, which represents a form of delivery with delivery confirmation, the identity must be proven using a citizen card. Since the involved party did not prove their identity with a citizen card, the complainant was also not permitted to deliver the information from 19.10.2020 to the involved party by simple email.

3. When the complainant states that as an authority they must apply the AVG and subsequently the ZustG, they are generally to be agreed with; however, it should be pointed out that the complainant must also apply the 3rd main section of the DSG: In the 3rd main section of the DSG, the data protection directive for the area of justice and internal affairs has been implemented domestically, which must be applied by the authorities responsible for the prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offenses, including the complainant as the highest "security authority." Since the AVG and the ZustG are to be applied by administrative authorities in general, but the 3rd main section of the DSG only by the authorities responsible for the prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offenses, the 3rd main section of the DSG constitutes a "lex specialis" in relation to the AVG and the ZustG.

Relevant in this case is therefore § 42 Abs. 4 last sentence DSG: "If the data subject submits the request electronically, they should be informed electronically if possible, unless they indicate otherwise."

§ 42 Abs. 4 last sentence DSG is to be interpreted in light of Art. 12 Abs. 1 of the data protection directive for the area of justice and internal affairs. As already stated, Art. 12 Abs. 1 requires that the transmission – among other things – of information occurs in any suitable form, which also includes electronic transmission, whereby, in principle, the responsible party transmits the information in the same form in which they received the request.

Thus, Art. 12 Abs. 1 of the directive posits that the transmission of information must, as a principle, occur in the same form in which the responsible party received the request for information. A deviation from this principle therefore requires justification.

It should be emphasized at this point that the wording of Art. 12 Abs. 1 of the directive, which – as already stated – regulates the processing of "criminal data," goes beyond that of Art. 12 Abs. 1 GDPR: Art. 12 Abs. 1 GDPR stipulates that the transmission of information must occur in writing or in another form, possibly also electronically. According to Art. 12 Abs. 3 GDPR, the data subject must be informed electronically if possible, unless they indicate otherwise.
Unlike Article 12(1) of the Directive, the electronic transmission is thus not established as a principle within the scope of the GDPR. From this, it can certainly be concluded that the Union legislator consciously opted for the principle—rather than merely the option—of transmission in the manner (electronic) in which the controller received the request, within the scope of the Data Protection Directive for the area of Justice and Home Affairs, even though it inherently concerns the processing of "more sensitive information"—namely, information about criminal offenses. 

The Data Protection Directive for the area of Justice and Home Affairs does not indicate that the Union legislator had a specific (secured) form of transmission in mind, which is also supported by the wording of Article 12(1), last sentence ("in the same form in which he received the request"). 

Furthermore, the Austrian legislator does not necessarily require a specific form of delivery in comparable cases, particularly not a personal delivery: According to §§ 47 and 48 VStG, the authority can issue penalty orders in an expedited procedure if the necessary conditions are met. These contain the accusation of a punishable act (administrative offense) and can thus—following the case law of the CJEU (see the judgment of June 22, 2021, C-439/19, para. 77 et seq.)—certainly be classified as "criminal data" within the meaning of Article 10 GDPR and thus also as "criminal offenses" within the meaning of the Data Protection Directive for the area of Justice and Home Affairs (see its Recital 13). 

While until the amendment BGBl. I No. 33/2013 penalty orders were to be delivered "to one's own hands" according to § 48(2) VStG, this is no longer provided for since this amendment. The explanations for this (2009 of the annexes XXIV. GP p. 22) state that personal delivery is to be omitted "in the interest of cost savings." 

As another example, it is permissible to deliver "sensitive data" under Article 9(1) GDPR by email, as found in § 27(12b) GTelG 2012 (as amended by BGBl. I No. 34/2021). 

In summary, it can thus be stated—contrary to the view of the complainant—that neither Union law foundations nor comparable domestic legal foundations necessarily require that information, which may also contain "criminal data" or even special categories of personal data under Article 9(1) GDPR, must be delivered personally and in no case by email. 

When the complainant argues that he is bound by § 21 AVG to the ZustG, this cannot be contested. However, he overlooks that the ZustG does not oppose delivery by email (§ 28(3) in conjunction with § 37(1) and § 2 No. 5 ZustG) and—as stated above—the delivery of information does not necessarily have to be carried out according to the provisions of § 22 AVG. 

4. The participating party, which submitted its request for information electronically by email and explicitly wished to receive the information electronically, should therefore have received it electronically in accordance with § 42(4), last sentence DSG. Since the complainant did not transmit the information electronically to the participating party, he violated their right to information. 

Finally, reference should be made to the provision of § 44(7) DSG, according to which the controller may require additional necessary information to confirm the identity of the person who—like in the present case—submitted a request under § 44 DSG. Additionally, the ruling of the VwGH from July 4, 2016, Ra 2016/04/0014, should be mentioned, which, although it pertains to the old legal situation of the DSG 2000, can be applied to the current legal situation: "The provision of § 26 DSG 2000 has the clearly recognizable purpose of preventing the misuse of the right to information for information gathering by third parties. A client may not transmit data to the information requester—of whom he can only assume at that moment that he is indeed the affected party—without proof of identity, as otherwise he could violate the data secrecy according to § 15(1) DSG 2000. The proof of identity must be provided in a form that allows the client to verify the identity of the information requester with the person whose data is the subject of the information. In light of the legislative intent and to prevent misuse, a high degree of reliability regarding the proof of identity is required (Note E of September 9, 2008, 2004/06/0221, with references)." 

Since the complainant— as established—made two delivery attempts to the participating party's own hands, he apparently had no doubts about the identity of the participating party, which is why the transmission of the information by email to the participating party could have occurred for this reason as well. 

Furthermore, it should be noted that according to the case law of the VwGH, personal delivery cannot replace the requirement of prior identity verification (VwSlg. 17.515 A/2008). 

Based on the above, a decision was to be made accordingly.
