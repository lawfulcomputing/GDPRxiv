D. From a legal perspective, it follows:  
D.1. Regarding the Mandate Decision in General  
Mandate decisions typically serve the immediate and official enforcement of non-deferrable measures of an administrative police nature in a one-party procedure. In this respect, they have a provisional character and thus do not fit into a procedure with multiple parties, in which a (final) decision could be issued that would definitively regulate the matter in dispute between the parties after appropriate investigations have been conducted.  
Thus, this is an atypical mandate decision of its own kind; however, from the clear wording of the law in light of the applicant's submission, it can be inferred that the legislator intended to grant the affected party a subjective right to legal protection through the Data Protection Authority. Therefore, the present application must be decided on its merits (cf. the decision of October 29, 2015, GZ: DSB-D215.861/0010-DSB/2015).  
According to § 22 Abs. 4 DSG in conjunction with § 57 Abs. 1 AVG, the Data Protection Authority is authorized to prohibit the continuation of data processing by mandate decision without prior investigative proceedings if the operation of data processing poses a significant immediate threat to the protected confidentiality interests of the affected person (imminent danger) and thus takes into account an increased risk potential in cases of imminent danger (cf. Thiele/Wagner, Practical Commentary on the Data Protection Act (DSG), § 22 DSG, Rz 8).  
Since no investigative proceedings by the Data Protection Authority need to precede the issuance of a mandate decision, it is the applicant's responsibility to certify the legal requirements for the issuance of the decision together with the application (to make credible, to provide circumstantial evidence; cf. Schmidl in Gantschacher†/Jelinek/Schmidl/Spanberger, Commentary on the DSG, § 22 Anm. 9). A later rectification of the certification or an evidentiary procedure regarding this would be incompatible with the character of the decision under § 22 DSG as a non-deferrable measure of an administrative police nature (data protection police emergency order) (cf. the decision of the Data Protection Authority dated September 24, 2015, GZ: DSB-D215.813/0012-DSB/2015).  
The issuance of a mandate decision requires, on the one hand, a material illegality of the data processing, for which a justified suspicion is already sufficient. Thus, there must be factual indications that justify the assumption of the likelihood that provisions of data protection law have been violated, and this must be presented in a rationally comprehensible manner (cf. VwGH March 21, 2012, 2012/16/0005). On the other hand, it is necessary that the operation of this data processing poses a significant immediate threat to the protected confidentiality interests of the affected parties (i.e., imminent danger) (cf. Thiele/Wagner, Practical Commentary on the Data Protection Act (DSG), § 22 DSG, Rz 15). Both criteria must be met for the issuance of a mandate decision.  
Imminent danger exists if the omission of the official measure would likely lead to the affected parties suffering (further) violations of privacy or even damages that could be prevented by issuing the provisional prohibition of data processing. § 22 Abs. 4 DSG represents a lex specialis to § 57 AVG. This means that the otherwise required examination of the non-deferrability of the measure in relation to the necessary duration of the investigative procedure in the data protection area is omitted (cf. Thiele/Wagner, Practical Commentary on the Data Protection Act (DSG), § 22 DSG, Rz 18).  
To fulfill the elements of the "imminent danger," a general concern regarding future events is not sufficient (cf. DSK March 30, 2012, K121.765/0008-DSK/2012). The existence of imminent danger must be substantiated with facts that are specific to the individual case. Pure speculation, hypothetical considerations, or merely everyday experience-based, case-independent assumptions are not sufficient (cf. Thiele/Wagner, Practical Commentary on the Data Protection Act (DSG), § 22 DSG, Rz 20).  
D.2.  
Regarding the Existence of a Significant Immediate Threat to the Protected Confidentiality Interests of the Applicant Due to the Operation of the Data Processing in Question (Imminent Danger):  
Regarding the First Respondent:  
In relation to the first respondent, the already issued decision GZ: D124.0686/24 (2024-0.175.712) dated March 5, 2024, must be noted, with which the application for the issuance of a mandate decision pursuant to § 22 Abs. 4 DSG in conjunction with § 57 AVG against the first respondent (not legally binding) submitted by the same applicant on March 1, 2024, was rejected.
In the application, the applicant requested that the data protection authority prohibit the first respondent from immediately "preparing and transmitting any personal data stored about [him] at the respondents, particularly [his] personal tax records, to the U-Committee."  
The first respondent in that procedure was, as in the present case, the Federal Minister of Finance, which means that the applicant's right to request regarding the processing operations of preparation and transmission by the first respondent has already been exhausted, as otherwise the identical request from the previous application would have to be assessed again in the present procedure.  
However, it must be discussed in the context of the present procedure regarding the application, insofar as it goes beyond the request of March 1, 2024.  
Thus, the processing types "further storage" and "any further transmission" to the third respondent must be assessed.  
Regarding the first respondent and the second respondent:  
As established, members of the Austrian National Council requested, with a request pursuant to § 25 para. 2 VO-UA dated February 1, 2024, a transfer of further data, including the complete tax file of the applicant, to the third respondent.  
The applicant explicitly stated that the danger in the issuance of a mandate decision pursuant to § 22 para. 4 DSG in conjunction with § 57 para. 1 AVG is that it is "unfortunately now common practice" for all documents and data transmitted to investigation committees to be immediately published (especially through "leaks" to the media), and that the transmission to the third respondent would therefore be associated with an irretrievable disadvantage arising from the publication of personal data.  
The admissibility of a mandate decision requires that a danger situation is to be feared from the respondent or the respondents and responsible parties in the sense of Art. 4 No. 7 GDPR.  
Even assuming that the "leaks" feared by the applicant occur, thus personal data of the applicant is transmitted and disclosed to the media by members (or parliamentary staff) of the third respondent, it is not apparent in this context to what extent this danger situation would be directly caused by the contested data processing by the first respondent or the second respondent, as the applicant's request in this procedure is directed against the further storage, preparation, and the already occurred transmission to the third respondent as well as any further transmission to the third respondent of data related to the applicant by the first respondent and the second respondent (as already stated, only the processing by storage and further transmission regarding the first respondent is examined, thus not the storage or the transmission to the third respondent).  
The applicant explicitly criticizes the transmission of the applicant's tax records to the third respondent. The disclosure of this personal data (e.g., to the media) directly by the first respondent or the second respondent has not been claimed by the applicant at any time. Likewise, the storage of personal data or the preparation prior to a transmission has not been directly criticized as realizations of an imminent danger. The criticized danger of further transmissions of personal data by the first respondent or the second respondent to the third respondent cannot be considered as an unsubstantiated assertion leading to the assumption of a concrete imminent danger.  
From the applicant's submissions, only a general concern regarding future events can be derived. In the fear that members of the investigation committee would disclose information they became aware of in the course of their work for the investigation committee to third parties—contrary to existing confidentiality obligations—it is viewed by the data protection authority merely as hypothetical considerations or merely assumptions based on everyday experience (the applicant states: "It is unfortunately now common practice for all documents and data transmitted to investigation committees to be immediately published (especially through 'leaks' to the media)."), which are not suitable to demonstrate the existence of a concrete danger situation in the sense of § 22 DSG (cf. again Thiele/Wagner, Practical Commentary on the Data Protection Act (DSG), § 22 DSG, Rz 20).  
In summary, it can thus be stated that the alleged endangerment of the applicant's interests is seen in the activities of members of the investigation committee, but not in the—regardless of the nature—data processing by the first respondent or the second respondent and therefore lies outside their sphere.  
Regarding the third respondent:  
As the applicant correctly states in his application, the CJEU recently ruled in its judgment of January 16, 2024, C-33/22, following a preliminary ruling request under Art. 267 TFEU (regarding the Austrian data protection authority), that "it cannot be assumed that an activity lies outside the scope of Union law and is thus exempt from the application [of the GDPR] solely because it is carried out by an investigation committee established by the Parliament of a Member State in the exercise of its control over the execution."
In another point of ruling, the CJEU recognized that if only a single supervisory authority is established in a Member State pursuant to Art. 51 para. 1 GDPR, but this authority is not endowed with the jurisdiction to monitor the application of the GDPR by an investigative committee established by the parliament of that Member State in the exercise of its oversight rights, then this authority is directly entrusted with the jurisdiction to decide on complaints regarding processing of personal data carried out by this investigative committee. 

It is now clearly established that the data protection authority, as the currently only established supervisory authority in Austria according to Art. 51 GDPR, is called upon to oversee parliamentary investigative committees, which is why the application for the issuance of a mandate decision by the applicant must also be considered permissible in relation to the third respondent and must be addressed substantively. 

As already stated, the applicant argues that the danger of delay lies in the fact that members of the third respondent or their parliamentary staff may pass on personal data of the applicant to the media, as has already occurred in the past with parliamentary investigative committees. 

As a result, the applicant's request must nevertheless be dismissed in relation to the third respondent. 

The applicant's assertion that the transmission of personal data concerning him to the third respondent and parliamentary staff gives a large number of people access to this data is to be agreed upon. 

The tasks and competencies of a parliamentary investigative committee are regulated in the annex to the rules of procedure of the National Council in the VO-UA. 

Not regulated and therefore not legally provided for is the transfer of documents or data to media representatives, regardless of whether such documents contain personal data or not. 

In the materials concerning the amendment of the parliamentary investigative committee, in which the right of establishment for a parliamentary minority according to Art. 53 para. 1 B-VG was also introduced, it is stated regarding complaints based on violations of personal rights to the Constitutional Court according to Art. 138b para. 1 no. 7 lit. b B-VG:  
“The complaint is only admissible if the member or the official has behaved ‘in the exercise of his profession’ (cf. Art. 57 para. I B-VG). Actions outside the investigative committee, such as in press conferences, are therefore not covered by the scope of application” (Explanations to the initiative request: IA 718/A XXV. GP, 19). 

According to the case law of the Constitutional Court, statements in interviews with the media or in press releases or the transfer of documents submitted by the investigative committee to persons outside the investigative committee cannot be the subject of a complaint under Art. 138b para. 1 no. 7 B-VG, because this behavior is not covered by the professional immunity of the members of the National Council in the sense of Art. 57 para. 1 B-VG (Constitutional Court 29 June 2023 UA 1/2023, para. 24). 

“In the exercise of the profession” means according to the case law of the Constitutional Court that only the behavior of a member of the investigative committee during and not outside the meetings of the investigative committee can be the subject of the proceedings under Art. 138b para. 1 no. 7 B-VG (VfSIg. 20.015/2015, para. 76; Constitutional Court 25 September 2021, UA 6/2021, para. 50 f). 

This important distinction must also be considered in this context: The transfer of files that have been submitted to an investigative committee by members of the investigative committee, parliamentary staff, or others to the media or other third parties is not legally provided for. 

Therefore, if official press conferences held by members of an investigative committee cannot be seen as actions “in the exercise of the profession,” any potential data transmission in the context of such press conferences or similar events cannot be attributed to the third respondent. 

The (data protection law) responsibility for such actions thus fundamentally lies – subject to an examination in individual cases – with the individual member of the investigative committee themselves. 

There are also no indications that in previous cases “leaks” to the media could be attributed to an investigative committee; rather, they originated from individual members who carried out such data processing independently, thus not in “the exercise of their profession as members of the National Council.” 

Thus, such data processing does not fall within the sphere of the third respondent, which is why the application must also be dismissed in relation to the third respondent. 

Furthermore, in addition to the ruling of the CJEU of 16 January 2024, C-33/22, the previous case law of the Constitutional Court and the accompanying decision-making practice of the data protection authority must also be taken into account, according to which the role of the data protection authority in the case of a duty to present to an investigative committee is limited to ex-post control (see, for example, the decision of 25 September 2021, UA 6/2021 with references; see also the – granting – decision of 8 September 2021, GZ: 2021-0.474.768). The recent case law of the CJEU does not change this ruling practice.
Whether the applicant's data was lawfully transmitted by the first respondent and the second respondent to the third respondent, or whether such transmission should have been omitted, for instance, because the applicant is not covered by the order of appointment, is thus reserved for subsequent examination.  
It was therefore to be decided accordingly.  
In the present case, the applicant submitted a request for the issuance of a mandate decision pursuant to § 22 para. 4 DSG in conjunction with § 57 para. 1 AVG in conjunction with Art. 58 para. 2 lit. f GDPR.  
For this reason, the legal bases for a mandate decision are also cited in the "decision header" as well as in the legal bases (§ 22 para. 4 DSG and § 57 AVG). Nevertheless, the rejection does not take the form of a mandate decision, as the required characteristic of urgency as per § 57 para. 1 AVG is certainly lacking in this case. A mandate decision – and thus a legal protection procedure that is extended and prolonged by the possibility of representation – only makes sense in the case of a positive administrative police intervention (cf. "urgent measures" in § 57 para. 1 AVG), but not in the present case of the rejection of such a requested intervention due to the absence of the legal prerequisites (see again the aforementioned decision of September 24, 2015).
