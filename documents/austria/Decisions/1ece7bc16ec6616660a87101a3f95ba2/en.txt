D E C I S I O N  
R U L I N G  
The Data Protection Authority decides on the data protection complaints of Gustav A*** (complainant) dated August 3, 2018, and June 25, 2019 (received on June 27, 2019) against N*** AG (respondent) due to alleged violations of the right to confidentiality as follows:  
-  
The complaints are dismissed.  
Legal bases: Art. 4 No. 11, Art. 7, Art. 51 para. 1, Art. 57 para. 1 lit. f, and Art. 77 para. 1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter: GDPR), OJ No. L 119 of May 4, 2016, p. 1; §§ 1, 18 para. 1, as well as 24 para. 1 and para. 5 of the Data Protection Act (DSG), Federal Law Gazette I No. 165/1999 as amended.

R E A S O N I N G  
A. Submissions of the Parties and Procedural History  
1. The complainant filed a complaint on August 3, 2018 (received on the same day) under Art. 77 GDPR regarding the "unlawful obtaining of consent for data transfer (violation of Art. 7 GDPR)" and requested a determination of the violation of his rights. The procedure was conducted under GZ: D123.289.  
In justification, he stated that when setting up a vacation *box (in principle), consent from the customer for the transfer of address data to third parties is obtained, and this transfer would include the transmission to companies for direct marketing purposes and address publishers. The consent is part of the contract and is not clearly separated from other matters. It is essential that in the corresponding box, the checkmark for consent is already set. If the customer does not wish for the data to be transferred, they must actively remove this checkmark. This is also evident from the consent text, which states: "You are entitled at any time and without giving reasons to prohibit the transmission to third parties for marketing purposes. In this case, remove the checkmark in the box below or direct your ...".  
Additionally, the complainant described that the contract processing is carried out at the counter via an electronic terminal. The screen size of this terminal is relatively small, and the consent text with the pre-selected checkmark is also displayed on this terminal. This makes it difficult to distinguish the consent text from the actual contract text. The customer's signature is also provided on this terminal. Due to the small font size of the contract and consent text, it is particularly difficult for persons with visual impairments to read the text correctly, which could lead to a misunderstanding of the actual intention of the already set checkmark, as its removal is what constitutes consent, potentially leading the customer to decide contrary to their own motivation. Finally, the consent for data transfer also includes data of any co-users of the *box.  
2. In the context of the statement requested by the Data Protection Authority on August 20, 2018, the respondent initially clarified that the complainant had objected to the data transmission in question when concluding a "vacation *box agreement" and that the respondent therefore does not use any personal data of the complainant for marketing purposes.  
Regarding the question of legally effective consent, the respondent argued that active consent to data transfer to third parties is not required in the form and that there is no data protection violation in the contested procedure. On the one hand, the respondent is the holder of a business license as an address publisher and direct marketing company according to § 151 GewO. On the other hand, every person applying for a vacation *box is provided with the information required by the GewO and is given the opportunity to prohibit otherwise permissible data use for marketing purposes by third parties. Thus, the complainant was able to prohibit this not only immediately upon application at the respondent's office but also later at any time and through multiple contact options. Furthermore, § 151 GewO derogates as lex specialis the provisions of the GDPR and does not provide for any active action/consent in this regard.  
The provision of information via touchpad in the accepting office is customer-friendly. The fact that the information text is located directly above the signature field serves to increase the attention of the affected person. All employees are instructed to assist those individuals who indicate that they have difficulties reading or understanding the text.
**Understanding of the Text**

The complainant – like any other customer – could have printed the application form for reading before signing it and only signed it afterwards. Attention is also paid to an adequate font size, which is 10 ** on both the touch pad and the physical form, exceeding the data protection law's possible "reference size" of 8 **. 

The respondent assumes in this case that the complainant also read the information provided and correctly understood that he could immediately prohibit the use of his data for marketing purposes by unchecking the pre-checked box. Even if he did not sufficiently notice the data information on the touch pad before his signature, the complainant still received a printed copy of the signed form in the branch and could have prohibited the use of his data there or at any later time.

Regarding the question of whether the requested "consent" was sufficiently delineated from the contract text, the respondent reiterated its view that this was not consent in the sense of Art. 7 GDPR, but rather (clearly delineated by paragraph) information that was not part of the subject matter of the contract. If the contested information were provided only after the customer's signature and thus outside the contract text, the respondent feared that it would no longer be read or perceived by the affected customers.

Concerning the contested rights of third parties, the respondent argued that the complainant's civil representation authority only existed regarding the specific contract and that he had also prohibited the use of data by other recipients of the vacation *box for marketing purposes.

3. The respondent's statement was communicated to the complainant with the resolution of the Data Protection Authority on March 24, 2021, GZ: DSB-D123.289/0002-DSB/2018, and he was simultaneously given the opportunity to provide additional comments, which the complainant did not utilize.

4. In the subsequent submission (initiating another complaint procedure) dated June 25, 2019 (received on June 27, 2019), improved on July 25, 2019, the complainant essentially repeated his submissions from August 3, 2018, in the context of complaint procedure GZ: D123.289, with the difference that the "vacation *box agreement" attached to the complaint was dated June 24, 2019. This procedure was recorded under GZ: D205.235.

5. In the following statement from the respondent, which essentially repeated the submissions from August 20, 2018, dated September 5, 2019, it was stated that in this case, the data had not been passed on due to a prior objection from the complainant. Therefore, there was a lack of standing to complain.

6. The two complaint procedures D123.289 and D205.235 were combined for a joint decision under GZ: D205.235 in accordance with § 39 Abs. 2 AVG, and the parties were granted the opportunity to be heard with the resolution dated June 17, 2021, along with the submission of the investigation results.

7. After an extension of the deadline, the respondent stated in a submission dated July 22, 2021, that the complainant's data had not been passed on to third parties for marketing purposes. The complainant had already exercised his right to object in both cases at the branch of the respondent. The information texts relevant to the proceedings from the years 2018 and 2019 were no longer in use.

8. The complainant did not make any further statements.

**B. Subject of the Complaint**

Based on the submissions of the complainant, the subject of the complaint is whether the respondent violated the complainant's right to confidentiality by processing the complainant's data for marketing purposes in the context of the contract form for setting up a vacation *box.

**C. Findings of Fact**

Among other things, the respondent offers its customers the opportunity to store incoming mail at the responsible office of their residential or business address for a predetermined period. The establishment of such a *box "Vacation" can be ordered both online and on-site at a branch. D. Legally, this results in:  
1.) The complainant stated in his constitutive submission for the proceedings under GZ: D123.289 dated August 3, 2018, that he is filing a complaint regarding the "unlawful obtaining of consent for data transfer" and based his assertion primarily on the fact that the respondent, in the context of setting up a vacation mailbox, requests consent for the transfer of personal data to third parties for marketing purposes through a pre-checked checkbox, thus violating Art. 7 GDPR.  
2.) The Data Protection Authority has already stated that the rights of the data subjects are exhaustively listed in Chapter III of the GDPR (Art. 12 to 23), but a data subject can rely on any provision of the GDPR within the framework of a complaint procedure.
as long as this ultimately leads to a conceivable violation of the right to confidentiality under § 1 para 1 DSG (see DSB of September 13, 2018, DSB-D123.070/0005-DSB/2018, according to which a violation by the controller against Art. 32 GDPR can lead to a violation of § 1 para 1 DSG).  
3.) In principle, it is possible that a data subject is violated in their right to confidentiality if the request for consent to processing is made using a method that does not meet the requirements for consent according to Art. 4 no. 11 GDPR and Art. 7 para. 2 GDPR.  
4.) In this context, and in light of the fact that according to the consistent case law of the VwGH, party statements (including submissions [Walter/Kolonovits/Muzak/Stöger9 Rz 152]) in the proceedings must be interpreted exclusively according to their objective meaning (see also VwGH November 6, 2006, 2006/09/0094; with further references) and it is decisive how the statement (see VwGH July 28, 2000, 94/09/0308 et al.) must be objectively understood considering the specific legal regulation, the purpose of the proceedings, and the file situation (VwGH January 24, 1994, 93/10/0192; November 6, 2001, 97/18/0160; January 19, 2011, 2009/08/0058; see also VfSlg 17.082/2003), it must be assumed regarding the submission of August 3, 2018, that there is a complaint due to the violation of the right to confidentiality.  
5.) Similarly, the complainant criticized in the submission initiating the complaint procedure for GZ: D205.235 on July 25, 2019, the unlawful obtaining of consent or, as a result, the unlawful processing of his personal data and inferred a violation of the right to confidentiality from this.  
6.) In light of the essentially identical complaint submissions and facts, the procedural connection in this case proved to be expedient for procedural economic reasons according to § 39 para. 2 AVG.  
7.) Regarding the complaint submissions, it should be noted at the outset that Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR as well as § 24 para. 1 DSG explicitly stipulate that the right to lodge a complaint with the supervisory authority only exists if the data subject believes that the processing of their personal data violates the GDPR or § 1 or Art. 2 of the first main section of the DSG. The wording of the law therefore presupposes an actual processing of the personal data of the affected person.  
8.) As can be seen from the findings, it is undisputed that personal data of the complainant (in particular first and last name, date of birth, address) were collected in the context of the contract conclusion "holiday" and consequently also processed by the respondent in accordance with Art. 4 no. 2 GDPR for the purpose of contract fulfillment. However, this was not contested by the complainant at any time.  

9.) Contrary to the complainant's view, there was neither an obtaining of data protection consent nor a (further) processing for third-party marketing purposes in this case:  
10.) Both from the wording of the agreement of the "holiday" from 2018 ("I do not object to data transfer.") and from 2019 ("I do not agree to data transfer.") it is evident that clicking the "checkbox" does not express an affirmative declaration of intent for data processing in the sense of Art. 4 no. 11 GDPR, but rather allows for a rejection in the form of an objection according to Art. 21 leg. cit. This is also evident from the information letter of the respondent underlying the findings, from which it clearly appears that the data processing for third-party marketing purposes is based on the legal basis of "legitimate interests" (Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR) and of which the complainant, according to his own statements, was also aware.  
11.) In the present case, the complainant effectively lodged an objection by clicking the (pre-checked) checkbox and consequently, no further transfer or other processing of his personal data for marketing purposes, as contested by the complainant, took place.  
12.) Thus, a decision was to be made accordingly.
