3. 
Legally, it follows:
3.1.  
On the Jurisdiction of the DSB and the Scope of the GDPR  
Art. 83 para. 4 lit. a GDPR stipulates that in the event of violations of the provisions of Art. 8, 11, 25 to 39, 42, and 43 GDPR, fines of up to 10,000,000 Euros or, in the case of an enterprise, up to 2% of its total worldwide annual revenue from the preceding financial year may be imposed, depending on which amount is higher.  
According to § 22 para. 5 DSG, the jurisdiction for imposing fines on natural and legal persons for violations of the DSG and the GDPR lies with the DSB.  
Pursuant to Art. 2 para. 1 GDPR, the regulation applies to the wholly or partially automated processing of personal data as well as to the non-automated processing of personal data that are stored or are intended to be stored in a filing system.  
There is no doubt regarding the existence of processing of personal data within the meaning of Art. 4 no. 1 and 2 GDPR. The role of the accused as the controller according to Art. 4 no. 7 GDPR has also never been disputed. Although the managing director named himself in the report pursuant to Art. 33 GDPR, the fact that the managing director made the report on behalf of the accused in his capacity as managing director was already conclusively clarified in the context of the first administrative fine procedure (GZ: D550.829).  
As the controller, the accused is subject to the relevant obligations of the GDPR. In the specific case, the accused, as the controller, was subject to the obligation to cooperate with the DSB in the context of the security breach procedure according to Art. 31 GDPR due to the security breach that occurred. This provision constitutes a penalized obligation for controllers according to Art. 83 para. 4 lit. a GDPR and will be examined in more detail below.  

3.2.  
On the Obligation of Controllers to Cooperate with the DSB  
Controllers and processors, and where applicable their representatives, must cooperate with the supervisory authority in fulfilling its tasks upon request according to Art. 31 GDPR. This provision also constitutes a penalized obligation for controllers according to Art. 83 para. 4 lit. a GDPR.  
From Art. 31 GDPR, in conjunction with the powers of the supervisory authority under Art. 57 and Art. 58 GDPR, both tolerance and cooperation obligations can be derived. The DSB must monitor and enforce the application of the GDPR for the entire territory of Austria ("in its territory") according to Art. 57 para. 1 lit. a GDPR. This is one of the central tasks of the DSB as the supervisory authority responsible for the Austrian territory. Furthermore, the DSB must fulfill any other tasks related to the protection of personal data according to Art. 57 para. 1 lit. v GDPR. For the fulfillment of these tasks, the DSB is granted both investigative powers and remedial powers (Art. 58 para. 1 and 2 GDPR).
The GDPR grants supervisory authorities, among other things, the power to instruct the controller to provide all information necessary for the performance of their tasks (Art. 58 para. 1 lit. a GDPR). From this provision, in conjunction with Art. 31 GDPR, a duty to cooperate arises for the addressees of the norms (see also Recital 82 second sentence GDPR). In the event of a lack of cooperation, the Union legislator therefore introduced the possibility of sanctions in Art. 83 para. 4 lit. a GDPR.

In the present case, the accused, as already mentioned, is to be qualified as a controller according to Art. 4 no. 7 GDPR and thus as the addressee of the relevant obligations of the GDPR. She submitted a data breach notification to the DSB and was subsequently involved as a party in the proceedings. All requests from the DSB were delivered to the accused in the context of the data breach proceedings, as established. Due to the lack of cooperation in this procedure, a penalty notice has already been imposed on the accused. Nevertheless, the accused subsequently failed to respond to the two additional requests for supplementary statements sent during the (here relevant) period of the offense. Thus, she did not cooperate with the DSB as the competent supervisory authority in fulfilling its tasks.

In light of the facts assumed to be proven, the accused also fulfilled the objective side of the offense under Art. 31 GDPR, as she did not cooperate with the DSB in fulfilling its tasks in response to both inquiries.

3.3. 
Regarding the subjective side of the offense

The CJEU has explicitly stated regarding the second preliminary question, as already assumed by the Data Protection Authority in its previous case law, that only violations of provisions of the GDPR committed by the controller with fault, i.e., intentionally or negligently, can lead to the imposition of a fine (cf. CJEU of 05.12.2023, C-807/21, para. 68). 

Regarding the subjective side of the offense, it should be noted that the requirement of fault for the imposition of a fine under Art. 83 GDPR is to be interpreted autonomously under Union law and, in particular, assessed in light of the case law of the CJEU. Also concerning the preliminary question regarding fault, the CJEU stated that the Union legislator did not grant the Member States any discretion in this regard for national regulations, as the material requirements are conclusively regulated in Art. 83 paras. 1 to 6 GDPR (cf. also CJEU of 05.12.2023, C-683/21, para. 64 et seq.).

Regarding the question of whether a violation was committed intentionally or negligently and can therefore be punished with a fine, the CJEU simultaneously clarified in its aforementioned judgment that such fault is already present if the accused could not be unaware of the unlawfulness of their behavior, regardless of whether they were aware that they were violating the provisions of the GDPR (cf. CJEU C-807/21, para. 76).
Referring to further case law, the CJEU also explicitly clarified that the application of Art. 83 GDPR against legal entities does not require any action or even knowledge on the part of the management body of that legal entity (cf. CJEU judgment of 05.12.2023, C-807/21, para. 77). The responsibility and liability of a controller extends to any processing of personal data carried out by or on behalf of that controller. In this context, the controller must not only take appropriate and effective measures but must also be able to demonstrate that its processing activities are in compliance with the GDPR and that the measures it has taken to ensure this compliance are also effective (cf. CJEU C-807/21, para. 38, referring to Recital 74). 

Applied to the present case, this means the following: 
First, it should be noted that there were no indications during the investigation that the violations in question were committed by a person acting outside the scope of the business activities and on behalf of the legal entity. The accused also did not make any submissions in this regard, and it is evident from the file that the managing director submitted a data breach notification to the data protection authority on behalf of the accused and subsequently communicated with the responsible caseworker at the data protection authority. As a result, the accused, through its managing director, became aware of the lack of cooperation of the accused in the context of the data breach procedure. 

It is irrelevant which specific natural person within the organization of the accused fulfilled the condition of lack of cooperation. According to the CJEU, it is not necessary for the imposition of a fine against a legal entity that the data protection authority identifies a specific natural person who acted in the course of business activities and on behalf of the legal entity in its decision and attributes the actions of that person to the legal entity (cf. CJEU C-807/21, para. 60). 

During the course of the investigation, there were no indications that the accused bore no fault in the violation of the applicable administrative regulations. Rather, the accused should have ensured that it responded to the letters sent to it by the data protection authority within the deadline set for it (cf. recently Administrative Court of Vienna judgment of 17.09.2024, W298 2295130-1). This is all the more true as an administrative penalty proceeding had already been conducted against the accused due to a violation of the duty to cooperate. 

In light of the CJEU's case law regarding the unlawfulness of its behavior, the accused could not have been unaware, regardless of whether it was aware that it was violating the provisions of the GDPR (cf. CJEU C-807/21, paras. 76 and 77; CJEU C-683/21, paras. 81 and 82 with further references).
The accused must, however, be aware that she is committing a violation of the obligation to cooperate as stipulated in Article 31 of the GDPR. This is partly because she is already (legally) convicted due to such a violation and must therefore be aware that violations of the obligation to cooperate are subject to a monetary fine. Furthermore, the requests for supplementary reporting in the data breach procedure underlying the current criminal decision have always pointed out the obligation to cooperate and the fact that a further administrative penalty procedure may be initiated in case of violation of this obligation. 

Thus, the accused has at least seriously considered the possibility of committing another violation of the obligation to cooperate but has resigned herself to it (dolus eventualis). As a result, there is culpability in the present case in the form of (conditional) intent (Article 83(2)(b) GDPR). 

This also fulfills the subjective aspect of the offense. 

4. 
Regarding the determination of the penalty, the following should be noted: 
According to Article 83(1) GDPR, the supervisory authority must ensure that the imposition of fines for violations of the provisions of the GDPR subject to sanctions (Article 83(4), (5), and (6) GDPR) is effective, proportionate, and deterrent in each individual case. 

Specifically, Article 83(2) GDPR stipulates that when deciding on the imposition of a fine and its amount, certain criteria must be duly considered in each individual case. 

The determination of the penalty within a statutory penalty framework is a discretionary decision that must be made according to the criteria established by the legislator (cf. VwGH 05.09.2013, 2013/09/0106). The criteria to be considered by the Data Protection Authority for the determination of the penalty are conclusively defined in Article 83(2) GDPR. 

In the context of penalty assessment, the Data Protection Authority has applied the EDSA guidelines regarding the calculation of fines under the GDPR (cf. EDPB Guidelines 04/2022 on the calculation of administrative fines under the GDPR, Version 2.1 of 24.05.2023 – hereinafter referred to as "Fines Guidelines"). 

The penalty range in the specific case amounts to up to EUR 10,000,000 according to Article 83(4)(a) GDPR. 

The relevant annual turnover of the accused could not be determined due to lack of cooperation. Therefore, an estimation had to be made by the DSB. The annual financial statement of B*** as of 30.11.2023 shows negative equity. According to the statements of the managing directors in the annex, however, this is covered by hidden reserves in the business property, and there is no over-indebtedness in the sense of § 225(1) UGB. Consequently, the accused is classified into the lowest category of the Fines Guidelines ("Undertakings with a turnover of up until € 2 million").
Through this classification, the size of the company is duly taken into account to ensure, in particular, the proportionality of the fine. The penalty range in the specific case amounts, according to Art. 83 para. 4 GDPR, to a sum of up to EUR 10,000,000 (static penalty range). The dynamic penalty range (2% of annual turnover) does not apply. 

In light of the facts assumed to be proven and taking into account the nature, severity, and duration of the violation (Art. 83 para. 1 lit. a GDPR [Note by the editor: obvious editorial error, likely meant Art. 83 para. 2 lit. a GDPR]), the intentionality of the violation (Art. 83 para. 2 lit. b GDPR), as well as the categories of personal data affected by the violation (Art. 83 para. 2 lit. g GDPR), the DSB assumes the severity of the infringement ("Seriousness of the infringement") to be medium ("medium level of seriousness"). In this context, the duration and the (conditional) intentionality of the violation were particularly significant. 

Regarding the present facts, the following aggravating factors were additionally considered in the penalty assessment (beyond the criteria already taken into account for determining the severity according to Art. 83 para. 1 lit. a, b, and g GDPR): 
- The accused has already committed a violation of the duty to cooperate under Art. 31 GDPR and was therefore previously convicted. This constitutes a relevant prior violation by the accused (Art. 83 para. 2 lit. e GDPR). 

Regarding the present facts, the following mitigating factors were additionally considered in the penalty assessment: 
- The accused has cooperated in the present administrative penalty proceedings and has now also answered those questions that should have been answered in the context of the duty to cooperate in the security breach proceedings, allowing this procedure to be definitively concluded (Art. 83 para. 2 lit. f GDPR). 

In determining the penalty, considerations of special prevention and general prevention may also be included according to the consistent case law of the VwGH (see VwGH 15.5.1990, 89/02/0093, VwGH 22.4.1997, 96/04/0253, VwGH 29.1.1991, 89/04/0061). The imposition of the specific penalty was necessary in terms of special prevention to deter the accused from committing further criminal acts, as the accused was already previously convicted and nevertheless committed another violation. Furthermore, the imposition of a monetary fine was also necessary in the general preventive sense to raise awareness among responsible parties and processors, particularly in connection with their duty to cooperate under Art. 31 GDPR. 

The penalty ultimately imposed in the amount of EUR 600 appears, therefore, in light of the realized value of the act, measured against the available penalty range of Art. 83 para. 4 GDPR (here up to EUR 10,000,000) and taking into account the relevant penalty assessment criteria according to Art. 83 para. 2 GDPR, to be appropriate in terms of the act and culpability and is at the lower end of the available penalty range. 

An (even) lower amount would no longer meet the criteria for a monetary fine as stipulated in Art. 83 para. 1 GDPR in the present case.
