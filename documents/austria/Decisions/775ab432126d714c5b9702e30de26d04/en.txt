A cookie is a small file or text information (typically smaller than one kilobyte) that is placed on the hard drive of a computer or mobile device by a website through the user's browser.  
A cookie allows the website to "remember" the user's actions or preferences. Most web browsers support cookies, but users can configure their browsers to refuse cookies. They can also delete cookies at any time.

D. From a legal perspective, it follows that:  
On the scope of application of the GDPR  
D.1. On the scope of application of the GDPR  
First, it must be examined whether the territorial scope of the GDPR is opened:  
The GDPR primarily relates to the use of data within the establishment of a controller or a processor within the territory of the Union, but Article 3 of the GDPR expands the territorial scope of European data protection law.  
Accordingly, the absence of an establishment in the Union does not necessarily lead to the exclusion of the processing activities of a controller based in a third country from the scope of the GDPR.  
Article 3(2) of the GDPR states:  
“This Regulation applies to the processing of personal data of data subjects who are in the Union by a controller not established in the Union.”
**niedergelassenen Verantwortlichen oder Auftragsverarbeiter, wenn die Datenverarbeitung im Zusammenhang damit steht,**  
a) **betroffenen Personen in der Union Waren oder Dienstleistungen anzubieten, unabhängig davon, ob von diesen betroffenen Personen eine Zahlung zu leisten ist, oder**  
b) **ihr Verhalten zu beobachten, soweit dieses Verhalten in der Union stattfindet.**  

Im gegenständlichen Fall kommen grundsätzlich Art. 3 Abs. 2 lit. a („Anbieten von Waren oder Dienstleistungen“) und Art. 3 Abs. 2 lit. b DSGVO („Verhaltensbeobachtung“) in Betracht.  

**D.1.1. Zu Art. 3 Abs. 2 lit. a DSGVO („Anbieten von Waren oder Dienstleistungen”)**  
Auch wenn der Begriff der „Ausrichtung einer Tätigkeit“ vom „Anbieten von Waren oder Dienstleistungen“ abweicht, hält der EDSA diese Rechtsprechung in der Rechtssache Pammer / Reederei Karl Schlüter GmbH & Co und Hotel Alpenhof / Heller (verbundene Rechtssachen C-585/08 und C-144/09) für hilfreich bei einer Prüfung der Frage, ob Waren oder Dienstleistungen einer betroffenen Person in der Union angeboten werden. Unter Berücksichtigung der besonderen Umstände des Falls könnten daher unter anderem folgende Faktoren berücksichtigt werden, möglicherweise in Verbindung miteinander:  

a) **Die EU oder mindestens ein Mitgliedstaat wird unter Bezugnahme auf die angebotene Ware oder Dienstleistung benannt;**  
b) **der Verantwortliche oder der Auftragsverarbeiter bezahlt einen Suchmaschinenbetreiber für einen Internetreferenzierungsdienst, um den Zugang zu seiner Website durch Verbraucher in der Union zu erleichtern, oder der Verantwortliche oder der Auftragsverarbeiter hat eine Marketing- und Werbekampagne gestartet, die sich an das Publikum in einem EU-Land wendet;**  
c) **die internationale Natur der in Rede stehenden Tätigkeit, wie bestimmte touristische Aktivitäten;**  
d) **die Angabe spezieller Adressen oder Telefonnummern, die von einem EU-Land zu erreichen sind;**  
e) **die Verwendung eines anderen Top-Level-Domain-Namens als desjenigen des Drittlands, in dem der Verantwortliche oder der Auftragsverarbeiter niedergelassen ist, z. B. „de“, oder die Verwendung neutraler Top-Level-Domain-Namen wie „.eu“;**  
f) **die Beschreibung der Reiseinstruktionen von einem oder mehreren anderen EU-Mitgliedstaaten zu dem Ort, an dem die Dienstleistung erbracht wird;**  
g) **die Angabe eines internationalen Kundenkreises, der aus Kunden mit Sitz in verschiedenen EU-Mitgliedstaaten besteht, insbesondere durch die Rechnungslegung durch diese Kunden;**  
h) **die Verwendung einer anderen Sprache oder einer anderen Währung als der im Land des Gewerbetreibenden üblichen, insbesondere einer Sprache oder Währung eines oder mehrerer EU-Mitgliedstaaten;**  
i) **der Verantwortliche bietet die Lieferung von Waren in EU-Mitgliedstaaten an (vgl. Leitlinien 3/2018 zum räumlichen Anwendungsbereich der DSGVO (Artikel 3) des EDSA vom 12. November 2019, S 20).**  

Wie den Sachverhaltsfeststellungen entnommen werden kann (siehe C.2.), bietet die Website Nachrichten, Features, Rezensionen und Interviews, die sich auf Fitness und Sport, insbesondere Laufen, beziehen. Die Website verweist auch auf Webshops anderer Unternehmen, in denen Kunden die präsentierten Produkte kaufen können und besteht ausschließlich auf der britischen Website die Möglichkeit ein Printabonnement nach Österreich zu bestellen.  

Demnach ist die Website für Besucher aus der EU ausschließlich „zugänglich“.
As already noted in Recital 23, third sentence of the GDPR, the mere accessibility of the website of the controller, an email address, or other contact details, or the use of a language that is commonly used in the third country where the controller is established, is not a sufficient indication of the territorial scope. Rather, other factors such as the use of a language or currency that is common in one or more Member States, in conjunction with the possibility of ordering goods and services in that other language, or the mention of customers or users located in the Union, may indicate that the controller intends to offer goods or services to individuals in the Union (see again Recital 23 of the GDPR). 

It is generally agreed that a print subscription can be ordered via a link on the website; however, this assumption is not supported by the understanding of the territorial scope of the GDPR as per the aforementioned case law, since the possibility exists solely through accessing a British link. A targeting of the website in question towards a European audience cannot be derived from this; rather, it suggests a European orientation of the British website.

The EDSA also holds the view that in processing activities related to the offering of services, the provision focuses on activities that are intentionally aimed at individuals in the EU, rather than unintentionally or randomly. If the processing relates to a service that is offered only to individuals outside the EU, but the service is not withdrawn when those individuals enter the EU, the corresponding processing is not subject to the GDPR. In this case, the processing is not related to an intentional targeting of individuals in the EU, but rather to targeting individuals outside the EU, which continues regardless of whether they remain outside the EU or visit the Union (see Guidelines 3/2018 on the territorial scope of the GDPR (Article 3) of the EDSA dated November 12, 2019, p. 17).

The EDSA also emphasizes that the processing of personal data of a natural person in the Union alone is not sufficient to trigger the application of the GDPR to processing activities of a controller not established in the Union (see Guidelines 3/2018 on the territorial scope of the GDPR (Article 3) of the EDSA dated November 12, 2019, p. 18).

D.1.2. Regarding Art. 3 para. 2 lit. b GDPR ("Behavioral Monitoring")  
Notwithstanding this, Art. 3 para. 2 lit. b GDPR triggers the application of the GDPR insofar as it serves to monitor the behavior of affected individuals, provided that their behavior occurs in the Union (see also Recital 24). Unlike the provision of Art. 3 para. 2 lit. a GDPR, neither Art. 3 para. 2 lit. b GDPR nor Recital 24 explicitly state a necessary degree of "intentionality" on the part of the controller.
or the data processor to determine whether the observation activity would trigger the application of the GDPR to the processing activity (cf. Guidelines 3/2018 on the territorial scope of the GDPR (Article 3) of the EDPB dated November 12, 2019, p. 23). The EDPB does not believe that "every" online collection or analysis of personal data from individuals in the EU should automatically be considered as "observation." However, the use of the term "observation" implies that the controller pursues a specific purpose when collecting and subsequently reusing the relevant data about the behavior of a person in the EU (cf. Guidelines 3/2018 on the territorial scope of the GDPR (Article 3) of the EDPB dated November 12, 2019, p. 23). 

Since suitability is key, the motivation of the controller is irrelevant. Therefore, observation also occurs when the controller primarily aims to understand the technical processes on its website, but processes personal data in a manner that makes the internet activities of a data subject traceable (cf. Ernst in Kühling/Buchner, General Data Protection Regulation Commentary, on Art. 3, para. 97). 

The application of Article 3(2)(b) GDPR can therefore encompass a wide range of observation activities, with the EDPB particularly focusing on the following elements (cf. Guidelines 3/2018 on the territorial scope of the GDPR (Article 3) of the EDPB dated November 12, 2019, p. 23): 
- Behavioral advertising 
- Geolocation activities, particularly for marketing purposes 
- Online tracking through the use of cookies or other tracking techniques such as fingerprinting 
- Personalized dietary and health analysis services online 
- CCTV

From the wording of Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR, it is evident that any requests made within the framework of a complaint procedure—assuming their admissibility—must relate to the person of the complaining party (“personal data concerning them”). D.7. On the request of the complainant dated December 30, 2022, for a determination of an alleged violation of the right to confidentiality (Complaint Point C) 
According to § 24 para. 4 DSG, the claim for the processing of a complaint expires if the complainant does not submit it within one year after becoming aware of the event giving rise to the complaint, but at the latest within three years after the event allegedly occurred. Late complaints are to be rejected in accordance with § 24 para. 4 last sentence DSG. 

§ 24 para. 4 DSG refers to an event giving rise to the complaint as the trigger for the running of the deadlines. In this case, the visit to the website https://www.runningsport***.com/ on September 24, 2021 – and the associated data processing – is considered such an event. 

The submission of the supplementary request on December 30, 2022, i.e., more than a year after becoming aware of the event giving rise to the complaint (website visit), is therefore deemed late, and the complainant's claim for the processing of this request had already expired at the time of the supplement, which is why the complaint in this regard was to be rejected in accordance with § 24 para. 4 DSG.

Neither the GDPR nor the DSG implies that in the case of asserting a violation of, for example, Art. 17 GDPR, there must simultaneously be a request to the data protection authority that a past legal violation be established. In other words: the subject of the complaint can also solely be a violation of, for example, Art. 17 GDPR, and it is not necessary to simultaneously assert a violation of § 1 para. 1 DSG or Art. 6 para. 1 GDPR. 

Thus, in this case, a complete complaint with clear requests was submitted with the original submission dated August 9, 2022, which was therefore not subject to a request for rectification.

D.8. On the performance order 
a) General 
The data protection authority has remedial powers under Art. 58 para. 2 lit. d GDPR that allow it to instruct a controller to change or carry out processing operations in a specific manner and within a specific timeframe. 

According to the case law of the BVwG, it is permissible for the data protection authority to also ex officio exercise its powers as defined in Art. 58 para. 2 GDPR in the complaint procedure (see the dg. ruling of November 16, 2022, Zl. W274 2237056-1/8E). 

The considerations of the BVwG are also in line with the case law of the CJEU, according to which a supervisory authority is obliged to use its remedial powers in the event of identified deficiencies (see the judgment of the CJEU of July 16, 2020, C-311/18, para. 111).
Although the complaint in question was ultimately dismissed, among other reasons, because the data of the bP has since been deleted, this does not change the fact that, in the opinion of the data protection authority, the cookie banner in question (or specifically: the request for consent) does not comply with the requirements of the GDPR. 

For the assessment of how the cookie banner and the interaction options are to be understood, the figure of an average informed, attentive, and understanding consumer is to be applied (cf. the judgment of the CJEU of July 16, 1998, C-210/96 [Gut Springenheide GmbH] para. 37; the decision of the BVwG of December 13, 2022, GZ: W214 2234934-1; Article 29 Data Protection Working Party, Guidelines on Consent under Regulation 2016/67, WP259 rev.01, 17/DE, p. 16; Greve in Sydow, Commentary Art. 12 para. 11; Illibauer in Knyrim, DataKomm Art. 12 para. 39; regarding the DSG 2000 also Jahnel, Handbook para. 7/22 with references). 

Based on this standard, the following can be stated for the case at hand: 
b) Regarding the performance order according to 4. a) of the ruling 
The granting of consent for cookies must be as easy as its withdrawal (cf. Art. 7 para. 3 GDPR as well as the judgment of the CJEU of October 27, 2022, C-129/21 para. 65). 

In conclusion, the non-granting of consent (or closing the cookie banner and continuing to surf without consent) must also be as easy as granting consent. Therefore, no more interactions with the cookie banner may be required for the non-granting of consent (or closing the cookie banner and continuing to surf without consent) than for granting consent. 

In the present case, a cookie banner is used as a request for consent for the use of cookies (and the associated processing of personal data). Specifically, on the first level of the cookie banner, there is only the option to accept the cookie banner (“ACCEPT”). If one wishes to refuse consent, it is necessary to decline consent (“Decline”) under the option “LEARN MORE” on the second level under “Cookies Choices.” 

Affected individuals cannot be required to make the decision not to grant consent on a button (such as a cookie banner) only on a second or third level, as in this case, it cannot be assumed that there is an unambiguous expression of will within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 11 GDPR. 

In particular, it cannot be ruled out that affected individuals selected the “Accept” option simply because, from their perspective, there was no immediate option to “Decline” available or because they did not even recognize that a “Decline” option was available due to the design. The BG bears the burden of proof for the validity of any consent (cf. the judgment of the CJEU of July 4, 2023, C-252/21 para. 95). 

In addition to the aforementioned case law of the CJEU, it should be noted that a procedure in which affected individuals are required to perform more interactions for the non-granting of consent than for granting consent does not comply with the principle of data processing in good faith (“fairly processed”) according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a GDPR nor with the principle of data protection by design (“privacy by design”) according to Art. 25 para. 1 of the same regulation. This circumstance also supports the interpretation of Art. 4 No. 11 in conjunction with Art. 7 GDPR advocated by the data protection authority. 

This view ultimately aligns with the opinion of the EDSA (cf. Report of the work undertaken by the Cookie Banner Taskforce, pp. 4 f, available at https://edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/report/report-work-undertaken-cookie-banner-taskforce_en). 

A guideline for a “Decline” option on the first level is furthermore that it is designed to be visually equivalent to the “Accept” option. This means in particular that both options must be equally visible. For the visual design of the cookie banner, the “FAQs on Cookies and Data Protection” can be used as a guide (available on the website of the data protection authority, cf. in particular Question 7 and Question 8). 

c) Regarding the performance order according to 4. b) of the ruling 
As already stated, granting consent must be as easy as withdrawing it (cf. again Art. 7 para. 3 GDPR as well as the judgment of the CJEU of October 27, 2022, C-129/21 para. 65). In the online context, it must therefore be clearly evident where or how consent can be withdrawn. 

In the present case, there is no simple and clearly visible indication in the cookie banner itself that explains where consent can be withdrawn. 

Although there is a “Cookie Choices” button at the bottom of the website https://www.runningsport***.com/ and various preferences regarding cookies as well as the withdrawal can be made in the menu (see factual finding C.6.), this alone is not sufficient, as it must already be clear from the cookie banner, i.e., at the time of granting consent, how it can be withdrawn. 

In particular, it cannot be expected of an affected person to search the website for the withdrawal. 

The BG will therefore need to implement a solution in which it is already evident in the cookie banner where consent can be withdrawn. 
d) Regarding the performance order according to 4. c) of the ruling
The use of cookies (and the associated processing of personal data), which are not technically necessary for the use of a website, requires prior consent (cf. Art. 29 WP Opinion 04/2012 on Cookie Consent Exemption, WP 194, 00879/12/EN p. 9 et seq.). According to the case law of the BVwG, Article 5(3) of Directive 2002/58/EC as amended (in conjunction with §165(3) TKG 2021) is also not to be interpreted in the sense of "economic necessity." This means that, for example, advertising cookies for delivering personalized advertising do not become "technically necessary" merely because delivering personalized advertising is necessary for financing the operation of the website (see the ruling of the BVwG of March 12, 2019, GZ: W214 2223400-1, as well as the "FAQ on Cookies and Data Protection," available on the website of the Data Protection Authority, particularly Question 5). 

It should be noted that the exception contained in § 165(3) TKG 2021, "provision of a service explicitly requested by the user," (as well as the associated formulation "absolutely necessary") must be interpreted restrictively. For the permissibility of cookies, this means that they must be absolutely necessary for the provision of the service and there must be a clear connection with the service explicitly requested by the participant or user (cf. Riesz in Riesz/Schilchegger (eds.), TKG (2016) § 96 Rn 48). Furthermore, the aforementioned statement of the former Art. 29 WP indicates that it must be assessed what is absolutely necessary from the user's perspective, not that of the service provider.

The BG qualifies analysis cookies, e.g., J***Data Analytics, as technically necessary cookies ("strictly necessary cookies"). However, it must be countered that analysis cookies, based on the considerations presented, cannot be classified as technically necessary cookies (cf. regarding J***Data Analytics and the personal reference of J***Data Analytics cookies also the ruling of the BVwG of May 12, 2023, W245 2252208-1/36E and W245 2252221-1/30E). 

The BG will therefore need to adjust its website accordingly so that prior consent is obtained for the use of such analysis cookies and the associated data processing. 

It was therefore decided accordingly.
