D. Legally, it follows: 
D.1. Regarding the standing of the complainant 
The complainant is a legal entity in the form of a limited liability company based in Austria. The respondent cites in the submission dated October 4, 2023, the BVwG ruling from September 19, 2023, GZ: W298 2261568-1/16E, which states that legal entities do not have active standing to file data protection complaints. Therefore, the present complaint should be dismissed. 

In the complaint case against this BVwG ruling, the Constitutional Court (VfGH) states in its ruling dated March 12, 2024, GZ: E 3436/2023-16, that it has no doubt that the fundamental rights provisions in § 1 DSG cover not only natural persons but also legal entities as bearers of fundamental rights (e.g., VfSlg. 19.673/2012). The GDPR has not changed this. However, a separate question is whether a legal entity can file a complaint with the data protection authority due to a violation of its right to the confidentiality of personal data according to § 1 Abs. 1 DSG and furthermore whether and under what conditions a legal entity can assert a violation of its right to information, rectification, or deletion of personal data according to § 1 Abs. 3 DSG - in the absence of corresponding implementing regulations in the Data Protection Act - before the data protection authority. 

However, the VfGH was able to leave this question open in the complaint case because the data protection authority is in no case competent to decide on complaints related to publications ("investor warnings") according to § 92 Abs. 11 WAG fourth sentence 2018 (see Rz 27 and Rz 28 in the ruling dated March 12, 2024, GZ: E 3436/2023-16). 

The data protection authority has previously held the view that legal entities, just like natural persons, can rely on § 1 DSG and that this domestically structured fundamental rights protection is also in accordance with the GDPR. 

The question of whether legal entities can invoke the fundamental right to data protection is - as the VfGH also stated in its ruling dated March 12, 2024 - to be distinguished from the question of whether the statutory provisions of the DSG - particularly § 24 - apply to legal entities. 

According to § 4 Abs. 1 DSG, the provisions of the GDPR and the DSG apply to the wholly or partially automated processing of personal data of natural persons as well as to the non-automated processing of personal data of natural persons that are stored or are to be stored in a filing system. 

According to the will of the legislator, the statutory provisions of the DSG, including the complaint under § 24 DSG, are therefore limited to the protection of natural persons. However, the data protection authority has already stated multiple times that § 1 DSG also protects legal entities. 

An interpretation of the statutory provisions - particularly §§ 4 and 24 DSG - in such a way as to grant only natural persons the opportunity to file a complaint before the data protection authority, while not allowing legal entities to do so, would attribute an unequal and thus unconstitutional content to these provisions in light of § 1 DSG. It cannot be assumed that the legislator intended to treat legal entities in the pursuit of their constitutionally guaranteed rights grossly disadvantageously compared to natural persons without a comprehensible reason (see the decision dated May 25, 2020, GZ: 2020-0.191.240, with references). That it is possible for the national legislator to provide for a purely national concept of the protection of personal data of legal entities has been affirmed by the CJEU (see the judgment dated December 10, 2020, C-620/19 Rz 47), so that the aforementioned case law of the data protection authority is not in contradiction to this.
There is therefore no specific reason to set the level of legal protection for legal entities lower. This is supported by the wording of § 24 Abs. 1 DSG, where the right to complain is granted not only in the event of violations of the GDPR but also with regard to § 1 DSG. It is also significant that, in addition to natural and legal persons, associations of persons also fall under this fundamental rights protection. Following the aforementioned statements of the Federal Administrative Court, groups of persons with corporate forms without legal personality, as well as citizens' initiatives and homeowners' associations, would also have no fundamental rights protection, because the GDPR does not include these associations of persons within its scope (cf. Lachmayer in Knyrim, DatKomm Art. 1 GDPR Rz 85; see also Eberhard in Korinek/Holoubek et al § 1 DSG Rz 25 and Ennöckl, Privacy 145 ff; see also regarding the assertion of claims by associations of persons ibid, 147). 

As a result, this means that legal entities – contrary to the statements of the Federal Administrative Court in its decision of September 19, 2023 – are entitled to lodge complaints to the extent of the rights granted to them by § 1 DSG. However, in order not to attribute an unconstitutional content of equality to § 1 DSG, the Data Protection Authority can only exercise its jurisdiction over legal entities to the same extent as it does for natural persons in similar circumstances. § 1 DSG cannot be interpreted in such a way that the Data Protection Authority is required to substantively address complaints from legal entities while not doing so for natural persons in the same situation. This would result in an unjustifiable preferential treatment of legal entities and thus attribute an unconstitutional content of equality to § 1 DSG. 

It must therefore be stated that the complainant, as a legal entity, is generally actively entitled to file a complaint under § 24 DSG before the Data Protection Authority, provided that it alleges a violation of the rights guaranteed by § 1 DSG, but only to the extent that this would also be possible for a natural person. 

In other words: The assertion of subjective rights by a legal entity is limited to the rights stipulated in § 1 DSG (confidentiality, information, deletion, correction). The additional rights stipulated in the GDPR (such as objection, restriction of processing, etc.) do not apply to legal entities. 

In the present case, the complainant is thus actively entitled to file a complaint in accordance with § 1 DSG in conjunction with § 24 DSG with the Data Protection Authority as the competent supervisory authority.  

D.2. Fundamental Right to Confidentiality 

§ 1 DSG stipulates the fundamental right to confidentiality, according to which everyone, particularly with regard to the respect for their private and family life, has a claim to the confidentiality of personal data concerning them, provided that there is a legitimate interest in doing so. However, the fundamental right to data protection does not apply absolutely and may be restricted by certain permissible interventions. 

According to § 1 Abs. 2 DSG, restrictions on the claim to confidentiality, as long as the use of personal data does not occur in the vital interest of the data subject or with their consent, are only permissible to safeguard overriding legitimate interests of another party, whereby interventions by a state authority may only occur based on laws that are necessary for the reasons stated in Art. 8 Abs. 2 ECHR. 

D.3. Vehicle License Plates as Personal Data 

With regard to vehicle license plates, the Data Protection Authority assumes that these are personal data within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 1 GDPR, as the registered owner can be identified through the license plate by means of a request under § 47 Abs. 2a KFG 1967 using proportionate means. 

Using a request according to § 47 Abs. 2a KFG 1967, only the registered owner and not the vehicle driver can be determined. In the specific case, through the use of the vehicle with the license plate number AT Ü**4*X, the complainant is identifiable as the registered owner and thus as a legal entity. 

D.4. Legal Basis 

In terms of procedure, restrictions on the claim to confidentiality are only permissible in the presence of a qualified legal basis, as the respondent is a "state authority" within the meaning of § 1 Abs. 2 DSG (responsible for the public sector) (cf. § 6 ASFINAG Authorization Act 1997, according to which the respondent has been authorized to collect tolls).

D.5. Requirements for a legal basis  
The constant case law of the Constitutional Court regarding the quality of an intervention norm in the sense of § 1 para. 2 DSG indicates that a legal basis must be sufficiently precise, i.e., foreseeable for everyone, and must also regulate under what conditions the collection or use of personal data for the performance of specific administrative tasks is permitted (see, most recently, the findings of December 11, 2019, G 72/2019 et al., and December 12, 2019, G 164/2019 et al.). The respective legislator must therefore provide a material-specific regulation in accordance with the provisions of § 1 para. 2 DSG, which specifies and limits the cases of permissible interventions in the fundamental right to data protection (VfSlg 18,643/2008).  
D.6. Result  
§ 16a para. 1 BStMG entitles the respondent to process the personal data necessary for toll collection, toll supervision, and the prosecution of toll evasion in an automation-supported manner.  
§ 16a para. 3 BStMG further stipulates that the respondent may process, among other things, transaction data within the scope of route toll (see Z 3 leg. cit.). A legal basis in the sense of § 1 para. 2 DSG is thus present.  
In conclusion, it can be stated that the storage of passage data is carried out in accordance with legal requirements. The legal basis is sufficiently precise and regulates the conditions under which data processing (and thus an intervention in the fundamental right to data protection) is to be permitted. The cases of permissible data processing are also sufficiently specified and limited by the maximum permissible storage durations.  
This consideration also corresponds to the case law of the Administrative Court, according to which it is sufficient if the task to be performed is sufficiently described in the legal basis and the data processing serves the purpose of task fulfillment (see the ruling of the VwGH of December 21, 2023, Ro 2021/04/0010).  
The processing is carried out in accordance with the principle of data minimization, appropriately and significantly for the legal purpose, and is limited to the necessary extent for the purposes of processing. Namely, only as long as necessary for the processing of the route toll, whereby fraud prevention and the handling of complaints make immediate deletion of the passage data impossible. The storage of passage data for one year from the toll station passage is appropriate for the purposes of fraud prevention and complaints. However, storage for billing purposes does not occur – as established.  
The respondent is therefore to be agreed with in conclusion that the collection and storage of passage data for electronic toll processing is legally permissible.  
A decision was made accordingly.
