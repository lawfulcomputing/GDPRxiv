B. Subject of the Complaint  
B.1. Determination of a legal violation that is in the past  
a) Regarding the request for determination  
Art. 58 GDPR does not contain an explicit legal basis for an independent determination regarding the potential unlawfulness of a data protection-relevant processing operation (cf. VwGH of September 1, 2022, Ra 2022/04/0066). Consequently, the mere determination of a violation of a data protection right under Art. 58 para. 6 GDPR in conjunction with § 24 para. 2 no. 5 DSG requires a request from the affected person.  
Although the complainant did not make an explicit request in the sense of § 24 para. 2 no. 5 DSG in his first submission dated August 17, 2020, the requests (further below) were directed at a (at the time of submission) ongoing legal violation.
In the course of the proceedings, the complainant clarified that, within the framework of his complaint, a legal violation should be established as of August 12, 2020: While the complainant requested this finding with a statement dated January 22, 2021, still under certain reservations (“[…] in the event that the DSB assumes a subsequent removal of the legal violation in accordance with § 24 (6) DSG, the complainant requests the finding that the respondent unlawfully transferred personal data of the complainant to the USA on 12.08.2020, […]”), the complainant made it clear in his statement dated September 24, 2021, and particularly on July 25, 2022, that the relevant date for the complaint is August 12, 2020, and that the legal violation is not subject to removal in the sense of § 24 (6) DSG. These statements were also communicated to the respondents. 

From the perspective of the Data Protection Authority, there is therefore a clear reference to § 24 (2) Z 5 DSG in the present case, and the will of the complainant is evident that a finding regarding a past legal violation should be discussed. 

b) Regarding the legal violations 

From the submission of the complainant dated August 17, 2020, and particularly his statement dated July 25, 2022, it is evident that the subject of the complaint generally concerns data transfers to the USA on August 12, 2020, related to N3*** Business Tools (“[…] On the website, the controller embedded the HTML code for N3*** services [including N3*** Connex]”). The complainant has at no time limited his complaint to data processing that occurred solely as a result of the implementation of N3*** Login. 

Considering this consideration as well as the further statements of the complainant from January 22, 2021, and September 24, 2021, the following legal violations are to be discussed: 

Complaint Point A): Did the respondents transfer personal data of the complainant to the USA on at least August 12, 2020, due to the implementation of N3*** Business Tools on the website www.n1***.at, without ensuring an adequate level of protection in accordance with Art. 44 GDPR? 

Complaint Point B): Did the second respondent violate Art. 5 ff in conjunction with Art. 28 in conjunction with Art. 29 GDPR during the contested data transfer on August 12, 2020? 

B.2. Regarding the processing ban 

Regarding the complainant's request to issue an immediate ban on any data transfers in accordance with Art. 58 (2) lit. d, lit. f, and lit. j GDPR, it is not to be decided, as the first respondent removed the N3*** Business Tools from its website www.n1***.at before the conclusion of the present proceedings. 

B.3. Regarding the request for the imposition of a fine 

Regarding the complainant's request to impose a fine against the respondents, it is not to be decided, as this request was withdrawn with the statement dated July 25, 2022 (and is now to be understood as a suggestion). 

C. Findings of fact 

C.1. Regarding the general functioning of cookies 

Cookies can collect information generated by a website and stored via the browser of an internet user. They are small files or text information (usually smaller than one kilobyte) placed by a website on the hard drive of a user's computer or mobile device through the user's browser. 

A cookie allows the website to "remember" the actions or preferences of the user. Most web browsers support cookies, but users can configure their browsers to refuse cookies. They can also delete cookies at any time. 

Websites use cookies to identify users, remember their customers' preferences, and enable users to complete tasks without having to re-enter information when switching to another page or revisiting the website later. 

Cookies can also be used to collect information for targeted advertising and marketing based on online behavior. For example, companies use software to track user behavior and create personal profiles that allow them to show users advertisements tailored to their previous search activities.

Pixel-specific data – including the pixel ID and the N3*** cookie.

Click data for buttons – all buttons that visitors to the website have clicked, the labels of those buttons, and all pages that were accessed as a result of these button clicks.

Form field names – including website field names such as email, address, quantity, for the purchase of a product or service. We do not collect field values unless they are part of Advanced Matching or optional values.

D.1. On the jurisdiction of the Data Protection Authority
a) On the media privilege according to § 9 para. 1 DSG: The first respondent disputed the jurisdiction of the Data Protection Authority to handle the present complaint in its statement dated December 28, 2020, and raised the applicability of § 9 para. 1 DSG to the data processing subject to the complaint – namely, the transfer of personal data of the complainant as a result of the implementation of N3*** Business Tools on www.n1***.at.

The following can be countered: The national legislator restricts the so-called media privilege under Art. 85 GDPR in conjunction with § 9 para. 1 DSG, by making the privilege accessible only to media companies or media services, provided that personal data is processed for journalistic purposes by media owners, publishers, and media employees or workers of a media company or media service (see the decision of the DSB dated April 21, 2020, GZ: 2020-0.239.741).

Processing of personal data for journalistic purposes is understood by the CJEU to occur when the processing solely aims to disseminate information, opinions, or ideas in the public domain (see the judgment of the CJEU dated December 16, 2008, C-73/07 para. 62).

To what extent the data processing subject to the complaint pursues a "journalistic purpose" in the sense of the CJEU's case law is not apparent. Rather, it follows from the statement of the first respondent dated March 7, 2022, that N3*** Pixels were implemented for tracking purposes and **** Login to simplify the login process for premium customers on www.n1***.at.

Moreover, N2*** Ireland receives data from website visitors due to the implementation of N3*** Business Tools on a website, which can in turn be processed for its own purposes (see, for example, factual finding C.4). Thus, regarding the data transfers subject to the complaint between the first respondent and N2*** Ireland (and ultimately the second respondent), no journalistic purpose in the sense of the CJEU's case law was pursued for this reason alone.

These considerations are also supported by the case law of the BVwG, which states, for example, that advertising cookies for displaying personalized advertising on a website of a media company or the management of a database by a media company for the purpose of sending print advertising do not fall under the media privilege (see the decision of the BVwG dated March 12, 2019, GZ: W214 2223400-1).

Since the requirements of § 9 para. 1 DSG are not met, the media privilege does not apply to the data processing subject to the complaint.

The (subsequently) issued decision of the Constitutional Court dated December 14, 2022, under reference numbers G 287/2022-16, G 288/2022-14, which declared § 9 para. 1 DSG unconstitutional, will not be addressed, as the annulment will only take effect on June 30, 2024.

b) On the e-Privacy Directive: The European Data Protection Board (subsequently: EDPB) has already dealt with the relationship between the GDPR and Directive 2002/58/EC ("e-Privacy Directive") (see the opinion 5/2019 on the interplay between the e-Privacy Directive and the GDPR dated March 12, 2019).

The Data Protection Authority has also addressed the relationship between the GDPR and the national implementation provision (in Austria now: TKG 2021, BGBl. I No. 190/2021 as amended) in its decision dated November 30, 2018, GZ: DSB-D122.931/0003 DSB/2018.

It was fundamentally stated that the e-Privacy Directive (or the respective national implementation provision) takes precedence over the GDPR as lex specialis. Article 95 GDPR stipulates that the regulation applies to natural or legal persons in relation to processing in connection with the provision of publicly accessible electronic...
**Communication Services in Public Communication Networks in the Union**

No additional obligations are imposed on communication services in public communication networks in the Union, as long as they are subject to specific obligations set out in the e-Privacy Directive that pursue the same objective. However, the e-Privacy Directive does not contain obligations in the sense of Chapter V of the GDPR in the event of the transfer of personal data to third countries or to international organizations. In this context, the GDPR is applicable to such data transfers.

There is jurisdiction of the data protection authority to address the complaint in question according to Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR.

**D.2. On Art. 44 GDPR as a Subjective Right**

Based on the previous case law of the data protection authority and the courts, it should be noted that both the legality of data processing according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a in conjunction with Art. 6 et seq. GDPR and the data protection rights of affected individuals postulated in Chapter III of the Regulation can be asserted as subjective rights within the framework of a complaint under Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR. In the present case, a violation of Art. 44 GDPR is also alleged. 

According to the data protection authority, it is crucial whether an affected person is impaired in an individual legal position by an alleged legal violation. The alleged legal violation must therefore have a negative impact on the affected person. This is to be assumed in the case of Art. 44 GDPR when personal data is transferred to a third country or an international organization, thereby undermining the level of protection guaranteed by the Regulation (see also the judgment of the CJEU of July 16, 2020, C-311/18, para. 135). 

The wording of Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR (and also the national provision of § 24 para. 1 DSG) only requires for the exercise of the right to complain that “[…] the processing of the personal data concerning them violates this Regulation.” In this sense, the CJEU stated in its judgment of July 16, 2020, that the determination that “[…] the law and practice of a country do not provide an adequate level of protection […]” as well as “[…] the compatibility of this (adequacy) decision with the protection of privacy and the freedoms and rights of individuals […]” can be asserted as a subjective right within the framework of a complaint under Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR (see the judgment of the CJEU of July 16, 2020, C-311/18, para. 158).

It should be noted that the question referred in the aforementioned proceedings did not concern the “scope of the right to complain under Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR”; however, the CJEU evidently considered the fact that a violation of provisions of Chapter V GDPR can also be asserted within the framework of a complaint under Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR as a necessary prerequisite. If viewed differently, the CJEU would likely have stated that the question of the validity of an adequacy decision cannot be clarified within the framework of a complaint procedure at all. 

Finally, it should also be assumed according to domestic case law of the VwGH that, in case of doubt, norms that prescribe administrative action also and especially in the interest of the affected person grant them a subjective, thus enforceable right through the complaint process (see, for example, VwSlg. 9151 A/1976, 10.129 A/1980, 13.411 A/1991, 13.985 A/1994). Thus, a violation of Art. 44 GDPR can be asserted within the framework of a complaint to the data protection authority.

D.6. To Ruling Point 3  
a) On the concept of "processing"  
According to the definition in Art. 4 No. 2 GDPR, "processing" refers to any operation or set of operations which is performed on personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, disclosure by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, alignment or combination, restriction, erasure or destruction.  

In the present case, the actions taken by the first respondent in relation to the data collected through the N3*** Business Tools clearly fall under the definition of processing as they involve the collection and transmission of personal data to the servers of the second respondent.  

D.7. To Ruling Point 4  
a) On the legal basis for processing  
The processing of personal data requires a legal basis as outlined in Art. 6 GDPR. In this case, the first respondent has not provided any evidence of a valid legal basis for the processing of the complainant's personal data. The absence of consent or any other legal justification for the processing of the data collected through the N3*** Business Tools constitutes a violation of the GDPR.

b) Role Distribution  
i) First Respondent  
As already stated, the first respondent, as the website operator at the time of the complaint, made the decision to implement N3*** Business Tools on its website www.n1***.at. Specifically, it inserted a JavaScript code provided by the second respondent into the source code of its website, which caused this JavaScript code to be executed in the complainant's browser when visiting the website (see C.8).  
The first respondent stated in its submission of March 7, 2022, that N3*** Pixel was implemented for tracking purposes and **** Login to simplify the login process for premium customers on www.n1***.at.  
By doing so, the first respondent decided on the “purposes and means” of the data processing associated with the tool, which is why it is to be regarded as the controller within the meaning of Art. 4 (7) GDPR.  
ii) Second Respondent  
Despite extensive investigative proceedings—such as an oral hearing of the second respondent on May 16, 2022—there are currently no sufficient indications for the Data Protection Authority to qualify the second respondent for the present data processing as the controller.  
In accordance with the data processing conditions (see C.6), the Data Protection Authority therefore assumes that the second respondent (then N3*** Inc.) was engaged as a processor within the meaning of Art. 28 (2) GDPR in the context of the present data processing on August 12, 2020.  
The question of whether the data access capabilities of US intelligence agencies change the role of the second respondent will be addressed further below.
c) Scope of Chapter V of the GDPR  
According to Art. 44 GDPR, any “[…] transfer of personal data that is already being processed or is intended to be processed after its transfer to a third country or an international organization, […] is only permissible if the controller and the processor comply with the conditions laid down in this chapter and also adhere to the other provisions of this regulation; this also applies to any further transfer of personal data from the relevant third country or the relevant international organization to another third country or another international organization. All provisions of this chapter must be applied to ensure that the level of protection for natural persons guaranteed by this regulation is not undermined.”  
The first respondent is based in Austria and is responsible for the operation of the website www.n1***.at as the data protection controller. Furthermore, the first respondent disclosed personal data of the complainant by proactively implementing N3*** Business Tools on its website www.n1***.at, resulting in, among other things, a data transfer to the second respondent (location: USA) (see also the EDSA Guidelines 5/2021 as of February 14, 2023 FN 15: “Finally, it should be noted that personal data disclosed via cookies are not considered as being disclosed directly by the data subject, but rather as a transmission by the operator of the website that the data subject is visiting”).  
It remains to be seen whether the personal data of the complainant was transferred directly to the second respondent or only in a second step after being processed by N2*** Ireland:  
The first respondent is obliged under Art. 28 para. 1 GDPR to only work with processors that provide sufficient guarantees that the data processing is carried out in accordance with the requirements of the GDPR (“selection fault”).  
According to established case law of the BVwG, the processor is the “extended arm” of the controller, and the processing by the processor is to be seen as part of the processing by the controller itself (see the decision of the BVwG of October 20, 2021, file no. W211 2231475-1; also explicitly see EDSA Guidelines 5/2021 as of February 14, 2023, para. 19 last sentence).  
In the present case, the first respondent accepted the data processing conditions of N2*** Ireland for N3*** Business Tools and agreed in accordance with Art. 28 para. 2 GDPR that N2*** Ireland may involve the second respondent (then N3*** Inc.) as a so-called sub-processor (see C.6: “[…] You hereby authorize N3*** to engage N3*** Inc. [and other N3*** companies] as its sub-processors”).  
d) Framework of Chapter V of the GDPR  
Subsequently, it must be verified whether the data transfers in question took place in accordance with the provisions of Chapter V of the GDPR to the USA.  
Chapter V of the regulation provides for three (protection) instruments to ensure the adequate level of protection required by Art. 44 GDPR for data transfers to a third country or an international organization:  
- Adequacy decision (Art. 45 GDPR);  
- Appropriate safeguards (Art. 46 GDPR);  
- Exceptions for specific cases (Art. 49 GDPR).  
e) Adequacy decision  
As can be seen from the factual findings (see C.6 and C.7), the respondents invoked the EU-US adequacy decision (“Privacy Shield”) for the data transfer on August 12, 2020.  
However, the CJEU already stated on July 16, 2020, C-311/18, that the EU-US adequacy decision does not provide an adequate level of protection for natural persons due to the relevant laws of the USA and the implementation of governmental surveillance programs – among other things based on Section 702 of FISA and E.O. 12333 in conjunction with PPD-28 (see para. 180 ff) and declared the EU-US adequacy decision invalid (see para. 201 f) without maintaining its effect.  
According to the opinion of the data protection authority, the second respondent is also to be classified as a provider of electronic communications services within the meaning of 50 U.S. Code § 1881(b)(4) and is thus subject to monitoring by US intelligence agencies in accordance with 50 U.S. Code § 1881a (“FISA 702”).  
Accordingly, the second respondent has the obligation to provide personal data to US authorities in accordance with 50 U.S. Code § 1881a. The transparency report of the N2*** group (see C.12) indicates that US intelligence agencies regularly make such requests.
ii) Suitable Guarantees  
As evident from the factual findings (see C.7), the N3*** contract amendment (including the conclusion of standard data protection clauses) was only implemented after August 12, 2020.  
ii) Exceptions for Certain Cases  
The respondents have not relied on Article 49 GDPR at any time during the investigation.  
From the perspective of the data protection authority, no condition of Article 49 GDPR is met, and in particular, no consent has been obtained in accordance with Article 49(1)(a) of the same legal text (see regarding the burden of proof of a controller the judgment of the CJEU of October 27, 2022, C-129/21, para. 81 et seq.).  
f) Result  
The data transfers in question on August 12, 2020, were not covered by any instrument of Articles 45 et seq. GDPR.  
It was therefore determined that there was a violation of Article 44 GDPR.  
D.6. Regarding Point 3  
a) Regarding Complaint Point A)  
It must be examined whether the second respondent (as data importer) is also subject to the obligations set forth in Chapter V of the Regulation.  
Based on the EDSA Guidelines 5/2021 as amended on February 14, 2023, it should be noted that a "transfer to a third country or an international organization" within the meaning of Article 44 GDPR only occurs when, among other things, the controller or processor (data exporter) discloses personal data that is the subject of this processing to another controller, a joint controller, or a processor (data importer) by means of transfer or otherwise (ibid. para. 9).  
This requirement does not apply in the present case to the second respondent, as it (as data importer) does not disclose the personal data of the complainant but (only) receives it.  
While the data protection authority does not overlook that a data transfer necessarily presupposes a recipient and that the second respondent (at least from a technical perspective) is part of the data transfer, it must be countered that the data protection responsibility in a processing operation (from a legal perspective) can still be "shared," meaning that there can be a different degree of responsibility depending on the phase of the processing operation (see the EDSA Guidelines 7/2020 on the concept of controllers and processors, para. 63 et seq. with references).  
According to the data protection authority, there is therefore no violation of Article 44 GDPR by the second respondent.  
b) Regarding Complaint Point B)  
Finally, a violation of Articles 5 et seq., Article 28, and Article 29 GDPR by the second respondent must be examined. In this regard, the complainant asserts that the second respondent processed his data contrary to the instructions and requests of the first respondent.  
The following should be countered:  
The data protection principles and the conditions for the lawfulness of data processing according to Articles 5 et seq. GDPR are, according to the explicit wording of Article 5(2) of the same legal text, an obligation of the controller.  
The (mere) possibility that the second respondent becomes the recipient of inquiries from US security authorities does not automatically lead to its liability under Article 28(10) GDPR, according to the data protection authority. Such a broad interpretation of Article 28(10) GDPR seems excessive.  
Furthermore, a violation of Articles 28 and 29 GDPR cannot be asserted as a subjective right insofar as the cited provisions (only) regulate the relationship between the controller and the processor, and a violation by the processor is attributable to the controller (see again the ruling of the BVwG of October 20, 2021, file no. W211 2231475-1).  
However, these statements are irrelevant for Point 2, as in connection with a violation of Article 44 GDPR, the success of the complaint is already fulfilled if personal data is transferred to the USA without a protective instrument.  
It was therefore decided accordingly.
