Legally, it follows:  
3.1. Regarding the scope of application of the GDPR and the jurisdiction of the DSB  
3.1.1. Both the material and territorial scope of the GDPR in the sense of Articles 2 and 3 of the GDPR apply in the present case. The accused argued in this context that the material scope was not applicable because the household exemption under Article 2(2)(c) of the GDPR was relevant. The accused claimed to have recorded the footage solely for personal interests and viewed it before deleting it (“personal use of the accused”). There was no disclosure to third parties (see written justification dated July 15, 2021).  
3.1.2. The DSB does not share the accused's view regarding the household exemption. The accused installed and operated the system within a public restroom facility, which was used by several individuals both for their bodily needs and for changing clothes. The recording area of the system thus captured public space and recorded individuals during a very intimate moment.  
3.1.3. The European Court of Justice (hereinafter “ECJ”) has already stated in this context that the operation of a video surveillance system installed by a natural person at their single-family home to protect the property, health, and life of the homeowner, which records videos of individuals on a storage device such as a hard drive while also monitoring public space, does not constitute data processing that is carried out exclusively for personal or family activities within the meaning of the provision under Article 3(2) second indent of Directive 95/46/EC (cf. ECJ December 11, 2014, C-212/13, para. 33 et seq.). The case law regarding this directive provision can be applied in the same manner to the provision under Article 2(2)(c) of the GDPR, as this provision was adopted within the framework of the GDPR. In the present case, it is therefore excluded that the exception of the household exemption applies due to the specific recording area (public space).  
3.1.4. Article 83(5)(a) of the GDPR stipulates that in the event of violations of the provisions of Articles 5, 6, 7, and 9 of the GDPR, fines of up to 20,000,000 Euros or, in the case of an enterprise, up to 4% of its total worldwide annual turnover of the preceding financial year may be imposed, depending on which amount is higher. According to Section 22(5) of the DSG, the jurisdiction for imposing fines on natural and legal persons for Austria as the national supervisory authority lies with the DSB.  
3.1.5. As a result, the GDPR applies to the specific case, and the DSB is both materially and territorially competent for the administrative penalty proceedings in question.  
3.2. Regarding the unlawful operation of the image processing system (Decision Point I.)  
3.2.1. According to the definition in Article 4(1) of the GDPR, personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (hereinafter “data subject”); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more specific characteristics that express the physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person.  
3.2.2. According to the case law of the VwGH regarding Section 4(1) of the DSG 2000, image data such as video recordings are generally included in the concept of personal data, as identifiability is usually given. There is no identifiability and thus no personal data only if the technical resolution of the image does not allow for identification (cf. VwGH September 12, 2016, Ro 2015/04/0011). The ECJ has also commented on the personal reference of image data and stated regarding the definition in Article 2(a) of Directive 95/46/EC that an image recorded by a camera of a person falls under the concept of personal data, provided it enables the identification of the data subject (cf. ECJ December 11, 2014, C-212/13, para. 22).  
3.2.3. In the present case, the DSB has determined during the investigation proceedings and after reviewing the seized videos that the image data from recording area 1 certainly allowed for the identification of the affected individuals. Thus, the image data in the recordings of the system, according to the above explanations, constitute personal data in accordance with Article 4(1) of the GDPR.  
3.2.4. The GDPR defines the term processing in Article 4(2) of the GDPR by enumerating a series of possible usage operations. This includes collecting, recording, organizing, structuring, storing, adapting or altering, retrieving, consulting, using, disclosing by transmission, dissemination or otherwise making available, aligning or combining, restricting, deleting or destroying.
3.2.5. The recordings made by the image processing system in the present case, which were triggered by motion detectors during the time of the offense, therefore represent a processing of personal data within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 2 GDPR.

3.2.6. The accused can also be qualified as the controller in terms of Art. 4 No. 7 GDPR for the specific data processing, as he alone made the decision to install and operate the system within the restroom facilities at the crime scene. No assertion was made throughout the proceedings by the accused that he would not be a controller. Rather, the accused admitted that he installed and operated the system out of a "certain thrill of the unlawful" within the restroom facilities.

3.3. Assessment of the processing in question in light of the principles according to Art. 5 GDPR

3.3.1. For the assessment of the processing in question, it can initially be stated that Art. 5 GDPR establishes the principles for the processing of personal data. The principle according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a GDPR stipulates in this context that personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject ("Lawfulness, Fairness, Transparency"). The requirements for lawful processing (of non-sensitive data) are specified in Art. 6 GDPR. According to this, the lawfulness of any processing requires that the processing—cumulatively with the other principles regulated in Art. 5 para. 1—must meet at least one of the legal grounds conclusively set out in Art. 6 para. 1 GDPR (cf. Selmayr in Ehmann/Selmayr, General Data Protection Regulation, Commentary², Art. 5 Rz 8f).

3.3.2. The principle according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b GDPR stipulates that the processed data may only be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and may not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes ("Purpose Limitation"). Furthermore, the principle according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. c GDPR states that the processing must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ("Data Minimization"). The accused violated all of the aforementioned principles during the data processing.

3.3.3. The accused only stated regarding the lawfulness of the processing that he pursued a "technical interest" (Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR). Furthermore, the accused did not present any additional legal grounds for the processing.

3.3.4. Processing based on the consent of the data subjects (Art. 6 para. 1 lit. a GDPR) is already excluded because the processing, as established, occurred secretly or without informing the data subjects. Thus, the data subjects could logically not have given consent to the data processing. It is also not to be assumed that the data subjects would have consented to such processing, as the processing concerns one of the most intimate moments of a person.

3.3.5. In the present case, after an ex officio examination, only the justification under Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR is applicable, as already presented by the accused. Therefore, the existence of legitimate interests of the accused or third parties in the sense of Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR must be examined.

3.3.6. Regarding the lawfulness of processing operations in relation to Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR, Recital 47 of the GDPR explains, among other things, that this can be based on the legitimate interests of a controller, provided that the interests or the fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not override them; the reasonable expectations of the data subject based on their relationship with the controller must be taken into account. In any case, the existence of a legitimate interest must be weighed particularly carefully, and it must also be examined whether a data subject could reasonably foresee at the time of the collection of personal data and in light of the circumstances under which it occurs that processing for this purpose may take place. Particularly when personal data is processed in situations where a data subject could not reasonably expect further processing, the interests and fundamental rights of the data subject will outweigh the interest of the controller.

3.3.7. Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f of the GDPR permits processing under three cumulative conditions: (i) Pursuit of a legitimate interest; (ii) Necessity of processing; and (iii) No overriding of the rights and freedoms of others (cf. Judgment of the CJEU of 11.12.2019, Case C-708/18, para. 36 with references).

3.3.8. In the specific case, even regarding the first condition (pursuit of a legitimate interest), it is questionable whether the "technical interest" cited by the accused can even represent a legitimate interest. Even if one were to assume in relation to Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR that the
**3.3.8.** Even if the accused had a legitimate interest during the time of the offense, nothing is gained for the accused, as the processing was, in any case, not necessary (not the least intrusive means) and the rights and freedoms of the affected individuals far outweigh the technical interest of the accused.  
**3.3.9.** The technical interests could have been ensured by the accused in any case in another way, without so severely infringing on the right to confidentiality of the affected individuals. Repeatedly recording numerous affected individuals within a public restroom to ensure technical interests related to an image processing system constitutes a violation of the data minimization principle according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. c GDPR. The processing in question was not appropriate and significant for the purpose and was not limited to what is necessary for the purposes of processing, and is in no way comprehensible for the DSB. Therefore, the necessity of processing in this specific case cannot be assumed under any circumstances.  
**3.3.10.** Consequently, there is no need for a balancing of interests, as processing under Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR cannot be considered due to the lack of necessity. However, it should be noted at this point (without going into detail) that even after conducting a balancing of interests in this specific case, one can only conclude that the confidentiality interests of the affected individuals far outweigh the technical interest of the accused. Furthermore, the system was not marked or was operated secretly, and therefore the affected individuals could not expect their personal data to be processed in this manner.  
**3.3.11.** Additionally, the accused also disregarded the principle of purpose limitation according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b GDPR. The principle of purpose limitation is constitutionally mandated. Art. 8 para. 2 sentence 1 EU Charter of Fundamental Rights guarantees affected individuals that their personal data may only be processed "for specified purposes [...] or on another legally regulated legitimate basis." A purpose that is clearly defined in advance (before the commencement of data processing) must therefore also be legitimate. The purpose is legitimate if the affected individual has approved it through their informed and free consent according to Art. 7 GDPR or if it has been approved by law (even against the will of the affected individuals) (cf. Roßnagel in Simitis/Hornung/Spicker, Data Protection Law, Commentary, Article 5 Rz 63 f).  
**3.3.12.** The DSB found in a similarly situated case that the covert recording of images in the women's changing room did not occur for legitimate purposes in the sense of Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b GDPR (cf. DSB 11.07.2019, D550.185/0002-DSB/2019). The BVwG stated regarding video surveillance in front of a gambling establishment for the purpose of identifying and categorizing individuals as "financial police" and "players" that no legitimate purpose could be recognized therein (cf. BVwG 02.06.2021, W211 2232587-1).  
**3.3.13.** In light of this, one cannot arrive at a different conclusion in the specific case. The purpose cited by the accused is neither legally provided for nor covered, nor have the affected individuals consented to the processing. The covert/secret recording of numerous affected individuals during a very intimate moment to ensure technical interests regarding the image processing system cannot, in the view of the DSB, under any circumstances represent a legitimate purpose in the sense of Art. 5 para. 1 lit. b GDPR. Furthermore, the accused was evidently aware of this at the time of the offense, as he stated during his interrogation with the LPD that he made the decision to test the camera within public toilet stalls because he felt a "certain thrill of the forbidden."  
**3.3.14.** As a result, the legal basis according to Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR for the specific processing is not applicable. No other legal basis according to Art. 6 para. 1 GDPR can be considered after an official review. The processing of the personal image data thus occurred unlawfully and in violation of the principles of data minimization and purpose limitation.  
**3.4. Regarding the lack of marking of the image processing system (Decision Point II)**  
**3.4.1.** The principle of transparency according to Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a GDPR is specified by Art. 12, 13, and 14 GDPR (see also Recitals 39 and 58 GDPR). Accordingly, it must be clear to affected individuals regarding an image processing system that their personal data is being processed, which specific data is being processed, and for what purposes it is being processed. The identity of the controller must also be communicated to the affected individuals so that they know by whom their data is being processed. Furthermore, affected individuals should be informed about risks, regulations, guarantees, and rights related to the processing, as well as about the assertion of these rights. This information must be precise, easily accessible, and understandable, and must be drafted in clear and simple language. The importance of the transparency of processing and thus the information obligation lies particularly in its function as a necessary prerequisite for the exercise of the rights of the affected individuals: If the affected individual is not aware that their data is being processed, and/or does not know who is processing this data...
conducts, he cannot assert his rights in this regard under Articles 15-21 GDPR” (cf. Hötzendorfer/Tschohl/Kastelitz in Knyrim, DatKomm Art 5 GDPR, para. 18f).  
3.4.2. Due to the amount of information that is to be provided to a data subject, a "layered approach" and a combination of means can be chosen by a controller to comply with the transparency requirement. In the context of video surveillance, the most important information should be presented in a warning notice, while the necessary additional information can be made available through other means (as a second layer) (cf. European Data Protection Board, Guidelines 3/2019 on the processing of personal data through video devices, 29.01.2020, pp. 28ff, available online at: https://edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-32019-processing-personal-data-through-video_de – hereinafter referred to as “Guidelines”). The data subjects must be able to recognize in a timely manner due to the labeling that they are entering an area monitored by an image processing system.  
3.4.3. In the specific case, the accused did not place any labeling at the crime scene regarding the image processing system. The accused did this deliberately, as he wanted to record the data subjects secretly. Thus, the accused violated the transparency requirement under Art. 5 para. 1 lit. a GDPR as well as his information obligation under Art. 12 and 13 GDPR by not informing the data subjects about the specific data processing when collecting their personal data in accordance with Art. 13 GDPR. Due to the lack of labeling or warning signs, the data subjects could not expect that they were in the recording area of the relevant system during a very intimate moment and would be recorded. Therefore, they could not assert their rights under Articles 15-21 GDPR against the accused.  
3.4.4. In light of the facts assumed to be proven, the accused, in his role as controller, has thus unlawfully processed personal data and violated his information obligation. Therefore, the objective element of both points of adjudication is fulfilled.  
3.5. Regarding the subjective element  
3.5.1. It should be noted regarding the subjective element that the accused consciously installed and operated the image processing system within a public restroom facility to fulfill his technical interests. The accused also consciously decided not to place any labeling at the crime scene regarding the image processing system or to inform the data subjects about the specific data processing, as he wanted to operate the system secretly (hidden by a cover). Throughout the entire proceedings, the accused did not present anything to the contrary but admitted to the act as well as the conscious decision to install the image processing system hidden in a restroom facility.  
3.5.2. Therefore, the DSB concludes that the accused acted intentionally in relation to both points of adjudication. Throughout the entire proceedings, there were no indications that the accused was not at fault for the violation of the relevant provisions. The accused's statement during his hearing at the DSB, that he used the recordings exclusively for personal purposes and not for harming or blackmailing a person or for economic interests, does not change this.  
3.5.3. Thus, there is culpability on the subjective element in the form of intent as defined in Art. 83 para. 2 lit. b GDPR.  
4. Regarding the determination of penalties, the following should be noted:  
4.1. According to Art. 83 para. 1 GDPR, the DSB must ensure that the imposition of fines for violations under paragraphs 5 and 6 is effective, proportionate, and deterrent in each individual case. Furthermore, para. 2 stipulates that when deciding on the imposition of a fine and its amount, the following must be duly considered in each individual case:  
a) the nature, gravity, and duration of the violation, taking into account the nature, scope, or purpose of the processing in question, as well as the number of data subjects affected by the processing and the extent of the damage suffered by them;  
b) the intentionality or negligence of the violation;  
c) any measures taken by the controller or processor to mitigate the damage suffered by the data subjects;  
d) the degree of responsibility of the controller or processor, taking into account the technical and organizational measures they have implemented in accordance with Articles 25 and 32;
e) any relevant previous violations by the responsible party or the processor;  
f) extent of cooperation with the supervisory authority to remedy the violation and mitigate its possible adverse effects;  
g) categories of personal data affected by the violation;  
h) manner in which the violation became known to the supervisory authority, in particular whether and to what extent the responsible party or the processor reported the violation;  
i) compliance with measures previously ordered against the responsible party or processor regarding the same subject matter under Article 58(2), if such measures were ordered;  
j) […]  
k) any other aggravating or mitigating circumstances in the specific case, such as financial benefits obtained directly or indirectly through the violation or avoided losses.  

4.2. The determination of penalties within a statutory penalty framework is a discretionary decision to be made according to the criteria established by the legislator in § 19 VStG (cf. VwGH 05.09.2013, 2013/09/0106).  

4.3. According to § 19(1) VStG, the basis for determining the penalty is the significance of the legally protected interest and the intensity of its impairment by the act. Furthermore, according to the purpose of the penalty, the relevant aggravating and mitigating factors, insofar as they do not already determine the penalty, must be weighed against each other. Special consideration must be given to the extent of culpability. Taking into account the nature of administrative criminal law, §§ 32 to 35 of the Criminal Code shall apply mutatis mutandis. The income and asset situation and any maintenance obligations of the accused shall be considered when determining fines; however, this only to the extent that the directly applicable provisions of the GDPR do not displace the provisions of the VStG and to the extent that is ordered by Article 83(8) GDPR and Recital 148 with regard to the procedural guarantees to be ensured.  

4.4. If a fine is imposed on a natural person, a substitute imprisonment must be set at the same time for the case of its uncollectibility according to § 16(1) VStG. The substitute imprisonment must not exceed the maximum of the imprisonment threatened for the administrative offense and, if no imprisonment is threatened and nothing else is specified, two weeks.  

4.5. Article 83(3) GDPR provides that, deviating from the accumulation principle established in § 22(2) VStG, in cases of identical or interconnected processing operations that intentionally or negligently violate multiple provisions of the GDPR, the total amount of the fine shall not exceed the amount for the most serious violation. Thus, within the scope of the GDPR – as applied in the present case – the absorption principle of Article 83(3) GDPR applies.  

4.6. The penalty framework in the specific case extends up to an amount of EUR 20,000,000 according to Article 83(5) GDPR.  

4.7. Regarding the present case, the following aggravating factors were considered in the determination of the penalty:  
- Nature, duration, and severity of the violation: The accused unlawfully interfered with the fundamental right to confidentiality of numerous affected individuals through the processing in question. The accused used the image processing system a total of three times (each for several hours distributed over the respective day of installation) within a period of two months. The intensity of the interference was considered particularly aggravating, as the accused made a highly significant intrusion into the fundamental rights of the affected individuals. The public restroom facilities at the crime scene were and are used by affected individuals to relieve themselves and/or change their clothing. The affected individuals could not have expected to be secretly recorded by an image processing system during a very intimate moment and that the recordings would subsequently be viewed by the accused. This severely violated their right to confidentiality under § 1(1) DSG as well as the respect for private and family life and the right to protection of personal data under Articles 7 and 8 EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (Article 83(2)(a) and (k) GDPR).  
- Categories of personal data affected by the violation: In this specific case, while there were no sensitive data per se in the sense of Article 9(1) GDPR, particularly intimate image data of the affected individuals were affected by the violation (Article 83(2)(g) GDPR).  
- The violation was committed intentionally by the accused. The fact that the accused knowingly and willfully made the decision multiple times to pursue his technical interest by installing the camera in a public restroom facility was considered particularly aggravating.
to comply, as it had a "certain allure of the unlawful" (Art. 83 para. 2 lit. b GDPR).  
4.8. In relation to the present case, the following mitigating factors were considered in the determination of the penalty:  
- There were no relevant prior convictions against the accused recorded with the DSB due to violations of the GDPR or the DSG.  
- Participation in the administrative penalty proceedings: The accused responded to the DSB's requests in the administrative penalty proceedings in a timely manner and complied with the summons for questioning via video conference. This demonstrated his cooperation in the DSB's investigative process and contributed to the establishment of the truth. This was taken into account as a mitigating factor.  
- The accused admitted to the DSB that he secretly recorded within the restroom facilities (confession) and stated multiple times that he would refrain from such processing in the future ("it was a one-time foolishness"). During his questioning on June 14, 2022, the accused appeared remorseful and gave the impression that he regretted his actions and wished to close this chapter. These circumstances were also considered as mitigating factors.  
4.9. The accused's assertion that there were no harmed individuals and that this should also be considered as a mitigating factor cannot be substantiated by the DSB. The interference by the accused with the fundamental right to confidentiality of the affected individuals was assessed by the DSB, as stated above, as serious, and the confidentiality interests of the affected individuals were severely violated by the accused. Furthermore, a lack of damage, according to the case law of the VwGH, is not considered a mitigating factor in the context of a disobedience offense as defined in § 5 para. 1 second sentence VStG (see VwGH March 31, 2000, 99/02/0352; December 16, 1998, 98/03/0222). From the assertion that the accused only viewed the recordings personally and did not use them to harm or blackmail a person or for economic interests, the DSB believes that no penalty reduction can be derived for the accused.  
4.10. Regarding the income of the accused, the DSB assumed a monthly net income of EUR 3,979 (12 times a year). The accused's view that the reserves he must set aside for the maintenance of the XXX should be deducted when determining his income, thus resulting in a monthly net income of EUR 2,018 (12 times a year) as the basis for calculating the fine, is not supported by the DSB.  
4.11. In this context, it should first be noted that according to the case law of the VwGH, the term "income" used in § 19 para. 2 VStG is not identical to the term "taxable income" under the Income Tax Act (EStG 1988) (see VwGH February 21, 1989, 88/05/0222). Liabilities such as leasing rates for a motor vehicle (see OLG Vienna June 28, 1988, 23 Bs 287/88) or loan rates for a condominium (see Wessely in Rauscher/Wessely, VStG2 § 19, Rz 21c with reference to OGH ZVR 1989/164) must therefore be disregarded. The same applies to the reserves cited by the accused for maintenance work concerning his rented XXX. For completeness, reference can also be made to the consistent case law of the VwGH regarding the imposition of fines, which states that fines can also be imposed on individuals who have no or only a low income. The fine is therefore permissible even if the financial and income circumstances of the punished individual make it likely that they will not be able to pay it (see VwGH January 30, 2014, 2013/03/0129). Furthermore, the existence of unfavorable income and asset conditions does not imply a right to impose the minimum penalty (see VwGH October 1, 2014, Ra 2014/09/0022 with reference to VwGH September 16, 2009, 2009/09/0150).  
4.12. In determining the penalty, considerations of special prevention and general prevention may also be included according to the consistent case law of the VwGH (see VwGH May 15, 1990, 89/02/0093, VwGH April 22, 1997, 96/04/0253, VwGH January 29, 1991, 89/04/0061). Supervisory authorities must also ensure, according to Art. 83 para. 1 GDPR, that fines are effective, proportionate, and deterrent in each individual case.  
4.13. One of the essential objectives of the GDPR, according to Art. 1 para. 2 GDPR, is the protection of the fundamental rights and freedoms of natural persons, and in particular their right to the protection of personal data under Art. 8 EU Charter of Fundamental Rights. The accused has severely violated the fundamental rights of the affected individuals, as stated above. The imposition of the specific fine is therefore necessary in any case in a general preventive sense to raise awareness among those responsible for operating image processing systems, particularly regarding the fact that such processing for the purpose of ensuring technical interests does not find justification under the legal basis of Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR.
4.14. The DSB assumes that the accused will refrain from making such recordings in the future. The accused has stated to the DSB on several occasions that he will refrain from such recordings in the future. Therefore, in the opinion of the DSB, there are no specific preventive reasons present.  
4.15. The specific fine imposed in the amount of EUR 25,000 appears, in light of the above-mentioned criteria for determining penalties, particularly the significant degree of wrongdoing involved, for general preventive reasons, and the gross culpability of the accused, as well as taking into account the income and asset situation of the accused and the available penalty range under Art. 83 para. 5 GDPR (here up to EUR 20,000,000), to be appropriate in relation to the act and the culpability.
