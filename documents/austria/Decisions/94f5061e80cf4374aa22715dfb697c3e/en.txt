The complaint is upheld, and it is determined that the respondent has violated the complainant's right to confidentiality by transmitting the judgment of the Regional Court for Criminal Matters *** in case * Hv **/20b dated August 5, 2020, as well as the appeal filed against it on September 22, 2020, to a third party via WhatsApp on October 4, 2020.  
Legal basis: Art. 4, Art. 51 para. 1, Art. 57 para. 1 lit. f, and Art. 77 para. 1 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation, hereinafter: GDPR), OJ No. L 119 of May 4, 2016, p. 1; §§ 1, 18 para. 1, and 24 para. 1 and para. 5 of the Data Protection Act (DSG), Federal Law Gazette I No. 165/1999 as amended.

In a statement dated December 13, 2021, the complainant, represented by counsel, partially reiterated her submissions and additionally stated that according to Art. 2 para. 2 lit. c GDPR, the processing of personal data by natural persons for the exercise of exclusively personal or family activities does not fall under the regulation. The respondent overlooks that the complaint is based on the constitutional provision of § 1 DSG and not on the GDPR. The DSG is not a direct implementation of the GDPR. Rather, it establishes the necessary implementing provisions and utilizes some of the opening clauses of the GDPR. The scope of protection of the fundamental right to data protection remains unrestricted. Thus, the limitation of Art. 2 para. 2 lit. c GDPR to merely economic activities of natural persons is not applicable to § 1 para. 1 DSG.  
The respondent claims that Barbara Z*** was unable to open the PDF files sent to her. For this reason, there would be no data processing. The transmission via WhatsApp is acknowledged by the respondent. The assertion that the PDF could not be opened is a mere protective claim; no evidence is provided for this. WhatsApp supports the transmission of PDF files. If Barbara Z*** can receive WhatsApp messages, she can also open PDF files. Every smartphone has the necessary technical applications installed or can easily download them for free from the internet if necessary.  
To substantiate the substantively incorrect protective claim, the assertion is made that the complainant should have resent the documents to Barbara Z***. However, it is not specified when and how this transmission was supposed to have taken place. The right to confidentiality does not only extend to personal data appearing in a file and is applicable regardless of the technical-organizational conditions of data processing. A transmission of the documents by post printed on paper or a personal handover would equally have violated the complainant's right to confidentiality.  
The transmission was not necessary to safeguard the interests of the accused in the private prosecution proceedings.

The respondent would never have thought that it could be unlawful to transmit the judgment. From his perspective, it was a public hearing. The transmission did not violate the legitimate confidentiality interests of the complainant. The justification for the non-violation of the legitimate confidentiality interests in the transmission of criminal law-related data is regulated in § 8 para. 4 DSG 2000. The use of data concerning judicially or administratively punishable acts or omissions violates, among other things...
then does not violate the legitimate confidentiality interests of the affected party when the legitimate interests outweigh the legitimate confidentiality interests. Moreover, there is no legitimate interest in the confidentiality of personal data when the data is generally available. This is certainly the case for data that appears in a public criminal trial. A limitation of the right to confidentiality is permissible according to § 1 para. 2 DSG if the use of personal data is justified by the overriding legitimate interests of a third party. § 4 para. 3 no. 2 DSG provides that the processing of personal data concerning criminal offenses is permissible if this is necessary to safeguard the legitimate interests of a third party in accordance with Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR and the manner in which the data processing is carried out ensures the protection of the interests of the affected person according to the GDPR and this federal law. This is precisely the case in the present instance. The legitimate interest of Ms. Z*** under the DSG in receiving the judgment and the transmission of the judgment arises from the threat made by the complainant that she would retrieve the cat herself, in connection with the fact that the complainant had already been convicted of two such offenses by the Regional Court for Criminal Matters ***.

The private prosecution according to § 111 StGB results in Ms. Z*** being required to provide evidence for her defense. She is therefore instructed to present the criminal judgment for her appropriate defense as a defendant in the private prosecution proceedings and has a legitimate interest.

Regarding the assertion that the PDF could not be opened, it should be noted that the constitutional provision of § 1 DSG includes a central protection against the transmission and disclosure of data. D. From a legal perspective, it follows: 
D.1. On the Right to Secrecy 
According to § 1 para. 1 DSG, everyone has the right to secrecy of their 
personal data, provided there is a legitimate interest in it. The existence of 
such an interest is excluded if data are not accessible to a claim for secrecy 
due to their general availability or because they cannot be traced back to the 
data subject. 
The GDPR and, in particular, the principles enshrined therein must be 
considered in the interpretation of the right to secrecy (cf. DSB 
October 31, 2018, GZ DSB-D123.076/0003-DSB/2018 with references). 
D.2. On the (Non-)Applicability of the GDPR and the so-called "Household 
Exception" 
According to Art. 2 para. 2 lit. c, the GDPR does not apply to the processing 
of personal data by natural persons for the exercise of exclusively personal or 
family activities (commonly referred to as the "household exception"). 
According to prevailing opinion, this exception – as correctly stated by the 
complainant – is to be interpreted restrictively (cf. in particular ECJ 
November 6, 2003, C-101/01, regarding the largely identical provision of Art. 3 para. 2).
second bullet point of Directive 95/46/EC). According to Recital 18 of the GDPR, this is intended to manifest a distinction from professional or economic activities. The central criterion for the applicability of the "household exception" – and thus the non-applicability of the GDPR – is the attribution of data processing to the private sphere (cf. Heißl in Knyrim [ed.], DatKomm Art. 2 GDPR Rz 70, with references). 

Similarly, the CJEU in its judgment of July 10, 2018, C-25/17, regarding the "household exception" under Directive 95/46/EC – referencing previous case law – stated that the terms "personal" and "family" refer to the activities of the person processing personal data, and not to the person whose data is being processed. Only activities that belong to the private or family life of individuals are covered. In this respect, an activity cannot be considered exclusively personal or family in the sense of this provision if it involves making personal data accessible to an unlimited number of people, or if it extends even partially into the public sphere and thus targets an area outside the private sphere of the person processing the data (Rz 41, 42). In the aforementioned judgment, the activities of a religious community, which consisted of conducting door-to-door preaching, were not considered to fall under the household exception. 

The respondent states in its submissions to the data protection authority that the transmission of data via WhatsApp was for the purpose of potential use by Barbara Z*** in legal proceedings. Barbara Z*** needed the relevant documents both for submission in the civil proceedings concerning the return of the cat and in the private prosecution proceedings under § 111 StGB against her. 

The central criterion for the applicability of the "household exception," namely the exclusive attribution to the private sphere, is therefore not present. Even a mixed use ("dual use"), meaning processing for both private and professional or economic purposes, would lead to the applicability of the GDPR in light of the wording of Art. 2 para. 2 lit. c. GDPR ("exclusively") (cf. again Heißl in Knyrim [ed.], DatKomm Art. 2 GDPR Rz 75, with references). Furthermore, there are no familial ties between the respondent and Barbara Z***. 

Following these statements, the exception provision is in any case not applicable in the present case, and the applicability of the GDPR is therefore given in this case. 

D.3. Regarding the material scope of application 

The judgment of the Regional Court for Criminal Matters *** in * Hv **/20b of August 5, 2020, against the complainant, as well as the related appeal of September 22, 2020, represent personal data of the complainant. Specifically, these are personal data concerning criminal convictions and offenses within the meaning of Art. 10 GDPR. The transmission via WhatsApp constitutes processing within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 2 GDPR, which opens the material scope of application. 

D.4. Restrictions on the right to confidentiality 

Restrictions on the claim to confidentiality are permissible according to § 1 para. 2 DSG when personal data is used in the vital interest of the data subject, the data subject has given consent (or in the terminology of the GDPR: permission), when there is a qualified legal basis for the use, or when the use is justified by overriding legitimate interests of a third party. 

According to Art. 10 GDPR, the processing of personal data concerning criminal convictions and offenses or related security measures may only be carried out under official supervision or when permitted by Union law or the law of the Member States, which provides appropriate safeguards for the rights and freedoms of the affected persons, based on Art. 6 para. 1 leg.cit. 

The Austrian legislator has made use of this deviation authority or opening clause and regulated the processing of criminally relevant data by private parties in § 4 para. 3 DSG (cf. Kastelitz/Hötzendorfer/Tschohl in Knyrim, DatKomm Art 10 GDPR, Rz 24). 

§ 4 para. 3 No. 2 DSG provides that the processing of personal data concerning judicially or administratively punishable acts or omissions, particularly concerning the suspicion of committing offenses, as well as concerning criminal convictions or preventive measures, is permissible in compliance with the provisions of the GDPR, if otherwise the permissibility of processing this data arises from legal due diligence obligations or the processing is necessary to safeguard the legitimate interests of the controller or a third party in accordance with Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR, and the manner in which the data processing is carried out ensures the protection of the interests of the affected person according to the GDPR and this federal law. 

Insofar as the respondent sees the legality in § 8 para. 4 DSG 2000, it is pointed out that this provision has expired as of May 24, 2018. The examination of the facts will be carried out in accordance with the applicable provisions mentioned above.
D.5. In the matter  
A vital interest of the complainant or her consent is evidently not present in this case. It must therefore be examined whether legitimate interests exist in the sense of the above statements.  
The respondent repeatedly states that the transmission in question occurred because Ms. Barbara Z*** needed the data to prove the actual conviction of the complainant in the private prosecution proceedings. It should be noted that the transmission in question took place on October 4, 2020, and the mentioned private prosecution, as established, was only filed on June 21, 2021. A justification in connection with the private prosecution cannot therefore be seen, as it was only initiated nearly a year after the transmission in question. Thus, the statements of both the respondent and the complainant regarding the private prosecution are irrelevant for this reason alone.  
Furthermore, the respondent states in his supplementary statement that Ms. Barbara Z*** needed the data in question for submission in the civil release proceedings concerning the cat, which was initiated by the complainant against Barbara Z***. However, as the respondent further states, the submission of documents in this context has not occurred. For this reason alone, it is not evident that legitimate interests on the part of Ms. Barbara Z*** exist in this context. Moreover, the complainant's statements should be followed in that the temporal component does not appear entirely accurate. As can be seen from the factual findings, the first-instance proceedings in this regard had already been concluded at the time of the transmission in question.  
In summary, there is no permissible restriction of the right to confidentiality in this case.  
Whether the unauthorized recipient actually viewed the personal data of the complainant can remain undecided, as the mere fact of the unlawful transmission into the electronic mailbox of a third party constitutes a violation of the fundamental right to confidentiality, which equally applies to a transmission via WhatsApp (cf. the ruling of the BVwG of September 3, 2019, GZ W214 2219944-1/9E, according to which neither § 1 para. 2 DSG nor Art. 4 no. 2 in conjunction with Art. 6 GDPR refers to a certain "minimum processing").  
If the respondent argues that there is no legitimate interest in confidentiality regarding the data due to the public court hearing and that these are not subject to a confidentiality claim, it must be countered that the general assumption of the non-existence of a violation of legitimate confidentiality interests for publicly published data is not compatible with European legal requirements (cf. the judgment of the CJEU of December 16, 2008, C-73/07 Satakunnan Markkinapörssi and Satamedia, as well as the ruling of the BVwG of July 29, 2020, GZ W211 2221963-1, and of February 25, 2021, GZ W274 2236016-1, as well as the decision of the Data Protection Authority of April 23, 2019, GZ D123.626/0006-DSB/2018).  
In this respect, the related argument also appears irrelevant.  
As a result, there is a violation of the complainant's right to confidentiality, and a decision was to be made accordingly.
