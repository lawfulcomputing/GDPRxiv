G R O U N D S  
A. Submissions of the Parties and Procedural History  
1. In an introductory submission dated April 4, 2022, improved by submissions dated May 6, 2022, and May 10, 2022, the complainants alleged a violation of the right to information and confidentiality. They essentially argued that the respondent refused to provide written information about the data processed by them and that this data contained false and criminally relevant allegations. Furthermore, the acquisition of additional products was unlawfully denied based on this information. The respondent had confirmed by phone the storage of these false statements.  
2. By partial decision dated June 15, 2022, the complaint of the first complainant regarding the alleged violation of the right to information was dismissed as unfounded.  
3. In a submission dated June 28, 2022, the respondent replied and stated that the second complainant had purchased a PC along with accessories from the respondent in 2016 and 2021. The invoices were issued intra-Community - that is, without Austrian VAT. A confirmation of arrival for the transport abroad must be submitted to the tax office. Upon collection, the customer must sign that the product will be taken out of the country and present a corresponding power of attorney or identify themselves as a managing director. The first complainant refused to allow a copy of their ID during collection and began writing complaint emails after a few days. At their insistence, the device was credited after return. Since the respondent assumed that the device had never left Austria, they informed the complainant that they would no longer issue intra-Community invoices. After the complainant threatened with a lawyer, they were informed that the respondent was no longer interested in a further business relationship. The second complainant is registered with the respondent, but as a legal entity, it is exempt from the GDPR.  
4. In a submission dated July 21, 2022, the complainants essentially repeated their previous submissions.  
5. Following a request for a supplementary statement, the respondent informed in a letter dated August 3, 2022, that no personal data of natural persons was stored in their IT system.  
6. Following a request for a supplementary statement, the respondent reiterated their previous submissions in a letter dated November 21, 2022, and also submitted a screenshot from their database (inventory management).  
7. In a communication dated January 12, 2023, the Data Protection Authority informed the complainants that, according to § 39 para. 2 AVG, it would only examine the alleged violation of the right to confidentiality in the present proceedings and would address the alleged violation of the right to information concerning the second complainant in a separate procedure.  
8. In a submission dated January 18, 2023, the complainants essentially repeated their previous submissions. They also requested that all data records of the complainants be deleted by the respondent. The refusal to supply materials constituted a legal violation, but they were no longer interested in a business relationship anyway. The creation of a copy of an ID is a legally controversial matter in the EU, and a database was specifically introduced to avoid such problems. According to the GDPR, professional, business, and company data also constitute personal data.  
B. Subject of the Complaint  
Based on the submissions of the complainants, the subject of the complaint is the question of whether the respondent violated the complainants' right to confidentiality by storing false information and criminal allegations about the complainants in their database (customer database/inventory management system).  
As already stated, the complaint, insofar as it concerned an alleged violation of the right to information, will be dealt with in a separate procedure.

D. From a legal perspective, it follows:  
D.1. Regarding the local jurisdiction of the DSB:  
It is noted at the outset that the complainants submitted the present complaint directly to the Austrian Data Protection Authority.  
According to Art. 3 para. 1 GDPR, the GDPR applies to the processing of personal data, insofar as this takes place in the context of the activities of an establishment of a controller or a processor in the Union, regardless of whether the processing takes place in the Union.  
The European Data Protection Board states in its "Guidelines 3/2018 on the territorial scope of the GDPR (Article 3)" (available on the website of the European Data Protection Board at https://edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-32018-territorial-scope-gdpr-article-3-version_de; pp. 5-6) that Art. 3 para. 1 GDPR ensures that the GDPR applies to processing by a controller or a processor that takes place in the context of the activities of an establishment of that controller or processor in the Union, regardless of the actual location of the processing.  
In this case, the respondent has several establishments in Austria and has not disputed that the processing was carried out within Austria. However, since the processing in question took place exclusively in the context of the activities of the respondent's establishments in Austria and did not have significant effects on data subjects in more than one member state, it cannot be qualified as cross-border processing within the meaning of Art. 4 para. 23 lit. a or b GDPR.  
D.2. Regarding the active legitimacy of the second complainant as a legal entity  
According to § 1 para. 1 DSG, everyone has the right to confidentiality of their personal data, provided that there is a legitimate interest in doing so. The existence of such an interest is excluded if data, due to their general availability or because of their lack of traceability to the data subject, are not accessible to a confidentiality claim.
According to § 4 para. 1 DSG, the provisions of the GDPR and the DSG apply to the wholly or partially automated processing of personal data of natural persons as well as to the non-automated processing of personal data of natural persons that are stored or are to be stored in a filing system.  
According to the will of the legislator, the statutory provisions of the DSG, including the complaint under § 24 DSG, are therefore limited to the protection of natural persons. However, the data protection authority has repeatedly stated that § 1 DSG also protects legal entities. An interpretation of the statutory provisions—especially §§ 4 and 24 DSG—that grants only natural persons the possibility of filing a complaint with the data protection authority, while excluding legal entities, would attribute an unconstitutional content of inequality to these provisions in light of § 1 DSG. It cannot be assumed that the legislator intended to treat legal entities significantly disadvantageously compared to natural persons in the pursuit of their constitutionally guaranteed rights without a comprehensible reason (see the decision of May 25, 2020, GZ: 2020-0.191.240, with references).  
That it is possible for the national legislator to provide for a purely national concept of the protection of personal data of legal entities has been affirmed by the CJEU (see in this sense the judgment of the CJEU of December 10, 2020, C-620/19, para. 47), so that the aforementioned case law of the data protection authority is not in contradiction. By including the protection of legal entities in the fundamental right to data protection under § 1 DSG, neither the level of protection of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights nor the primacy, unity, and effectiveness of Union law is affected (see in this sense the judgment of the CJEU of February 26, 2013, C-399/11, para. 60).  
Since, as already stated above, the matter at hand does not involve cross-border processing within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 23 lit. a or lit. b GDPR, the second complainant was therefore able to assert a violation of her right to confidentiality under § 1 para. 1 DSG with the DSB.  
Based on these considerations, it can be assumed that the requirements of Art. 3 para. 1 GDPR are met, thus the first complainant as a natural person as well as the second complainant as a legal entity are legitimized to exercise their right to complain under Art. 77 GDPR or § 24 para. 1 DSG with the Austrian data protection authority as the competent supervisory authority according to Art. 55 GDPR for filing a complaint.  
D.3. Regarding the limitation of the confidentiality claim:  
In this case, the complainants are thus actively legitimized to file a complaint according to § 1 DSG before the data protection authority as the competent supervisory authority.  

The GDPR and, in particular, the principles enshrined therein must be taken into account when interpreting the right to confidentiality (see the decision of the DSB of October 31, 2018, GZ DSB-D123.076/0003-DSB/2018).  
In this case, the scope of application of § 1 para. 1 DSG is opened, as the information according to Art. 4 No. 1 GDPR relates to the complainants.  
Restrictions on the claim to confidentiality are permissible according to § 1 para. 2 DSG if personal data is used in the vital interest of the data subject, if the data subject has given their consent (or in the terminology of the GDPR: approval), if there is a qualified legal basis for the use, or if the use is justified by overriding legitimate interests of a third party.  
As can be seen from the findings, the respondent has recorded in its database the note visible under C.5, which indicates that due to the behavior of the first complainant, no contracts will be concluded with the complainants in the future.  
As an expression of the general idea of private autonomy, the principle of freedom of contract applies in the law of obligations, which also includes the freedom to decide whether and with whom a contract is concluded (RIS - RS0013940). There are also no indications of a monopoly position of the respondent that would restrict this.  
Contrary to the assertions of the complainants, the note in the internal inventory management system of the respondent does not constitute criminal allegations, nor is there any inaccuracy to be recognized in light of the purpose of the entry, thus the processing of the data. If this is merely a documentation of opinions or assessments, the data is correct from a data protection perspective if this opinion or assessment is accurately represented (see the decision of the Data Protection Commission of March 21, 2007, K121.246/0008-DSK/2007).  
In this case, it is an internal note in the customer database/inventory management system of the respondent, through which the employees of the respondent can see that they will refrain from further contractual agreements with the complainants.  
In light of the private autonomy granted to the respondent, no unlawful data processing can be seen in this note; rather, it represents, in the opinion of the data protection authority, a legitimate interest according to § 1 para. 2 DSG and Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR of the respondent to document in its internal inventory management system that it intends to refrain from future contractual agreements with certain (legal) persons with whom there have been conflicts in previous business contacts.  
The complaint is thus ultimately found to be unfounded.
Finally, it is reiterated that the alleged violation of the right to information of the second complainant will be addressed in a separate proceeding.  
It was therefore to be decided accordingly.
