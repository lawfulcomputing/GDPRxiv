D. From a legal perspective, it follows that:
A data controller is, according to Art. 4 No. 7 GDPR, the natural or legal person, authority, institution, or other entity that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of processing personal data. In the present case, there is no doubt, and this has not been disputed by the data controller, that it is the data protection responsible party for the data processing carried out.

According to Art. 4 No. 1 GDPR, personal data refers to any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. To determine whether a natural person is identifiable, all means that may reasonably be used by the data controller or a third party to identify the person directly or indirectly should be considered ("objective" theory). This broad definition of personal data means that practically all information about a natural person is covered, as long as it can be attributed to a specific individual, at least in combination with other information.

According to Art. 4 No. 2 GDPR, the term processing encompasses any operation or set of operations performed on personal data, whether or not by automated means, such as collection, recording, organization, structuring, storage, adaptation or alteration, retrieval, consultation, use, [...]. In the present case, the processing does not occur in the vital interest of the affected service technicians; it is also undisputed— as stated in point 7 of the findings and presented in the statement of January 11, 2022, by the legally represented data controller— that there is no consent for data processing. The data controller based the processing of the personal data of the affected service technicians on Art. 6 para. 1 lit. c and lit. f GDPR.

Consequently, it must be examined whether the stated justifications are present.

Regarding the existence of a legitimate interest
According to the established case law of the CJEU, three cumulative conditions are required for invoking Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR. First, a legitimate interest must be pursued by the data controller or a third party; second, the processing of personal data must be necessary for the realization of the legitimate interest; third, the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the person whose data are to be protected must not outweigh those interests.

The balancing of the respective opposing rights requires an assessment on a case-by-case basis (see most recently the judgment of June 17, 2021, C-597/19). In the specific case, the interests of the data controller and the right of the affected service technicians to the protection of their personal data must be weighed against each other.

The data controller argues that, in addition to legal obligations, it has a legitimate interest in the use of the GPS system. It is of interest to them that records regarding work assignments, working hours, travel expense accounting, etc., are maintained correctly, as they have a responsibility towards their employees to ensure that they adhere to their working hours and are compensated accordingly. Additionally, there is an economic interest in deploying individual employees in an economically sensible manner and avoiding unnecessary downtime or detours. Regarding customers, the data controller has an economic interest in ensuring that the services provided are billed correctly, and quick responses to inquiries are important, especially for customer retention. Furthermore, the data controller has a legitimate interest in using the system to locate the service vehicles during working hours as a lessee.

In contrast, there is the interest of the respective affected employees not to be controlled or monitored by the use of the GPS tracker during their working hours.

In its judgment of December 11, 2019, C-708/18, the CJEU stated regarding the second requirement of Art. 7 lit. f of Directive 95/46 (now Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR), the necessity of processing personal data for the realization of the perceived legitimate interest, that exceptions and restrictions concerning the protection of personal data must be limited to what is absolutely necessary (see, for example, CJEU, May 4, 2017, C-13/16 or CJEU, November 9, 2010, C-92/09 and C-93/09). In other words, the question arises whether the same protective purpose can also be achieved by a milder means or whether the intended goal can be reached with less intrusive data processing.

If the data controller claims that knowledge of the current location allows for a response to a short-term need for deployment or a new order or emergency, it must be countered that the location data of the respective employee of the data controller is already known to the internal service coordinators, and therefore the allocation of a new order is possible without the need for a GPS device.

As credibly presented by the complainant in the complaint procedure with the reference number D124.3940 in his statements of November 10, 2021, and November 17, 2021, and as also...
From the official investigation conducted on January 14, 2022, it is evident that the service employees are responsible for maintenance and repair orders. These account for a high percentage (approximately 70%) of all orders. For these service tasks, almost always order-specific and person-specific spare parts are required. This is derived from the official investigation conducted on January 14, 2022, on the homepage of the responsible party. Logically, the respective service technician does not carry all the necessary spare parts, so they must first collect the order-related parts from another spare parts warehouse. Consequently, in many cases, spontaneous acceptance of orders in the conventional sense is not possible.  
Thus, this argument does not hold.  
The argument of the responsible party that the GPS system was installed to maintain a digital logbook and to prevent false working time records is not comprehensible. As the responsible party stated in their response on November 29, 2021, there is no concrete evidence that employees have ever actually reported false working hours.  
For the data protection authority, it is not clear to what extent the GPS system "L**Track" provides significant administrative relief for the respondent. The employees of the responsible party had to independently record their working hours and maintain the logbook. Logically, for the responsible party, even before the installation of the "L**Track" system, it must have been evident from the order distribution carried out by their internal coordinators to their service technicians where each service technician had worked or been present on the respective days. The responsible party also confirms this in their statement from March 4, 2020, stating that the data was known to them even without the GPS system due to the daily order distribution by the coordinators in the internal service. There is no indication of false working time records. This is evident from the statement of the responsible party dated November 7, 2021.  
Thus, this argument also does not hold.  
Furthermore, the billing to employees (travel expense accounting, calculation of benefits in kind, overtime payments, etc.) was also possible without the use of the GPS tracker in question, as the responsible party had managed the billing through the coordinators or the records maintained by the employees even before the use of the GPS tracker.  
The preceding statements indicate that the requirements for the proportionality of the data processing in question have not been met. It is established that the originally implemented measures (such as coordination of the fleet or the orders by the coordinators and the recording in a logbook, etc.) have proven sufficient to comply with legal obligations.  

Furthermore, it should be noted again that there are no indications of false working time records or theft.  
The data protection authority does not overlook that data processing through a GPS tracker represents a facilitation of work and an economic relief for the responsible party. However, this alone does not justify a reliance on Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR.  
Based on the aforementioned considerations, the data protection authority concludes that there is a lack of necessity for the data processing (second step of the assessment), which is why a reliance on Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR is excluded.  
Regarding the existence of a legal obligation  
If the responsible party further claims that it bases the use of the GPS system on the fulfillment of legal obligations, the following should be noted:  
According to Art. 6 para. 1 lit. c GDPR, processing is lawful if it is necessary for the fulfillment of a legal obligation.  
A legal obligation under Art. 6 para. 1 lit. c GDPR is to be understood as an obligation under objective law (cf. Frenzel in Paal/Pauly, General Data Protection Regulation Art. 6 para. 16), which may arise particularly from a member state or EU legal basis and directly relates to the data processing (cf. Kastelitz/Hötzendorfer/Tschohl in Knyrim (ed.), DataComm Art. 6 GDPR para. 39).  
As the CJEU has consistently held, in addition to the existence of at least one condition under Art. 6 GDPR, all requirements of Art. 5 para. 1 GDPR must also be demonstrated (Judgment of June 22, 2021, C-439/19, para. 96).  
The requirement of necessity ensures that the responsible party does not use a predetermined goal as an excuse to process excessive personal data. From the condition of necessity, it follows that the responsible party must limit itself to the necessary extent required by the legal obligation and may not process the data beyond the required purpose and scope. If the legal obligation allows the responsible party the option to refrain from processing and, in particular, from disclosing personal data, it corresponds to the principle of data minimization (Art. 5 para. 1 lit. c) to refrain from processing personal data (cf. Heberlein in Ehmann/Selmayr (ed.), General Data Protection Regulation Art. 6 GDPR and Buchner/Petri in Kühling/Buchner (ed.) Commentary on the General Data Protection Regulation Art. 6 GDPR).
The Responsible party primarily relies on the provisions of the AZG. Therefore, it must be examined whether the provisions of the AZG establish a legal obligation for the processing of personal data pursuant to Art. 6 para. 1 lit. c GDPR through a GPS system. 

According to § 17b AZG, the employer is required to keep records of all working hours performed by drivers and to retain all driver records for at least 24 months. These records must be made available to the labor inspectorate in a complete and orderly manner by driver and date. §§ 17 and 17a AZG refer in this context to the possibility of using or deploying a digital control device. According to § 13 para. 1 no. 5 AZG, a digital control device is understood to mean a digital tachograph within the meaning of Art. 2 para. 2 lit. h of Regulation (EU) No. 165/2014. According to this regulation, a digital tachograph is a tachograph in which a tachograph card is used in accordance with this regulation.

The data protection authority additionally notes - as also put forward by the Responsible party itself - that until July 2021, the function of the tachograph had not been activated. This means that until that time, according to § 17 para. 4 AZG, a logbook had to be maintained independently of the L**Track system, and there were no indications that incorrect or false records were kept.

In summary, the data processing based on Art. 6 para. 1 lit. c GDPR is already deemed unlawful because the purpose could also be achieved through less intrusive means that involve less data processing (Art. 5 para. 1 lit. c).

It was therefore decided accordingly.
