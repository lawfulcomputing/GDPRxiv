Thus, the Austrian legislator has balanced the right to protection of personal data on the one hand and the right to freedom of expression and freedom of information on the other.  
It is undisputed that the N*** broadcasting company is a media company within the meaning of the Media Act.  
Data processing for journalistic purposes is to be understood holistically and encompasses everything from "initial information" to initial publication and beyond. The CJEU understands "journalistic purposes" as activities aimed at "disseminating information, opinions, or ideas (...) in the public domain." (cf. CJEU, judgment of December 16, 2008, C-73/07, Satamedia, para. 61) The Advocate General in this case emphasized the role of a free press as a "public watchdog" to further define the term of journalistic purposes and derived the obligation to "convey information and ideas on all matters of public interest." Editorial processing is not relevant in this regard; even the provision of raw data can contribute to public discussion. The CJEU ultimately adopted this view.  
The processing of the data was carried out for journalistic purposes, as it served to disseminate information in the public domain.  
From the above, it follows that the DSG and the GDPR do not apply to the present case to the extent mentioned above, which is why there is also no legal claim for data deletion, particularly under Art 17 GDPR.  
I am available for any further questions."

D. From a legal perspective, it follows:  
D.1. Regarding the case-specific effect  
As can be seen from the course of the proceedings, the Federal Administrative Court has submitted an application for judicial review to the Constitutional Court based on the present proceedings, and § 9 para. 1 DSG was annulled by the Constitutional Court due to unconstitutionality, effective from June 30, 2024.  
A provision annulled by the Constitutional Court continues to apply to facts realized before its annulment (cf. Art. 140 para. 7 second sentence and Art. 139 para. 6 second sentence B-VG). The annulment of a legal provision by the Constitutional Court thus only has future effect (pro-futuro effect; VfSlg. 1415/1931). Previous facts are to be assessed according to the previous legal situation. Acts of enforcement based on a provision annulled by the Constitutional Court remain unaffected (VfSlg. 3303/1957). The same applies in the event that the Constitutional Court finds that the law or regulation was unconstitutional or unlawful (cf. VfSlg. 10.834/1986, 17.020/2003). The only exception to this principle that is materially justified (VfSlg. 3519/1959, 5141/1965) is the case-specific effect:  
The case is to be assessed as if the provision deemed unconstitutional had not belonged to the legal order at the time of the realization of the facts to be decided in the case (cf. VfSlg. 3674/1960, 7651/1975). The case is that legal matter which gave rise to the initiation of the norm control procedure (VfSlg. 8234/1978).  
Since the present proceedings can be qualified as a case, it is to be decided in substance as if § 9 para. 1 DSG had not belonged to the legal order at the time of the realization of the facts.  
Therefore, it must be examined—disregarding § 9 para. 1 DSG—whether the respondents violated the second complainant's right to deletion (see decision point I), whether the respondents violated the complainants' right to information (see decision points II - IX), and whether the first respondent violated the second complainant's right to confidentiality (see decision point X).  
D.2. Regarding the standing of the second complainant  
If the first respondent implicitly contests the lack of standing of the second complainant due to her status as a legal entity, the following counterarguments are made:
According to § 4 para. 1 DSG, the provisions of the GDPR and the DSG apply to the wholly or partially automated processing of personal data of natural persons as well as to the non-automated processing of personal data of natural persons that are stored or are to be stored in a filing system.  
According to the intent of the legislator, the statutory provisions of the DSG, including the complaint under § 24 DSG, are therefore limited to the protection of natural persons. However, the data protection authority has repeatedly stated that § 1 DSG also protects legal persons. An interpretation of the statutory provisions—particularly §§ 4 and 24 DSG—restricting the possibility of filing a complaint with the data protection authority to natural persons, while excluding legal persons, would attribute an unconstitutional content to these provisions in light of § 1 DSG, suggesting a violation of equality.  
It cannot be assumed that the legislator intended to treat legal persons in a grossly disadvantageous manner compared to natural persons in the pursuit of their constitutionally guaranteed rights without a justifiable reason (see the decision of May 25, 2020, GZ 2020-0.191.240, with references).  
The European Court of Justice has confirmed that it is possible for the national legislator to provide for a purely national concept of the protection of personal data of legal persons (Judgment of December 10, 2020, C-620/19, para. 47), so that the aforementioned case law of the data protection authority does not contradict this.  
As a result, this means that legal persons—within the scope of the rights granted to them by § 1 DSG—are entitled to file complaints, contrary to the view of the respondents. Accordingly, the second complainant also has a right to complain regarding an alleged violation of the right to erasure according to § 1 para. 3 no. 2 DSG in conjunction with Art. 17 GDPR, regarding an alleged violation of the right to information according to § 1 para. 3 no. 1 DSG in conjunction with Art. 15 GDPR, and regarding an alleged violation of the right to confidentiality according to § 1 para. 1 DSG.  
Regarding Item I  
D.3. General Information on the Right to Erasure  
In the absence of an implementing provision in the DSG, § 4 DSG must be considered for the interpretation of § 1 para. 3 no. 2 DSG, referring to Art. 17 GDPR.  
According to Art. 17 para. 1 GDPR, every data subject has the right to request the erasure of their personal data from a controller. To assert such a request-based right, an application to the controller is required. Although the GDPR does not specify a particular form or wording for this request, it must at least be clear to the controller that it is a request based on a specific right under the GDPR, as the receipt of the application triggers obligations for the controller.  
In assessing whether a request is recognizable to the controller as based on a specific right under the GDPR, the content of the request must be examined, applying the standard that also applies to unilateral private law declarations of intent.  
Thus, the wording and understanding of the declaration must be viewed from an objective perspective, namely as the recipient could understand it based on its wording and purpose upon objective consideration (cf. BVwG May 3, 2018, W256 2190554-1, regarding the request for information under § 26 DSG 2000 and concerning the interpretation with reference to the case law of the OGH, e.g., OGH September 15, 1999, 9 ObA 148/99a).  
D.3.1. In the Matter  
According to the findings, the second complainant requested erasure from the respondents in writing on September 2, 2021 (see Item C.6.).  
While these are undisputed requests for erasure and were recognized as such by the respondents, it must be assumed from an objective recipient perspective that the second complainant conducted a "change of request" after the respondents rejected the erasure.  
Although the second complainant expressed regret that their request for erasure was not fulfilled, they subsequently requested information from the respondents regarding which data and business secrets were processed for what purpose and to whom they were transmitted or are to be transmitted (see Item C.9.).  
Therefore, it cannot be assumed that the request for erasure remained in effect. Rather, these were replaced by requests for information (for the requests for information, see immediately under Item D.4.).  
This can also be justified by the fact that requests for information and requests for erasure have contradictory natures and thus cannot logically be pursued simultaneously.  
Since the request for erasure was no longer valid at the time of filing the complaint, a violation of the right to erasure is excluded.
The complaint of the second complainant against the respondent was therefore to be dismissed in this respect due to the lack of valid requests for deletion. Even in the event that – contrary to the view of the data protection authority – one would not assume a change of request, the complaint regarding an alleged violation of the right to deletion would have to be dismissed for the following reasons: The right to deletion according to Art. 17 para. 3 lit. a GDPR does not exist insofar as the processing is necessary for the exercise of the right to freedom of expression and information. This addresses a typical and fundamental conflict of fundamental rights in data protection, which occasionally expresses itself in the fear of censorship or unjustified control. In the balancing of interests, the fundamental rights of the affected person guaranteed by Art. 7 and 8 EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (EU-CFR) as well as Art. 8 European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) regarding respect for private and family life and the protection of personal data must be taken into account, alongside the fundamental right of the controller or third parties to freedom of expression or free information according to Art. 11 para. 1 EU-CFR and Art. 10 para. 1 ECHR (cf. Peuker in Sydow [ed.], European General Data Protection Regulation, Commentary, Art. 17, para. 59). 

As can be inferred from the findings (see points C.3.-C.5.), the "email messages" targeted by the deletion requests are the core element of the media reporting of the respondent. Deleting this message would therefore destroy the core content of the media reporting. Furthermore, in the present case, the provision under Art. 17 para. 3 lit. e GDPR is also relevant, which concerns processing that may be necessary for the assertion, exercise, or defense of legal claims. It should be noted that the "defense of legal claims" – not contradicting the wording – also means "defense against legal claims." This provision applies temporally at least when the assertion, exercise, or defense of (or against) legal claims is already taking place or is certainly imminent. The mere abstract possibility of legal disputes is not sufficient (cf. Herbst in Kühling/Buchner, GDPR Commentary, Art. 17, para. 83). 

According to § 32 Media Act, the criminal liability for a media content offense through the content of an accessible periodic electronic medium expires after three years, starting from the time when the dissemination of the content begins. The respondent does not refer generally to a potentially future procedure but names specific claims that could be asserted against them within a specific period. The proceedings before the data protection authority are also an indication that this data is still needed to clarify legal claims. 

Regarding points II to IX 
D.4. General information on the right to access 
According to Art. 15 para. 1 GDPR, the data subject has the right to request confirmation from the controller as to whether personal data concerning them is being processed and, if that is the case, the data subject has the right to access this personal data and to receive the information according to lit. a to h of the same article. The right to access under Art. 15 GDPR serves as a tool that enables a data subject to become aware of the processing by a controller and to verify the lawfulness of the processing (cf. Recital 63 first sentence GDPR). In other words, the right to access allows the data subject insight into the "whether and how" of the processing (cf. Paal in Paal/Pauly [ed.], General Data Protection Regulation. Commentary, Art. 15, para. 3). 

The second complainant bases their claim – as a legal entity – for access on § 1 para. 3 no. 1 Data Protection Act (DSG). This grants the data subject the right to know who processes what data about them, where it comes from, for what purpose it is used, and to whom it is transmitted. 

In the absence of an implementing provision in the DSG, Art. 15 GDPR must be interpreted with regard to § 4 DSG. Since a legal entity must not be placed in a worse position than a natural person, its right to access – taking into account the peculiarities of a legal entity – must not be less than that of a natural person.

It should be noted at the outset that the letters mentioned in points C.9. and C.10. are undoubtedly requests for access, which have indisputably reached the sphere of influence of the respondents – thus they have been received by them (cf. regarding the application of the "receipt theory," the decision of the Data Protection Authority dated February 22, 2019, GZ: DSB-D124.098/0002-DSB/2019, available in the RIS). 

Since it is undisputed in the present case that the respondents process personal data of the complainants within the meaning of Art. 4 no. 1 GDPR as controllers within the meaning of Art. 4 no. 7 of the same article, which also establishes a right to access according to Art. 15 GDPR or § 1 para. 3 DSG, they are fundamentally obliged to provide the complainants with information about the processed data concerning them.

At the core of their complaint regarding this matter, the complainants argue—assuming that no grounds for refusal of access exist—that they received incomplete or omitted information in connection with media reporting on September 1, 2021, pursuant to Art. 15 para. 1 and Art. 15 para. 3 GDPR or § 1 para. 3 no. 1 DSG. 

It is therefore necessary to first examine to what extent the grounds for refusal of access cited by the respondents are applicable: 

In this regard, the respondents consistently stated that the right to access is excluded by the editorial secrecy under Art. 15 para. 4 GDPR in conjunction with Art. 11 CFR and § 31 MediaG. 

In contrast, the complainants maintained that Art. 15 para. 4 GDPR only relates to Art. 15 para. 3 GDPR and not to Art. 15 para. 1 GDPR, and that the editorial secrecy under § 31 MediaG does not apply against the complainants. 

It should first be noted that the legislator of the GDPR—contrary to the latter view—has indeed allowed for a margin of discretion in the area of Art. 15 para. 1 GDPR for the balancing of interests: 

Thus, the right to access under Art. 15 GDPR is initially limited to one's own data, meaning data that, according to the wording of Art. 15 para. 1 GDPR, "concerns [i.e., the data subject] personal data." Therefore, there is fundamentally still no right to access personal data of third parties unless special reasons argue otherwise in the individual case (see, for example, the decision of the Data Protection Authority dated April 18, 2019, GZ: DSB-D122.913/0001-DSB/2019). 

Moreover, it is inherent in the GDPR that complaint procedures are case-by-case decisions in which potentially conflicting fundamental rights must be weighed against each other. It cannot be said that the legislator has already conducted a balancing of interests; rather, such a balancing must be carried out in the specific individual case (see the decision of the DSB dated June 1, 2022, GZ: D124.0442/22; 2022-0.277.100). A systematic interpretation of the GDPR reveals that the legislator has provided for deviations based on opening clauses and that the granted rights of the data subjects do not apply unconditionally due to the provision of Art. 1 para. 2 GDPR. 

Furthermore, the Data Protection Authority refers to the following case law of the BVwG dated November 9, 2021, GZ: W176 2244155-1/5E: 

According to Recital 63 of the GDPR, the exception under Art. 15 para. 4 GDPR is intended to protect trade secrets and intellectual property rights, particularly copyright in software. However, it must be assumed that all rights and freedoms recognized by Union law or the MS will generally be relevant. If the controller had to provide information about an email that also names other persons whose interests are to be rated higher, then these documents are not covered by the right of access (Haidinger in Knyrim, DatKomm Art. 15 GDPR [as of October 1, 2018, rdb.at] Rz 49). 

Art. 15 para. 4 DSVG stipulates that the right to receive a copy "in accordance with paragraph 3" must not impair the rights and freedoms of other persons. This explicit reference to the right to a copy in paragraph 3 seems at first glance to contradict the previously developed systematics, as it refers only to the right to a copy and not to the substantive right of access under Art. 15 para. 1. However, this contradiction resolves itself when one considers the right to a copy as a more specific provision regarding the manner in which information about the personal data of the data subject is to be provided under Art. 15 para. 1 GDPR. Then, the obligation to conduct a balancing of interests refers to the entire substantive provision of information (Jahnel, Commentary on the GDPR, Art. 15, Rz 42). 

The refusal to provide information contained under Art. 15 GDPR would therefore be justified if the confidentiality interests of the respondents or third parties outweigh the complainant's interest in access. 

However, the balancing of interests must be conducted separately for each type of data required under Art. 15 para. 1 lit. a to h GDPR to allow for a verifiable review by the Data Protection Authority or a court. A blanket exclusion of all information—as indicated by the respondents—is not provided for. 

Consequently, the information provided by the respondents must be examined in detail according to the aforementioned criteria.

Regarding Decision Point X  
D.5. Regarding the alleged violation of the right to confidentiality  
In this context, the second appellant seeks the establishment of an alleged violation of the right to confidentiality by the first respondent, by disclosing the receipt and content of the response to the deletion request submitted by the second appellant to the first respondent to the second respondent.  

D.5.1. General Information on the Right to Confidentiality  
According to § 1 para. 1 DSG, everyone has the right to confidentiality of personal data concerning them, as long as there is a legitimate interest in it. The existence of such an interest is excluded if data, due to their lack of traceability to the data subject, are not accessible to a confidentiality claim. The GDPR and, in particular, the principles enshrined therein are to be used for the interpretation of the right to confidentiality (cf. the decision of the DSB of October 31, 2018, GZ DSB-D123.076/0003-DSB/2018).  

Restrictions on the right to confidentiality are permissible according to § 1 para. 2 DSG when personal data are used in the vital interest of the data subject, the data subject has given their consent (or in the terminology of the GDPR: consent), when there is a qualified legal basis for the use, or when the use is justified by overriding legitimate interests of a third party.  

D.5.2. On the Legality of Processing  
In the present case, as also implicitly stated by the first respondent, a justification for data processing based on overriding legitimate interests in the sense of § 1 para. 2 DSG or Art. 6 para. 1 lit. f GDPR is conceivable.  
In this regard, an assessment of the legitimate interests of the second appellant must be made and these must be weighed against the legitimate interests of the first respondent (as well as possibly third parties).  
In the context of this balancing of interests, it must be considered that two cumulative conditions must be met for the first respondent to rely on this permissive provision: on the one hand, the processing must be necessary to safeguard the legitimate interests of the controller or a third party, and on the other hand, the fundamental rights and freedoms of the affected person (specifically: the second appellant), which require the protection of personal data, must not outweigh (cf. the judgment of the CJEU of November 24, 2011, C-468/10 and C-469/10 [ASNEF and FECEMD] para. 38; see also the judgment of the CJEU of May 4, 2017, C-13/16 [Rīgas satiksme] para. 28).  
In the context of the balancing of interests, it must also be considered whether a data subject could reasonably foresee at the time of the collection of personal data and in light of the circumstances under which it occurs that processing for this purpose might take place. The weighting must be done from an objective perspective and not from the subjective perspective of individual affected persons; thus, individual feelings should not be taken into account (cf. Kastelitz/Hötzendorfer/Tschohl, op. cit. para. 51).  

D.5.3. On the Substance  
In this context, the first respondent stated that the processing occurred due to the necessary coordination with the second respondent within the framework of the research community in its capacity as a media company.  
Media play a central role as a "public watchdog" in the public interest in a democratic society (cf. e.g. ECtHR 8.11.2016 [GC], case Magyar Helsinki Bizottság, App. 18.030/11; VfGH 4.3.2021, E 4037/2020), which is why coordination within a
Research community has a legitimate interest - as an expression of the freedom of expression

Art. 11 EU Charter of Fundamental Rights establishes two interrelated areas of protection: On one hand, the (active) freedom of expression of the speaker and on the other hand, the (passive) right to information of the recipient. The interaction of these two elements ensures an exchange of information and opinion in the sense of comprehensive communication freedom.  
It must therefore be examined whether the legitimate interests of the first respondent – namely the exchange of information between the respondents – outweigh the interest of the second complainant in protecting her personal data.  
It should first be noted that the second complainant requested both respondents on September 2, 2021, in identical requests (see point C.6.), to delete the message that formed the basis for the reporting of the respondents on September 1, 2021 (see points C.4. and C.5.). In light of the necessary reaction prompted by the requests, a uniform approach or decision regarding the deletion of the message underlying the reporting was warranted due to the identical requests and the existing research community.  
Furthermore, the second complainant could have anticipated such an approach, as it was well known that the respondents were operating a research community; this fact was even presented by them in their initiating submission of November 26, 2021 (see point A.1.).  
Additionally, it is not apparent to the data protection authority in what way there would be less intrusive means to achieve the intended purpose of exchanging information within the research community existing between the respondents, which is why this is the least intrusive means available.  
Therefore, the data protection authority concludes that, based on the conducted balancing of interests, there is no violation of the right to confidentiality, as the interests of the first respondent (freedom of expression) outweigh the interests of the first complainant (fundamental right to confidentiality).  
It was therefore decided accordingly.
