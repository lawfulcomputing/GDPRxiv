**D. From a legal perspective, it follows that:**  
**D.1. General**  
According to Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR or § 24 para. 1 DSG, every data subject has the right to lodge a complaint with the data protection authority if they believe that the processing of their personal data violates the GDPR or § 1 or Article 2 of the first main part of the DSG.  
According to Art. 55 para. 1 GDPR, each supervisory authority is responsible for fulfilling the tasks and exercising the powers assigned to it by the GDPR within the territory of its own Member State.  
The data protection authority is thus the supervisory authority responsible for the territory of the Republic of Austria (see § 18 para. 1 DSG).  
**Regarding Point 1:**  
**D.2. Distribution of Data Protection Roles**  
The determination of the distribution of data protection roles is crucial for the complaint procedure under § 24 DSG or Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR, as it determines who is responsible for compliance with the respective data protection provisions, how the data subject can exercise their rights, and ultimately against whom (i.e., which controller) the data protection complaint must be directed (respondent).  
According to Art. 4 no. 7 GDPR, the controller is the natural or legal person, authority, institution, or other body that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of processing personal data. The essential...
**Criterion: The Decision-Making Component.** The role of the controller thus primarily arises from the fact that a specific entity has decided to process personal data for its own purposes. The "purpose" describes an expected outcome, while the "means" determine how the expected outcome is to be achieved (cf. the EDSA Guidelines of September 2, 2020, 07/2020 on the concept of the controller and processor, para. 15 et seq.).

Although the DSG does not contain a legal definition of the term "controller," it is generally appropriate to refer to the definition in Art. 4 No. 7 of the GDPR – thus to the decision-making authority regarding purpose and means – by analogy, even outside the material scope of the GDPR (cf. for example, the ruling of the BVwG of September 30, 2020, GZ: W274 2225135-1). The former Art. 29 Data Protection Working Party also stated that processing by a natural person working for a company and using the data within the company's activities is attributed to the company as the responsible entity (cf. Art. 29 Data Protection Working Party, Opinion 1/2010 on the terms "controller" and "processor," WP 169, 00264/10/DE p. 21; see also the EDSA Guidelines 07/2020 of September 2, 2020).

**D.2.1. On the Data Protection Role of the Second and Third Respondents as well as the Fourth and Fifth Respondents**  
As stated by the Data Protection Authority in point 3 of the findings, the Second and Third Respondents as well as the Fourth and Fifth Respondents are all in an employment or labor law relationship with the First Respondent. 

An exceeding of their competencies as **** (Second Respondent), as Director of the Office for **** (Third Respondent), as *** Inspector (Fourth Respondent), or as Inspector of the Office for **** (Fifth Respondent) cannot be derived from the submissions of the complainants. Since the First Respondent takes such complaints or allegations "very seriously" (cf. the submission of the complainants dated April 18, 2023) and corresponding investigations are also provided for in the internal regulations (Staff Instruction No. 21/Rev.1 or its Annex 2, Paragraph 2.9, or Codes of Conduct as well as the Financial and Administrative Instruction 12 "Policy on Use of OSCE Computing Resources"), the respondents have been tasked with conducting inquiries.

Since the individual actions performed by the employees were carried out in the course of their professional activities for the First Respondent and are therefore also attributable to it, no exceeding of their competencies could be established, nor has it been claimed by the complainants. 

It is therefore to be assumed that there is no data protection responsibility of the Second and Third Respondents as well as the Fourth and Fifth Respondents, which is why the...
complaint regarding all raised complaint items should be dismissed, as they do not pertain to responsible parties within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 7 GDPR.  
To point 2:  
D.3. Competence of the Data Protection Authority concerning the first respondent  
D.3.1. Regarding the immunity of international organizations  
The first respondent, an international organization within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 26 GDPR, has its seat in Vienna. This undoubtedly establishes a fixed and permanent establishment within the territory of Austria. The definition of "responsible party" in Art. 4 No. 7 GDPR does not exclude international organizations. The criterion of having an establishment within the European Union for the territorial scope of the GDPR is also fulfilled in this case (cf. Art. 3 GDPR; see in detail Schmidl, The GDPR and International Organizations, in Jahnel [ed.] Yearbook Data Protection Law 21 [2022], pp. 20 to 22).  
Within the seat - and also in the present case, as stated in point 2 of the findings - automated data processing took place in accordance with Art. 4 No. 2 GDPR (evaluation of the data from the service mobile phone of the first complainant by the first respondent). Thus, the material scope of Art. 2 para. 1 GDPR is also opened; an exception under Art. 2 para. 2 to 4 is not apparent.  
Furthermore, the agreement between the Republic of Austria and the OSCE regarding the seat of the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (Federal Law Gazette III No. 84/2018) applies, which regulates the legal status and immunities of the OSCE.  
The Data Protection Authority does not overlook that international organizations – according to established case law of European supreme courts – do not enjoy complete immunity from actions of the authorities of the host state.  
However, a fundamental jurisdiction of the courts and authorities of the host state for actions within the seat area only comes into question if this is provided for by the respective seat agreement or is not explicitly denied.  
This leads to the interim conclusion that international organizations cannot completely evade the legal requirements of the host state. Generally, most seat agreements stipulate that the laws of the host state apply within the seat area. Only if the respective international organization issues its own regulations for the seat area – specifically for the protection of personal data – would the GDPR not apply (cf. Schmidl, loc. cit. pp. 27 ff).  
The Constitutional Court states in its ruling of September 29, 2022, regarding SV 1/2021, that according to the case law of the European Court of Human Rights, the widespread practice of granting contractual immunity to international organizations serves the legitimate aim of ensuring that
Ensuring the proper functioning of organizations free from unilateral interference by individual states. The significance of this practice should be viewed in light of the expansion and strengthening of international cooperation in all areas of modern societies (ECtHR February 18, 1999 [GC], Case Waite and Kennedy, Appl. 26.083/94 [Z 63]; February 18, 1999 [GC], Case Beer and Regan, Appl. 28.934/95 [Z 53]).

Whether the restriction of access to a court resulting from the exemption of an international organization from state jurisdiction is proportionate in the sense of Article 6(1) of the ECHR essentially depends, according to the European Court of Human Rights, on whether there is an adequate alternative legal remedy (fundamentally ECtHR, Case Waite and Kennedy, Z 68; Case Beer and Regan, Z 58; see also ECtHR January 6, 2015, Case Klausecker, Appl. 415/07, [Z 69 ff.]). It is not necessary for the alternative legal protection to correspond in every respect to a state court system; what is required is comparable, i.e., equivalent, not identical legal protection (see ECtHR September 9, 2008, Case Boivin, Appl. 73.250/01 [Z 2]). Slightly lower guarantees do not constitute a violation of Article 6(1) ECHR; however, a violation of Article 6(1) ECHR occurs when the alternative legal protection system of an international organization is manifestly inadequate (see ECtHR May 12, 2009, Case Gasparini, Appl. 10.750/03; June 16, 2009, Case Rambus, Appl. 40.382/04).

For international organizations, it is generally assumed that an adequate alternative legal remedy may exist in the possibility of invoking court-like internal organizational facilities (see, for example, regarding the procedure before the NATO Complaint Committee ECtHR May 11, 2000, Case A.L., Appl. 41.387/98; regarding the procedure before the Appeals Board of the European Space Agency ECtHR, Case Waite and Kennedy, Z 69; Case Beer and Regan, Z 59). As the European Court of Human Rights has established, the possibility of appealing to the Administrative Court of the International Labour Organization or the possibility of arbitration can also represent an adequate alternative dispute resolution mechanism (see ECtHR, Case Klausecker, Z 70 ff).

It is therefore necessary to examine the specific design of the headquarters agreement concluded between the Republic of Austria and the first respondent more closely: The headquarters agreement with the OSCE provides in Article V Section 5 lit. b that, in principle, the laws of Austria apply in the headquarters area, whereby the GDPR can be considered an integral part of the legal order of Austria as a directly applicable Union legal act. Furthermore, lit. c provides that, unless otherwise stipulated in the headquarters agreement, the actions and legal transactions carried out within the headquarters of the OSCE are subject to the jurisdiction of the courts and other competent authorities of the Republic of Austria in accordance with the applicable legal provisions.
According to Art. V Section 6 lit. a of the Headquarters Agreement, the OSCE is authorized to issue regulations applicable to its headquarters that create all necessary conditions for the full exercise of its roles and mandates in every respect. Laws or regulations of the Republic of Austria that are incompatible with any of the provisions issued by the OSCE under this section shall not be applicable to the OSCE headquarters to the extent of such incompatibility.

D.3.2. Regarding the Applicability of the GDPR in the Present Case  
Applied to the present proceedings, this means that it must first be examined whether the first respondent has issued its own internal regulations.  
It is acknowledged that the complainants, in their statement of September 11, 2023, denied the existence of their own regulations within the first respondent for the protection of personal data.  
However, the data protection authority was presented, as part of the request for administrative assistance dated August 29, 2023, with the data protection service instruction of the first respondent titled "OSCE Personal Data Protection Administrative Instruction No. 2/2022" and the Financial/Administrative Instruction 12 "OSCE Policy on Acceptable Use of OSCE Computing Resources" in the statement of October 5, 2023, from the BMEIA on behalf of the Austrian Permanent Representation to the OSCE.  
The official review of the submitted documents has revealed that the affected person is granted a right to complain due to data protection violations within the first respondent - at the competent data protection office - and that the possibility of legal remedy protection is also provided.  
Since the first respondent has relevant internal regulations, the possibility of a complaint to the data protection authority must already be denied at this stage, as the GDPR and § 1 DSG do not materially apply, and the jurisdiction of the data protection authority is derived from the GDPR and the DSG (see Art. V Section 6 lit. a of the Headquarters Agreement).  
Furthermore, it is noted that the complainants stated in their initiating complaint of April 18, 2023, under point 2.5, that "according to the wording of the agreement, there is only an exemption from jurisdiction; however, there is no exemption from administrative procedures, such as that complaint procedure before the data protection authority, according to the clear wording of the agreement."  
However, this legal opinion (of the complainants) is contradicted by the fact that the explanations to the Headquarters Agreement indicate that this also includes immunity from the activities of administrative authorities (see 12 dB XXVI. GP S 2).  

The same applies - as indicated in Article XV Section 28 lit. a of the Headquarters Agreement - to the second and third respondents as well as to the fourth and fifth respondents, who are indisputably - and also as stated by the complainants - employees of the first respondent.  
The data protection authority does not overlook that the internal regulations of the OSCE regarding the protection of personal data are not comparable in scope and particularly in terms of legal protection possibilities to the GDPR.  
Whether these regulations are unconstitutional for this reason cannot be examined by the data protection authority, as this is exclusively the matter of the Constitutional Court according to Art. 140a B-VG (see again the decision of September 29, 2022, SV 1/2021).  
D.3.3. Summary  
In summary, it can be stated that the GDPR, both spatially (Art. 3) and materially (Art. 2), fundamentally applies to the present case; however, the first respondent enjoys immunity from actions of the data protection authority due to the Headquarters Agreement and the fact that it has issued its own regulations for the protection of personal data. There are no indications that the Headquarters Agreement would be manifestly contrary to EU law and thus should not apply.  
By virtue of this international legal regulation, the first respondent named by the complainants cannot be held accountable by the data protection authority (see again essentially Schmidl aaO S 19 ff), unless the first respondent were to waive its granted immunity. Such a waiver is not evident from the previous course of proceedings. Furthermore, it is not evident in this case that the employees of the first respondent acted outside their area of responsibility.  
Consequently, the complaint regarding the first respondent was to be dismissed.  
For the sake of completeness, it is finally noted that the first complainant may be affected in his constitutionally guaranteed rights by the first respondent's own internal data protection regulations.  
However, the data protection authority is not competent to examine such a matter.
