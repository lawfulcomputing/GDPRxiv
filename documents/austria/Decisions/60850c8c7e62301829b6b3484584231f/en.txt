1. The applicant is an Austrian municipality.  
For the purpose of creating a geodata infrastructure as well as for local spatial planning and management of traffic areas, the capture of the entire municipal area of the applicant was announced on August 22, 2023 [date of announcement] in accordance with procurement regulations "using a vehicle-mounted, imaging and LiDAR data capturing Mobile Mapping System with associated data processing (anonymization of persons and vehicle license plates, georeferencing, quality control) and visualization in a web-based Mobile Mapping Viewer" [short designation: "Mobile Mapping Survey of the N*** City Area 2023"].  
2. In the context of the announcement from August 22, 2023, it was noted, among other things, as follows [excerpt, as far as relevant to the proceedings]:  

“[…] All objects in the visible street space should be captured in three dimensions and visualizable, digitizable, and measurable in a suitable browser-based viewer under the condition of highly accurate georeferencing. The image and laser data should be stored cloud-based on a server secured against unauthorized access within the territory of the European Union.  
To ensure data protection regarding the image information, persons and vehicle license plates must be rendered unrecognizable. […]”  
3. On September 4, 2023, DataViewing*** Deutschland AG [hereinafter: DataViewing***] submitted an offer regarding the tender mentioned in point B.2.  
DataViewing*** is a company based in Germany [Commercial Register of the District Court P*** No. HRB *4*1], which specializes in capturing 360° panoramic images [so-called "Roundview***s"] of public roads. These are captured using specially equipped vehicles utilizing public geometry and land registry data. There are no video or real-time recordings.

4. DataViewing*** independently determines (possibly with affiliated companies) when [“Survey Calendar”], from which specific areas, and at what granularity recordings will be made, as well as which specific technical means (camera systems, etc.) will be used for this purpose.  
The panoramic images [“Roundview***s”] created by DataViewing*** are made available to third parties, public and private institutions [“licensees”] through (license) contract conclusion. No exclusive or separate recordings are made at the request of licensees. However, areas for which there are already interested parties are prioritized for capture.  
The images are not publicly accessible, and licensees only receive access to pixelated representations (see point B.3.), which in turn only encompass limited areas of the image database of DataViewing*** for their own purposes. Images that are not or not sufficiently pixelated (e.g., due to algorithm errors) can be reported to DataViewing*** by licensees or affected persons, for example, via the so-called “Web Viewer,” which then checks the report and may take measures for pixelation.
All licensees in a specific area also receive the identical image dataset.

C. Legally, it follows: 
C.1. General and applicable legal provisions 
Personal data within the meaning of the GDPR are all information relating to an identified or 
identifiable natural person (Art. 4 No. 1 GDPR). 
According to Recital 26 of the GDPR, all means that are likely to be used, according to common 
sense, should be considered when assessing identifiability. In particular, objective factors such as the costs of identification and the time required for it. 
The image of a person recorded (captured) by a camera falls under the term of 
personal data, provided it allows for the identification of the affected person (cf. CJEU 
December 11, 2014, C-212/13, para. 22). Moreover, image processing (via cameras) regularly constitutes 
automated processing within the meaning of the GDPR. The legal definition under Art. 4 No. 2 does not require 
"minimum processing," such as continuous recording or storage (cf. BVwG decision of September 3, 2019, GZ: W214 2219944-1).  
If individuals entering the monitored area can be identified based on their appearance or other 
specific 
characteristics, 
this 
constitutes 
the 
processing 
of 
personal data. The potential risk of misuse of this data increases with the 
size of the monitored area and the number of individuals frequenting the area (cf. 
EDPB Guidelines 3/2019 on the processing of personal data by video devices, para. 7).  
However, image data do not per se constitute a special category of personal data (cf. the 
decision of the DSB of June 7, 2018, GZ: DSB-D202.207/0001-DSB/2018). 
The personal reference of vehicle license plates arises from the possibility of providing information as 
regulated in § 47 para. 2a KFG 1967, which allows for the identification of a registration owner with 
proportional means (cf. e.g., the decision of the DSB of August 27, 2019, GZ: DSB-
D124.104/0005-DSB/2019). 
The controller within the meaning of the GDPR is the natural or legal person, authority, institution, or 
other entity that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of processing personal data (Art. 4 No. 7 GDPR).  
The determination and specification of the data protection roles is not only a necessary starting point in 
the complaint procedure according to Art. 77 para. 1 GDPR, but in any conceivable procedure before 
the Data Protection Authority for a further substantive examination. 
Only in this way can it be assessed who is actually responsible for compliance with the obligations set out in the GDPR (Art. 24 et seq.; in this case: Art. 36 GDPR) or to whom any potential violations 
and infringements would be attributable.
The role of the controller primarily arises from the fact that a specific entity has actually decided to process personal data for its purposes. The "purpose" describes an expected outcome, while the "means" determine how the expected outcome is to be achieved (cf. Art. 29 Data Protection Working Party Opinion 1/2010 on the terms "controller" and "processor," WP 169). 

According to Art. 36 para. 1 GDPR, the controller consults the supervisory authority prior to processing if a data protection impact assessment pursuant to Art. 35 of the same regulation indicates that the processing would result in a high risk, provided that the controller does not take measures to mitigate the risk. If the supervisory authority believes that the planned processing would not be in compliance with this regulation, it submits corresponding written recommendations to the controller and may exercise its powers as stated in Art. 58 GDPR (para. 2 of the same regulation).

The controller provides the supervisory authority with the information specified in Art. 36 para. 3 lit. a to lit. f GDPR – particularly the data protection impact assessment. Even though this is not unequivocally evident from the German version – unlike other language versions (for example, English uses "shall") – it must be assumed, based on a systematic consideration of the GDPR (regulation of the controller's obligations in Chapter 4) as well as the penalization of a violation (cf. Art. 84 para. 4 lit. a GDPR), that the prior consultation, when the conditions are met, constitutes an obligation of the controller – not merely an option.

Art. 35 para. 1 GDPR, which is abstractly (regulation-systematically) and concretely (temporally) prior to the consultation procedure, stipulates that a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) is required when it can be assumed, based on certain circumstances (nature, scope, purposes), that the data processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

From Art. 35 para. 1 and para. 3 GDPR, it can also be inferred that the EU legislator assumes a high risk particularly in the case of the use of new technologies and (automated) data processing that involves systematic and comprehensive evaluation of personal aspects of natural persons (including profiling), extensive processing of data as defined in Art. 9 and Art. 10 GDPR, or systematic extensive monitoring of publicly accessible areas.

Recital 75 of the GDPR elaborates on the concept of risk as follows (emphasis by the DSB): "The risks [...] may arise from processing personal data that could lead to physical, material, or immaterial harm, particularly when the processing leads to discrimination, identity theft or fraud, financial loss, reputational damage, loss of confidentiality [...], unauthorized access."
**Revocation of Pseudonymization or Other Significant Economic or Social Disadvantages**

This may occur if the affected individuals are deprived of or hindered in their rights and freedoms concerning the control of their personal data, particularly when personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, […], is assessed, especially when aspects related to work performance, economic situation, health, personal preferences or interests, reliability or behavior, location or relocation are analyzed or predicted to create or utilize personal profiles, […] or when the processing involves a large volume of personal data and a significant number of affected individuals. 

**Recital 84 of the GDPR** states regarding risk assessment (emphasis by the DSB):  
“[…] If a data protection impact assessment indicates that processing operations pose a high risk that the controller cannot mitigate through appropriate measures regarding available technology and implementation costs, the supervisory authority should be consulted before processing.” 

**Recital 94 of the GDPR** addresses the consultation of the supervisory authority as follows (emphasis by the DSB):  
“If a data protection impact assessment indicates that processing would pose a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons in the absence of guarantees, security measures, and mechanisms to mitigate the risk, and the controller believes that the risk cannot be mitigated through reasonable means regarding available technologies and implementation costs, the supervisory authority should be consulted before the processing activities begin. Such a high risk is likely associated with certain types of processing and the scope and frequency of processing, which may also result in harm or impairment of the personal rights and freedoms of natural persons. The supervisory authority should respond to the consultation request within a specified period. However, it may intervene according to its tasks and powers set out in this regulation, even if it has not responded within this period, which includes the authority to prohibit processing operations. As part of this consultation process, the outcome of a data protection impact assessment conducted concerning the processing of personal data may be submitted to the supervisory authority; this particularly applies to the measures planned to mitigate the risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.” 

**As Sources of Risk (in accordance with Art. 36 GDPR)**, according to the case law of the DSB, in addition to purely “technical” risks, all risks of data processing that may have negative impacts on an affected person are considered. To mitigate the risk, the GDPR exemplarily mentions three different types of remedial measures, namely guarantees, security measures, and procedures that ensure the protection of personal data and demonstrate compliance with the GDPR. Therefore, technical, organizational, and legal (especially contractual) measures that are intended to provide remedy are crucial. In assessing the remaining residual risk, all planned measures to ensure GDPR-compliant processing must be included (cf. the decision of the DSB dated February 2, 2021, GZ: 2021-0.024.862, among others, referring to Trieb in Knyrim, DatKomm Art. 35 GDPR, para. 116).
**C.2. Consultation on the Subject Matter**

**C.2.1. Subject Matter and Preliminary Remarks**  
The subject of the present consultation procedure is, according to the applicant's submissions, among other things, the collection or capturing, querying, and (re)use of information in connection with the creation of panoramic images of the N*** road network, whereby natural persons and vehicle registration plates are also captured in this context.  
It is initially pointed out that the applicant has failed, despite multiple reminders from the data protection authority – at least within the framework of the submitted copy of the Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) – to make a clear delineation of data protection responsibility concerning the potential risks. This is evident, for example, in that they have assigned the risk factor "High" to the questions of responsibility, the number of affected individuals, and the scope of processing, while largely quoting from the draft license agreement or the terms and conditions of DataViewing*** without further explanation.  
In a first step, it was therefore essential for an actual risk assessment to clarify the data protection roles – per processing step.

**C.2.2. Individual Processing Steps and Related Responsibilities**  
**Collection and Capturing**  
As can be inferred from the factual findings (Points B.3. and B.4.), individuals and vehicle registration plates are first captured in the course of creating panoramic images using vehicles and a special driving calendar. At this time, individuals and registration plates are at least recognizable (identifiable), which means that it concerns the processing of personal data within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 1 and No. 2 GDPR (see comments on image data under Point C.1.).  
In this process, large parts of the public road network within the municipality of the applicant are captured. In this context, the applicant can be supported in that such a high scope of processing with potentially many affected individuals (residents according to the DPIA: nearly 300,000 persons) could indeed present a high risk.  
In contrast, the applicant overlooks that DataViewing*** independently decides on both "means" (timing, areas, granularity of recordings, technical instruments) and "purposes" (conclusion of economically lucrative licensing agreements in connection with the created panoramic images).  
This is evident not least from the DPIA itself as well as from the other submitted documents (draft license agreement, terms and conditions, annex, and guidelines) of DataViewing***.  
In a first intermediate step, it can therefore be noted that the circumstances assessed by the applicant in the DPIA as "high-risk" (number of affected individuals, scope of data processing, responsibility) and the associated processing operations cannot be attributed to the applicant as the responsible party within the meaning of Art. 4 No. 7 GDPR.  
From this, it is clear that the considerations regarding "Google Street View" cannot be transferred to the present procedure. If at all comparable, this would primarily apply to the processing activity by DataViewing***.  
Furthermore, the mentioned driving was already conducted in October (see Point B.8.) and the processing operation would not have been subject to prior consultation solely due to this fact.

**Querying and (Re)Using**  
After the respective road network has been captured (independently of the order) and panoramic images ("Roundview***s") have been collected or created, the individual recordings are processed by the...
DataViewing*** examined and further processed and only in processed form passed on to licensees (specifically: to the applicant). As can be inferred from the findings, this processing particularly involves the (technical) alteration of image sharpness, so that faces and vehicle license plates are no longer recognizable to an average viewer (“pixelation”). Although this approach significantly reduces the possibility of identifying natural persons and thus has a data-minimizing effect (cf. Art. 5 para. 1 lit. c GDPR), it appears that the (full) personal reference for the processing purposes of the applicant (creation of a geodata infrastructure and local spatial planning) is not necessary for potential licensees, which may also represent a suitable technical and organizational measure in the sense of Art. 25 and 32 GDPR. 

At the same time, however, due to the lack of standard “full-body pixelation” with the inclusion of further characteristics - particularly the local location as well as temporal aspects - the data protection authority believes that the identifiability and consequently the personal reference is not completely eliminated (in the sense of “anonymization” according to Recital 26 GDPR). This must also be viewed against the background that, based on general life experience and according to the applicant's own statements in the context of their submissions and the DPIA (again referring to the terms and conditions of DataViewing***), one can expect insufficient “pixelation” due to technical deficiencies, meaning that personal data is present in these cases as well. 

By deciding to query or further use the image material processed by DataViewing*** via the web viewer (means) for the creation of a geodata infrastructure, local spatial planning, etc. (purposes), the applicant establishes an independent responsibility in the sense of Art. 4 No. 7 GDPR. 

C.2.3. Risk of data processing by the applicant 
The DPIA underlying the findings (Point B.9.) indicates that the applicant has assigned the risk factor “high” exclusively to those data processing operations which - as explained in Point C.2.2. - do not fall under the data protection responsibility of the applicant. This applies both to the (high) number of affected persons and to the scope of data processing. 

To what extent the question of who is responsible for data processing in the sense of Article 4 No. 7 GDPR is supposed to represent a high risk is neither evident from the reasoning in the context of the DPIA - which is essentially limited to the mere reproduction of the data protection information (including annex) of DataViewing*** - nor has this been further specified in the context of the present procedure. 

As explained in Point C.1., (risk) remedial measures - of a technical, organizational, and legal nature - must also be included in the assessment of the remaining residual risk. Point 4 of the DPIA (“Measures for risk limitation”) indicates that this has been classified as “low” by the applicant in all respects. 

A review beyond the risk determined by the applicant in the context of the DPIA is, however, in no way the task of the data protection authority in the context of a consultation procedure and must be firmly opposed by the applicant based on the cited case law and literature, insofar as it suggests an “excessive use” of the consultation mechanism for the purpose of “practical legal certainty.” 

Furthermore, the consultation procedure is not suitable for answering general questions regarding the permissibility of processing activities and even less for assessing the appropriateness of any additional offers (or the associated costs). Moreover, no “final legal certainty” can be granted by the data protection authority through a (permissible) consultation procedure in any case. 

Overall, the data protection authority therefore concludes that the conditions for prior consultation according to Art. 36 GDPR are not met due to the lack of (demonstrated) high risk. 

It was therefore decided accordingly.
