**Hearing of Antonello Soro, President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, on the use of data mining methodologies for conducting medical check-ups on public sector workers before the 11th Permanent Commission (Labor, Social Security) of the Senate of the Republic (September 18, 2018)**  
*(text of the intervention)*

The introduction of a targeted scheduling system for medical checks by INPS, aimed at preemptively identifying possible unjustified absences from work due to illness, undoubtedly constitutes a goal of general interest. By directing controls towards cases most likely to reveal abuses, this procedure would indeed promote the efficiency of the social security system and also lead to significant savings in resources.

The pursuit of this objective through a predictive statistical model, based on the analysis of the probability of fraudulent behavior by workers or of illness events that could be resolved earlier than expected, offers the possibility of concentrating controls on cases deemed most at risk of abuse. The system adopted by INPS, in particular, is based on automated decision-making processes consisting of profiling workers, which assigns a score of greater or lesser reliability to medical certificates based on statistical judgments of the prognosis regarding fitness to return to work or unjustified absence.

The use of such techniques, even for entirely legitimate purposes such as – in this case – the prevention of fraudulent behavior, is permitted by the regulations on personal data protection under specific circumstances, provided that it adheres to criteria of fairness and transparency and observes sufficient safeguards, especially when it concerns health data. Such safeguards should, first and foremost, allow the individuals concerned to be informed of the existence of an automated processing system that may affect them, so that they can verify and, if necessary, contest the results that arise from it.

Secondly, measures should be adopted to reduce the margins of error inherent in these statistical processing procedures – characterized by the automatic application of predetermined inference rules – and, therefore, the risks of erroneous prognostic judgments, as well as of abuses, unjustified discrimination, and, above all, violations of the rights of the individuals concerned.

More generally, automated decision-making processes, including profiling, are being used in an increasing number of sectors, both in the private sector (based, among other assumptions, on the explicit consent of the individual concerned) and in the public sector. Banking and finance, healthcare, taxation, insurance, marketing, and advertising are just some of the fields where profiling is carried out more regularly to support decision-making processes.

The Office of the Authority intervened on the methods of scheduling control visits adopted by INPS in February of this year, after learning from some press reports about the development of a data mining system, called SAVIO, operating not only without the knowledge of the workers concerned but also in the absence of specific precautions and guarantees aimed at preventing, for example, inaccuracies in the data collected or inconsistencies in the logic of the algorithms used, which could lead to erroneous decisions with negative impacts on individuals.

During the investigation, we learned that INPS had been using a targeted scheduling system for medical checks based on automated processing of personal data for over five years, which, following the establishment of the Unified Pole of Medical Control, had also been extended to public employees. The Institute had indeed failed to notify the Authority – as was required at the time – of the adoption of such a predictive data mining mechanism based on profiling. As clarified by the Institute, it did not consider that the SAVIO system constituted profiling aimed at identifying workers for whom the condition or prognosis of illness did not fully correspond to the actual condition.

The Authority, therefore – in addition to offering its full willingness to collaborate in creating a scheduling system for medical checks that is both efficient and respectful of the right to personal data protection – noted the omission committed by the Institute and, above all, the absence of the necessary safeguards. This led to the decision by the Institute to suspend profiling activities for the purpose of scheduling medical checks.

During the hearing, the President of INPS emphasized that among the variables considered in the SAVIO model, there is no diagnosis, that is, the pathology from which the worker suffers, as reported in the medical certificate. The predictive software would, in fact, only take into account the frequency and duration of individual episodes of the worker's illness, along with other variables such as the number of previous fitness assessments for medical checks, the worker's qualification, the type of employment relationship, salary, sector, and company size.

However, the very fact of absence from work due to illness constitutes, for that reason alone, – as per the established orientation of the Authority, confirmed also by the jurisprudence of legitimacy – health data and, as such, deserving of the enhanced protection that the European legal framework and domestic regulations grant to this category of sensitive data. All the more so if alongside such information, like that considered by the predictive software developed by INPS, there are data related to the frequency and duration of individual episodes of the worker's illness and the worker's previous history.
**Analysis of Statistical Profiling and Data Protection Regulations**

The analysis of statistical profiling, particularly based on medical certificates produced in the last two years, as well as the outcomes of previous check-up visits, leads to the conclusion that the statistical model in question, contrary to what has been believed, indeed constitutes a true profiling of the workers involved.

1. **Definition of Profiling**  
   According to the indications contained in the recommendation of the Council of Europe regarding profiling and the provisions of the new European regulation on data protection, this is a technique that, by correlating a certain number of individual pieces of information, allows for the categorization of the individual into a predetermined category, the "profile," constructed based on certain variables or behavioral characteristics that are common among the subjects included in the reference category.

2. **Effects of Profiling**  
   The inclusion of the individual within a specific profile has, however, not only a descriptive effect but also a predictive and ascriptive effect, as it leads to the inference, based on statistical reasoning (which is also subject to error), of the propensity to engage in specific future behaviors. In this case, the profiling of workers results in the attribution to each of them a certain degree of propensity for unjustified absenteeism due to illness.

3. **Nature of Statistical Analysis**  
   It is important to clarify that the statistical analysis conducted in this manner on medical certificates and other variables related to the worker does not consist of a mere selection of individuals based on their actual characteristics: this activity, from a technical standpoint, does not require data mining. Instead, such analyses result in an actual profiling, as they create a behavioral prognosis based on statistical inferences, which, being founded on probabilistic calculations, is always subject to a margin of error and therefore requires adequate safeguards to avoid false attributions and erroneous evaluations of individual behaviors.

4. **Regulatory Framework**  
   For these reasons, the new European regulation specifically addresses profiling and automated decision-making related to natural persons (and not legal persons, which, as is known, do not benefit from the protection accorded by data protection legislation, except in the field of electronic communications). This regulation has introduced new provisions to address the risks arising from the use of such techniques concerning the protection of private life.

5. **Prohibition on Sensitive Data Use**  
   Specifically, the regulation prohibits the use of sensitive data, including health data, in fully automated decision-making processes that significantly affect the individuals concerned unless such activities are necessary for reasons of substantial public interest, based on the applicable national law (which must still provide adequate and specific measures to protect the rights and freedoms and legitimate interests of the individuals involved), and must also be proportionate to the pursued purpose, while respecting the essence of the right to personal data protection.

6. **Need for Safeguards**  
   In this context, therefore, while the relevance and legitimacy of the objectives pursued through this system by the INPS (National Institute for Social Security) are not in question, it is essential that adequate safeguards are adopted to protect the individuals concerned in accordance with the new European legal framework. In this regard, it is not sufficient to have a regulatory intervention that simply authorizes the Institute to use such techniques by identifying the relevant public interests for which these can be employed.

7. **Internal Regulation Requirements**  
   It is, in fact, necessary for the internal regulation to introduce specific rules aimed at providing the necessary guarantees for the respect of the rights of the affected workers. The regulatory proposal developed by the INPS, as formulated, does not seem suitable to achieve the desired objective. It is, in fact, a framework norm of general scope that does not meet the requirements set by European law, as it merely states in a generic manner the public interests for which the Institute would be authorized to employ automated decision-making processes with personal data, including sensitive data, without clearly and unequivocally delineating the relevant purposes that could justify the use of such techniques concerning the specific objectives pursued, nor providing the necessary guarantees for the affected workers.

8. **Information and Transparency**  
   In particular, provisions should be made regarding the methods of informing the individuals about the existence of profiling, the criteria used, as well as the consequences arising from the attribution of a specific profile to their individual situation. Appropriate technical and organizational measures should also be adopted to allow for the rectification of any factors that may affect the accuracy of the data and ensure their constant updating, thus avoiding the construction or attribution of profiles to individuals based on inaccurate data.

9. **Data Retention and Quality**  
   Data should be retained for a proportionate time. Furthermore, the relevance and quality of the information and statistical inferences used should be periodically reassessed and within a reasonable timeframe. The algorithms used should be configured to minimize the risk of errors or distortions.

10. **Data Minimization**  
    Particular attention should be paid to data minimization, especially in cases of statistical correlations between information relating to different areas of the private lives of the individuals concerned. From this perspective, it would be appropriate, for example, to justify, based on objective and reasonable elements, the inclusion in the statistical model of data regarding remuneration, professional qualification, sector, and company size, or even the identifier of the doctor who issued the certificate.

11. **Access to Information**  
    Individuals should have access to a simple way to...
to exercise their rights and, in particular, the right to access the information used and to correct any incorrect information.  
Finally, the data controller should conduct an impact assessment in order to adequately evaluate, in concrete terms, the risks associated with automated decision-making processes and the necessary preventive measures.  
Such provisions (which could also be included in a secondary regulatory act to which the primary source refers) would allow for the establishment of a system capable of best reconciling the efficiency of checks on absences from work and the right to personal data protection, which increasingly represents an essential safeguard of freedom and dignity in the digital society.
