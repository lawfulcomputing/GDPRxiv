**Hearing of the Guarantor for the Protection of Personal Data - Conversion into law of the decree-law of October 8, 2021, No. 139, containing urgent provisions for access to cultural, sporting, and recreational activities, as well as for the organization of public administrations and in the matter of personal data protection**  
Senate of the Republic - 1st Committee on Constitutional Affairs  
(November 2, 2021)  

I thank the Committee for the opportunity for discussion provided to me regarding a provision – that of Article 9 of the decree-law – which introduces significant changes to the regulation of personal data protection.  
I will get straight to the point, but I would first like to emphasize how the intervention on the regulation of a fundamental right, with particularly stringent European legal constraints, requires careful reflection, to avoid that systemic reforms, which significantly affect the guarantee of such a right, express contingent evaluations.  
The overall aim underlying the reform, of simplifying the regulatory framework and the related procedural rules, must indeed be pursued with foresight, so as not to unduly compress the effectiveness of the right to personal data protection. This objective can well be achieved with some targeted amendments to the decree-law, hopefully accompanied by some organizational measures necessary to equip the Authority with the prerequisites to perform its role effectively. A role that is constantly measured against the need to guarantee the effectiveness of a right, such as that to personal data protection, which significantly the Charter of Nice, in its articulated taxonomy, inscribes within the realm of "freedom."  
This is the ultimate parameter to which the modifications introduced to the (pre)existing regulation by the decree-law must refer, combining the need for procedural simplification (particularly concerning processing carried out in the public sector) with the necessity of ensuring the effectiveness of the right to personal data protection.  
Regarding the specific interventions that I believe are necessary to avoid the risk that the provisions of the decree-law exceed the indicated objective of simplifying the regulation of the matter and translate into substantial reductions in the level of protection that, in the future, we will be able to guarantee to citizens concerning processing carried out by public entities and for the pursuit of public interest purposes, it is appropriate to make some observations.  
The aim pursued by the Government through the decree-law is to adapt the domestic legal system, in due respect of the European one, to the needs for speed characteristic of the historical phase we are experiencing, so as to allow the achievement of the ambitious objectives of the PNRR, among other things in terms of the digital transformation of the Country. However, one cannot overlook the risk that, although driven by shared and justifiable intentions, one might end up, almost as if it were an unintended and uncalculated side effect, compressing the privacy rights of millions of citizens, in disagreement with European law.  
It is in this perspective and only in this perspective that I present to you the opportunity that, in converting the decree into law, at least the few corrections that I will now indicate are adopted.  
The most significant of the changes introduced by the Decree is that of Article 2-ter (Article 9, paragraph 1, letter a) of the decree-law, aimed at essentially excluding the need for a legislative or regulatory provision but only in the cases provided for by law, for the processing of common data for public interest purposes.  
In this regard, I express a methodological consideration and a substantive one.  
As for the method, the choice to introduce a new paragraph 1-bis instead of intervening on paragraph 1, which indeed sets out the legal bases legitimizing the processing of personal data by public entities for the pursuit of public interest purposes, is not understood.  
As for the substance – if the objective pursued is to simplify and deregulate the preliminary activity for the initiation of processing by public entities for the pursuit of public interest purposes – it is difficult to understand the choice not to limit the intervention to a surgical modification of paragraph 1 of Article 2-ter, simply expanding the list of possible legal bases for processing to regulations and, possibly, to general administrative acts.  
Such an intervention, in addition to being compatible with the European regulation of the matter, would not have burdened – and would not burden if carried out in the context of the conversion into law of the decree – the burdens of public entities that may be the data controllers, as it would simply impose on them the adoption of a general administrative act through which to identify the characteristics of the processing that is deemed necessary to undertake for the pursuit of the institutional purposes assigned to them by law.  
And with reference to general administrative acts, it would have probably been useful to identify the President of the Council of Ministers as the holder of the power to direct and regulate the processing of personal data necessary to allow the various branches of the Administration to pursue their institutional purposes: this would have ensured uniformity of regulation, an adequate level of the preliminary investigation leading to the issuance of the act, and consequently a solid motivation.  
If, therefore, the aim pursued was to expand the range of legitimate sources for processing carried out for public interest purposes by deregulating and thus simplifying the preliminary administrative activity for the initiation of processing, the described solution would have allowed – and would now allow in the context of conversion – to combine the need for simplification with the guarantee of the right to personal data protection.
**For the flexibility of the regulation with the requirements of determinacy and predictability (of application) of the legal basis required by C 41 Reg. 2016/679 and confirmed by the conclusions of the Advocate General presented to the CJEU on September 2 in the context of case C 175/20.**

This solution would also allow for compliance with the requirement of determinacy, explicitness, legitimacy, and compatibility of purposes, which must be inscribed in the legal basis, as established by Articles 5, paragraph 1, letter b), and 6, paragraph 4, of the Regulation, also affirmed by the CJEU in the judgment of September 14, 2010, Sanoma Uitgevers v. Netherlands, case no. 38224/03, paragraphs 81-82, as well as in the previously cited precedents.

However, even where the Government's intention, through the intervention in question, is to go beyond this framework and to provide that the mere pursuit of a public interest is sufficient in itself to constitute the legal basis for processing, the correct placement of the provision should have been paragraph 1 of Article 2-ter. This could have clearly stated that the legal basis for processing by a public entity for the pursuit of its institutional purposes can be the law, a regulation, a general administrative act, or the mere instrumental nature of the processing to its institutional purposes.

Moreover, I would like to point out that, in this latter case – in my opinion not strictly instrumental to the declared purposes of simplification – the compatibility with the European legal order of personal data processing initiated by a public entity based on this provision will inevitably need to be assessed on a case-by-case basis since the European legal order always requires – regardless of the national law's approach regarding the legal source legitimizing the processing – that the processing, in its purpose and methods, be transparent, predictable, and known to citizens.

And this requirement, of course, does not always seem easily integrable in the absence of even a simple general administrative act that provides for and regulates the processing. This, rather than simplifying administrative activity, threatens to introduce elements of legal uncertainty into the system.

In this regard, the same German solution referred to in the explanatory report of the decree-law is not, after all, very different from that represented above as a possible corrective measure of the intervention. Article 3 of the Datenschutzanpassungsgesetz (Data Protection Adjustment Act) - while authorizing public entities to process personal data necessary to fulfill the task or power conferred upon them - does not have an autonomous legitimizing function but requires integration through sector-specific regulations that provide for the processing from time to time.

In any case, the provision in the first sentence of paragraph 1-bis appears difficult to interpret (and for this reason worthy of suppression), since, while not derogating from the need for normative typification referred to in paragraph 1, it establishes a general legitimization of (deemed) necessary processing for public interest purposes. This generates doubt about the sufficiency of the (deemed) necessity of processing for the performance of the public interest task or the exercise of public power, even in the absence of a regulatory provision and the fulfillment of the various and numerous obligations imposed, even on public entities, by European legislation.

It is therefore appropriate to suppress the sentence, or at least to circumscribe the scope of the assertion of the general legitimacy of processing, referring it solely to the aspect of the legal basis. To this end, it may be useful to include a safeguard clause regarding any other obligation provided for by the Regulation and the Code. In this way, it would be clarified that the general legitimacy of processing asserted by the first sentence of paragraph 1-bis does not prejudice the assessment concerning every other aspect (notably: transparency, proportionality, security, minimization) of the processing itself.

Lastly, I would like to observe that in the "German model" to which the Government, in the accompanying report to the decree, claims to have inspired its reform intervention, the protection of personal data even by public entities is guaranteed by a Federal Commissioner who can count on 270 human resources and a series of local commissioners – one for each Land – who collectively account for an additional 800 resources for a total cost of the personal data protection system of about 100 million euros per year.

In Italy, as is known, currently, the protection of personal data is carried out by a single national Commissioner who can count on a staff of 162 resources and a total budget of just over 30 million euros. 

In this context, where there is an intention to effectively inspire the national regulation on personal data protection to the German model – which, as highlighted, is at least imperfectly done – it would be appropriate to also intervene on strengthening the resources of the Authority responsible for ensuring the protection of personal data: it is evident that, in the absence of this, the multiplication of processing by public entities in the absence of any legitimizing legal basis would make it difficult, if not impossible, to exclude a significant decline in the overall level of personal data protection.

to disseminate them to the public equally in the absence of any law or regulation, as well as in the absence, even, of prior authorization – moreover in the simple mode of tacit consent – from the Authority as provided until the approval of the decree-law.  
The communication and dissemination of personal data constitute, as is known, two of the processing operations that can significantly prejudice the rights of the data subject, as they are capable of causing the latter to lose control over their personal data.  
In this context, during the conversion of the decree, if it is not deemed appropriate to accept the preceding suggestion regarding the advisability of limiting the legal basis for processing carried out by a public entity to be a law, a regulation, or at least a general administrative act, it will be necessary, at the very least, to reintroduce the obligation for prior communication, by the public entity that is the next data controller to the Authority, of the intention to proceed with the communication or dissemination of data with a notice that allows the Authority to verify the compatibility of the initiative with the European regulations on the matter.  
The other interventions on the personal data protection regulations introduced by the decree also appear significant, but of lesser impact, although in some cases they are inadequate to ensure the achievement of the declared purposes.  
In this perspective, for example, the amendment referred to in paragraph 3 of Article 9 - aimed at reducing the time frame for the Authority's opinion regarding reforms, measures, and projects of the National Recovery and Resilience Plan, the National Plan for Complementary Investments, and the National Integrated Energy and Climate Plan 2030, after which one can proceed in the absence of a ruling from the Authority - is understandable due to the need to accelerate these specific procedures, but it merely formalizes a now consolidated practice, according to which - in the face of a declaration of particular urgency of the opinion - the Authority compresses its procedural deadlines in order to exercise its advisory function in a timely manner relative to the needs presented and well below the new deadline of 30 days.  
The issue regarding the abolition of the institution, as per the repealed Article 2-quinquiesdecies, of the general prescriptions on processing for public interest purposes, at high risk (Article 9, paragraph 1, letter b) is different.  
The norm, in fact, paradoxically had an essentially simplifying function, legitimizing the Authority to adopt general prescriptive measures instead of (a series of) individual measures to be issued in the context of prior consultation, which is due in cases of persistent risk following an impact assessment.  
The norm has so far been applied in the context of prior consultation on specific treatments (for example, that related to the national alert system associated with the Immuni app, or the retention of electronic invoicing). However, the same provision has also been referenced in some legislative proposals (for example, regarding video surveillance in nurseries and care facilities) concerning multiple treatments carried out for public interest purposes and with high risks, for which the Authority could have adopted model-type prescriptions on general models, without the need to evaluate individual treatments.  
The repeal of the norm therefore necessitates the Authority to conduct individualized prior consultations even on high-risk treatments carried out for public interest purposes based on similar characteristics.  
The simplifying effectiveness of the repeal is, therefore, questionable, as it does not affect (nor could it affect) the obligation of prior consultation in cases of high-risk treatments but, rather, imposes individualized evaluations even where they could be conducted on model-type bases or, in any case, with measures of general effectiveness.  
The repeal of the institution also has an important ablative effect concerning the retention of logs for justice purposes, regarding which the Authority could adopt general prescriptive measures towards the interested operators.  
For this reason, the impact arising from the suppression of the entire provision that legitimizes the Authority to prescribe, with measures under Article 2-quinquiesdecies, security measures for the integrity and confidentiality of logs retained for justice purposes, which are also relevant according to established jurisprudence of the CJEU, should be contained.  
To this end, therefore, by restoring the repealed provision, the reference to Article 2-quinquiesdecies should be replaced with a reference to the Authority's power to adopt prescriptions in this area, with a general measure.  
Naturally, all the innovations introduced by the decree-law (from protection against behaviors leading to revenge porn to the increased control requirements related to the new treatment regime in the public sector, up to the reduction of the time frame for expressing opinions) presuppose an adequate administrative structure of the Authority (in terms of quantity and quality) for the functions assigned to it.  
Although enclosed in a single article within an urgent provision of different content, the reform of the data protection regulations introduced is anything but marginal and therefore deserves great attention. In the background lies the guarantee of a fundamental right, such as that to data protection, which increasingly constitutes a general prerequisite for both personal freedom and democracy.  
I cannot, therefore, do anything but thank the Commission.
Thank you for the valuable opportunity for discussion provided to us and for the attention that I am sure you will give to the needs expressed.
