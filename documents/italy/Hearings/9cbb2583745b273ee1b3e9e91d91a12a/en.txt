**Hearing of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, Prof. Pasquale Stanzione - Inquiry into Wiretapping**  
Senate of the Republic - 2nd Justice Commission  
(24 January 2023)  
- **THE VIDEO OF THE HEARING**  
Honorable Senators, the Authority is honored to contribute to the work of the Commission. In reforming the regulation of wiretapping, the legislator has the delicate task of balancing the right to privacy with investigative needs, the right to defense, and, with regard to extrajudicial circulation, the right to (and to) information. This balancing act must, of course, be conducted with an awareness of the profound implications for privacy inherent in the use of technology, both in the investigative phase (consider the use of trojans) and in the extrajudicial circulation of captured content, with the amplification that the web provides to every type of publication. In this sense, it is necessary to clearly distinguish the assumptions and limits of the procedural use of intercepted conversations from the assumptions and limits of their dissemination for informational purposes, ensuring that the two aspects of the regulation maintain the autonomy derived from their differing purposes and underlying needs.  
The right to privacy is particularly relevant in the context of wiretapping regulation under two profiles: with respect to the capturing operations themselves and the circulation (both intra- and extra-procedural) of the captured content, while still noting that the data protection regulation (Legislative Decree 51 of 2018) also applies to the processing of personal data in criminal judicial proceedings.  
Regarding the first profile, it is certainly significant to define the admissibility perimeter of wiretapping, which has been variously modulated by the legislative proposals put forward in previous legislatures not only concerning the category of interceptable crimes but also regarding the individualizing assumptions of the interceptions. These choices(1) are, of course, left to political discretion, albeit with the limit of respecting the principle of proportionality between investigative needs and privacy, as required by the jurisprudence of both the Constitutional Court (since rulings 366/91 and 63/94, as well as ruling 173 of 2009) and the European Court of Human Rights(2) and the Court of Justice of the EU(3). In this balancing act, the degree of invasiveness of the investigative means will naturally carry significant weight, certainly greater for potentially omnivorous (and ubiquitous) tools such as trojans.  
Particularly important is the intra-procedural circulation regime of the captured content. In this regard, the regulation in force since 2020, resulting from the legislative succession between the Orlando reforms (Legislative Decree 216 of 2017) and Bonafede (Law Decree 161 of 2019, converted into Law 7 of 2020), albeit with some attenuation of the latter compared to the former(4), contains important measures aimed at limiting the intra-procedural circulation of interceptions that exceed investigative needs, while respecting the adversarial principle (for and) regarding evidence. These measures reflect a shared guarantee need also recognized by the judiciary itself, as evidenced by directives issued by some Prosecutor's Offices in 2016 (particularly in Turin, Rome, and Florence), as well as the best practices indicated by the autonomous governing body in July of the same year.  
Particularly relevant are the anticipated exclusion (entrusted to the supervisory duty of the Public Prosecutor) of the transcribability of irrelevant sensitive data and content harmful to reputation, as well as the transfer of such data (and unusable conversations) to the digital archive, consequently subjecting them to the regime of official secrecy.  
If implemented rigorously(5), the new regulation can indeed contribute to reducing the intra-procedural circulation of excessive personal data. Naturally, this presupposes (especially for the phase of archiving the extracted content) the adoption of adequate security rules in accordance with those indicated by the Authority first in 2013(6) and then in the opinion on Ministerial Decree 20.4.18 (the decree specifically referred to, for the implementation of the 2017 reform, pending the establishment of interdepartmental server rooms, to technical specifications that, however, do not appear to have been adopted). The real challenge of the reform depends, in fact, largely on how the effective impermeability of the archive will be guaranteed, through security measures regarding which the Authority can offer, in a primarily collaborative perspective, its assessment.  
Furthermore, where the criteria of "content sobriety" and selective minimization imposed during transcription by the current regulation have not been effective, an important remedial protection for the parties (but also for third parties involved in indirect wiretapping) can derive, as President Santalucia recalled, from the innovative procedure introduced by Article 14 of Legislative Decree 51/2018. This provision indeed legitimizes "anyone with an interest" (not just the procedural parties, as in Article 269, paragraph 2) to request the judge, provided the conditions are met, the correction, deletion, or limitation of the data concerning them, even during the criminal proceedings. This is a provision with considerable potential that, when combined with the destruction procedure provided for in Article 269, could significantly strengthen the guarantees of privacy, especially for third parties whose conversations have been indirectly captured.  
Naturally, the effectiveness of the provision would be enhanced by the introduction of an informational burden on the Public Prosecutor - as was provided for by Article 268-sexies of the Code of Criminal Procedure, which Article 10 of the Mastella draft law (AS 1512, 15th legislature) proposed to introduce - to prevent the subject from learning of the existence, in procedural acts, of their own...
concerning the interception of communications, directly from the press, when the ablative intervention would already be late. 

Alternatively (if such informational burden is deemed excessively burdensome, especially in light of a plurality of third parties to be notified), the third party could be legitimized, upon confirmation of the existence of interceptions involving them, to listen to them for the purpose of activating the destruction procedure referred to in Article 269, paragraph 2, of the Code of Criminal Procedure (cpp) or, in the case of more articulated requests, to exercise their rights to limit or (more rarely) rectify the data pursuant to Article 14 of Legislative Decree 51 of 2018. 

In this way, through the procedural connection between the new right referred to in Article 14 of Legislative Decree 51 and the institution of destruction referred to in Article 269 of the cpp, third parties whose data are occasionally captured during interceptions could be granted truly effective protection. 

The issue of publication, in violation of the (merely) external secrecy under Article 114, paragraph 2 of the cpp, of often extensive excerpts of intercepted conversations is more complex. Although this prohibition is imposed to protect not so much privacy as the neutral knowledge of the judge, its violation (which can certainly harm confidentiality) still constitutes an unlawful processing of personal data, punishable since 2018 (just like the disclosure of content not relevant for informational purposes) with administrative monetary sanctions that can reach up to 20 million euros or 4% of turnover (Articles 166, paragraph 2, Legislative Decree 196 of 2003, 5 and 83, paragraph 5, EU Regulation 2016/679, Article 6 ethical rules for the processing of personal data in the journalistic field). 

Such sanctions can play a significant deterrent role against the uncritical and indiscriminate disclosure of intercepted conversations, well beyond the actual needs of reporting (the "reporting journalism" referred to by President Violante). 

Naturally, it is also possible to hypothesize (as in previous legislatures) to differently modulate the publicity regime of investigative acts, but before changing a balance between privacy and information that is fundamentally reasonable, it may be preferable to verify the resilience and effects of recent reforms, including that of Legislative Decree 188 of 2021 on the presumption of innocence. By imposing significant rules of sobriety and accuracy in the communication (of and about) justice, it could indeed induce a change, primarily cultural, that is far from irrelevant. 

As for interceptions through devices, the intrusive potential of such tools requires adequate guarantees to prevent their degeneration into means of excessively broad surveillance or, conversely, into factors that exponentially multiply the vulnerabilities of the evidentiary bundle, making it extremely permeable if allocated on insecure servers or, in any case, relocated even outside national borders. 

The necessity of such guarantees seems, moreover, corroborated by recent events (consider the Exodus case of 2019), relating to the particular methods of carrying out interceptions through malware by the companies appointed under Article 348, paragraph four, of the cpp. They highlight the risks associated with the use of computer interceptors by the appointed companies resorting to infiltration techniques lacking the necessary selectivity. 

This refers, in particular, to the use, for interception purposes, of software connected to apps, which are therefore not directly injected into the only device of the investigated person, but placed on platforms (such as the Google Play Store) accessible to all. If made available on the market, even if only by mistake in the absence of the necessary filters to limit their acquisition by third parties - as seems to have occurred in cases known to the news - these spy apps would indeed risk transforming into dangerous tools of mass surveillance. 

Furthermore, the use of cloud systems for the storage, even in non-European states, of captured data is dangerous. The relocation of servers in territories not subject to national jurisdiction constitutes, in fact, an evident breach not only for the protection of the rights of the interested parties but also for the very effectiveness and secrecy of the investigative action. 

The recourse to these two types of systems (apps or software that are not directly injected into the host device but downloaded from freely accessible platforms, and, on the other hand, storage through cloud systems on servers located outside the national territory) could, therefore, be subject to a specific prohibition. 

In the alternative, it could be provided that the actual installation in the portable electronic device and the consequent acquisition functionalities of the computer interceptor can be fully realized only after verifying the unequivocal association between the device affected by the software and that considered in the authorizing judicial provision. 

In any case, also due to the rapid evolution of the characteristics and functionalities of the software available for interception purposes, as already noted in the report to Parliament and the Government of 2019, it would be appropriate to prohibit the use of interceptors capable of modifying the content of the host device and erasing traces of the operations carried out, as also noted by Engineer Reale. For the correct reconstruction of evidence, the guarantee of the right of defense as well as privacy, it is indeed essential to have software capable of reconstructing, in detail, every activity carried out on the host system and on the data present therein, without altering its content, correspondingly valuing the need for an analytical record of the operations performed. 

In this sense, the requirement, legally provided, of "reliability, security, and effectiveness" of the software that can be used could be explicitly stated.
**Final Considerations on Captation** 

(1) The operations must indeed be limited to the only "authorized operations," thus effectively ensuring the completeness of the "chain of custody of digital evidence." This requirement is all the more indispensable concerning investigative operations, such as those under examination, which have a high degree of outsourcing and, as such, present greater vulnerabilities, being largely entrusted to private entities that must, therefore, be adequately held accountable for the security obligations to be ensured. 

It will also be appropriate to clarify, in Article 89, paragraph 2, of the implementing provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure, the consequences of resorting to software programs that do not comply with the security requirements established by the Ministerial Decree. 

While the introduction of the aforementioned precautions is advisable, the particular invasiveness of spyware certainly merits reflection by Parliament regarding the actual scope of application of this means of evidence collection. Certainly positive is the provision for the necessity of indicating, in the authorization decree, the reasons for the indispensability of using the trojan (introduced by Decree Law No. 132 of 2021, converted into Law No. 178 of 2021) and, for crimes other than those of a district nature or the more serious offenses against public administration, the locations and times of microphone activation. In this way, it is possible, at least in part, to circumscribe the potential ubiquity of the means and the difficult predetermination of the development of interceptions. 

However, should Parliament consider rethinking the admissibility perimeter of this type of interception, useful insights can be derived from a strong reading of the proportionality scrutiny between investigative needs and privacy (in the context of the inviolability of digital life) offered, regarding trojans as well, by the German Constitutional Court, in rulings dated February 27, 2008, and April 20, 2016. Particularly relevant is the consideration of how the proportionality standard imposes a modulation of guarantees that takes into account the potential of the investigative means actually used and its capacity to impact the intangible core of the individual's private life. (9) These considerations are certainly useful for a legislator who has the difficult task of balancing fundamental legal goods such as privacy, the right to defense, and the needs of justice. 

Thank you.
