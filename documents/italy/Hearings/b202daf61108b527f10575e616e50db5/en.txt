**Hearing of the President of the Data Protection Authority in the context of the examination of the bill C. 1433 concerning measures for the effectiveness of actions by public administrations and the prevention of absenteeism before the Joint Committees I (Constitutional Affairs) and XI (Labor) of the Chamber of Deputies (February 6, 2019)**  
**Text of the intervention**

I thank the Committee for this opportunity for discussion on a topic whose complexity we have already had the chance to highlight not only in our opinion on the draft bill but also during the hearing in the previous reading.  
With respect to the text on which we have already expressed our views, a limited modification has been made to the provision of Article 2.  
This modification must be evaluated in light of the principle of proportionality, which in the context of data protection serves as an essential parameter for the legitimacy of processing, under a dual aspect.  
On one hand, it represents an essential regulatory criterion in the conduct of processing by the data controller, particularly concerning the choice regarding the scope and modalities of the processing itself.  
On the other hand, the principle of proportionality represents a general parameter for the legitimacy of limitations on the right to data protection, to be observed—in accordance with the criteria set out in Article 52 of the Charter of Nice—even in the exercise of legislative power.  
The Charter indeed provides a series of specific requirements for the limitations on the exercise of the rights and freedoms recognized therein (among which is also the right to data protection). Among these, particular importance is given to the necessary respect for the essential content of the right and the principle of proportionality, which allows limitations on fundamental rights only if they are "necessary and genuinely meet objectives of general interest recognized by the Union or the need to protect the rights and freedoms."  
The principles of necessity and proportionality have been articulated with particular clarity by data protection legislation (first Directive 95/46, now Regulation 2016/679 and Directive 2016/680 (for criminal justice and police)) and interpreted with implications of absolute relevance by the Court of Justice.  
In this regard, it should be emphasized that with the Digital Rights judgment concerning data retention, the Court of Justice declared an entire legislative act of the Union invalid for violation of the principle of proportionality. The right to data protection was deemed excessively compromised (despite the indisputable merit of the aim of combating crime) by a measure, such as the retention of telephone and telematic logs, which was massive in nature as it targeted the generality of citizens and was not limited to the repressive needs of only serious crimes.  
The principle of proportionality thus imposes, first and foremost, the limitation of restrictive measures on the right to only those cases supported by specific and differentiated needs, thereby generally rendering massive measures illegitimate.  
Secondly, the principles of necessity and proportionality lead to the conclusion that restrictive measures on the right are illegitimate whenever it is possible to adopt equally effective but less invasive measures: a reason that led the Court of Justice to annul, for violation of these principles, a European regulation concerning the publicity of agricultural subsidies.  
These principles thus legitimize more invasive measures only in the face of the inadequacy for the purpose of less restrictive systems of the right, as "derogations and restrictions" on fundamental rights must occur "within the limits of what is strictly necessary."  
Regulation 2016/679 has further enhanced the principle of proportionality, especially in the public sector, referring it to the very legislative provision even before the processing itself, thus asking the legislator to balance the specific needs underlying the processing with the right to data protection.  
Naturally, the scrutiny of proportionality must be even more stringent concerning data recognized as having enhanced protection due to the risks inevitably associated with their processing.  
Thus, for particular categories of data, including biometric data, the Regulation generally establishes a prohibition on processing, which can only be overcome in the presence of certain prerequisites, including, in particular (and also in labor matters):  
- the existence of a specific legislative provision  
- the necessity of processing for achieving the legitimate aims pursued, as well as  
- the respect for appropriate safeguards.  
In light of these parameters, Article 2 of the draft bill must be read, which provides for all public administrations—except for personnel under public law and for agile work—the introduction of biometric identity verification systems and video surveillance of access in place of the various automatic detection systems currently in use for the purpose of verifying compliance with working hours.  
The introduction of such systems must occur—according to the provision introduced in the Senate—"in compliance with the principles of proportionality, non-exceedance, and graduality pursuant to Article 5, paragraph 1, letter c)" of the Regulation.  
The implementing modalities of the norm are delegated to regulations to be adopted, subject to the opinion of the Authority on the modalities of processing biometric data, in compliance with Article 9 of the Regulation and the safeguards defined by the Authority.  
The norm provides, through the simultaneous use—and not alternative—of the relevant systems, for the processing of both personal data such as the image of the person (using video surveillance tools) and biometric data, which, as mentioned, are subject to enhanced protection that they...
**Limits the use only in the presence of specific requirements.**  
Despite the clause regarding compliance with the principles of proportionality, non-exceedance, and graduality, the norm must be considered incompatible with such principles, insofar as it intends— as would seem given the literal wording— to continue to configure biometric detection—along with video recordings—as mandatory in every public administration.  
In fact, it cannot be considered in any way compliant with the principle of proportionality the hypothesized systematic, generalized, and undifferentiated introduction for all public administrations of biometric attendance detection systems, due to the constraints imposed by European law regarding the invasiveness of such forms of verification and the implications inherent to the particular nature of the data.  
The requirement to respect the principles of proportionality and minimization, introduced in the Senate, would have effective normative significance only if the norm were intended to provide for:  
a) the alternative use of biometrics or video surveillance: but the legislative text is clear in configuring these systems instead as cumulative, which in itself contradicts the principle of necessity and proportionality;  
b) the introduction of such new detection systems not as mandatory but permitted in the presence of specific needs and where other attendance detection systems are deemed unsuitable for the pursued objectives.  
It would therefore be appropriate to modify the text to expressly provide for:  
a) the alternative use of biometric detection and video recordings;  
b) the admissibility of biometric detection in the presence of specific risk factors or particular conditions such as, for example, the size of the entity, the number of employees involved, the recurrence of critical situations that could also be influenced by the environmental context. The detailed articulation of such requirements could well be delegated to the regulations referred to in paragraphs 1 and 4.  
If, on the other hand, the current version of the norm were confirmed—interpreting it as aimed at establishing the indiscriminate and abstract obligation of the new detection systems—it would be difficult to reconcile with the criterion of "necessity in compliance with the principle of proportionality" as per Article 52 of the Charter of Nice.  
The Air (regulatory impact analysis) recalls, in this regard, the need to counter a phenomenon such as false attendance at work, undoubtedly serious but for which no univocal data seem to emerge regarding its systematic and generalized spread in public administrations. Statistics indeed tell us that 10% of disciplinary dismissal measures adopted in the last year stem from the detection of false attendance at work: in absolute terms, 89, half of which were resolved with another type of measure, in some cases even due to changed allegations.  
This is certainly a significant figure, but not symptomatic of the general pervasiveness of the phenomenon or sufficient to justify the adoption, in each public administration, of such an invasive attendance detection system.  
Therefore, the abstract, generalized, and undifferentiated normative presumption of the existence, for all public administrations, of risk factors such as to consider biometric detection the only system capable of ensuring compliance with working hours does not appear compatible with the principle of proportionality.  
To achieve the commendable goal of combating absenteeism and false attendance at work, it should therefore be done by first resorting to less restrictive measures on the right to data protection, using biometric detection systems only in the presence of specific risk factors, where less invasive solutions must reasonably be deemed unsuitable for the purpose.  
Such a limitation should clearly emerge from the textual wording of the norm.  
The mere reference to the principles of Article 5, paragraph 1, c) of the Regulation, if irrelevant for the configuration as non-mandatory of such new detection methods, resolves into a mere stylistic clause, inadequate to exclude the conflict of the provision with European legislation.  
Nor, moreover, would such a conflict be remedied by relating compliance with the principle of proportionality to the specific implementation modalities of this biometric detection obligation (modulating differently, for example, the types of data used or the retention period).  
In the first place, in fact, even using— as has been read as the Government's intention— technologies based on applications and software available to the employee, it would still be necessary to identify the subjects authorized to process the detected data and the specific conditions of use, as well as the guarantees suitable to prevent unauthorized access or data breaches.  
But even so delimiting it, such a provision would still be incompatible with the principles of proportionality, non-exceedance, and minimization in configuring the introduction of biometric identity verification systems and video surveillance of access as abstractly mandatory regardless of any concrete and specific need in this regard.  
The norm should therefore be reformulated, avoiding not only its intrinsic contradictions but also, and above all, its incompatibility with European legislation.
