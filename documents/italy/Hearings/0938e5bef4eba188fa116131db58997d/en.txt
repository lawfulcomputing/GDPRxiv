Observations of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, Antonello Soro, regarding Bill No. 535 and related bills concerning the National Network of Cancer Registries

before the 12th Hygiene and Health Committee of the Senate of the Republic (October 15, 2018)

The Authority has already had the opportunity to be consulted on the matter of cancer registries during the 17th legislature, by the Social Affairs Committee of the Chamber, and in the previous one by the Hygiene and Health Committee of the Senate. Moreover, several opinions have been issued by the Authority on regional laws and regulatory acts governing their operation. 

Bill No. 535 and related bills – which essentially mirror the unified text already approved in the last legislature – address a topic that is both complex and relevant for improving healthcare and the health conditions of the entire population. 

Compared to previous occasions when the Authority has had the opportunity to express its opinion on this regulation, the regulatory framework regarding data protection has changed, as outlined by the EU General Regulation 2016/679, applicable since May 25, and by the national adaptation provisions introduced by Legislative Decree No. 101/2018. 

In particular, the bills provide for the establishment of a National Network of Cancer Registries and surveillance systems, as well as the epidemiological report, specifying the purposes intended to be pursued through such databases. 

These provisions deserve particular attention as they involve the use of sensitive data, such as health data, to which European law and internal regulations grant special guarantees. Unauthorized access to such data, their abusive use, loss, or alteration can expose the individual to dangerous forms of discrimination, made possible by the knowledge of the most intimate and private aspects of the person, which touch upon their very dignity. Furthermore, the vulnerability of health databases poses a risk to the health of the patients to whom the data pertains, as it can lead to diagnostic or therapeutic errors, with potentially lethal consequences for those involved. 

In other words, inadequate security or inaccuracy of the data can lead to cases of medical malpractice. Conversely, the protection of personal data is an essential factor for the guarantee and efficiency of healthcare systems. 

For these reasons, the new European regulatory framework, recognizing the great utility of population registries (and particularly those concerning cancer) – as they are essential for expanding knowledge about the risk factors of diseases and for developing effective health policies based on this knowledge (see Recital 157 of EU Regulation 2016/679) – continues to grant such data enhanced protection. 

In particular, European regulation reiterates the general prohibition on using health data, unless necessary for certain strictly indicated reasons, including: reasons of significant public interest based on Union or national legislation, which must, however, provide for appropriate and specific measures to protect the rights and freedoms of the data subjects (see Article 9 of EU Regulation 2016/679). 

In this regard, the subject matter addressed by the bills is not without regulation. Following the legal establishment of the electronic health record, surveillance systems, and health registries (Article 12 of Decree-Law No. 179/2012), the DPCM of March 3, 2017, on which the Authority provided an opinion in 2015, identified a list of surveillance systems and health registries to be implemented or maintained in operation, where already established, defining their purposes and geographical scope of operation. The same law also delegated to a regulatory source the identification of guarantees for the protection of personal data. 

However, at present, this regulation – which is essential for the protection of data subjects, as it aims to outline the fundamental guarantees for the protection of data processed in surveillance systems and registries – has not yet been adopted. Therefore, there is a lack of comprehensive implementing regulation specifically concerning the most delicate aspects of processing, relating to the guarantees for patient confidentiality. 

Moreover, the DPCM of 2017, implementing the rules establishing registries and surveillance systems, provides for the implementation of a cancer registry and certain surveillance systems articulated at a regional level (which collects data from health organizations operating in its territory) and at a national level, whose data controller is the Ministry of Health (which collects data from the regional level). Furthermore, the purposes pursued by the DPCM through the activation of the aforementioned registries and surveillance systems largely overlap with those attributed, by the same bills, to the National Network. 

Given this regulatory context, this Committee should first assess the actual necessity of introducing a new (albeit more comprehensive and specific) regulation on the matter, also in relation to the risk that this may further delay the effective implementation of registries and surveillance systems. 

It might be more appropriate, instead, to make the necessary modifications to the existing regulation to improve the functioning and efficiency of cancer registries and surveillance systems, integrating them within a more comprehensive regulatory framework. This could also involve assigning to the national level tasks of coordination, standardization, and validation of data, as well as monitoring the effectiveness of screening programs and epidemiological studies (or even accreditation).

In any case, the following observations are made regarding the individual provisions of these texts. 

First of all, it is appropriate that the scope of operation of the National Network of Cancer Registries and surveillance systems be clearly defined, as well as its relationship with the "cancer registry" and other "nationally relevant" surveillance systems already established under the aforementioned DPCM at the Ministry of Health. This is to avoid duplicating databases and health archives, in compliance with the principles of proportionality and privacy by design and by default provided for by the Regulation, which require minimizing the processing of personal data, considering the nature, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the state of the art and implementation costs (see Articles 5 and 25 of EU Regulation 2016/679). The risks associated with the proliferation of informational obligations on health bodies and regional health facilities should also be taken into account, not only in terms of the burden imposed on such entities but also in terms of the increased danger to the confidentiality of the individuals concerned, especially regarding the needs for accuracy and updating of the data. 

Similar evaluations should be conducted regarding the provisions of the bills that foresee the establishment of the so-called epidemiological report, referring to a decree from the Ministry of Health, on which the Guarantor must express an opinion, for the parts of competence, regarding the identification of the subjects responsible for the collection and processing of data, as well as the definition of other processing procedures. In this regard, aside from certain concerns regarding the chosen name for this tool (which refers to a clinical report on the health status of individual patients, rather than to a representation with aggregated data of the overall health status of a community), there is a certain ambiguity in the provisions that foresee its content, implementation, and related use "in order to ensure a permanent monitoring of the health status of the population, also within the framework of surveillance systems, mortality registries, cancer registries, and other diseases." 

In particular, it is not clear who the data controller is and what types of data should be included, nor are the legitimately pursuable purposes adequately delineated through this tool. 

It could indeed be considered that the epidemiological report could also be fed with additional information beyond that contained in the surveillance systems and registries, thus falling outside the scope of the bills under examination and laying the groundwork for a possible breach of European principles of purpose limitation and data minimization. 

Some refinements should also be made to the provisions of the bills that refer to a regulation, on which to acquire the opinion of the Guarantor, regarding the identification of the data processed within the Network of Cancer Registries and surveillance systems, the methods of processing, the subjects authorized to access it, the measures for the custody and security of the data, as well as the rights of the individuals concerned. 

In light of the changed regulatory framework regarding data protection, it is indeed essential that the implementing regulation introduces specific provisions not only in relation to the reasons for pursuing relevant public interest, the types of data subject to processing, and the related methods (including the aspects of custody and security of the same), but also provides "appropriate and specific measures to protect the fundamental rights and interests" of patients (see Article 6, paragraph 1, letter e) and 3, and Article 9, paragraph 2 of EU Regulation 2016/679 and Article 2-sexies of Legislative Decree No. 196/2003 as amended by Legislative Decree No. 101/2018). In particular, this act should also be tasked with identifying specific methods for informing the individuals concerned, the provision of appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure data minimization and quality, as well as easy methods for exercising rights, and, in particular, the right to object to processing for reasons related to their particular situation. 

Further aspects that need to be explored in the implementing regulation concern the application of technical and organizational measures that satisfy, in particular, the principles of privacy by design and by default, such as pseudonymization and the identification of proportionate time periods for data retention. This is to proactively reduce risks while ensuring ample margins for analysis and research on personal data, without violating patient confidentiality. 

Regarding the activities and tasks of the Network, the bills provide that it shall provide "at the national level, continuous and comprehensive information to the population, also through the publication of data on the official website of the Ministry of Health." As already highlighted during the hearing in March 2016, this objective can only be understood with reference to appropriately anonymized and aggregated information to exclude any risk of re-identification, due to the current prohibition on the dissemination of health data. 

A similar consideration should be extended to the publication of data from the epidemiological report, relating to the incidence and prevalence of diseases that most frequently cause death, on the websites of the subjects responsible for its compilation. Likewise, the very definition of the epidemiological report should be clarified with reference to the need that the aggregated data that constitutes it does not...
allow for the identification of the data subject, even indirectly (e.g., through the cross-referencing of data present in other archives). Furthermore, the provisions of the draft laws that mention the rights of data subjects need to be updated to reference the relevant articles of the European Regulation, rather than the provisions of the Code which have now been repealed by the domestic adaptation legislation (see Articles 15 et seq. of EU Regulation 2016/679 and no longer Article 7 of the Personal Data Protection Code). 

Finally, since the management of cancer registries requires a type of processing that may present a high risk to the rights of the individuals involved—given the nature of the information processed, the number of data subjects, the qualitative and quantitative relevance of the data collected, as well as the systematic and geographical scope of the processing—a data protection impact assessment should be conducted in accordance with European legislation, so that the data controller can assess the risks involved in concrete terms, either in advance (or subsequently, due to changed risk conditions). Regarding this assessment, it is suggested that the draft laws under consideration refer to the obligation to consult the Supervisory Authority pursuant to Article 36, paragraph 5 of the European Regulation. Moreover, since these are processing activities carried out for the execution of tasks in the public interest, the Supervisory Authority may prescribe measures and precautions to safeguard the data subjects (see Article 2-quinquiesdecies of the Code as amended by Legislative Decree No. 101/2018). 

Such improvements would allow for the establishment of a system capable of best reconciling the efficiency of preventive and curative healthcare services with the right to personal data protection: an essential guarantee of dignity and self-determination for the patient in an increasingly digitalized healthcare context.
