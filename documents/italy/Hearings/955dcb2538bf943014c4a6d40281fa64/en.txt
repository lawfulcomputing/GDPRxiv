Hearing of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, Antonello Soro. Continuation of the inquiry into forms of violence among minors and against children and adolescents
Parliamentary Commission for Childhood and Adolescence
(July 8, 2020)

I thank the Commission for this discussion on a topic that is one of the most expressive of the contradictions of our time. The context in which abuses against minors develop and amplify - in a sort of complacent display of violence - is increasingly the internet, which has a decisive impact on the lives of young people. 

For digital natives especially, it represents not just one of many possible means of communication but rather the dimension, even the main one, of life. The relationships woven online, the sharing on social media of every intimate fragment of life, and the perception of the world increasingly mediated by the internet impose new protection needs, in light of the inadequacy of traditional legal categories to regulate phenomena in continuous evolution, such as the technologies that shape them.

Cyberbullying, the harassment displayed by young people against their peers, revenge porn, but also the more rigid conformity and the marginalization of anyone who thinks or acts differently from the majority, are just some of the implications of the distorted use of the internet: even more dramatic because they involve minors. 

From a vehicle of extraordinary opportunities for growth and emancipation, the web risks, if experienced without the necessary awareness, exposing increasingly fragile young people to underestimated dangers, in the gap between the illusion of autonomy and the internalization of rules, the experience of freedom and the exercise of responsibility. 

Also because the internet is the space where today we leave minors most alone: precisely those whom we accompany, with concern, step by step in the streets of our cities, have already “navigated,” as “autonomous” as they are vulnerable. And the solitude we grant our children on the web is not the same as that which we granted children in front of the TV: there it was enough to select programs; online there are no adequate filters and, above all, there is a continuous interaction from which children should be protected.

According to research by the Italian Society of Preventive and Social Pediatrics (Sipps) presented on the occasion of Safer Internet Day, 80% of the sample reports having received limitations from parents only regarding the time of use of electronic devices, the closed nature of social profiles, and the prohibition of access to pornographic sites, but not more specific rules of conduct. 

The research also reports that over 50% of young people aged 11 to 17 have experienced episodes of bullying, and among those who use their mobile phones daily (85.8%), as many as 22.2% have been victims of cyberbullying, as well as “trolling” by peers, perceived as particularly humiliating. 

The internet is, in fact, also the place where, in the illusion of anonymity, minors violate other minors. From sexual violence - acted offline and then displayed online - to hate speech; from the “voluntary servitude” to which a girl exposes herself by selling herself online, to cyberbullying. 

This is perhaps the most tragic aspect of the violent use of the internet: in which both the perpetrator and the victim share the same fragility and the same unawareness of the “real” and very concrete consequences of every action we take in the digital realm. And it is perhaps the most paradigmatic expression of the ambivalence of the digital world and its dangerous misunderstandings. 

The “bully” deludes himself into thinking he can hide behind anonymity or underestimates the impact of what he does, lacking the perception of how a click can lead to the destruction of a life. Publishing even the most serious insult online, or the recording of an indecent act of violence against a sick child, is much easier: because one does not have to deal with the idea of “social sanction” - even before legal - with the stigma that such behavior would expose if committed offline, in front of everyone. 

It does not require audacity, however vile: the “transition to action” is much easier. Unfortunately, the consequences are even more devastating, because that violence tends to remain forever, accessible to anyone at any latitude: it has no end, it never gives respite to its victim because it is omnipresent. 

On this, some important protections have been introduced with the law on cyberbullying, which, in recognition of the complexity of this phenomenon, not reducible to a mere criminal issue, has chosen to combine prevention, accountability (both of minors and social media managers), and victim protection. 

The main reparative measure consists of a special urgent procedure for the removal of harmful content, with an initial request directed to the manager and, if there is inertia, rejection, or impossibility of identifying the owner, then submitted to the Authority. 

It is an innovative and effective remedial protection that, on one hand, avoids any form of interference by the manager in users' communications, while on the other hand holds them accountable if they are notified of the presence of illegal content online, as demonstrated by the prompt and voluntary adherence of most platforms. 

Moreover, the code of co-regulation provided for by the law is soon to be adopted, and the Authority has made a significant contribution to its drafting. It will promote further accountability of social network managers and other internet operators, binding them to adhere to a significant level of guarantees and raising their awareness of the need to remove illegal content present on their platforms. 

The protection granted by the Authority then serves as a last resort guarantee and a closing link.

The warning procedure, modeled on that of stalking, is also a particularly useful tool for breaking the spiral of hatred that fuels this phenomenon.  
The measures provided by law for educating on the conscious use of the internet are also significant, as they could truly address the deep-rooted cultural and social causes of cyberbullying if properly implemented.  
Among the foremost issues are the loneliness and digital immaturity of increasingly abandoned young people, left without tools in front of a screen, unaware that a post is not just a note on a desk: it can remain online forever, visible and "replicable" by anyone, with a harmful potential of which young people must be aware.  
A real education, ethical and civic, in the digital realm is the indispensable tool to enable young people to draw from the internet all its extraordinary resources to become aware digital citizens, as emphasized by the Council of Europe’s Guidelines on the Rights of the Child in the Digital Environment.  
The immaterial dimension is, in fact, very real, and behind every "profile" there is a flesh-and-blood person, with their vulnerabilities and their right not to be violated.  
This is the first and most important frontier on which we must all invest, with an educational alliance that also serves us adults to reflect on the viral evolution of resentment that a certain use of the internet risks producing.  
It is significant, in this regard, that the draft law S1690 – in addition to extending the regulation of "online" bullying to "offline" bullying – proposes the introduction of training modules for education in emotional intelligence, as well as further measures for the protection of victims and the rehabilitation of offenders, proposing personalized assistance paths for the former and educational support for the latter, possibly including mediation interventions.  
Particular attention must be paid – in the event of the draft law's approval – to the implementation of both the app offered by the emergency child service 114 for victim assistance and the training and monitoring platforms of the phenomenon, made available to schools by the Ministry of Education.  
Since both of these systems would involve a flow of very sensitive data concerning minors, including victims of crime, it is necessary to outline their architecture in order to ensure the maximum confidentiality of the information processed, to prevent leaks or unauthorized access, which could lead to secondary victimization phenomena that should not be underestimated.  
Important protections have also been introduced regarding the no less concerning phenomenon of sexual abuse against minors and the commodification of the body, often carried out online.  
Consider the crime of grooming, with the typification of online contact as a mode of conduct, or revenge porn: a phenomenon that is alarmingly on the rise.  
Equally important is the provision of an aggravating circumstance related to crimes of sexual exploitation of minors, committed using means designed to prevent the identification of access data to online networks.  
This norm aims to counter the tendency of abusive individuals to conceal the traces of their illicit contacts: which explains the proliferation of online grooming.  
However, even when such tricks are not employed, identifying the actual perpetrator of the offense (essential for attributing the corresponding responsibility, especially if criminal) is not always easy, as it sometimes requires overcoming – often with considerable difficulty – the screen of anonymity online.  
This institution must certainly be safeguarded as it is functional, among other things, to freedom of expression, but every abuse of it must be prevented, especially if it translates into facilitating the commission of crimes against the most vulnerable individuals.  
We should then perhaps reflect on the regulation of the use of anonymity on the web, making it truly reversible so that investigative authorities can ascertain the actual responsibilities for offenses committed under this shield.  
On the other hand, the right, protected by criminal law, to anonymity for victims of crimes against sexual freedom and individual personality is indispensable, and we have intervened several times regarding its violation, including by the media, to contain the effects of secondary victimization that may arise from it.  
The effectiveness of such repressive tools is, however, conditioned by the speed of technological evolution, which often manages to evade legislative prohibitions and by the limits imposed on jurisdiction and the application of the law by the principle of territoriality, as these are often crimes committed abroad and conveyed online.  
But above all, criminal protection concerning minors encounters the limits (indispensable and necessary, let it be clear) of non-imputability (whether absolute or relative), the principles of minimal offensiveness, destigmatization, and the residual nature of the sanction (especially if custodial).  
Thus, regarding such cases, the combined effect of effective reparative protection and a compensation regime particularly favorable to the victim, but also strongly deterrent, assumes a decisive importance.  
The data protection regulation provides, in this sense, important tools.  
The right – exercisable before the Guarantor or the judicial authority, in case of inertia of the manager – to request, among other things, the deletion of unlawfully disseminated content represents a particularly effective measure.

What can, however, have a real preventive effect, more than any other measure or sanction, is adequate digital education, which bridges the gap between the use of the web by young people as the main agency of socialization and their actual awareness of how to use it to promote rather than violate freedoms. 

In this sense, it is essential to invest in digital literacy as a true form of "civic education" in the age of digital citizenship. It is significant that the Regulation identifies the age range between 13 and 16 years as the threshold for digital consent, with a parallel modulation of the principle of transparency in processing and a significant accountability of the data controllers. They will indeed have to adapt the information provided to a younger audience, making its scope truly understandable and thus allowing minors to consciously exercise their decision-making autonomy.

The threshold for digital consent has been set by the national legislator at 14 years, in analogy with the law on cyberbullying, but also with other provisions that at that age recognize a partial capacity to act (corresponding to a presumption of adequate discernment), granting "personal" rights that are thus not subject to representation. 

While this choice captures a reality—because young people, at that age if not earlier, live much of their relationships in the digital dimension—it also presupposes adequate training to make minors aware of what the processing of their data may entail. Otherwise, the right to informational self-determination that allows young people to exercise on their own risks being illusory or, worse, resulting in a detriment to their own freedom, security, and identity.

The prevailing jurisprudential orientation, aimed at requiring the consent of both parents for the dissemination of photos of minors, considered an act beyond ordinary administration due to the risks it entails, is significant in this regard. Recognizing the over-14 minor's ability to express "their own" views regarding the management of their data, even concerning the use made by parents, can represent an important factor of accountability.

It would also be necessary to strengthen the adoption, by providers, even in codes of conduct, of precautions useful to protect minors (e.g., differentiated browsing services, selective access that inhibits the consultation of certain content classified as unsuitable for minors), provided that they do not presuppose the preventive control of content.

Naturally, it is not the tool itself that should be condemned: the web is a theater of both violence and harassment, as well as solidarity and the promotion of freedom. Consider Keaton, the young American cancer patient, humiliated offline and protected online. The same network that has driven more than one minor to suicide, in this case, saved a child from offline harassment.

The "neutrality" of the web—understood as susceptibility to harmful or, conversely, supportive uses—places the responsibility for online conduct on users and imposes on adults the delicate task of making minors aware of how extraordinary, but also risky, this new—and never sufficiently "explored"—dimension of life can be.
