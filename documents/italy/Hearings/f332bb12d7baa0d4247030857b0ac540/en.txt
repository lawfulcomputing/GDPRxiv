**Report of the Data Protection Authority - Exploratory Investigation on the Spread of Pathological Addictions Among Young People**  
Parliamentary Commission for Childhood and Adolescence  
(August 19, 2021)  

1. **The Context**  
I thank the Commission for this discussion, which is extremely useful, and for the sensitivity shown regarding the data protection implications of a topic such as youth addictions, which is so relevant and broad that it intersects with a multitude of competencies.  
Precisely because it is extremely vast, the topic will be addressed with regard to the aspects most contiguous to the Authority's attributions and, therefore, to the issue (to some extent subject to measures by the Authority) of internet addiction and pathological use (internet addiction disorder).  
Moreover, in light of the most recent statistical surveys, this is a phenomenon that is on a non-negligible rise among adolescents, particularly in the age range of thirteen to nineteen years. It has also, especially during the lockdown, experienced an additional manifestation, albeit not entirely overlapping, with the phenomenon of hikikomori, an expression of virtual hyperconnection that leads to disconnection from real relationships.  
Like any addiction, internet addiction has increasingly detrimental implications, especially when it concerns individuals whose personality is still developing, as in the case of minors, and, as such, is more susceptible to being significantly impacted, with effects that may be, if not permanent, at least more lasting.  
In the case of young people, particularly during adolescence, it is much more difficult to distinguish pathological use of the web from physiological use. This is because the internet today represents, for our youth, not just one of the many dimensions in which to engage in various activities, but the horizon along which their lives unfold, woven with a fabric of virtual friendships that often remain such, played out on the telematic replication of real actions, crystallized in mosaics of photos entrusted to the discretion of others, and too often ending up on social media with effects that can be fatal.  
It is precisely this constant intersection, almost to the point of overlap, of the real and virtual dimensions that makes the boundary between correct use of the internet and destructive and self-destructive dependence on it tenuous. If, in fact, everything becomes the subject of online projection, the entire life risks being absorbed by the internet and reduced to a telematic representation of itself, with the consequent unlimited expansion of internet use, which makes it exceedingly difficult to identify the threshold of the pathological.  
For this reason, it is all the more relevant to ensure that the experience of the online world is not lived in solitude by the youngest, but with the vigilant and far-sighted guidance of adults, and by adolescents, with the necessary awareness of the implications that each of their clicks can have on their own lives and those of others.  
Data protection regulation represents, in this perspective, a crucial aid to ensure that, on the one hand, minors under fourteen do not access the internet in total solitude (as, below that age, parental consent is required for the processing of children's data) and, on the other hand, those over fourteen experience this (all-encompassing as few others) with a minimum baggage of information on the correct use of new technologies and the risks associated with their misuse. One need only consider how detrimental (and potentially fatal) contact with content or individuals may be for which the young person does not possess the necessary skills and psychological autonomy to manage, or involvement in self-harming or even suicidal "challenges." It is significant that these, with increasing and dramatic frequency, spread online, exploiting the allure of what the latest Freud termed the "death drive."  
On one hand, therefore, the internet exposes minors to the concrete risk of suffering from criminal behaviors unfortunately widespread on the web (also due to the apparent anonymity it offers), such as child pornography, revenge porn, cyberbullying, sextortion, incitement to suicide, grooming. These are just some of the forms that violence, discrimination, and various abuses take online, directed against the most vulnerable subject (due to a lack of awareness and a personality still in formation) who can access digital space: the minor, as emphasized by the Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe in its declaration of April 28, 2021.  
Also as a result of the digitization of life induced by the pandemic, in 2020 there was an increase of about 132% compared to 2019 in cases handled by the National Center for the Fight Against Child Pornography and a 77% increase in cases of minor victimization due to grooming, cyberbullying, digital identity theft, and sextortion. 68% of adolescents reported having witnessed cases of cyberbullying in 2020 (Terres des Hommes). These are alarming data that cannot but demand a collective assumption of responsibility regarding subjects such as minors, whose vulnerabilities can make them the elective victims of the distortions of the web.  
On the other hand, access to the internet not supported by the perspective and skills of adults—along with the limits that must be imposed on the quantity and manner of internet use—risks inducing, due to the inherent attractive capacity of the internet, forms of addiction from which it is sometimes even more difficult to emancipate oneself due to the substantial impossibility, today, of entirely prescinding from recourse to the internet. The identification and management of limits are, for what Massimo Recalcati defines as “the new melancholies,” even more difficult.

3. The Role of Parental Guidance
In light of the aforementioned issues, the role of parental guidance becomes crucial. Parents must be equipped with the knowledge and tools necessary to monitor their children's online activities effectively. This includes understanding the platforms their children use, the types of content they are exposed to, and the potential risks associated with online interactions. 

4. Educational Initiatives
Moreover, educational initiatives aimed at both parents and children are essential. Schools and communities should promote digital literacy programs that teach children how to navigate the internet safely, recognize harmful content, and understand the importance of privacy and data protection. Such initiatives can empower minors to make informed decisions and foster a healthier relationship with technology.

5. Conclusion
In conclusion, while the digital landscape presents significant challenges for minors, it also offers opportunities for growth and learning. By implementing robust age verification systems, encouraging parental involvement, and promoting digital education, society can create a safer online environment for children. The responsibility lies not only with the platforms but also with parents, educators, and policymakers to ensure that minors can engage with the digital world in a way that is both safe and enriching.
The objective pursued by the Guarantor with the enforcement action promoted, also at the European level, against TikTok, with the prohibitory and prescriptive measure of January 22 of this year and the acceptance—subject to constant monitoring—of the platform's commitment to adopt measures deemed significant by the Authority. In this way, a considerable cooperation from the operator has been achieved in fulfilling the obligations imposed on them, precisely to prevent the improper use of the network by minors, who lack the necessary support from adults.

3. Digital Pedagogy and Responsibility  
Naturally, age verification is a necessary condition, but not sufficient to make the web a safe environment, at least not inhospitable and less “addicting” for minors.  
To achieve this objective, a real digital pedagogy must be promoted, and effective responsibility for the illegal content disseminated must be established (generally, and paradoxically, those that are most likely to generate addiction).  
The first aspect (that of digital pedagogy) is, in fact, crucial in providing adolescents, who access the web independently and without mediation, with the necessary resources to govern its use physiologically, to prevent it from turning into pathology. It is therefore essential that young people fully understand the implications of every “virtual” action they take, the possible impact on recipients, but also the ways in which the digital ecosystem is fed, the “dictatorship” of likes that risks being experienced by many adolescents as a measure of their self-worth, often generating crises of self-esteem, intolerance, and conformity. It is necessary for young people to experience the limits, delineating the boundary between the correct use of the network and pathological dependence, almost to the point of slavery to the telematic means, allowing life to take on, as Massimo Recalcati observes, “forms through desire.”  
The second aspect involves the more general theme of the responsibility of platforms for the illegal conduct carried out by users through their spaces. One, perhaps the most paradoxical form of web addiction concerns the consumption of illegal, violent content, even incitements to suicide. Thus, the theme of internet addiction disorder can also be analyzed through the lens of the operator's responsibility for the content conveyed through the platform. As is known, to date, providers—subject to the prohibition of preventive and generalized monitoring of the content uploaded online—are not liable for the content (albeit illegal) uploaded to the network by users, except in cases of inaction regarding a request for removal from the competent judicial or administrative authority or, in any case, for active hosting, where they are aware of the illegal nature of the disseminated content. This is a regulatory model that does not fully manage to counterbalance the growing private power of platforms with adequate forms of responsibility for them.  
However, it is precisely on this balance that the Digital Services Act presented by the EU Commission on December 15 intervenes, with the provision of obligations, especially of a proactive nature, imposed on platforms, differently modulated based on the number of active users, in the spirit of their preventive accountability. Reaffirming—and indeed strengthening with a new Good Samaritan Clause—the exemption from secondary liability of operators concerning the illegal acts committed by users on their platforms and the necessary prohibition of general and preventive monitoring of content, the DSA compensates for this safe harbor with regulatory obligations aimed at minimizing the risk of violations or, in any case, containing their harmful effects. To this end, internal procedures for deciding on requests for the removal of illegal content or content contrary to company policies are imposed, with obligations for justification and appeal of the choices made, as well as the referral of disputes to ADR bodies with adequate independence requirements.  
In this sense, the path taken by jurisprudence towards greater accountability of the operator (consider the figure of active hosting) is “positivized,” aimed at preventing the network, with the force of viral sharing and the ubiquity of its services, from becoming a sounding board for the most diverse violations of individual rights.  
The responsibility of the operator is also addressed—specifically regarding the contrast to the dissemination of content inciting suicide—by the bill AS 2086, Pillon, containing Amendments to the Penal Code on incitement to violence, self-harm, and suicide, currently under examination by the Justice Committee of the Senate in the drafting phase.  
The bill, in amending Article 414 of the Penal Code, introduces a special case of incitement or apology aimed at the commission of acts of violence or self-harm by minors, aggravated in the event of the occurrence of the event. Furthermore, an additional case of incitement to suicide is introduced, aggravated by the use of computer or telematic tools. A specific case of complicity is also outlined, relating to anyone who contributes in any way, intentionally or negligently, to the dissemination “of incitements or excitements or aids” qualified as illegal.  
In this context, the regulation of the “primary” responsibility of platforms is inserted, stemming from the failure to exercise functional control to prevent the dissemination of content inciting violence, self-harm, or suicide. To this end, an Article 57-ter is introduced into the Penal Code, replicating, albeit in a very different context, the case.
of liability for the lack of control by the newspaper director against the "responsible party for the IT or telematic system who fails to exercise the necessary control over the content of the system to prevent the commission of crimes" of incitement and apology introduced. The provision aims, rightly, to strengthen the responsibility of platforms for potentially harmful content disseminated, while not regulating the limits that the controls performed by the operators must observe, so as not to incur the prohibition of preventive surveillance of content, which is a cornerstone principle of European law in this area. In this regard, it would therefore be appropriate to introduce specific limits on the controls carried out by the operators, in order not to legitimize abuses that could also result in violations of users' privacy.

Among the proposed amendments, worthy of consideration is 3.1, which essentially transposes, in the area of content inciting suicide, the remedial protection model entrusted to the authority's competence regarding cyberbullying by Law 71 of 2017, with the ability to request (also by minors over fourteen) from the operator or the data controller (and in case of inaction, from the authority) the blocking, removal, or disabling of illegal content. The excellent results being achieved by the implementation of the corresponding provision regarding cyberbullying strongly support this amendment proposal, which would allow for the blocking of the deleterious mechanism of viral sharing of content inciting suicide, limiting its circulation and ultimately removing it from the web within a timeframe appropriate to the rapid pace of the internet.

This amendment proposal, as well as the Digital Services Act, represents certainly desirable innovations that, if combined with greater responsibility from parents in the digital education of their children and in discreet but constant supervision of their online actions, could significantly improve the quality of the web. In this way, it could also contribute, to a significant extent, to combating the causes of internet addiction, promoting a physiological and even beneficial use, renewing that promise, too often betrayed, of the internet as a space for the promotion of rights, freedom, and equality.
