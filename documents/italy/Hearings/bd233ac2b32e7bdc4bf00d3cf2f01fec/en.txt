**Memorandum of the Guarantor for the Protection of Personal Data - COM 2021(206) Proposal for a Regulation (EU) on Artificial Intelligence**  
Chamber of Deputies - Joint Commissions IX and X  
(March 9, 2022)  

1. **General Lines**  
I thank the Commissions for this opportunity for discussion, which is underpinned, first and foremost, by the awareness of the profound interrelationship between AI and data protection. These are, indeed, both cross-cutting matters, certainly, but united by representing the current and future challenge posed by technology to law and its ability to regulate even what appears, in its incessant evolution, more resistant to norms. We will examine, in more detail, the various points of contact between the two disciplines, as well as between the two subjects, but it is crucial to start from an essential observation: the only legislation currently in force regarding AI is that of data protection. It will continue, moreover, to regulate the core of AI (the processing of personal data functional to automated decision-making processes) in general terms and, in particular, in the field of police and justice, even following the approval of the AI regulation, which does not specifically extend to this area. Moreover, the point of unavoidable incidence of the two subjects is not marginal: the processing of data (particularly personal data) is functional, in this case, to feeding AI systems for their machine learning. It is evident, therefore, how errors or inaccuracies in the processing of data that feed the machine (both in the "training" phase and in the execution phase) reflect in corresponding distortions of the algorithmic process.  
Not by chance, the draft regulation is based (also) on Article 16 of the TFEU, as an appropriate legal basis insofar as the protection of personal data represents one of the essential components of the proposed regulation.  
This profound interrelationship between data protection and AI must be taken into account, both in the examination of the proposal (as emphasized by the joint opinion of the EDPS-EDPB 5/2021), and in the internal implementation, in order to avoid antinomies and make the regulation of the subject overall more organic and effective; truly "future-proof."  
In this regard, it must first be considered that - with Articles 22 of the GDPR and 11 of Directive 2016/680, which constitutes the corresponding provision for the police and criminal justice sector - Europe, already in 2016, introduced a first, essential legal statute for AI. In particular, regarding automated decision-making (which constitutes a decisive aspect of AI), the data protection regulation has established the right to explanation, to human review of the automated decision, and the prohibition of discrimination. The latter has, moreover, been significantly strengthened in the implementation of Directive 680, in relation to criminal justice and police, precisely to better counter the risk of over-policing based on subjective characteristics susceptible to discrimination, such as ethnicity. The principles enshrined in privacy regulation assume a decisive value in the regulation of algorithmic processes, to the point that they have already allowed, for example, administrative jurisprudence to find the regulation of certain specific cases.  
The draft regulation underlies important choices, both in method and in the merits of the regulation. On one hand, indeed, the very choice in favor of regulation is crucial, in a context where the frequent tendency towards deregulation ends up delegating to the law of the market the definition of the perimeter of rights and freedoms. This is a choice that characterizes the entire European digital policy, from the GDPR to the Data Governance, Digital Services, and Digital Markets Acts, united by the option in favor of regulatory sources, which is increasingly consolidating as the typical form of European digital regulation. It expresses, in fact, that unitary aspiration ("one continent, one law") underlying policies on which the Union invests, above all, its identity, just as it is doing around the relationship between law and technology, to impart to innovation a human-centered and, moreover, personalist governance (the principle of human oversight and measures aimed at countering cognitive distortions of algorithms are, in this sense, significant).  
In terms of substance, this need is expressed in particularly relevant solutions, many of which are borrowed from data protection regulation, which has represented in a certain sense the vanguard in the regulation of the digital. From it, in particular, derives the main structural framework: the risk-based approach (modulated according to an ascending severity pyramid), with the corresponding impact assessment, although the concept of "risk to fundamental rights" should be aligned with that of the GDPR; the duties of transparency towards users (which should, however, be extended to the entire lifecycle of AI); the criterion of the localization of the recipients of the productive offer as a parameter for the territorial application of the regulation; certifications and codes of conduct in a co-regulatory function; the modulation of the sanctioning treatment according to turnover, so as to exert greater deterrence; the mandatory communication of potentially harmful "incidents"; the coordination among national authorities within the European AI Committee, in which the European Data Protection Supervisor also participates, who is also the recipient of specific competencies in the matter concerning the activities carried out by European bodies and institutions, although with a significant deviation from the model of Regulation (EU) 2016/679, which will be discussed.

**1. Risk Levels of AI Applications**

The risk levels of AI applications recall the parameters set forth by data protection legislation. In particular, the use of AI systems related to subliminal techniques capable of conditioning the behavior of others or exploiting the vulnerabilities of social groups is prohibited; as well as social scoring systems based on monitoring individual behaviors or real-time biometric identification for law enforcement purposes, except in cases of strict necessity for imperative public needs specifically stated, with prior authorization (concurrent or subsequent only in cases of urgency) granted by a judicial authority or an independent administrative authority of the involved Member State (art. 5, p.3). 

This latter prohibition, in particular, formalizes a now consolidated practice of the Data Protection Authorities and the Guarantor in particular, which has repeatedly excluded the admissibility of biometric detection systems for public safety purposes (and, a fortiori, for mere facilitative purposes in the private sector), in the absence of regulatory provisions accompanied by appropriate guarantees. This approach has, moreover, crystallized into law during the conversion of Legislative Decree 139 of 2021, through a general moratorium on the use of facial recognition systems except for public safety or justice purposes, but in the presence of appropriate regulatory provisions and with a favorable opinion from the Guarantor (except for processing carried out by the public administration) during the preventive consultation (art. 9, c.9, Legislative Decree 139 of 2021, converted, with amendments, by Law 205 of 2021).

**2. Prospects for Intervention**

The many commendable (and indeed, meritorious) proposals in the draft regulation do not exclude, however, some solutions that could be improved (already subject to remarks by the EDPS and the EDPB), which it is hoped can be enhanced during the examination of the act by the European legislator. Limiting myself in this context to indicating only the main points, first of all in relation to the compliance obligations of AI systems, I note that they must refer to the observance of European legislation as a whole (thus also including data protection legislation, including the principles of minimization and privacy by design), which should represent a necessary requirement for the very authorization for market entry in Europe.

Furthermore, a more precise coordination of the proposed certification system with that of data protection legislation should be guaranteed, involving the relevant Authorities in drafting harmonized rules and clarifying whether the protection of personal data should be considered one of the "supplementary requirements" within the codes of conduct. More generally, an express and general safeguard clause in favor of data protection legislation should also be included in the text, as well as in the recitals, to avoid possible antinomies between the two regulatory frameworks and ensure coherent application.

Regarding the prohibitions, a more protective approach suggests banning any AI system functional to social scoring, used in any context, or to the deduction of emotions, as well as those aimed at categorizing individuals into groups based on biometric data, ethnicity, gender, political or sexual orientation, or based on other discriminatory reasons as per Article 21 of the Charter of Nice.

In terms of governance, it should first be noted that, despite the impact on fundamental rights of much of the activity subject to regulation (of a cross-cutting nature affecting various sectors), the list of entities indicated by the draft regulation as holders of supervisory competencies ("notification authorities" and "market surveillance authorities" reporting to a "national control authority") is not limited to independent administrative authorities (without prejudice to what has already been observed in relation to art. 5, p.3), although it requires that their objectivity and impartiality [in the exercise] of their respective tasks and activities be safeguarded.

This is a significant aspect, as it leads to a substantial weakening of the guarantees of fundamental rights and, in particular, of the right to the protection of personal data, whose effective protection is based precisely (pursuant to Articles 8 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union and 16 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union) on the necessary supervision of independent Authorities.

Therefore, greater independence should be recognized for the European Committee for Artificial Intelligence—consequently resizing the role of the Commission—as well as the power to act ex officio, on its own initiative. Similarly, in order to ensure the effective respect of the guarantees introduced by the regulation, specific independence requirements in favor of the supervisory authorities should be expressly established, which are also subject to reporting obligations to the Commission that are not very compatible with a status of real independence.

Overall, therefore, the entire configuration of governance, both at the European level and at the internal level of AI, should be characterized by greater and effective independence, but also by the necessary involvement, at least in their areas of competence, of Data Protection Authorities.

It is indeed evident that, to the extent that the regulation of AI impacts the conditions and guarantees of personal data processing, the lack of involvement of Data Protection Authorities in this context would result in a subtraction of (or, at least, a limitation of) their competencies, contrary to the principle set forth in Article 8, p.3, of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union. Given, then, the close interrelation between AI and privacy, the competence already acquired by the supervisory authorities regarding automated decision-making processes and the characteristics of the AI systems...
pending that characterize its statute, it would be useful to consider the solution proposed by the EDPS and the EDPB, aimed at suggesting the identification, within the Data Protection Authorities, of the supervisory authorities for AI. As already noted by the EDPS and the EDPB in the aforementioned joint opinion, indeed, the “designation of data protection authorities as national supervisory authorities would ensure a more harmonized regulatory approach and contribute to the adoption of a consistent interpretation of the provisions regarding data processing as well as to avoid contradictions in their application across different Member States.” This solution would also guarantee a significant simplification for users, who would have to refer to a single authority for AI systems operating on personal data, a greater consistency of the overall regulation, as well as the extension of the guarantees statute (also in terms of independence) of the Data Protection Authorities to the AI sector. This choice would also address the concerns expressed by the Government regarding the administrative and financial burdens associated with the implementation of the regulation, as well as the excessively long implementation times, attributable to the complexity of the governance mechanism, which “would shift a series of responsibilities and competences to the national authorities that are currently difficult to identify in the Member States” (as stated in the report submitted to Parliament in implementation of Law No. 234 of 2012). 

The identification of the Guarantor as the supervisory authority for the purposes of this regulation would indeed allow for a timely adjustment to the obligations therein, even in the absence of significant allocations, as it could draw on the experience already gained regarding that crucial aspect of AI represented by automated decision-making processes. The Data Protection Authorities (and the Italian Guarantor, of course, no less) already possess the necessary competence and, together, independence to ensure a fully coherent implementation of the regulation and a forward-looking application of its provisions. The Guarantor – like all other Data Protection Authorities of the Member States – could indeed guarantee both of these objectives, in a perspective of simplifying administrative burdens (by unifying in a single Authority the obligations provided for by the two regulations) and of overall consistency in the application of European legislation in this area. 

I can therefore only suggest to the Committees a reflection (in particular) on this aspect, aware of how the synergy between the two regulations – and, therefore, their application by a single Authority – is decisive for the effectiveness of the rights and guarantees they establish, with significant foresight.
