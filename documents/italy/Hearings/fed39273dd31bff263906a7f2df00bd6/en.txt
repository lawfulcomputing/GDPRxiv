3. The role of data protection in the workplace
The protection of personal data, especially in the context of employment, plays a crucial role in maintaining a balance between the rights of workers and the legitimate interests of employers. It is essential to ensure that any data processing activities carried out by employers are not only lawful but also fair and transparent. This includes providing clear information to employees about the types of data being collected, the purposes of such collection, and the measures in place to protect their privacy.

4. Conclusion
In conclusion, the ongoing pandemic has highlighted the importance of data protection in the workplace, particularly in the context of public health measures. It is vital for the legislative framework to adapt to these new challenges while safeguarding the fundamental rights of workers. The balance between public health interests and individual privacy rights must be carefully managed to prevent potential abuses and ensure that workers are not subjected to undue surveillance or coercion. The role of the Parliament in overseeing these developments is crucial to uphold democratic principles and protect the rights of all individuals in the workforce.

It is important to emphasize the relevance, even in the current emergency context, of the distinction of duties—and thus, by extension, of informational power—between the employer and the competent physician, as established by labor law.  
The employer fulfills their obligations to ensure the safety of workers, without having direct knowledge of their health conditions but only possessing the foundational elements for the assessment of fitness for specific tasks. Only the latter, as a healthcare professional, is responsible for evaluating the necessity of subjecting workers to particular diagnostic tests, if deemed useful, particularly for containing infections, as provided for in the aforementioned Protocol between the Government and Social Partners.  
The assessments must, in any case, be conducted by the competent physician or other healthcare personnel and may include the proposal of serological tests, the results of which must, however, remain confidential to the physician. The latter is also the only subject authorized to suggest them as specific examinations to be arranged, where appropriate, based on objective epidemiological parameters.  
This also applies to healthcare personnel, whose specific professional risks must be taken into account by the competent physician for this purpose and even more so.  
The protocol between the Government and social partners does allow for the adoption of "more incisive" measures for preventing infections than those indicated, following union consultation, but from a data protection perspective, they can only be accepted in the presence, among other things, of an adequate legal basis. It is important to carry out a privacy impact assessment (and if necessary, also to consult the Authority in advance) whenever the proposed processing presents a high risk (pertaining to "special" data, being carried out on a large scale, using innovative technologies).  
A preventive measure that certainly cannot be imposed on workers (whether employees or candidates) is contact tracing.  
Based on the indications provided by the bodies of the Council of Europe and the European Union, in fact, the specific provision contained in AS 1786 has expressly established the exclusively voluntary nature of adherence to the contact tracing system, the refusal of which must—notably—result in no detrimental consequences for the individual concerned.  
The particular breadth of the expression used demonstrates that the choice regarding adherence to the system must not be conditioned in any way, even indirectly, by the fear of possible unfavorable implications, which, if presented (in both public and private law relationships), would render the data collection illegitimate, making the data unusable for this reason.  
The exercise of the right to informational self-determination cannot, in short, lead to any type of discrimination, especially in the workplace.  
The necessary voluntariness of tracing, along with other guarantees provided by law in the area of data protection, constitutes one of the essential prerequisites for citizens' trust in this system. It must be able to trace—not individuals but only the reflection of their epidemiologically relevant activity: close and prolonged contacts that could lead to contagion.  
And trust is essential to ensure both a free and widespread adherence within the population, to be effective for diagnostic purposes, thus promoting voluntary choices made in the spirit of responsibility and solidarity.  
3. New Forms (and Vulnerabilities) of Work  
The social distancing imposed for the purpose of containing the epidemic has ended up reshaping the times and spaces of life marked by established customs, exponentially accelerating the transition to digital that would otherwise have been much slower and more considered.  
Technology has undoubtedly come to our aid by bridging physical distances through connection and recreating, in the digital space, places for discussion and dialogue, which data protection can make environments that are not anomic but respectful of individuals in their specific position as workers, students, or teachers, thanks to the light yet comprehensive regulation it can offer.  
The online transposition of nearly all our activities is, in fact, not a neutral process, but entails, if not assisted by adequate guarantees, exposure to unexpected vulnerabilities in terms not only of cybersecurity but also of subjection to interferences and controls that are often more insidious, because less perceptible, than traditional ones.  
In this case, the work context is particularly significant. The widespread recourse to smart working—generally necessitated and improvised, by both employers and workers—has catapulted a significant portion of the population into a dimension of which the implications are not always fully understood and which must be protected from any misuse.  
The intensive use of new technologies to render work performance cannot, therefore, represent an opportunity for the systematic and ubiquitous monitoring of the worker, but must occur in full respect of the guarantees established by the Statute to protect self-determination, which presupposes, above all, adequate training and information for the worker regarding the processing to which their data will be subject.  
In particular, the purpose-bound nature of the work performance must be understood rigorously, which, concerning controls through tools used precisely to render the performance, legitimizes the exemption from the procedure.
**Concertative or Authorizing Agreement (Art. 4, c.2, l.300)**

It would not be legitimate, for example, to provide a computer for smart working that is equipped with functionalities that allow the employer to exercise systematic and pervasive monitoring of the activities performed by the employee through this device. 

It must also be ensured – in a manner more explicit than already provided – that right to disconnection, without which there is a risk of nullifying the necessary distinction between private life and work activities, thus undermining some of the oldest achievements attained in traditional work.

To ensure that new technologies represent a factor of progress (and not regression) in social terms, enhancing rather than compressing the freedoms established in the labor field, it is essential to guarantee their sustainability from a democratic perspective and compliance with certain indispensable principles.

The common denominator of these guarantees must be identified in the right to data protection: a necessary prerequisite for that free self-determination of the worker which has represented, as mentioned, one of the most important achievements of labor law.

In a context such as the current one, characterized by both emergency and a reliance on digital means, the self-determination of the worker risks being the first freedom violated, even unintentionally. The right to data protection allows us to prevent this: let us therefore enhance it, in times of emergency and beyond.
