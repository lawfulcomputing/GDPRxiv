**Hearing of the Guarantor for the Protection of Personal Data on the European Delegation Bill 2021**  
Senate of the Republic - 14th Committee on European Union Policies  
(March 8, 2022)  
- THE VIDEO OF THE HEARING

First of all, I would like to thank the Committee for involving the Guarantor in a legislative procedure, such as the one under examination, which is of significant interest to the Authority.  
The bill, as such, presents some provisions of particular interest: specifically, Articles 13 and 14 containing two legislative delegations, respectively for the transposition of the directive on whistleblowing and for the adaptation of the internal legal system to Regulation 2019/816 on the ECRIS-TCN system.  
On the other hand, among the amendments presented, Amendment 13.01 deserves attention, which among the guiding criteria for the exercise of the delegation for the adaptation of national legislation to Regulation (EU, Euratom) 2020/2092, provides for the assignment to the Guarantor of the role of independent national institution for the protection and promotion of human rights, pursuant to United Nations General Assembly Resolution No. 48/134, of December 20, 1993. The relevance of this latter provision is such that it deserves a broader space for reflection, immediately after a brief mention of the provisions of the bill of interest to the Authority.  
With reference to Article 13, it should first be noted how Directive (EU) 2019/1937, to which the norm delegates the Government for transposition, significantly impacts the regulatory framework currently in force in this area, both subjectively and objectively. In the areas covered by the directive, in fact, the protection of whistleblowers does not differentiate between the public and private sectors, unlike what is provided for by Law 179 of 2017. Furthermore, the subjective scope of application is broader than that contemplated by domestic legislation, which should therefore be revised in terms of the category of protected informants.  
Quite delicate, then, is the "public disclosure" allowed by the directive, under certain conditions, by the whistleblower, which is not provided for by domestic legislation. It generally imposes a broad prohibition on revealing the identity of the whistleblower, not only in disciplinary proceedings but also in criminal and accounting proceedings. In criminal proceedings, the confidentiality of identity is protected within the limits of the secrecy of investigative acts (Article 329 of the Criminal Procedure Code). In accounting proceedings, identity cannot be revealed until the end of the investigative phase. In disciplinary proceedings, the identity of the whistleblower cannot be revealed without their consent (provided that the disciplinary charge is based on elements different from those on which the report is based). However, if the disciplinary charge is based (even partially) on the report, the identity may be revealed with the consent of the whistleblower, under penalty of the report being deemed unusable for the purposes of the disciplinary proceeding. The confidentiality of the report implies its exclusion from procedural access pursuant to Law 241 of 1990. However, this enhanced protection of the whistleblower's confidentiality is counterbalanced by the inadmissibility of anonymous reports.  
In exercising the delegation for the transposition of the directive, an appropriate balance must be sought between the need for confidentiality of the report—functional to the protection of the whistleblower—the necessity of ascertaining unlawful acts, and the right to defense and contradiction of the reported party. The protection of personal data is, of course, a determining factor for the balance between these interests, and therefore it is appropriate to involve the Guarantor during the exercise of the delegation. It should be noted, in this regard, that the Authority has been constantly and fruitfully involved in the work for drafting the legislative decree for the transposition of the directive, in the exercise of the delegation contained in the previous delegation law.  
The delegation, as is known, has not been exercised in the terms and therefore it has been necessary to provide for a new one, which essentially resumes what is provided for in Article 23 of Law No. 53 of 2021 (European delegation law 2019-2020).  
In exercising this latter delegation, it is therefore desirable that the Guarantor be involved again and that, of course, the indications provided, especially regarding the overall coherence of the proposed provisions with data protection legislation, the security guarantees to be granted to the information flows concerning the report to prevent even negligent leaks, as well as the need for clarity in the application parameters of the legislation, starting from the definition of the possible subject matter of the report, are also taken into account at this stage. It is clear, in fact, that in the absence of a sufficiently clear delimitation of the behaviors susceptible to reporting, the related processing of personal data risks being, at least in certain cases, of dubious legitimacy.  
In this regard, moreover, a greater definitional effort is probably desirable already in relation to the guiding criteria for the exercise of the delegation. In letter d), in fact, reference is made to the concept of "improper behaviors" as a possible subject of the report, with a choice that is probably too broad from a semantic perspective and thus leaves an excessive margin of discretion not only during the exercise of the delegation but also subsequently, in the application phase. The delimitation made in the norm, with reference to those improper behaviors "that compromise the impartial care of the public interest or the regular organization and management of the entity," also does not seem entirely sufficient to exclude possible interpretative doubts and sufficiently circumscribe the category of violations.
**to report.**  
Particular attention, in drafting the legislative decree, must be paid to the issue of identifying the competent Authority to receive the so-called "external reports" concerning violations of the regulations on personal data protection (included among those covered by Directive (EU) 2019/1937). The understandable need to centralize the management of various reports within a single Authority must, in fact, be reconciled with the attribution to the Guarantor (conceived by EU Regulation 2016/679 and Legislative Decree 196/03 as exclusive) of the function of overseeing the application of data protection regulations.  
Article 14, on the other hand, delegates the Government to adapt the internal legal framework to Regulation 2019/816, aimed at establishing a centralized system to identify Member States holding information on convictions against citizens of third countries and stateless persons (ECRIS-TCN). Regarding the draft regulation, the European Data Protection Supervisor, in Opinion 11/2017, emphasized—beyond the opportunity for a general assessment of the proportionality and necessity of establishing a dedicated centralized information system—the need for the residual collection of fingerprints from third-country nationals, to be used only where less invasive identification methods are not feasible, as well as further reflection on the actual necessity of acquiring facial images.  
In this regard, the margins of discretion for the domestic legislator are significantly reduced due to the regulatory nature of the European source. However, even within this limited scope, it will be necessary to ensure the most appropriate balance between the needs of justice and individual privacy, as appropriately required by the directive criterion referred to in letter b), which should, however, be more correctly reformulated by extending the safeguard clause to (also) internal data protection regulations.  
Naturally, in exercising the delegation, the Guarantor will be pleased to offer its contribution for the better implementation of that directive criterion.  
As for the amendments presented, as previously mentioned, particular importance is given to 13.01, which in paragraph 4 includes, among the delegation principles, the attribution to the Guarantor—based on AS 2109—of the role of an independent national institution for the protection and promotion of human rights, pursuant to United Nations General Assembly Resolution No. 48/134 of December 20, 1993, in order to promote and protect the fundamental rights of individuals recognized by the Constitution and the international conventions to which Italy is a party. To this end, the functions and powers of the Guarantor are consequently extended to the field of human rights as a whole, and the organizational structure undergoes a significant increase. The regulations regarding the requirements for the members of the Board are also recalibrated based on the different competencies of the Authority.  
On this last aspect, in relation to the delegation criterion referred to in letter b) of paragraph 4, it might be possible to clarify (possibly also in the decree) that the competence (also) in the field of human rights must be present within the Board of the Guarantor as a whole, or, in any case, it could be considered to remove the reference to the proven and multi-year nature of such a requirement. It could otherwise be difficult to identify individual members who can simultaneously boast such specific competencies as those in the field of human rights and specialized competencies such as those in data protection.  
In order to ensure that the Board as a whole possesses adequate competencies for the functions assigned to it, it might indeed be preferable to have an overall assessment rather than an individual one of the ownership of the requirements or, in any case, a lesser formalization.  
It would also be desirable to clarify that the reform applies from the election of the new Board of the Guarantor, as it is incompatible with European regulations— as established by the CJEU in the judgment of April 8, 2014, in case C 288/12—to prematurely end the mandates of the supervisory Authorities.  
Now, beyond these aspects, it should be emphasized how the amendment proposes a solution that is entirely commendable, both in method and in substance. From the first perspective, in fact, this would seize the opportunity of the European delegation law to comply with the international obligation undertaken by our Country since UN Resolution No. 48/134 of 1993, which has not yet been fully observed, despite numerous recommendations highlighting the absence, in our Country, of an independent national institution ad hoc for human rights. As reported in the report on bill 2109, in 2019 alone, as many as 41 recommendations out of 306 addressed this issue.  
The solution envisioned by the amendment would thus allow overcoming the stalemate that has characterized the legislative process in this area for various legislatures, which seems to have also become stuck (in the Chamber).  
From the substantive perspective, moreover, the solution proposed by the amendment is highly commendable because it is forward-looking and adheres to the reality of the right to data protection today, which is increasingly characterized as not only a fundamental right in itself but also a prerequisite for the exercise of every other right and freedom.  
The ontologically cross-cutting nature of the right to data protection (which, as a fundamental right under Article 8 of the ECHR, is not merely a citizenship right) and consequently also of the activities of the Guarantor is, in fact, the true strength of this solution. **Translation of the Legal Document Text:**

It is difficult to imagine a more suitable authority than the Guarantor for assigning competence in the field of human rights to an already established independent Authority, due to the cross-cutting nature of its areas of intervention, the variety of contexts considered, and the constitutional guarantees of independence.

From the first perspective, it must be considered that the Guarantor is the Authority responsible for protecting the individual sphere from undue interference by unfair commercial activities, invasive online profiling, or digital tracking carried out by large platforms. It is the body to which one can request protection against the unscrupulous use of microtargeting, aimed at influencing behaviors and choices, not only in consumption (as demonstrated by the Cambridge Analytica case).

The Guarantor is the Authority tasked with ensuring that the legitimate exercise of the right to (and to) information does not degenerate into media lynching or peeping through the keyhole, thereby violating individual dignity and privacy. It is the Authority before which anyone can exercise their right to be forgotten concerning the risk of a distorted use of the internet and its "eternal memory."

Today, minors (if over fourteen, even independently) can turn to the Guarantor for protection against violations of their dignity caused by cyberbullying, revenge porn, and hate speech. Moreover, a bill has been approved by this branch of Parliament that grants the Guarantor the competence to decide on requests presented by minors, if over fourteen, for the removal of content inciting suicide.

The Guarantor faces daily the danger that the digital transformation of the economy degenerates into the monetization of freedom, with the risk of a true re-feudalization of social relations. It is the Authority called upon to ensure that the use of AI occurs with the necessary guarantees to prevent discriminatory outcomes, amplifying rather than mitigating (as it should) the preconceptions and prejudices from which it is more difficult to liberate human thought. The Guarantor has also confronted the "digital caporalato" that an uncontrolled drift of the gig economy risks leading to, anticipating the governance solution proposed today by the draft EU directive for improving working conditions in work through digital platforms (COM(2021) 762 def).

The Authority has had to evaluate multiple times the legitimacy of resorting to investigative techniques or even to particularly invasive public safety measures such as those based on facial recognition, providing guidance for a correct balance between individual dignity and freedom and the needs for crime prevention. The Guarantor constantly faces the challenges that algocracy poses—if devoid of a human-centered guidance—to fundamental rights and freedoms, with the risk that algorithms (which could well reduce inequalities and promote equality) instead underlie new forms of discrimination.

During the pandemic, the Authority consistently contributed to delineating the most appropriate balance between public health needs and privacy, ensuring that the use of new technologies in health matters (from digital contact tracing to the green pass) promotes rather than violates freedoms and fundamental rights, averting socially regressive solutions.

From this not exhaustive description of the areas of intervention and functions of the Guarantor, it emerges that it is today an Authority dedicated to the protection of individuals, their rights, and their freedoms against vulnerabilities amplified or induced by new technologies. To the extent that freedoms and rights are exercised, today, significantly (if not predominantly) online or, in any case, in forms and ways strongly conditioned by new technologies, a right—such as that to data protection—that allows for a human-centered and democratically sustainable governance of technology, must indeed represent the unavoidable prerequisite for the guarantee of such rights.

What would happen, in fact, to the right to self-determination in matters (not only) of health but also reproductive and existential if confidentiality could not be guaranteed on essential choices such as vaccination, end-of-life decisions, or abortion? And how can pluralism and freedoms in political-electoral matters be guaranteed if the very contents offered by the internet are pre-selected and shaped based on the type of voter attributed to the individual by the algorithm? How can it be ensured that AI does not evoke, even deepening, the preconceptions from which it should liberate us, in the absence of effective control over the data used to train the algorithm (consider the Loomis case)?

These are just some possible examples to account, albeit without a detailed taxonomy, for how the right to data protection today represents the necessary condition for the effectiveness of the guarantee of fundamental rights.

This is why the Guarantor, in protecting the right to personal data protection, inevitably also ends up protecting fundamental rights with an action that operates broadly, due to the cross-cutting nature of the competences entrusted to it. And this is also why the solution proposed by the amendment demonstrates considerable foresight and sensitivity in capturing a trend (such as that of the increasingly close interrelation between data protection and fundamental rights) that will become more pronounced in the near future.

Assigning competence over human rights to the Guarantor means, moreover, identifying a precise model not only of protection but also of independence of the Authority, due to the specific regulatory status that characterizes it. The Guarantor is, in fact, supported by guarantees of independence.

Also from the perspective of guarantees of institutional independence, therefore, the choice to assign the Garante the competence in the field of human rights can only be considered commendable.
