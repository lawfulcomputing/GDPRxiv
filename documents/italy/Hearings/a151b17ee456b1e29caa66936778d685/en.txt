Hearing of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data on the draft law AS 1659 (conversion into law of legislative decree 161/2019, regarding interceptions)  
Commission 2a (Justice) of the Senate of the Republic  
(February 4, 2020)  

I thank the Commission for this opportunity for discussion.  
Examining the issue of interceptions from the perspective of data protection allows for a comprehensive assessment of the impact that this means of evidence gathering has on individuals: consequently modulating the balance between investigative needs, the right to defense, and privacy.  
The necessity for a reconfiguration of this relationship has been emphasized by us several times, particularly in a note sent to the President of the Council in 2015, regarding the draft delegation law on criminal procedure.  
In particular, we represented the need for "a more precise selection of investigative material ensuring, in due respect for the rights of the defense, that entire segments of private life (of the parties but especially of third parties), completely unrelated to the subject of evidence, are not included in the procedural acts."  
We hoped that the principle of proportionality between privacy and investigative means, on which the Court of Justice has based its most forward-looking jurisprudence, would be valued, as well as the indications provided by the European Court of Human Rights, particularly regarding the use of irrelevant interceptions.  
The objective, enshrined in the delegation law, of strengthening the guarantee of confidentiality by anticipating the preliminary hearing, in which to eliminate irrelevant interceptions and omitting, in the minutes, the irrelevant parts, while fully respecting the adversarial principle for (and on) evidence, seemed commendable to us.  
Furthermore, the standard of content sobriety provided in precautionary proceedings, imposing on the public prosecutor an adequate selection of interceptions to be sent in support of the request, excluding irrelevant ones to be archived in a reserved manner with the relevant secrecy regime, appeared positive.  
The measures aimed at limiting the intra-procedural circulation of interceptions exceeding actual investigative needs have thus represented, from our point of view, an important innovation of the 2017 reform, which on this point received a guarantee need shared even by the judiciary itself, as demonstrated by the directives issued by some Prosecutor's Offices in 2016, as well as the good practices indicated by the CSM in July of the same year.  
It is therefore crucial that the decree under conversion responds to this need, while differently modulating the burdens of judicial police and public prosecutor regarding the rules to be observed during transcription and, therefore, transforming what for the judicial police was a prohibition on the transcription of irrelevant data into a duty of oversight for the public prosecutor regarding the absence of such data in the minutes.  
The modification imposes some essential considerations:  
1. First, the omission from the minutes should concern all irrelevant personal data and not just sensitive data contained in defamatory expressions, under penalty of violating the principles of proportionality and minimization expressly applicable to criminal proceedings according to legislative decree 51/2018;  
2. Second, the reclassification of the prohibition on transcription into a duty of oversight for the public prosecutor requires, in order not to nullify the innovative scope of the reform, careful scrutiny by the prosecuting body regarding the actual compliance with this minimization standard.  
3. This modification should also be read in the context of the abandonment of the precise procedural timeline of the preliminary hearing and the related secrecy regime, as established by the 2017 reform.  
4. A similar weakening of confidentiality guarantees arises from the elimination of the burden on the public prosecutor to make a prior selection of only the relevant interceptions for the request for precautionary measures, which would have allowed for at least partial containment of the risk of data exfiltration, particularly prevalent at this stage.  
5. Should such choices be confirmed in conversion, it would therefore be necessary to strengthen the confidentiality guarantees (at least) of the acts not acquired because, in particular, irrelevant or unusable, contained in the specific archive.  
In this regard, the Authority will be able to provide its contribution in the opinion on the ministerial decree governing the criteria for access and consultation of the archive, the subject of which could, however, be extended to the provision of particular security measures for the archive itself.  
Its security and impermeability are, in fact, the essential prerequisite for the confidentiality of the acts stored there.  
Similarly, the separate decree (on which it would be appropriate to hear the Authority) on the telematic deposit of interceptions must provide for protection measures adequate to the particular relevance of these information flows, which must not present any permeability or vulnerability, as we have already emphasized with the provision of July 2013 on security measures in the context of interception activities.  
6. Furthermore, if only for deterrent purposes, it will be necessary to clarify the punitive consequences of the dissemination of the content of unacquired interceptions, now subject to a generic prohibition.  
On one hand, in fact, the secrecy covering the acts contained in the archive would lead one to consider that, in the event of dissemination of such acts and provided that the conditions exist, the crime of revealing secrets could be configured.  
On the other hand, however, the terms of applicability of the administrative offense of arbitrary publication should be clarified, the mild penalty provided being inadequate to express the particular offensiveness of such conduct.  
The occasion
could then be appropriate for a comprehensive review, in accordance with the case law of the European Court of Human Rights (ECHR), of the regulation concerning the unlawful publication of investigative acts.

7. It will then be appropriate to coordinate the provision of the right of the interested parties to request the destruction of unnecessary documentation from the judge, in order to protect confidentiality, with the innovative procedure introduced by Article 14 of Legislative Decree 51/2018. This provision indeed legitimizes “anyone with an interest to request the judge for the rectification, deletion, or limitation of data concerning them, even during the criminal proceedings.” This is a provision with significant potential, which, when combined with the destruction procedure referred to in Article 269, could substantially strengthen the guarantees of confidentiality, especially for third parties whose conversations have been indirectly captured.

8. As for interceptions through spyware, this occasion should be seized to fill the regulatory gaps that the Guarantor had already pointed out in its opinion, as well as in the report addressed to Parliament and the Government. The extraordinary intrusive potential of such tools indeed imposes adequate guarantees to prevent them, from being valuable aids to investigators, from degenerating into means of mass surveillance or, conversely, into factors that exponentially multiply the vulnerabilities of the evidentiary collection, making it extremely permeable if stored on insecure servers or, worse, relocated outside national borders. The necessity for such guarantees seems, moreover, corroborated by news reports (consider the Exodus case) regarding the particular methods of carrying out interceptions through malware by the companies involved. They highlight (unless contradicted by the Prosecutor of Naples, who is conducting the investigations) the risks associated with the use of computer spyware by private parties that employ infiltration techniques lacking the necessary selectivity. This specifically refers to the use, for interception purposes, of software connected to apps, which are not directly injected into the device of the suspect but placed on platforms (such as the Google Play Store) accessible to all. If made available on the market, even if only by mistake in the absence of necessary filters to limit their acquisition by third parties, these spy apps would indeed risk becoming dangerous tools for mass surveillance. Furthermore, the use— which appears to have been made in the cases under investigation—of cloud systems for storing captured data, even in non-European states, is dangerous. The relocation of servers in territories not subject to national jurisdiction constitutes, in fact, a clear breach not only for the protection of the rights of the interested parties but also for the effectiveness and confidentiality of the investigative action itself. The recourse to these two types of systems (software that is not directly injected into the host device, and, on the other hand, storage through cloud systems) should, therefore, be subject to a specific prohibition. In any case, also due to the rapid evolution of the characteristics and functionalities of software available for interception purposes, it would be necessary to prohibit the use of spyware capable of erasing traces of operations performed on the host device. For the correct reconstruction of evidence and the guarantee of the right to defense, it is indeed essential to have software capable of detailing every activity performed on the host system and the data present therein, without altering its content. In this sense, the requirement for “reliability, security, and effectiveness” of software usable for interception purposes could be explicitly stated—this should be detailed by the Ministerial Decree referred to in Article 2, paragraph 3, thus effectively ensuring the completeness of the “chain of custody of digital evidence.” This need is all the more essential concerning investigative operations, such as those under examination, which have a high degree of outsourcing and thus present greater vulnerabilities, being largely entrusted to private parties who must, therefore, be adequately held accountable for the security obligations to be ensured. It would also be appropriate to clarify the consequences (in terms of the inadmissibility of captured content) of using computer programs that do not comply with the security requirements set forth by the Ministerial Decree. It is also desirable to clarify the terms of applicability of the regulation on spyware to preventive interceptions, requiring for these guarantees no less than those applicable to judicial interceptions. Finally, the anticipated transfer of data captured with trojans directly to the reserved archive even before selection (and not, instead, to the Prosecutor's server), in determining access to the archive by the police for investigative purposes, represents an anomaly that contradicts the very nature of such an archive, as a place for the custody of documents that are not relevant to the proceedings and should be excluded from intra-procedural circulation. While maintaining the appropriateness of introducing the aforementioned safeguards, the particular invasiveness of spyware and their propensity to degenerate, even in pathological cases, into tools for illegal control would merit further reflection by Parliament regarding the progressive extension of the application scope of such investigative tools, which should instead remain exceptional. **Imitation of Means of Evidence**

Such invasive means of evidence, therefore exceptional in their application, should only be used in cases where they are truly indispensable for the protection of primary legal goods such as life or safety, with the adoption of measures that limit their impact on third parties.

In excluding the analogical applicability of the regulation of traditional wiretaps to these new investigative tools, the Court emphasizes their intrinsic differences, particularly regarding their invasive capacity and their ability to exercise ubiquitous control over the suspect as well as on third parties close to them.

These considerations are similar to those expressed by our Supreme Court, also in relation to the implications that the difficult predetermination of the development of interceptions, due to the itinerant and ubiquitous nature of the means, has on the reserve of jurisdiction.

The low predictability of the development of interceptions risks, in fact, weakening the guarantee function inherent in the authorization review by the investigating judge, rendering it meaningless.

For this reason, we have repeatedly emphasized the need to compensate for this weakening of the ex ante review with greater detail in the documentation of the operations carried out, in order to at least strengthen the ex post control over the legitimacy of the activities performed.

Control on which the German Constitutional Court rightly insists, demanding, however, a more rigorous respect for the principle of proportionality, in protection of the "general right to the freedom of the citizen against the State."

This must also be, for us, the essential parameter to be observed in the regulation of investigative tools that must guarantee both security and freedom, according to the synergy required by constitutional and supranational legislation.
