**Informal hearing, via videoconference, of the President of the Data Protection Authority on the use of new technologies and the internet to combat the epidemiological emergency from Coronavirus**  
**Commission IX (Transport, Post and Telecommunications) of the Chamber of Deputies**  
**(April 8, 2020)**  

1. **Rights, Derogations, Limits**  
The severe emergency that the country is facing has necessitated the adoption—through regulations of various ranks—of measures limiting many fundamental rights, necessary to hopefully contain the number of infections.  
The protection of personal data—a fundamental "freedom" right enshrined in the Charter of Nice—could not, of course, be an exception, although the limitations adopted so far are generally contained.  
Some derogations from the ordinary data management regime have been provided since the very first ordinances issued a few days after the declaration of the state of emergency, primarily concerning the communication of health data.  
Article 14 of Decree Law 14/2020 has essentially replicated this provision, elevating its source and emphasizing its temporary nature, without, however, currently referring to particularly "innovative" data collections.  
New and more invasive data collections could be based on public health needs that—like "emergency assistance"—constitute autonomous grounds for legality, provided there is a regulatory provision compliant with the principles of necessity, proportionality, adequacy, as well as respect for the essential content of the right.

2. **Epidemiological Maps and Surveillance**  
The hypothesis of collecting data on the location or interaction of mobile devices of individuals who tested positive, with other devices, in order to analyze the epidemiological trend or to reconstruct the chain of infections should be evaluated within this framework.  
First of all, since very different measures can be hypothesized, a criterion of graduality should be favored, and thus it should be assessed whether less invasive measures may be sufficient for epidemiological prevention purposes.  
In this perspective, the acquisition of genuinely anonymous mobility trends does not pose particular problems. Article 9 of the e-Privacy Directive legitimizes the processing, even in the absence of the consent of the data subject, of location data, provided they are anonymous.  
This solution allows for the creation, for example, of descriptive maps of the epidemic's progression, which are extremely useful for prognostic and statistical purposes, but less so for diagnostic purposes in the strict sense.  
On the other hand, the use of identifying data on location or interaction with other devices may serve different purposes.  
In any case, it requires—also pursuant to Article 15 of the e-Privacy Directive—a sufficiently detailed regulatory provision containing adequate guarantees.  
The various possible uses of such data can theoretically be aimed at (and reasoning in terms assumed by the public debate of these weeks):  
a) verifying the position of the individual subject to home confinement because they tested positive, thus using the geolocation of the phone (which is assumed, but not guaranteed, to follow the subject step by step) to ascertain actual compliance with the prohibition of leaving the home, or:  
b) the retrospective acquisition of data on the subject's interaction with other individuals who later tested positive, to verify, during the period when they had viral capacity, any contacts ascertainable through various techniques: cell towers, GPS, Bluetooth.  
The two hypotheses differ in purpose: this element is undoubtedly relevant for assessing the overall legitimacy of the processing.  
The first hypothesis, in fact, by using phone localization as if it were a sort of atypical electronic bracelet, presupposes the replacement, with the electronic eye, of "human" controls, but assumes that those who decide to violate home confinement obligations carry their phone with them, which is evidently counterintuitive.  
Among the other measures used to verify compliance with social distancing obligations is the use, by public security authorities, of drones.  
Such tools must also be used in compliance with the principle of proportionality, especially due to their particularly invasive potential on privacy.  
If used by law enforcement, not to signal "impersonal" gatherings, but to monitor strict compliance with home confinement obligations, this measure will hardly guarantee adherence to the principle of proportionality, as it could lead to a very broad collection of personal data.  
It would be desirable, on this point, for a regulatory clarification, considering that the reference norm generically recalls the (not entirely overlapping) needs for territorial control for public safety purposes, combating terrorism, and organized crime (cf. Article 5, paragraph 3-sexies of Decree Law No. 7/2015, converted, with modifications, by Law No. 43/2015, as amended by Decree Law No. 113/2018, converted with modifications by Law No. 132/2018).

As we will see later, the choice of the most effective type of data also affects the overall assessment of proportionality, as greater selectivity reduces the scope of the measure's impact to only what is strictly necessary, with socially appreciable effects in terms of protection of health, both individual and collective.  
In general terms, however, the aim pursued by this measure is particularly commendable because it is not repressive (as is the case with the surveillance of individuals in mandatory quarantine through geolocation), but rather solidaristic.  
The pursued objective would coincide, in fact, with the need to subject to checks those who have potentially come into contact with an individual who tested positive for the virus or, in any case, to adopt measures useful to prevent contagion.  
Thus, one would pursue that solidaristic component of the right to health as a collective interest, valued by constitutional jurisprudence on vaccination obligations.  
The use of such technology would, moreover, have few valid alternatives for the reconstruction of the epidemiological chain.  
The simple interview of the patient can, in fact, be incomplete or suffer from the lack of knowledge of many individuals with whom they may have come into contact in various contexts (in pharmacies, supermarkets, etc.).  
One element of fragility of solutions based on data acquired from phones, however, relates to the assumption that everyone carries their phone with them. While this occurs almost systematically for the younger segments of the population, it does not happen as reliably for the elderly, who should instead be the first to be contacted in case of suspected contagion, to be treated as quickly as possible.  
“Technological” solutions are, in fact, excellent allies of epidemiological prevention efforts but evidently require complementary measures of a different nature, suitable to overcome the limitations imposed, among other things, by the digital divide.  
This consideration, regarding the intrinsic limits of technological options, has a dual order of implications.  
First, the assessment of the expected effectiveness of the measure cannot disregard an analysis concerning complementary actions and, therefore, the phase—which should reasonably follow—of health checks of individuals identified through data tracing as potential carriers.  
In fact, it is possible to collect all possible data on potential carriers (whether healthy or not), but if there are no resources (and even reagents!) to ascertain their actual positivity, one does not get very far.  
Secondly, the need to reconstruct the chain of infections through data from electronic devices makes the imposition of a generalized obligation to use such systems problematic. This would, in fact, presuppose the possibility (not only economic but also cognitive) of using smartphones and their functionalities, which are not objectively accessible to everyone.  
Moreover, such an obligation to use would be difficult to enforce unless resorting to a true electronic bracelet.  
Even if it were deemed, as is currently being hypothesized, to activate Bluetooth directly from an app, how could one impose, in fact, that individuals leave their homes only if ‘accompanied’ by their smartphone, which, moreover, should be sufficiently charged?  
These considerations lead to a preference for systems based on the voluntary adherence of individuals that allow for the tracking of their position. However, to ensure real freedom (and thus the validity) of consent to data processing, it should not be conditioned in any way.  
Therefore, consent given for the processing of data acquired through such systems could not be considered effectively valid, because it is unduly and inevitably conditioned, if it is envisioned as a necessary prerequisite, for example, to access certain services or goods (consider the Chinese system).  
The diagnostic effectiveness of such a solution depends, in any case, on the degree of adherence it encounters among citizens, as detection could, by definition, occur only for that part of the population that allows itself to be “traced.”  
The minimum percentage for effectiveness is estimated to be around 60%.  
And while in Singapore this solution has seen the participation of nearly the entire population, this seems attributable primarily to the specific culture and the very advanced level of digital innovation in that country.  
However, this does not exclude that adequate awareness of the opportunity to resort to such a technique, even if only for selfish reasons—namely, to be informed of having potentially and unknowingly been infected through contact with positive individuals—could instead allow for a broad adherence from citizens.  
In this sense, therefore, the voluntary activation of an app functional to the collection of data on device interactions could well represent the basis for a regulatory framework founded on public health needs, with adequate guarantees for the interested parties (art. 9, p.2, lett.i) Reg. (EU) 2016/679).  
The second phase of processing (that is, the one following the data collection) essentially consists of the preservation of the data, in view of its eventual subsequent use to alert potential carriers.  
This “personalization” process should occur only for those individuals who subsequently test positive and for those who have had significant contact with them, for the sole period of potential contagiousness.
