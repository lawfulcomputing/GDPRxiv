VIDEO OF THE HEARING  
Hearing of the President of the Data Protection Authority, Prof. Pasquale Stanzione, on issues related to the Covid-19 green certification  
Joint Committees I, II, and XII of the Chamber of Deputies  
(May 6, 2021)  

1. Primary Legislation and Preliminary Consultation with the Authority  
I thank the Committees for this opportunity for dialogue, which allows for the institutional conversation that the Authority has urged the Government to engage in since the phase prior to the adoption of the decree-law and its presentation to the Chambers for conversion into law.  
The European legislator, with Regulation 2016/679, has indeed made the opinion of the Authority mandatory, albeit non-binding, even with respect to primary legislation (art. 36, p.4). This is precisely to ensure compliance of legislative acts with the European regulatory standard, which is also relevant under art. 117, c. I of the Constitution.  
The consultation of the Authority—specifically defined by the Regulation as preliminary—on primary legislation allows for the necessary privacy guarantees to be inscribed within the legislative framework, which may be subject to further integration, if necessary, with implementing regulations for detailed aspects. In this way, the latter can also be directed, with appropriate guiding criteria, towards solutions suitable for achieving the best balance between various legal interests, taking into account the impact that each regulatory provision may have on individual privacy.  
The experience of these first three years of application of the regulatory provision on the advisory powers of the Authority has demonstrated its foresight and, above all, its suitability to realize that "social function" of data protection referred to in Recital 4 of the Regulation. This presupposes a constant balancing of this right with the various legal interests—of an individual or meta-individual nature—at stake, to be resolved according to the principle of proportionality that, precisely in this area, the Constitutional Court has emphasized in ruling no. 20 of 2019, rapporteur Zanon.  
Regarding the emergency measures adopted over the past year for the purpose of containing infections, the dialogue of the Authority with Parliament and the Government—on drafts as well as on decree-laws and ordinances—has allowed, in a virtuous circuit, to improve, often significantly, the proposed norms. The need for the involvement of the Authority in the legislative process is, moreover, a reflection of the centrality of data protection in a society, such as the current hyper-connected one, as it regulates the conditions for the circulation of what, like data, represents the constitutive element of the digital.  
These three years of application of the Regulation have shown that, with respect to ordinary legislative proposals, the consultation of the Authority can also and well take place in the form of a parliamentary hearing, preferably in the phase prior to the setting of the deadline for amendments in Committee, in the first reading, in order to be able to intervene on the text without incurring the preclusions arising from its complete consolidation.  
With respect to norms that may have implications for data protection, adopted through urgent decrees, it is instead necessary to obtain the opinion of the Authority in the phase prior to the presentation of the text to the Chambers for conversion into law. The general immediate effectiveness of the provisions (except for the delegation to implementing measures for the regulation of complementary aspects) indeed implies the need for a consultation with the Authority before they come into force and can, therefore, legitimize a processing that may, hypothetically, violate data protection regulations.  

2. The Regulation of Covid-19 Green Certifications  
Having clarified the methodological aspects related to the consultation of the Authority, I will now address the merits of Article 9 of the decree-law, limiting myself to some general observations regarding the critical issues and, above all, the shortcomings of the text, highlighted in the warning measure of April 23, 2021. With this act, addressed to all parties involved in the processing, it was intended to emphasize how the conduct of the latter, under the terms provided by the decree-law not previously submitted to the Authority, may constitute the elements of certain violations of data protection regulations.  
It should be noted from the outset that this warning measure has been followed by ongoing discussions with the Government, currently taking place in these days, aimed at adapting, presumably with the amendments that will be presented, the text of the decree-law to the observations of the Authority. The following considerations, therefore, refer to the current formulation of the norms, excluding any hypotheses of modification under discussion.  

2.1. Determinacy of the Regulation and Exhaustiveness of the Purposes of Processing  
For the legitimacy of the processing, it is essential, first of all, that the relevant regulatory provision sufficiently determines its scope from both a subjective and objective point of view, introducing adequate guarantees commensurate with the impact of the processing on the rights and freedoms of citizens and the nature of the data processed. In this regard, it should be considered that the processing related to the system of green certifications is a systematic treatment, on a large scale, of data that may include health information, as it is susceptible to inference, albeit indirect, based on the data contained in the certification. On one hand, indeed, the data regarding recovery present in the relevant pass model reveals the previous pathological condition of the individual. On the other hand, even data regarding vaccination could indirectly suggest the existence of pathologies in cases of individuals who do not meet...
**ntrino, at the time of issuing the pass, within the age range of individuals eligible for vaccination.**  
This demonstrates, therefore, the considerable impact of the processing on individual privacy and, hence, the existence of the prerequisites for submitting the impact assessment to the Guarantor pursuant to Article 35, paragraph 10 of the Regulation, which would have been necessary before the start of the information flow.  
But beyond this aspect, the quantitative and qualitative relevance of the processing also demonstrates the need for the provision, already in primary legislation, of adequate guarantees against the risks associated with the data flow in question, as well as the stringent observance of the principle of proportionality.  
In this regard, the regulation presents various shortcomings, among which the indeterminacy of the purposes (incompatible with the principle set out in Article 5.1.b. of the Regulation) that legitimize the subordination of certain activities to the display of the pass is particularly noteworthy. It can indeed be requested not only for territorial movements in the cases referred to in Article 2, paragraphs 1 and 3 of the decree-law, but also for participation in events indicated by the guidelines adopted pursuant to Articles 5, paragraph 4 and 7, paragraph 2.  
This particular heterointegration of the provision by virtue of mere acts of soft law (which must still be submitted to the Guarantor's opinion) leaves a margin of indeterminacy in the regulatory provision, which could significantly extend the scope of the processing. Such indeterminacy of the circumstances legitimizing the processing of personal data, including health-related data, functional to the green certification system, appears to be poorly compatible with the specific requirements that must characterize the legal basis for this purpose (Articles 6, paragraph 2 and 9 of the Regulation; Articles 2 ter and 2 sexies of Legislative Decree 196/03 and subsequent amendments).  
Moreover, that the regulation, drafted in these terms, lends itself to discretionary interpretations is also demonstrated by the implementation proposed at the regional level, with ordinances that have extended its scope of application and with respect to which, so far in one case, the Guarantor has had to intervene. The state law reserve on the matters affected by these measures(1) thus risks being circumvented due to norms lacking the necessary determinacy.  
It is therefore appropriate to introduce a clarification that excludes the use of passes for purposes other than those expressly provided for by the decree-law, hopefully more narrowly defining ex-ante the scope left to the determinations of the guidelines (unless it is deemed preferable to refer, rather than to acts of soft law, to acts that are at least general administrative).  
This need is all the more relevant due to the lack of explicit reasons for which it was deemed necessary to introduce national certifications, provisionally and in anticipation of those provided for at the European level by the draft regulation currently under discussion.  

**2.2. Compliance with the principle of minimization**  
In order to ensure compliance with the principle of data minimization set out in Article 5, paragraph 1 letter c) of the Regulation – all the more relevant due to the "particular" nature of some of the data processed – it is essential to remove from the provision regarding the content of the certificates any data that is extraneous to the purposes of verifying the condition of the individual, which does not hinder free movement. For these purposes, the only necessary information appears to be the identifying data, the unique certification number, and its validity period (which will naturally vary depending on the subject of the certification).  
In this sense, not only the indication of the number of vaccine doses administered to the individual appears excessive, but also the provision of different models of green certifications depending on the condition (vaccination, recovery, negative test) under which they are issued.  
The verification of the validity of the certification requires, in fact, only the indication of its temporal extent, which is not included among the fields provided in Annex 1 to the decree. The latter, in particular, must be the only suitable source to regulate the types of data contained in the certifications, where the subject of the dPCM referred to in paragraph 10 must be, in particular, the categories of data susceptible to processing within the National DGC Platform.  

**2.3. The architecture of the processing, the role of the parties involved, and the principle of transparency**  
For the legitimacy of the processing, it is necessary to identify the data controllers, with particular reference to the activities of issuing and controlling green certifications and the operations carried out on the "National DGC Platform," which would represent the national information system for the issuance, verification, and acceptance of interoperable Covid-19 certifications at the national and European level. The failure to identify the entity where the platform will be established and the institutional subjects to whom the ownership of the processing carried out through this information system is assigned, in addition to depriving the reference regulation of the necessary legal certainty, makes it impossible for the interested parties to exercise the rights and remedial protections granted to them by personal data protection legislation.  
It is therefore necessary, in compliance with the principle of transparency, to define these structural elements of the processing, in addition to adequate guarantees for the certifications referred to in the last sentence of Article 9, paragraph 10, namely those issued, pending the adoption of the dPCM to which the detailed regulation is entrusted, by public and private health structures, pharmacies, general practitioners, and pediatricians of free choice. For such certifications...
It is indeed provided only a “minimum” content obligation, as they must include the information specified in the annex to the decree-law. For these certificates, none of the guarantees that will instead be duly introduced with the aforementioned dPCM are contemplated, regarding the data retention period, the technical and organizational protective measures suitable to prevent unauthorized or unlawful processing or accidental loss, destruction, or damage, and finally, the methods for updating the certifications themselves. In the absence of measures aimed at ensuring the updating of such attestations, as will subsequently be possible thanks to the aforementioned platform, it will not be possible to verify their current validity in the event of subsequent changes in the conditions of validity (for example, due to a subsequent positive result).

It is therefore appropriate that such guarantees, if achievable even within the framework of the transitional system prior to the issuance of the dPCM, be directly included in the decree-law; otherwise, it would be preferable to eliminate the last sentence of paragraph 10. 

With the aforementioned improvements, the regulation of national green certifications can achieve a proper balance between privacy, health needs, freedom of movement, and economic initiative. In this sense, a valid tool can be outlined (albeit temporarily in relation to European regulations) to allow for safe reopenings and, with them, at least a partial economic recovery.

However, for the best effectiveness— as well as the full legitimacy— of this tool, it is essential that it includes the essential guarantees to ensure, even in this case, that synergy between individual rights and collective needs on which the action against the pandemic has so far been based. Indeed, it has managed to avoid the logic of exception (according to which necessitas non habet legem), reaffirming the value of democratic mediation in the search for the highest balance between the individual and the collective, the person and the State, freedom and power.
