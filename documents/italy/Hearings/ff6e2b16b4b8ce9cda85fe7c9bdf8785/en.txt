VIDEO OF THE HEARING

Hearing of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, Prof. Pasquale Stanzione - Inquiry "Digitalization and Interoperability of Tax Databases"
Senate of the Republic - Parliamentary Commission for the Supervision of the Tax Registry
(July 7, 2021)

I thank the Commission for this discussion on a topic, such as that of the inquiry, which is of central importance, especially in light of the significance attributed to it within the framework of the reforms related to the National Recovery and Resilience Plan (PNRR). The digitalization of public databases is indeed a process that is currently in the phase of its most pronounced development, but it is certainly not new and has often represented an opportunity for fruitful dialogue between the Authority and the Government, especially concerning tax matters. The objective, largely achieved, of this dialogue has always been to outline the best conditions to make the fight against tax evasion and avoidance more effective while being as compliant as possible with the substantial and procedural guarantees of correctness and security that privacy regulations also provide in this area.

Some misunderstandings have undoubtedly characterized the dialogue between the Authority and the Government on this point, which I believe can be attributed to a misrepresentation of privacy as an obstacle, an unnecessary burden that the tax administration would like to bear, but which has instead proven to be a necessary prerequisite for the effectiveness and proper conduct of the fight against tax offenses. By ensuring, in particular, the accuracy (also understood as non-obsolescence) of the data on which assessments are based, privacy regulations have provided important assistance in the correct representation of the taxpayer's capacity and, consequently, in the preliminary identification of tax risk profiles based on which to orient checks. Compliance with data protection regulations has thus contributed to mitigating the risk of errors, which is even more significant given that analyses are increasingly conducted using automated procedures based on the most advanced information technologies such as machine learning and artificial intelligence, on collections of personal information comparable in size and variety to so-called big data.

It is therefore important to clarify from the outset that the Authority has never obstructed the digitalization process itself, let alone in the tax field, being aware of the valuable contribution that new technologies can offer to the fight against tax evasion by better selecting the targets of control. This is also in order to ultimately ensure that tax equity underlying the very idea of the progressivity of taxes, as wisely enshrined by the Constitution, is upheld.

And if this need for innovation in tax activities has emerged, particularly clearly, since 2011, today it is undoubtedly stronger also due to the objectives set by the PNRR, among which the digitalization of administrative activities occupies a prominent role. However, the process of digitalization is, in itself, neutral and can, if well-directed, lead to significant innovations and improvements in terms of effectiveness and efficiency of administrative action, but it can also pose significant risks and, ultimately, dysfunctions if not adequately governed. In the overall governance of the digitalization process, data protection regulations can thus play an important role in ensuring the security of the information flows necessary to prevent the permeability of databases and the accuracy requirements of the data processed, which are essential to avoid untrue representations of the individual citizen's position.

On one hand, it is certainly true—as Director Ruffini emphasizes—that in order to develop the risk profiles functional to controls and to spontaneous compliance, it is necessary for the Agency to have the data necessary for this purpose, collected in various databases. On the other hand, however, it is true that what is necessary is not so much and not only a greater quantity of data, but better, accurate, relevant, and updated data. The same Director highlighted in the hearing how often the databases accessed by the Agency contain data that are “heterogeneous in structure and size, (...) subject to rapid changes and, therefore, also to obsolescence,” with the risk of misalignment of the information assets between one archive and another. It is evident, therefore, how such deficiencies weaken the effectiveness of tax controls (and the same international cooperation provided, for example, by the various DAC directives). The solution, therefore, lies not so much in the extension of the information assets, even through the interconnection of databases, in itself, but in the selection and standardization of the data accessed, so that they are actually functional to the purpose. The actions that AgID is undertaking to implement the provisions of the CAD regarding the interoperability of databases, in which the Authority is fully involved to ensure compliance with data protection regulations, can certainly yield good results in this regard.

Similar considerations apply to the simplification of procedures in tax matters according to the principle of "once only"—which is, in truth, already underpinned by Law 241 and the Taxpayer's Statute—insofar as they exclude the possibility of requesting from the taxpayer data already in the possession of the Administration, thus avoiding unnecessary interactions. For these data to be used profitably, they must indeed be accurate and updated, as well as—as stipulated by data protection regulations—pertinent.

In accordance with (and not exceeding) the purpose pursued by the accessing Administration, which must be able to draw upon it based on an adequate regulatory provision that legitimizes the processing of data for the specific purpose pursued. Interoperability, in fact, as reiterated in Article 50 of the CAD, cannot be understood as an indiscriminate possibility of circulation, for various purposes, of personal data (especially if belonging to the special categories referred to in Articles 9 and 10 of the GDPR: the former sensitive and judicial data) among the most diverse administrations, solely because they have been acquired "once" by one of them. If this were the case, it would result in the implicit repeal of the data protection regulations in the part that imposes, precisely, a specific regulatory provision for processing for public interest purposes or in the exercise of public powers, in violation of the principles of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency, and of limitation of the purpose of processing (Article 5, paragraph 1, letters a) and b) of the Regulation).

Moreover, the implications regarding security, understood also as national security and not only in its technical IT dimension, related to a proposed interoperability model – as in the case of the National Digital Data Platform referred to in Article 50-ter of the CAD, which also includes databases of significance for the Agency – in the form of centralization and concentration of even "particular" data, referring to the entire population, for purposes not adequately circumscribed and with the management entrusted to a Company, such as PagoPA, whose tasks regarding processing are not sufficiently defined.

In the absence of adequate measures, in fact, the concentration of such quantitatively and qualitatively significant data on a single platform can increase its vulnerability risks and constitute, in itself, a preferred and highly lucrative target – even if limited to the induced unavailability – for various types of cyberattacks, including those directed from outside, thus nullifying the improvements made over time to the various databases now interconnected, in compliance with the indications of the Guarantor. In any case, interoperability alone is not a requirement of efficiency; it can only be so (and even significantly so) when supported by adequate guarantees of security and data protection, which, in the case of the National Data Platform, require even significant improvements. As already noted, for example, by the European Data Protection Board regarding the interoperability of COVID tracking apps, but with more general relevance, the objective of interoperability cannot, by itself, legitimize a collection of personal data exceeding the specific purposes pursued from time to time.

It is significant, moreover, that the final document of the exploratory investigation by the joint finance committees of the two Chambers recognizes that the interoperability of databases and the digitization of tax activities must occur in compliance with privacy regulations. A real innovation and simplification of tax activities, in accordance with data protection regulations, therefore requires a comprehensive development plan that articulates specific regulatory provisions to clearly delineate the legitimate scope of data circulation, rationalizing its acquisition (which must only concern fiscally relevant information), and avoiding duplication, based on adequate security guarantees and stringent subjective and objective prerequisites for access to prevent unauthorized data exfiltration.

These needs have been repeatedly represented by the Guarantor, in the context of the activities carried out in this area, in the search for the best balance between greater effectiveness of tax audits and the guarantee of the right to personal data protection of citizens, regarding the former being more synergistic than antagonistic. Some of the observations expressed regarding the most recent measures – concerning tax risk analyses, synthetic income determination tools necessary for the emergence of the tax base, and those aimed at facilitating taxpayer compliance, such as the spending monitor and electronic invoicing – deserve further reflection, in view of future developments.

Regarding the experimental processing of tax risk profiles through algorithmic detection of inconsistencies between savings and declared income, based on the data contained in the Financial Reports Archive, the Guarantor suggested, particularly between 2017 and 2019, some corrective measures aimed at avoiding incorrect representations of tax capacity, thus improving – rather than hindering – the effectiveness of the analysis.

Concerning the interconnection of databases – provided for since Legislative Decree 201/2011 for the processing of tax risk parameters based on which audits are directed – during the 2020 budget law that introduced the requirement for data pseudonymization, it would have been appropriate to include those specific guarantees that Article 22, paragraph 2, letter b), of the GDPR prescribes for decisions based on automated processing as mandated by law. Such precautions, far from weakening the effectiveness of the fight against tax evasion, could instead promote it – correcting potential errors or distortions in the automated decision-making process – and provide it, even in the perception of citizens, with that stronger legitimacy that a fair combination of technology and "human factor" can ensure for administrative action, guaranteeing maximum transparency to the taxpayer in order to encourage voluntary compliance with positive repercussions on the effectiveness and efficiency of control activities. should also be adequately considered within the framework of the implementing reforms of the PNRR, where the use of AI techniques and advanced data analysis is foreseen, between 2025 and 2026, as a relevant component "to be able to tackle the tax gap," reduce the propensity for tax evasion, and promote compliance with obligations. An aspect present in the plan and to be enhanced will be that of conducting preliminary analyses on previously anonymized data, following a process that removes any elements capable of allowing for a re-identification, even if only indirectly, of the individual concerned, while ensuring that any potential individual repercussions of the treatments carried out must be safeguarded by an adequate system of guarantees to protect the rights and freedoms of the individuals involved.

This is even more pertinent considering that the same budget law has introduced a specific "tax exemption," that is, a provision that enables the administration to limit, for circumscribed periods, the exercise of the rights of the individuals for the purposes of combating (and preventing) tax evasion, as well as for tax-related needs. Following the adoption of some of the recommendations made by the Guarantor during the hearing, the final text of the provision includes significant improvements compared to the initial version. For example, the rectification has been correctly excluded from the list of rights that can be limited, recognizing the usefulness of exercising this faculty for the accuracy of the information processed and, therefore, for the correct representation of the asset capacity and, consequently, also of the tax risk of the individual concerned. However, the provision – although improved in its text – significantly constrains the rights of the individuals (and, therefore, the corresponding obligations of the data controller), further reinforcing the need to introduce general guarantees, at least concerning treatments based on automated decision-making processes. In this regard, the final document of the exploratory investigation on the reform of personal income tax voted by the finance committees fails to consider that, concerning automated decision-making processes, data protection guarantees cannot be excluded outright and represent, indeed, a significant safeguard not only for the taxpayer but also for the correctness of the tax activity itself.

An additional reflection would be warranted on the electronic invoicing regulation, the scope of which has been significantly extended regarding the storage of invoice files with Decree Law 124 of 2019, converted, with amendments, by Law No. 157 of 2019, and the consequent directorate provision, both in terms of the type of data collected and in terms of the scope and methods of their use. In particular, the use of so-called "integrated invoice data" for tax purposes has been provided, which includes detailed information concerning the description of the goods sold or the services rendered to which the invoice refers. Many of these data – such as those contained in the attachments of the invoices – are not relevant for tax purposes and may instead reveal health-related data or the individual's involvement in criminal proceedings, as in the case of invoices for services in the legal field or specific information about goods or services purchased.

The storage, regardless of any potential use, of electronic invoices in their entirety thus entails the massive acquisition of a very significant amount of data contained in the approximately 2 billion invoices issued annually, concerning, among other things, the relationships between the seller, buyer, and any third parties, customer loyalty, consumption habits and types, payment regularity, and the user's membership in particular categories. This extension of the range of data processed by the administration to non-tax-relevant information contrasts – as noted by the Guarantor both during the hearing and in the opinion on the directorate provision – with the principle of proportionality and risks creating significant vulnerabilities in tax databases, which are extensive and interconnected to such an extent that they become much more difficult to safeguard. Following the Guarantor's opinion in July on the directorate provision – which highlighted the incompatibility aspects of the processing with data protection regulations – a technical table was established to identify the best measures, despite the intrinsic limitations of the provision that merit reflection.

In any case, before considering any expansion of the information assets of the tax administration, the legislator should also take into account that, based on the constant jurisprudence of the Council of State, the declarations, communications, and documents acquired by the Revenue Agency, containing income, asset, and financial data, and included in the databases of the tax registry, including the archive of financial relationships, constitute administrative documents and are, therefore, subject to access under Articles 22 and following of Law No. 241/1990 by anyone with an interest. This results in a potential, significant exposure of the private sphere where the data available to the tax administration are not necessary for the concrete pursuit of the public interest underlying the processing.

The interventions of the Guarantor in this area demonstrate, therefore, how the digitalization of tax activity needs to be inscribed within a far-sighted and organic project, which considers data protection not merely as a regulatory obligation but as a necessary requirement for the effectiveness and efficiency of verification and tax compliance activities.
and to strengthen citizens' trust in the use of new and particularly incisive analytical techniques. In this sense, particular emphasis should be placed on: the principles of proportionality and minimization – limiting the scope of processing to only the data and operations strictly necessary for the pursued purposes, adopting privacy by design and by default measures that prevent the interpretation of data protection as a sort of retrofit to be applied to large databases to make the processing compliant with the Regulation, and favoring, whenever possible, the use of truly anonymous data –; the regulatory provision for processing in light of new interoperability scenarios of systems, so as to avoid uncontrolled and unmanageable multiplication that could hinder the exercise of rights by the data subjects; adequate guarantees regarding automated decision-making processes; security and resilience requirements for systems, which are essential also for cybersecurity purposes. These measures will promote innovation in tax activities such that they are both effective and respectful of taxpayers' privacy, according to the balance between collective needs and individual rights on which both the domestic and European legal systems are based.
