**THE VIDEO OF THE HEARING**

Memorandum of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data - Assigned Case No. 621 (impact of integrated digital teaching (DDI) on learning processes and the psychophysical well-being of students) Memorandum of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data  
Joint 7th and 12th Committees of the Senate of the Republic  
(April 27, 2021)

1. **The Context**  
I thank the Committees for this invitation to discuss a topic that, like few others, reflects the social, cultural, and I would say even anthropological implications of the pandemic. The closure of schools in March 2020, preceding the total lockdown that would soon follow, unequivocally represented the seriousness of the pandemic situation. On the other hand, the continuation of the closures of many schools until now has imposed— for over a year, precisely on the students in the most delicate phase of existence, which is adolescence— distance learning (d.a.d.) as an unequal substitute for educational activities.  
The definition of "unequal substitute" encapsulates the profound sense of what the past year has represented for many students. On one hand, it must be emphasized that the social and relational dimension of school as a community is irreplaceable. In this sense, teaching, at all ages, is necessarily in-person teaching, characterized by the coexistence of bodies, the sharing of spaces and times, and it is precisely in this that the irreducible uniqueness of traditional school emerges as an irreplaceable human and social experience, a synthesis of school-light and school-device, to borrow the definition from Massimo Recalcati. Among the many deprivations that children and adolescents—truly the segment of the population most disadvantaged by the pandemic—have suffered this year, that of life within the school community is certainly the most significant, having deprived those who most need intersubjective relationships of the extraordinary opportunities for comparison and growth that only "in-person" school can offer.  
On the other hand, one cannot underestimate the substitute function, of "exceedingly difficult replacement," that d.a.d. has played in relation to traditional school, exponentially accelerating a process, such as the digitalization of educational activities, which before the pandemic was, at best, only in its infancy. In just a few weeks, schools, teachers, students, and their families had to face a truly epochal challenge: to transform platforms into virtual classrooms for educational activities of all levels, to recreate, behind screens, communities united by dialogue and the magic of the educational relationship. Overcoming the significant generational gap in relation to new technologies, teachers and students had to acquire significant digital skills in a very short time, which allowed for the continuation of teaching. However, the inequalities—which traditional school strives to mitigate in every aspect—have re-emerged forcefully, often in dramatic forms, in terms of access to technological resources, demonstrating also in this respect how access to the internet is today, if not an independent fundamental right, certainly the prerequisite for many other fundamental rights.  
Despite these limitations, d.a.d. has represented an extraordinary challenge to the resilience of the entire school community, has educated to face reality with all its hardships, and in this sense, has provided a life lesson that will be extremely useful for students in the future. In this perspective, the definition of school-Telemaco coined once again by Massimo Recalcati well expresses the idea, underlying d.a.d., of an educational model capable of accepting difficulties as challenges around which to build a process of subjectivation.

2. **Online School and the Privacy of Students and Teachers**  
But like every aspect of the online translation of our lives, d.a.d. also presents risks and management difficulties that had to be addressed from the very beginning. Among the main ones are the prejudices against individual privacy that a misuse of these distance learning methods can create. The category of potential risks is vast: from the dissemination of conversations or scenes of daily life of a confidential nature, due to a microphone or camera being improperly activated, to the infiltration (which has occurred more than once) of hackers or external agents offering inappropriate content to students; from the secondary and improper use of data from students and teachers by the platforms used for e-learning, to the negative implications for the quality of teaching of the excessive and constant interference of the electronic eye in the lesson.  
Aware of these risks as well as the need to prevent them from becoming an obstacle to the otherwise commendable solution of d.a.d., the Authority adopted a specific directive as early as March 28, 2020, recognizing the most important implications of distance learning on privacy, providing initial guidelines to ensure its conduct in the most correct manner. In that context, it was particularly emphasized how the use of generalist platforms equipped with various functionalities, including those typical of social networks, naturally involved greater risks, proportional to the broader scope of data usage, and therefore required greater awareness in use and a corresponding digital pedagogy.  
For this reason, on one hand, with a specific note to the Minister, it was hoped—pending the refinement of the regulatory framework—that the electronic register be valued as a suitable platform for interaction.
**Distance between Teachers and Students in Safer Conditions**

(1) On one hand, it has been emphasized that the distance between teachers and students is more secure (and more consolidated in terms of the relationships between the parties involved in the processing) compared to those offered by generalist platforms. On the other hand, it has been highlighted that these latter cannot condition the enjoyment of educational services on the consent to the processing of data for the provision of additional non-educational online services.

In the case of resorting to generalist platforms, the need to activate only the services strictly necessary for training has been underscored, configuring them in such a way as to minimize the personal data to be processed and - as far as teachers are concerned - always in compliance with the guarantees established by Article 4 of the Workers' Statute regarding the prohibition of remote monitoring of work activities, without interfering with the freedom of teaching.

In this sense, the stable introduction of distance learning into the education system must be accompanied by adequate guarantees regarding the processing of the data of the interested parties (minors, families, and teachers), including through initiatives that may develop in the public sector, provided that they respect the principle of proportionality, avoiding the creation of new national databases on the assessments of individual students' learning.

Returning to the indications provided by the Guarantor with the guidance document of March 26, 2020, it has been clarified that the use of distance learning does not imply the consent of parents or teachers, as the consequent processing of personal data is attributable to the institutional functions of educational institutions. The need to ensure transparency and fairness in the processing has also been emphasized, enhancing the informational obligations also in terms of the actual comprehensibility, by minors, of the information provided.

These indications have been further developed by the Guidelines of the Ministry of Education on integrated digital teaching (DDI) and the protection of privacy - to which the Authority contributed - particularly emphasizing, on the side of teacher protection, the need to verify that e-learning platforms are configured to prevent the collection, even accidentally, of information related to their private lives (2). It has also been clarified that a privacy impact assessment should only be prepared in cases of resorting to new and particularly invasive technological solutions or those involving systematic monitoring of users or, in general, a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. Further indications on the subject have been provided with specific FAQs published on the Guarantor's website and with others available on the Ministry of Education's website, to the drafting of one of which the Guarantor participated.

**3. Proctoring**

Among the most complex implications of the remote delivery of educational activities, particularly at the university level, is proctoring, that is, the use, primarily by universities, of systems to supervise the behavior of students during remote exams to ensure their proper conduct. The issue is extremely delicate: on one hand, especially concerning university exams, it is necessary to avoid that the remote administration of tests equates to a systematic violation by students of the rules of fairness in their execution. On the other hand, however, detection systems must not be unduly invasive and must not involve monitoring of the student that exceeds actual needs. The urgency to address this issue is certainly evident. While the respect for the rules of conducting tests must be guaranteed online as well, neither systems such as the unacceptable practice of blindfolding students, which we have witnessed (not even in the university context), nor electronic surveillance devoid of necessary limits and guarantees would be tolerable.

Some universities have independently activated monitoring software programs aimed at verifying, for example through facial recognition, the absence of impersonation phenomena and, more generally, of improper behaviors, despite the absence of suitable regulatory premises.

In the cases so far presented, there has generally been a recognized need for a regulatory provision that governs such processing - which cannot be legitimized on a consensual basis due to the lack of real freedom in the expression of will by someone, like the student, who is in a position of weakness in the relationship with the university - and introduces adequate measures to ensure that the controls, certainly necessary, do not exceed the actual needs of verifying the proper execution of the tests. In some cases, in fact, programs have been used that do not limit themselves to inhibiting certain functionalities of devices that could facilitate improper conduct, but carry out extensive profiling of the student's behavior during the test (web activity, applications in use, keys pressed on the keyboard, and mouse movements) which risks exceeding actual needs or which, in any case, would require adequate guarantees and limits.

There have also been reports of not always precise information, data retention limits exceeding real needs, and hypotheses of data transfer abroad - particularly to the USA - lacking adequate legal grounds.

The previous government had informally submitted to the Guarantor a draft guidance document regarding the modalities of conducting public competitions remotely, for which the need to regulate whether and to what extent the use of such software programs is legitimate is even more pressing. has been superseded by Article 10 of Decree Law 44 of 2021, which does not appear to allow the use of such systems for competitive examinations while requiring the use of IT tools. In any case, to the extent that the remote delivery of training activities and, indeed, of examination or competitive tests should continue, it would certainly be necessary to condition the admissibility of such detection techniques on a specific regulation aimed at reconciling privacy protection with the need to monitor any improper conduct by candidates. Should Parliament wish to heed this invitation, the Authority will certainly not fail to contribute.

(2) On the relationship between distance learning and the protection of teachers' informational self-determination, a German court has raised a preliminary interpretative question before the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), concerning the correct interpretation to be given to the provision of the GDPR (Article 88) that allows Member States to introduce more specific rules on the processing of employee data in the context of the employment relationship. The Court asks, in particular, the judges in Luxembourg whether, for this purpose, the internal regulation must meet the substantive requirements prescribed by Article 88.2 of the GDPR (provision of appropriate and specific measures to protect human dignity, legitimate interests, and the fundamental rights of the data subjects). The second interpretative question concerns the possibility of further application, in the current emergency context, of internal regulations in this area, which, however, lack the aforementioned requirements.
