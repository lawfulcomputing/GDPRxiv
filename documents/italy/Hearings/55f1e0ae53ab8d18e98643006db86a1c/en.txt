VIDEO OF THE HEARING  
Informal hearing, via videoconference, of the President of the Data Protection Authority, Prof. Pasquale Stanzione - Proposal for a Regulation on a Single Market for Digital Services (Digital Services Act) (COM(2020) 825 final) and Proposal for a Regulation on Fair and Competitive Markets in the Digital Sector (Digital Markets Act) (COM(2020) 842 final)  
Commission IX of the Chamber of Deputies (Transport, Post, Telecommunications)  
(23 June 2021)  

1. The Context  
I thank the Commission for the invitation to discuss two legislative proposals of absolute relevance for the governance of the digital realm. Both the Digital Services Act and the Digital Markets Act (along with the Artificial Intelligence Act) decisively characterize the legislative policy line followed by the European Commission regarding the relationship between innovation and the protection of individuals, which is a crucial issue for democracy.  
This is indeed one of the decisive challenges for establishing Europe as a global leader in sustainable innovation that is human-centered, as clearly emerges from the statements of President Von der Leyen.  
Regarding these legislative proposals, the President has claimed the primacy—chronological, as well as axiological and political—of Europe in the attempt to "legalize" the internet, understood as emancipation from a state of anomie that is not freedom, but subjection to mercantile law, so that the perimeter of freedoms online is defined by the general terms of contract drafted by the platforms.  
They increasingly assume the role (which became evident with the suspension of the Facebook and Twitter accounts of then-President Donald Trump) of true private powers, or de facto authorities that, however, at this stage, the most recent European legislation is attempting to regulate as authorities, at least in part, "by law," limiting their powers and assigning them corresponding responsibilities functional to the guarantee of fundamental rights affected, to varying degrees, by their actions.  
At this present time, in which the acquisition of goods, the enjoyment of services, access to knowledge and information, and social relations are necessarily mediated by various platforms, their role indeed becomes central to the exercise of fundamental rights, of which the gatekeepers (a significant definition!) risk becoming arbiters with unchallengeable power.  
The two proposals under discussion intervene in this area, from different perspectives, both of which are relevant for privacy, as they affect the conditions for the legitimate circulation of data in the digital ecosystem and the limits to be imposed, for the protection of individuals, on platform capitalism.  
Also due to the close interrelationship between the subject matter of the proposals and data protection, the Authority expresses from now on its willingness to contribute, in ways deemed appropriate, to the subsequent adaptation of the internal legal system to this new regulation, offering its experience and expertise, which is particularly transversal, on digital matters.  
I would like to preface that both texts are advanced and certainly necessary to regulate, indeed, the conditions for exercising a power, that of the platforms, for which there is currently no adequate statute of responsibility, and which the proposals under examination aim to introduce, one primarily for the protection of users' rights, also through the guarantee of a safe online environment, and the other to ensure market competitiveness.  
However, we will indicate some possible improvements already suggested, at the European level, by the European Data Protection Supervisor in opinions 1 and 2/2021, many of which have also been reiterated within the coordination table established at the Department of European Policies of the Presidency of the Council of Ministers.  

2. The DSA  
2.a. General Profiles  
The Digital Services Act regulates the provision of online intermediary services at a cross-border level, in order to ensure a safe online environment; improve the conditions for the provision of innovative cross-border digital services; offer adequate protections, including remedial ones, to users; establish effective oversight of digital services, also promoting collaboration among competent authorities.  
Significant are the obligations, especially of a proactive nature, imposed on online platforms, differently modulated based on the number of active users, in the spirit of their essentially preventive accountability.  
The subjective scope of reference of the provision includes service providers of online intermediation, including hosting services, which in turn encompass online platforms in the strict sense (characterized by the dissemination of information to the public); a category that includes "very large platforms," with a number of active recipients in the EU equal to or greater than 45 million, to which more significant obligations are reserved for managing the "systemic risks" associated with their activities.  
It is significant that among the parameters for assessing the systemic risks associated with the operation of platforms are included the negative implications, current or foreseeable, on public health, minors, public safety, and electoral processes, particularly arising from the intentional manipulation of the service.  
Furthermore, obligations for traceability of commercial operators are introduced—for the purposes of accountability and facilitation of enforcement—requiring platforms to enable their services only to subjects that provide specific information functional to their identification.  
In reiterating—and, indeed, strengthening with a new...
**Good Samaritan Clause** – the secondary liability exemption for operators regarding unlawful acts committed by users on their platforms and the necessary prohibition of general and preventive monitoring of content, the DSA compensates this safe harbor with regulatory obligations aimed at minimizing the risk of violations or, in any case, containing their detrimental effects. 

To this end, the establishment (differently modulated according to the relevance of the platform) of internal procedures for deciding on requests for the removal of unlawful content or content contrary to company policies is imposed, with obligations for justification and appeal of the choices made, as well as the referral of disputes to ADR bodies with adequate independence requirements. 

In this sense, the path taken by jurisprudence towards greater accountability of the operator is "positivized" (consider the active hosting figure), aimed at preventing the network, with the force of viral sharing and the ubiquity of its services, from becoming a sounding board for the most diverse violations of individual rights. 

The narrow ridge on which the Digital Services Act operates is the maintenance of the underlying fundamental option of Directive 2000/31, namely the general regime of (only) conditional liability of the operator(1) - with the corresponding prohibition of general monitoring of content - combined, however, with a series of procedural and substantive obligations expressive of both the principle of accountability and the standard of responsibility. 

The Commission's proposal intervenes at a time of heightened need - felt not only in Europe - to ascribe to large platforms liability at least commensurate with the significance of the power (not only economic) exercised by them. 

Consider, in this sense, the (objective) liability of Amazon for the intermediation carried out concerning the sale of a defective product, affirmed in the Bolger case by the ruling of August 13, 2020, of the Fourth District Court of Appeal of California, based on the reliance placed by users on the quality of the products hosted by the platform. 

Although not free from criticism, this ruling undoubtedly reflects the need (felt even in the legal system where this regime of only conditional liability of operators was born) to surround the power of platforms with an adequate system of limits and responsibilities. 

In this context, the choice of the European Commission not to yield(2) to the model of objective liability of the provider for the services intermediated or the content disseminated by users(3) is significant, while not renouncing a revision of the regulation in a more rigorous sense (consider the principle of know your business customer, which requires platforms to adequately assess the reliability of the professionals to whom they grant spaces(4)). 

**2.b. DSA and GDPR: synergies and complementarity**  
The hallmark of this legislative reform lies, therefore, in the transition from (only) liability to accountability, which could have decisive effects even in terms of the correctness of the information conveyed online, especially thanks to the transparency obligations regarding online advertisement and commercial suggestions. Disinformation and the resulting manipulative effects are, after all, specific systemic dangers that the DSA imposes evaluation of, within the framework of a risk analysis related to the activity carried out, in order to consequently modulate the guarantees to be adopted, including the codes of conduct to adhere to. 

The obligation to justify the decisions made and their appeal, introduced by the measure, should then contribute to making the exercise of the removal power by platforms more transparent, which, if not adequately circumscribed and made 'visible' and subject to scrutiny, risks making them arbiters of rights online. 

This is certainly not a radical or "revolutionary" change - as it leaves the essential foundations of the prohibition of general monitoring and the only subsequent nature of removal obligations unchanged - but it is undoubtedly important and balanced; which may perhaps prove to be even decisive in future applications. 

Both the general political option and the individual substantive choices underlying the provisions are, in fact, far from irrelevant and seem to systematize some of the most successful normative solutions experimented at the national level (from the distinction of obligations imposed on platforms based on their size expressed by the number of users, to the role of monitoring bodies, still private but assisted by stringent independence requirements(5)) and some of the cornerstone institutes of disciplines, such as data protection, that intervene in the digital sector (consider impact assessments, bodies like the Board, the figure of the compliance officer adapted from the data protection officer, the function of codes of conduct, or even the structure of sanctioning norms). 

But - and here too the experience of the GDPR has been important - what matters in general political-legislative terms is, above all, the regulatory form that underlies a need for unification. This, in the awareness - claimed by the European Parliament, for example, in the Resolution of October 20, 2020, on the liability regime of artificial intelligence systems - that the regulation of the digital space requires truly uniform norms: a necessary prerequisite for digital sovereignty understood as (anthropocentric) governance of innovation. 

The very affinity of the regulatory model between the GDPR and the DSA should suggest valuing the experience gained by Data Protection Authorities, particularly in the context of...
**Cooperation and Consistency Procedures** (6) and in matters concerning the removal of illegal content such as cyberbullying (7), also assigning them the role of Digital Service Coordinator (DSC). This is due to the cross-cutting competence (and thus particularly functional for coordination purposes) of such Authorities in the digital field, arising from the intersectorality of the data protection subject matter itself. 

The identification of the DSC within bodies of such cross-cutting competence, which has a strong vocation for safeguarding fundamental rights (and in particular data protection, which constitutes the prerequisite for exercising rights in the online environment), would contribute to greater coherence and effectiveness of the multi-level governance system provided for by the DSA. 

Among the possible improvements to be made to the text, based on the indications of the European Data Protection Supervisor, is the precise clarification of the prerequisites that legitimize the use, by providers, of automated means for the identification and removal of illegal content, limiting their admissibility to the necessity of addressing systemic risks and always avoiding content moderation tools with potentially discriminatory implications or unjustified outcomes. 

This is a very delicate aspect, as the necessary accountability of providers, with the associated attribution of powers (mirroring obligations) for managing content conveyed online, requires essential regulatory criteria to protect the rights that may be affected, primarily the freedom of expression. 

It could also be emphasized the need to circumscribe the voluntary investigations of intermediary service providers aimed at identifying potentially illegal content, according to a proportional balance between the interest in combating the spread of such content and the risk of a chilling effect, which is never entirely mitigated by such forms of network monitoring. 

Furthermore, the specific offenses that impose notification to law enforcement authorities should be detailed, as well as the types of data to be disclosed in such cases. Regarding targeted advertising, while recognizing the increased transparency obligations introduced, these guarantees could be strengthened, suggesting, for example, to prohibit behavioral advertising based on pervasive monitoring, limiting the categories of data susceptible to processing for this purpose and informing about the potential exclusion of certain subjective groups from promotional communications. It would also be appropriate to provide that content recommendation systems are not, by default, based on profiling. 

Finally, an express safeguard clause in favor of the protections, both judicial and administrative, already provided by sector regulations in related matters (for example, for content removal procedures introduced by specific regulations) could be introduced.

**3. The DMA**

If the DSA aims at holding platforms accountable, making the exercise of their powers affecting users' rights transparent and thus also "accountable," the Digital Markets Act aims to promote competition by imposing specific obligations on gatekeepers (large platforms that act as intermediaries between end users and businesses), to which correspond penetrating supervisory powers of the Commission. 

In terms of data protection, particularly relevant are the obligations and prohibitions established regarding the processing of personal data, also to prevent undue concentration for antitrust purposes. The enhancement of consent as articulated in the text based on the principles of EU Regulation 2016/679, in order to ensure its effective freedom as a non-conditionality, is commendable. 

The possible improvements to be made consist of: the clarification (even only in the recitals) that Article 5 does not represent an indirect legitimization of platforms other than gatekeepers to combine personal data derived from different services in the absence of the data subject's consent; the imposition on gatekeepers of the obligation to ensure users an easy management of the consents given, as well as to guarantee the effective anonymization of data derived from online activity, with the provision of an obligation for the gatekeeper to demonstrate that it has adopted adequate measures to prevent re-identification risks in addition to suitable tools; the clarification of the scope of the right to data portability with particular reference to data generated by end users through their online activity; recalling the risks associated with the "commodification" of this right; the introduction of minimum interoperability requirements for platforms. 

Moreover, due to the close complementarity between data protection and competition, the obligation for the Commission to provide the EDPB (European Board) - or the competent data protection authorities represented therein - upon request, with useful elements for the exercise of their competencies, collected within the framework of the supervision procedure of the consumer profiling techniques applied by the gatekeeper to its core platform services (Article 13) should be foreseen. 

More generally, to strengthen the governance framework - which is less structured than the "multi-level" one of the DSA - a cooperation procedure among all the involved authorities, including data protection authorities, should be institutionalized: the Advisory Committee for Digital Markets should include, among others, representatives of the European Data Protection Committee. 

These are, of course, proposals for integration regarding texts that are already, nonetheless, innovative and commendable.
when they strive to assert rights, obligations, and responsibilities in a sector such as the digital one, which has always oscillated between almost anomic liberalism and the hegemony of surveillance, of which the Chinese dirigisme, based on the alliance between computing power, nudging, and coercion, is a significant example. It is desirable that in the continuation of the examination of the documents, as well as in the subsequent adjustment, adequate consideration is given to the aspects related to data protection, also leveraging the experience gained, particularly by the Authority, in the application of what is, to date, the most comprehensive and forward-looking regulation of the digital space.
