**VIDEO OF THE HEARING**

Hearing of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, Prof. Pasquale Stanzione - Inquiry into the nature, causes, and recent developments of the phenomenon of hate speech  
Senate of the Republic - Extraordinary Commission for the Contrast of Intolerance, Racism, Antisemitism, and Incitement to Hatred and Violence  
(13 July 2021)

1. Age of Anger and Virality of the Web  
First of all, I would like to thank the Commission for this opportunity for discussion and for the sensitivity shown regarding the implications, in terms of data protection, of the phenomenon of hate speech, which (not born with, but certainly) exacerbated by the web.  
Indeed, the web, with its structural disintermediation, has certainly, on one hand, exponentially multiplied the possibilities for free expression and access to information, representing a powerful tool for democratic progress. On the other hand, however, it has also favored a social polarization never seen before in what has been defined as the age of anger, largely due to the effect of “engagement,” the virality of sharing, and the functioning of algorithms.  
These algorithms tend, in fact, to highlight in the presentation of content those that are most attractive in terms of likes and views, generally the most extreme ones, less mediated by rational reflection and often more aggressive and discriminatory. This type of content frequently and paradoxically proves capable of aggregating consensus by pouring out on the “opportune enemy” of the moment that sense of revanchism and social envy widespread for various reasons among a large segment of the online audience. It is unnecessary to note that those who rise to the role of scapegoat are generally minorities, particularly vulnerable individuals, or those perceived for various reasons as other-than-us (due to ethnic origin, gender, religious or political beliefs, etc.). And, as always in contexts characterized by the weakening of social ties, opposition to the other reinforces, in its perception, the identity of those who exclude; it functions as an identity device as much as it is discriminatory.  
The phenomenon of hate speech is therefore evidently complex, with deep and multiple roots that imply solutions that are certainly not unilateral and far from reductionist, but based on an integrated and multidisciplinary protection strategy. Criminal protection – which, from a substantive point of view, is generally complete, except for some integrations that will be discussed – must necessarily be combined with civil protection, which is essentially remedial in nature. Public and private enforcement must converge, maximizing the standard of protection, alongside a wide range of preventive measures that address the (immediate and remote) causes of the problem and do not limit themselves to the epiphenomenon, preventing the formation of those cultural and social stereotypes that fuel it.  
The procedural aspect should not be underestimated, not so much for the lack of investigative tools but for the intrinsic difficulty of overcoming real technical obstacles, such as resorting to the deep web or systems that make it almost impossible or exceedingly difficult to identify real users or that immediately erase exchanged content. It is no coincidence that, in our legal system, the use of means aimed at preventing the identification of access data to telematic networks constitutes an aggravating circumstance, limited, however, to crimes against sexual freedom (art. 609-duodecies of the Penal Code) and which could well be extended to further cases.  
Moreover, enforcement is not easy either, to the extent that the localization in foreign states of many providers (who alone often hold the useful information for identifying the agent) makes it necessary to initiate complex judicial cooperation activities in order to obtain the essential elements for investigative purposes.

2. Possible Solutions and Internal Legislative Proposals  
In light of this intrinsic difficulty of ascertaining online offenses, it is necessary to:  
a) make remedial protection effective, especially in specific form (removal in particular), which also allows limiting the effects of the permanence online of harmful content, preventing its aggravation;  
b) hold users and providers accountable, while ensuring more accessible means of identifying the authors of offensive content;  
c) invest in digital pedagogy as awareness, on the part of all web users, of the often irreparable implications that every “click” has on the dignity of real people, even if dematerialized behind a social profile.  
Regarding the first aspect, as demonstrated by the cases of the right to be forgotten and cyberbullying, the mechanism based on the request to the provider for removal and the subsequent appeal to the Authority in case of inaction or rejection is a useful tool for protecting personality rights online. It indeed combines the need for the prompt removal of content (especially in cases of spontaneous adherence by the provider) with the reservation of the public authority for the final decision, in the presence of both parties. This is an overall balancing act that responds to the needs highlighted by the Court of Justice and the European Court of Human Rights, the latter in particular with some rulings that have even established a positive obligation on states to ensure measures suitable for protecting personal dignity (see, in particular, Delfi AS v. Estonia, 2015).  
It would certainly be appropriate, therefore, to extend the model of remedial protection entrusted to the Authority regarding cyberbullying to hate speech, thus holding platforms accountable, even under current legislation, for the offenses committed online by users and especially intervening.
**Endo in ablative sense on harmful content in tight timelines, before the game of viral sharing makes it impossible to contain its effects, as in the tragic case of Tiziana Cantone. In this sense, the Boldrini bill (AC 2936) moves appreciably, as did the Moretti-Sanna bill from the last legislature and the Zanda bill regarding misinformation.**

The Boldrini bill, in particular, based on the model of the German law on social networks, circumscribes the notion of unlawful content with reference to the types of offenses, including incitement, generally identifiable in hate speech, in order to reconcile the needs for protection and the determination of the offense. In this regard, it could also be considered to re-expand (as before the reform under Legislative Decree 101/2018) the scope of application of the crime of unlawful processing of personal data (Article 167 of the Code), which would offer broad protection against multiple forms of violation of identity and dignity, committed by abusing personal data. In this sense, among the special illegality requirements that could integrate the crime, consent should be reintroduced (removed, except for telemarketing, by the 2018 reform), thus covering multiple cases (the most frequent) of violations of individuals perpetrated online.

In any case, regarding unlawful content, the proposed law obliges platforms to adopt easy procedures for reporting and verifying the nature of such content, with particularly swift procedures, by a self-regulatory body but with the final decision-making authority of the Guarantor, in case of rejection of the removal request. Based on the jurisprudence on dynamic injunctions, it is then established that the operator has the obligation to ensure that the unlawful content removed or blocked is not republished. A partly analogous system (modeled on the law on cyberbullying) is then provided for content that, although not integrating specific criminal elements, is nonetheless harmful to dignity. In this case, removal requests—subject to decisions in particularly swift times—can also be submitted by minors over 14 years of age to the Guarantor if the request to the operator has not been resolved.

The attribution to the Guarantor of the function of making final decisions on requests not satisfied by operators ensures, in particular, that such a delicate balance as that between dignity and freedom of expression is not left to the autonomous decision of a mere private entity such as the platform. This is the most delicate point of protecting individuals (and particularly dignity) online: promoting the active cooperation of platforms, while not making them arbiters of fundamental freedoms.

**3. The Digital Services Act and the Accountability of Platforms**

Systemic innovations will derive, precisely in this respect, from the Digital Services Act: the proposed European regulation that incorporates some of the most relevant measures introduced—also regarding the procedures for removing unlawful content—by the German law on social networks, the loi Avia from last June as amended by the Conseil Constitutionnel, as well as by sectoral EU regulations. The DSA introduces several obligations, especially of a proactive nature, on online platforms, differently modulated based on the number of active users, in the sign of their preventive accountability. Reaffirming—and indeed strengthening with a new Good Samaritan Clause—the exemption from secondary liability of operators for unlawful acts committed by users on their platforms and the necessary prohibition of general and preventive monitoring of content, the DSA, however, compensates this safe harbor with regulatory obligations aimed at minimizing the risk of violations or at least containing their harmful effects. To this end, the establishment (differently modulated based on the relevance of the platform) of internal procedures for deciding on requests for removal of unlawful content or content contrary to company policies is imposed, with obligations for justification and appeal of the choices made, as well as the referral of disputes to ADR bodies with adequate independence requirements.

In this sense, the path taken by jurisprudence towards greater accountability of the operator (consider the figure of active hosting) is "positivized," aimed at preventing the network, with the force of viral sharing and the ubiquity of its services, from becoming a sounding board for the most diverse violations of individual rights. Moreover, the renunciation of introducing a form, albeit limited, of accountability for the operator risks reflecting a shift from the idea of freedom to that of anomie, ignoring that the absence of regulation, in a context devoid of any limits on the expansion of private power, does not produce equality but subordination to market imperatives.

The narrow ridge on which the Digital Services Act operates is the maintenance of the underlying option of Directive 2000/31, namely the general regime of (only) conditional liability of the operator—with the corresponding prohibition of general monitoring of content—combined, however, with a series of procedural and substantive obligations expressive of both the principle of accountability and the standard of responsibility.

In this context, the choice of the Commission not to yield to the model of objective liability of the platform for content disseminated by users is significant, while not renouncing a revision of the regulation in a more rigorous sense. The hallmark of this legislative innovation lies, therefore, in the transition from (only) liability to accountability.

The obligation to provide reasons for the decisions made and their appealability should contribute to making the exercise of removal power by platforms more transparent, which, if not adequately circumscribed and made 'visible' and subject to scrutiny, risks attributing to them an arbitrary role over rights online.  
While awaiting the approval, which is certainly not immediate, of the DSA, solutions such as those proposed by the Boldrini bill could contribute not only to anticipating that path of accountability suggested by European regulation but also to offering essential protection to the elective victims of intolerance.  
None of these interventions, however, can ever be deemed conclusive in the absence of a widespread and strategic education (in) digital matters, which plays a decisive role in preventing the various distorting phenomena of the web, including hate speech. This is a crucial aspect, especially for the education of future generations, who, despite being digital natives, still require deep awareness of the risks to which the web exposes us and from which the web itself must be liberated, so that it can return to being that space of freedom and democracy that we increasingly need.
