**Hearing of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data on the Use of New Technologies and the Internet to Counter the Epidemiological Emergency from Coronavirus**  
**Commission IX (Transport, Post and Telecommunications) of the Chamber of Deputies**  
**(April 8, 2020)**  

1. **Rights, Derogations, Limits**  
The extremely serious emergency that the country is facing has necessitated the adoption—through regulations of various ranks—of measures limiting many fundamental rights, required to hopefully contain the number of infections.  
The protection of personal data—a fundamental "freedom" right enshrined in the Charter of Nice—could not, of course, be an exception, although the limitations adopted so far are overall contained.  
Some derogations from the ordinary data management regime have been provided since the very first ordinances issued a few days after the declaration of the state of emergency, with predominant reference to the communication of health data.  
Article 14 of Decree Law 14/2020 has essentially replicated this provision, elevating its source and emphasizing its temporary nature, without, however, currently referring to particularly "innovative" data collections.  
New and more invasive data collections could be based on public health needs that—like "emergency assistance"—constitute independent grounds for legality, in the presence of a regulatory provision compliant with the principles of necessity, proportionality, adequacy, as well as respect for the essential content of the right.

2. **Epidemiological Maps and Surveillance**  
The hypothesis of collecting data on the location or interaction of mobile devices of individuals who tested positive, with other devices, in order to analyze the epidemiological trend or to reconstruct the chain of infections should be evaluated within this framework.  
First of all, since very different measures can be hypothesized, a criterion of graduality should be favored, and thus it should be assessed whether less invasive measures may be sufficient for epidemiological prevention.  
In this perspective, the acquisition of genuinely anonymous mobility trends does not pose particular problems. Article 9 of the e-Privacy Directive legitimizes the processing, even in the absence of the consent of the data subject, of location data, provided that they are anonymous.  
This solution allows for the creation, for example, of descriptive maps of the epidemic's trend, which are very useful for prognostic and statistical purposes, but less so for diagnostic purposes in the strict sense.  
On the other hand, the use of identifying data on location or interaction with other devices may be functional for different purposes.  
In any case, it requires—also pursuant to Article 15 of the e-Privacy Directive—a sufficiently detailed regulatory provision containing adequate guarantees.  
The various possible uses of such data can be theoretically aimed at (and reasoning in terms assumed by the public debate of these weeks):  
a) either to verify the position of the individual subject to a home confinement obligation because they tested positive, thus using the geolocation of the phone (which is presumed, but not guaranteed, to follow the subject step by step) to ascertain actual compliance with the prohibition of leaving the home, or:  
b) to acquire, retrospectively, data on the interaction of the subject later found positive with other subjects, to verify, during the period when they had viral capacity, any contacts ascertainable through various techniques: cell towers, GPS, Bluetooth.  
The two hypotheses differ in purpose: this element is undoubtedly relevant for assessing the overall legitimacy of the processing.  
The first hypothesis, in fact, by using the phone's location as if it were a sort of atypical electronic bracelet, presupposes the replacement, with the electronic eye, of "human" controls, but assumes that anyone who decides to violate the home confinement obligations carries their phone with them, which is evidently counterintuitive.  
Among the other measures used to verify compliance with social distancing obligations is the use, by public security authorities, of drones.  
Such tools must also be used in compliance with the principle of proportionality, especially due to their particularly invasive potential on privacy.  
If used by law enforcement, not to report "impersonal" gatherings, but to monitor the precise compliance with home confinement obligations, this measure will hardly be able to guarantee adherence to the principle of proportionality, as it may lend itself to a very broad collection of personal data.  
It would be desirable, on this point, for a regulatory clarification, considering that the reference norm generically recalls the (not entirely overlapping) needs for territorial control for public safety purposes, combating terrorism and organized crime (cf. Article 5, paragraph 3-sexies of Decree Law No. 7/2015, converted, with modifications, by Law No. 43/2015, as amended by Decree Law No. 113/2018, converted with modifications by Law No. 132/2018).

3. **Contact Tracing**  
The second hypothesis, related to the retrospective mapping of contacts held during the incubation period by individuals who tested positive, is more complex. This reconstruction of contacts can occur, at least abstractly, through the cross-referencing of different types of data: those on commercial transactions, on cell towers, and those on interaction with other mobile devices derived from the use of Bluetooth technologies.  
It should be noted that each type of this data has, of course, a different significance for epidemiological purposes, greater the more suitable it is to select the most relevant contacts because they are closer and, therefore, more susceptible.
I have determined, at least potentially, an infection.  
As we will see later, the choice of the most effective type of data also affects the overall assessment of proportionality, as greater selectivity reduces the scope of the measure's impact to only what is strictly necessary, with socially appreciable effects in terms of the protection of health, both individual and collective.  
In general terms, however, the aim pursued by this measure is particularly commendable because it is not repressive (as is the case with the surveillance of individuals in mandatory quarantine through geolocation), but rather solidaristic.  
The pursued objective would coincide with the need to subject to checks those who have potentially come into contact with an individual who tested positive for the virus or, in any case, to adopt measures useful for preventing infection.  
Thus, it would pursue that solidaristic component of the right to health as a collective interest, valued by constitutional jurisprudence on vaccination obligations.  
The use of such technology would, moreover, have few valid alternatives for the reconstruction of the epidemiological chain.  
The simple interview of the patient can, in fact, be incomplete or otherwise suffer from the lack of knowledge of many individuals with whom contact may have occurred in various contexts (in pharmacies, supermarkets, etc.).  
One element of fragility of solutions based on data acquired from phones, however, relates to the assumption that everyone carries their phone with them. While this occurs almost systematically for the younger segments of the population, it does not happen as reliably for the elderly, who should instead be the first to be contacted in case of suspected infection, in order to be treated as quickly as possible.  
“Technological” solutions are, in fact, excellent allies of epidemiological prevention action but evidently require complementary measures of a different nature, suitable for overcoming the limitations imposed, among other things, by the digital divide.  
This consideration, regarding the intrinsic limits of technological options, has a dual order of implications.  
Firstly, the assessment of the expected effectiveness of the measure cannot disregard an analysis concerning complementary actions and, therefore, the phase—which should reasonably follow—of the health assessment of individuals identified through data tracing as potential infected.  
In fact, it is possible to collect all possible data on potential carriers (whether healthy or not), but if there are no resources (and even reagents!) to ascertain their actual positivity, little progress will be made.  
Secondly, the necessity to reconstruct the chain of infections through data from electronic devices makes the imposition of a generalized obligation to use such systems problematic. This would, in fact, presuppose the possibility (not only economic but also cognitive) of using smartphones and their functionalities, which are not objectively accessible to everyone.  
Moreover, such an obligation to use would be difficult to enforce unless resorting to a true electronic bracelet.  
Even if it were deemed, as is currently being hypothesized, to activate Bluetooth directly from an app, how could one impose leaving the house only if 'accompanied' by their smartphone, which must also be sufficiently charged?  
These considerations lead to a preference for systems based on the voluntary adherence of individuals that allow for the tracking of their position. However, to ensure real freedom (and thus the validity) of consent to data processing, it should not be conditioned in any way.  
Therefore, consent given for the processing of data acquired through such systems could not be considered effectively valid, because it is unduly and inevitably conditioned, if it is envisioned as a necessary prerequisite, for example, to access certain services or goods (consider the Chinese system).  
The diagnostic effectiveness of such a solution depends, in any case, on the degree of adherence it encounters among citizens, as detection could, by definition, only occur for that part of the population that allows themselves to be "traced."  
The minimum percentage for effectiveness is estimated to be around 60%.  
And while in Singapore this solution has seen the participation of nearly the entire population, this seems primarily attributable to the specific culture and the very advanced level of digital innovation in that country.  
However, this does not exclude that adequate awareness of the opportunity to resort to such a technique, even if only for selfish reasons—namely, to be informed of having potentially and unknowingly been infected through contact with positive individuals—could instead allow for broad citizen participation.  
In this sense, therefore, the voluntary activation of an app functional to the collection of data on device interactions could well represent the basis of a regulatory framework founded on public health needs, with adequate guarantees for those involved (art. 9, p.2, lett.i) Reg. (EU) 2016/679).  
The second phase of processing (that is, the one following the data detection) essentially consists of the preservation of the same, in view of their eventual, subsequent use to alert potential infected individuals.  
This "personalization" process should occur only for those individuals who subsequently test positive and for those with whom they have had significant contact, for the sole period of potential contagiousness.

**Contact Tracing**

The "contact diary" should be maintained solely on the individual device available to the subject. This would avoid the retention of personal data in the databases of operators, which would raise the same issues identified by the jurisprudence of the CJEU regarding data retention.

The criteria of necessity, proportionality, and minimization emphasized by European jurisprudence indicate the need to limit such privacy restrictions to the strictly necessary extent to pursue relevant objectives, with the least possible sacrifice for the individuals involved.

Following this criterion, we should first consider the more selective measure preferable, which ensures the least possible reliance on identifying data, both during collection and retention phases.

In this sense, for the purposes of collection, Bluetooth, providing data on interactions that are more intimate than those identifiable in much broader cellular areas, appears to be better at selecting potential infected individuals within a more reliable sample because it is limited to significant contacts (as suggested by Singapore and Germany).

In particular, technologies that maintain the contact diary exclusively on the user's device, reasonably for the maximum potential incubation period, would be appreciated.

The subject who tests positive should provide the IMEI identifier of their device to the local health authority (ASL), which would then be required to transmit it to the central server to allow it to reconstruct, through an algorithmic calculation, the contacts held with other individuals who have similarly used the Bluetooth app.

These individuals would then receive a notification (in the form of an alert on the system) of potential contagion, with an invitation to undergo checks that, of course, will be effective to the extent that they are responsibly followed.

In this way, tracking would rely on a flow of pseudonymized data, susceptible to re-identification only in the case of confirmed positivity.

Even in such circumstances, however, the communication between the central server and the app of potential infected individuals would occur without allowing re-identification, thus minimizing the impact of the measure on individual privacy.

As an alternative to the in-app alert, it could be hypothesized that the ASL directly notifies and, therefore, subjects to checks those individuals who, based on Bluetooth readings, are found to have had significant contact with the positive subject.

The retention of contact data by the server should still be limited to the strictly necessary time for detecting potential infections.

The medical history entrusted to the doctor would then allow for that human intervention in the algorithmic process required by Regulation 2016/679 to avoid exclusive human subjection to automated decisions, also correcting possible distortions and inaccuracies.

In any case, it is desirable that the complex chain of contact tracing can be fully realized in the public sector.

However, if this is not possible and even a segment of the processing must be entrusted to private entities, they should possess suitable requirements of reliability, transparency, and controllability, rigorously verified.

It may finally be useful to foresee specific offenses that could be committed by those who, having access to the data for any operational reason, use it for other purposes.

The proposed solution would likely reduce its impact on confidentiality to the strictly necessary. However, although not massive, the processing of personal data still carried out would ideally require a primary-level norm (even a decree-law, which ensures the timeliness of the intervention, while not omitting parliamentary oversight or subsequent constitutional review, unlike ordinances).

If no ad hoc legislative intervention were to be undertaken, it would be appropriate at least to integrate Article 14 of Decree-Law 14/20, possibly with guarantee measures to be provided by a subordinate source.

The norm would also have a significant performative function, providing a general framework of rules and guarantees to which local levels should also conform. This would avoid autonomous initiatives, differentiated from area to area, which—often uncoordinated and poorly verifiable—risk weakening the overall effectiveness of the counter-strategy. This need for uniformity applies both at the internal and supranational levels. In this sense, the hope expressed by the European Data Protection Supervisor for the adoption of a single data tracing project at the European level is absolutely shareable.

Naturally, as prescribed by the Constitutional Court for emergency provisions, the temporally limited effectiveness of the norm is fundamental, to be revoked as soon as the state of necessity ends or, in any case, if practice demonstrates its limited utility (in this sense, periodic checks would be appropriate).

It is essential to establish (with the imposition of appropriate sanctions) the obligation to delete data after the potential use period has elapsed (except for retention in aggregated or otherwise anonymous form for statistical or research purposes only) and the illegality of any reuse of data for purposes other than contact tracing, as indicated above.

Thus circumscribed, the use of contact tracing could also contribute to the eventual formation of the "digital health passport."

We refer, in particular, to the various initiatives that could be adopted in the phase of resuming activities, for assessing the individual degree of epidemic risk.

Therefore, the methods and extent of the measures to be adopted should be studied in view of their effectiveness, gradualness, and adaptability.
Equality, without abstract or ideological preclusions, but also without improvisations or fanciful delegations to technology alone, of activities that are both necessary and complex.  
The key lies in the proportionality, foresight, and reasonableness of the intervention, as well as, of course, in its temporariness.  
The risk we must exorcise is that of an unconscious slide from the Korean model to the Chinese one, exchanging the renunciation of all freedom for efficiency and blind delegation to the algorithm for the saving solution.
