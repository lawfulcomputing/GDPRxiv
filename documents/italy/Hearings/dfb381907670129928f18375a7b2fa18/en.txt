Hearing of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, Prof. Pasquale Stanzione - Examination of Resolution 7-00055, concerning initiatives to counter the spread of challenges in online social networks  
Chamber of Deputies - VII Commission Culture, Science and Education  
(April 26, 2023)  
- THE VIDEO OF THE HEARING  

First of all, I would like to thank the Commission for the sensitivity shown in wanting to address, also from the perspective of personal data protection, such an important issue as the spread, on social networks, of challenges among adolescents that are sometimes even lethal. This phenomenon drew significant attention as early as 2017 with "Blue Whale," a challenge with manipulative effects and almost inciting content to suicide, which led to a conviction in Italy in 2021 for private violence and stalking. Other well-known cases include the death, in January 2021, of a ten-year-old girl from Palermo who had engaged in practices of suffocation as part of a macabre challenge on TikTok. Among the more recent cases, particular attention should be given to the "French scar," which has also spread in Italy and is the subject of the resolution under discussion.  

Naturally, social networks are not only this, and as the resolution correctly points out, their use should not be demonized nor, I add, banned outright, but rather governed with awareness. The challenges on social media do not all present self-harming or otherwise dangerous content: there are also harmless ones, such as those related to posting lists of one's favorite books or movies or photos of users as children. However, it is evident that even the mere possibility of minors being involved in "games" that could potentially be deadly, due to exposure to content with manipulative or otherwise strongly conditioning value, cannot leave institutions inactive.  

The issue of challenges on social media must be framed within the broader question of the inappropriate use of the internet and, in particular, social networks by minors. It must be regulated and managed with care, to allow young people to take advantage of the many opportunities (for knowledge, information, social relationships) offered by the internet, while ensuring safety.  

In particular, exposure of young people to inappropriate content and contexts for their level of development, including in terms of self-determination, must be avoided. It should be remembered that even in adolescents, personality is still in formation and, therefore, more susceptible to conditioning. Dramatic phenomena such as grooming, cyberbullying, revenge porn, and involvement in deadly "games" share, despite their differences, the exposure of minors to a medium, such as the internet, which, if used incorrectly or in the absence of the necessary awareness and capacity for self-determination, can have severely harmful effects.  

For a minor, it is often difficult to refuse a request to share images or, in any case, content whose harmfulness they may not always be aware of: this explains much of the dynamics of cyberbullying, revenge porn, and grooming. Many lures, in fact, begin with a request to the minor for the transfer of images or very private data in exchange for something else, with the dangerously possible outcome of meetings that are no longer just virtual. Regarding involvement in potentially self-harming challenges, there is also the additional component of initiation rites, the desire of the minor, especially if fragile, to feel accepted by the "group" by showing themselves to be uninhibited, reckless, and willing to risk, sometimes even their own life. These are, of course, well-known and typically adolescent dynamics, but with social networks and the infinite possibilities for interaction they allow, they often expand beyond the precautions set by parents in the offline world, with the selection of the circle of reference friends, contexts, and activities permitted for the minor, and their possibilities for autonomous choice.  

These dangers are, moreover, amplified exponentially the younger and, therefore, more immature the users of the platforms are: establishing the threshold for minors' autonomous access to the internet thus becomes a crucial issue to prevent the risks of "digital loneliness." Data protection regulation offers, in this regard, an important safeguard, as it identifies the age range between thirteen and sixteen years (according to the choices of individual States) as the threshold for the minor's digital consent, ensuring that below the established age, they cannot access information society services autonomously.  

The GDPR thus aimed to promote a reasonable balance between the self-determination of minors (functional to their conscious participation in the information society) and the protection of their personality against behaviors that could, in various ways, harm it. The need to recognize the adolescent (as presumed capable of discernment) a first opportunity for autonomous experience of the digital world has been balanced with specific protections granted to them both in terms of information and remedial measures.
Under the first aspect, indeed, the requirement for greater comprehensibility of the information stands out, which must be calibrated to the cognitive abilities of minors (art. 12.1). Under the second aspect, the minor's consent to processing is not only always revocable (just like the consent of an adult), but it also legitimizes a broad exercise of the right to erasure of the data collected in this way (art. 17.f GDPR), in a remedial sense. The truly informed nature of the consent on one hand, and the criterion of reversibility of the choices made by the minor on the other, thus balance, in terms of preventive and subsequent protection respectively, the extension of the minor's decision-making autonomy in an area, such as the digital projection of the person, which is increasingly relevant for today's youth.

Italy, after extensive debate, has set the threshold for the autonomous provision of consent by minors at 14 years, in analogy with other institutions that, starting from that age, allow minors to make their own determinations in certain fields.

Naturally, the peculiarities of the digital context create challenges in verifying the age of the user, which the Authority has recently focused its actions on. The numerous measures adopted in this area are, in fact, collectively aimed at ensuring that the anticipation of decision-making autonomy granted to minors does not lend itself to abuses or result in harm to the minors themselves.

The shortcomings found in TikTok's age verification system have, in particular, prompted the Authority first to promote the establishment of a task force at the European level and then, following the death of the young girl from Palermo, to initiate, in 2021, a complex procedure. This procedure, through initially prohibitory and then prescriptive measures, has raised awareness among that and other similar platforms about the need to adopt reliable age verification systems, in order to prevent the undue access of younger children, in dangerous solitude, to 'games' that are far too advanced for them. It has also been prescribed to make the information more comprehensible to such a peculiar audience, thereby enhancing the principle of transparency.

Of course, these are still evolving realities, for which age verification systems must be identified that, while effective, do not paradoxically result in the profiling of minors and that, in any case, do not anticipate control beyond the moment of the user's registration on the platform. In this direction, for example, moves the table established with the recent memorandum of understanding between the Authority and Agcom, for the promotion of a code of conduct regarding age verification systems of platforms. To minimize the risk of centralizing minors' data within the platforms, reliable third parties could also be tasked with demographic verification, capable of certifying the attainment of the necessary age for admission.

Moreover, at the European level, with the European strategy for a "Better Internet for Children," the Commission has committed, in particular, to propose, starting in 2023, a European regulation for age verification in the context of the proposal on European digital identity and to support, starting in 2024, the development of a digital age proof based on the date of birth recognized throughout the EU within the framework of the proposal on European digital identity. Furthermore, with the same act, the Commission has committed to promoting an integrated approach to the protection of minor digital users, which encourages their conscious use of online resources in safe conditions. To this end, the Commission identifies as objectives the promotion of safe digital experiences to protect minors, digital empowerment, and their active participation.

And it is precisely this convergence of actions that is the methodological key to addressing a complex problem such as the sustainable use of the web by minors, of which involvement in dangerous games is one of the most dramatic aspects. On one hand, it is essential to ensure that autonomous access to platforms is effectively prohibited for those under fourteen. On the other hand, however, even where this limit is observed, it is still necessary to protect minors, even those over fourteen, from exposure to various types of inappropriate content. One initial solution is the selection, through parental control, of content that is not freely viewable (according to solutions regulated, in particular, by the Agcom guidelines of 2023, adopted pursuant to art. 7-bis of Law Decree 28 of 2020, converted by Law 70 of 2020, and in accordance with art. 37 of Legislative Decree 208/21). The limitations of this measure are, of course, its voluntary nature, which relies on the sensitivity of parents, and its limited effectiveness regarding platforms with more varied content—input by the users themselves—such as social networks.

In any case, measures of privacy by design and by default should be enhanced, which would allow for the secure configuration of programs and devices from the outset (for example, with the parental control preset, which can only be deactivated through specific and conscious actions).

The issue of generalized protection of minors against inappropriate content is more complex, not merely relying on parental choice or limiting access to certain sites with content unsuitable for minors (because, for example, it is violent, sexually explicit, etc.). The goal, in this regard, is to achieve a sustainable balance between freedom of expression, the absence of an obligation for the provider to conduct preventive monitoring of content, and the protection of minors.
The preventive filter provided, for example, in the area of child pornography - and temporarily extended by Regulation 2021/1232 to individual communications (email and messaging) - represents a derogation from the guarantees of confidentiality of communications and from the exclusion of providers' obligations for preventive monitoring of content, as reiterated by Article 8 of the Digital Services Act (EU Regulation 2022/2065). This is a derogation limited to the matter of child pornography not only for reasons of the severity of the offense but also due to the characteristics of the content. The filter, in fact, presupposes a more reliable algorithmic selection if referred to objective parameters (such as, for example, the portion of "skin" exposed for the purposes of child pornography), and less so if related to the semantic evaluation of the content, which involves an appreciation that is somewhat discretionary and difficult to delegate to a machine.

Also in the area of revenge porn, the blocking of non-consensual uploads of intimate content is provided for, pursuant to Article 144-bis of the Code, following a decision by the Authority upon notification from a potential victim, thus not in a generalized manner and in the presence of specific evidence.

The protection of minors from exposure to inappropriate content is, outside of these cases, entrusted to the tool of removal upon notification, which is also enhanced by the Digital Services Act as a form of remedial protection suitable for reconciling the fight against the improper use of the internet and the absence of preventive monitoring. The same regulation also holds platforms accountable regarding the obligation to design the online interfaces of systems according to the highest level of privacy, security, and protection by default, including adherence to those codes of conduct that have proven effective in the area of data protection. Also significant is the prohibition of advertising based on profiling concerning minor users, which can at least partially protect them from the conditioning of microtargeting.

And it is precisely the removal of content that is the preferred solution regarding platforms, such as social networks, that are open in content, resulting from user interaction and thus difficult to classify in advance, especially when the same content may be more or less inappropriate due to variables that can only be appreciated in concrete terms. This solution is, for example, provided for content hosted on video-sharing platforms that may harm the physical, mental, or moral development of minors pursuant to Article 38, paragraph 1 of the TUSMA, from the draft regulation by Agcom currently under public consultation (n. 76/23).

But more generally, especially to limit the spread of content that encourages minors to engage in dangerous challenges, a proposal approved in the last legislature by the Senate (as an amendment to bill AS 2086) could be considered, aimed at transposing, in the area of incitement to self-harm, the specific protection granted by the Authority to minors concerning cyberbullying. The provision indeed legitimized the request (also by minors over 14 years old) to the platform operator or, in case of inaction, to the Authority, for the removal of illegal content (because, precisely, inciting violence or self-harm).

This solution can be reflected upon again, as it is capable, better than others, of balancing the need to protect minors from the risk of exposure to inappropriate content and freedom of expression.

However, no protection can ever be fully effective in the absence of a real awareness of the minor, which promotes their capacity for discernment and, therefore, self-determination, necessary to experience the digital dimension as a true resource and not as a place where one can encounter death as a result of a perverse challenge. The campaigns promoted in recent years by the Authority and Telefono Azzurro, aimed at raising parents' awareness of their role in supervising and informing their children about the correct use of the web, also aimed to promote, with the contribution of families, an essential digital literacy for young people.

Innovation policies must therefore be based primarily on a true digital pedagogy, which makes young people aware of the opportunities but also the risks that the internet exposes them to, leveraging all its extraordinary resources to be more aware, never less free.
