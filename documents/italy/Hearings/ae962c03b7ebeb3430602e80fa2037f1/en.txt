**Hearing of Antonello Soro, President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, on Government Act No. 22 (Adjustment of national legislation regarding the protection of individuals with regard to the processing of personal data)**  
before the Special Committees on Urgent Government Acts of the Senate and Chamber - Presidency Office (June 7, 2018)  
(text of the intervention)  

1. **The Regulation, the Legislative Delegation, the Decree**  
I thank the Committee for this discussion, which I am sure will be useful in improving the text of the decree that, together with the Regulation and Legislative Decree No. 51/2018 for the police and criminal justice sectors, will constitute the essential regulatory framework in which to inscribe the relationship between the individual and the digital society.  
The opportunity for discussion is all the more valuable given that the subject matter under examination is absolutely transversal, impacting nearly all sectors of private and public life: from work to health, from research to administrative transparency, from electronic communications to journalism, from justice to the protection of minors, from schools to intelligence.  
And it is precisely this last reference that provides me with the opportunity to clarify from the outset how even matters (such as intelligence or electronic communications) not strictly covered by the Regulation are instead governed within the decree, in a manner substantially unchanged from the Privacy Code, because they are not incompatible with the new European legal framework.  
The delegation that the Government was called upon to exercise was, in fact, essentially conservative in nature, allowing for the repeal of only those parts of the Code that are incompatible with the Regulation, its modification to the extent necessary to implement the provisions of the GDPR that are not directly applicable, and the general coordination with it. This choice is essentially explained by the peculiarities of the Code, which, having been issued relatively recently, already contains an extremely advanced regulation and is therefore in many respects absolutely aligned with the GDPR.  
Nonetheless, it is precisely the transversality of the subject matter and the need to ensure legal certainty that have made a broad intervention necessary, informed by the directive criterion of reorganization that Article 32 of Law No. 234 of 2012 generally admits for legislative delegations provided for by the European delegation law.  
Moreover, the numerous flexibility clauses contained in the Regulation make the intervention of the national legislator even more decisive, placing upon it the responsibility of balancing the legal interests involved.  
In this perspective, the draft decree should be read, also taking into account the further complexity determined by the overlap of regulations in this area and, in particular, the recent decree implementing Directive 680, which has removed from the Code the regulation of processing for police and criminal justice purposes, whereas the processing of personal data carried out in the exercise of judicial functions remains governed by the Regulation and, therefore, by the decree under examination.  
Also for these reasons, in many parts, the text is susceptible to further coordination, which could provide greater legal certainty to the subject matter as a whole. The opinion of the Authority has also provided various indications on this aspect, to which I will limit myself to alluding.  

2. **The Opinion of the Authority**  
First of all, reiterating what has already been observed in the opinion on the legislative decree implementing Directive 680/2016, we have emphasized the incompatibility of the norm – whose validity is confirmed by AG 22 – regarding the retention of telephone traffic data (including unanswered calls) and telematic data for 72 months, introduced in derogation of the provisions of the Code by Law No. 167 of 2017. The provision for the retention, for seventy-two months indiscriminately of telephone and telematic logs, although functional to their acquisition only in proceedings for district crimes, seems to conflict with the principle of proportionality between the limitation of privacy and investigative needs. Based on a strong reading of this principle, the Court of Justice of the EU, with the judgments Digital Rights Ireland of April 8, 2014, and Tele2-Watson of December 21, 2016, annulled Directive 2006/24 and declared the Swedish regulation incompatible with European law, even in the face of provisions for much shorter data retention periods than those of Law No. 167. The Court also subordinated the admissibility of data retention to the existence of individualizing requirements, so that the measure concerns only those subjects involved, to some extent, in criminal activities of a certain severity and prior to adequate temporal delimitation of the duration of retention.  
We have therefore requested the Government to assess the appropriateness of repealing the aforementioned norm, hopefully also reviewing – as we emphasized in the opinion on the decree implementing Directive 680 – the regulation established by Article 132 of the Code, in order to ensure full compliance of the internal legal system with the new European legal framework.  
In order to ensure compliance of the legal system with the principles of the Regulation, we have requested a modification of the norm of the CAD (inserted by Legislative Decree No. 217/2017) establishing the National Digital Data Platform, which, as it aims to promote the sharing of databases of public entities, legitimizes a duplication of such archives (containing often sensitive data) of dubious compatibility with the principles of minimization, necessity, and proportionality of processing. We have therefore suggested modifying the relevant regulation to replace the sharing of databases with the communication of data.
**Notification of Information, with express clarification of the prohibition on duplicating archives of public entities containing personal data.**

Regarding criminal offenses, while being aware of the need to avoid any possible violation of the ne bis in idem principle, we considered the suppression of cases of unlawful processing of personal data aimed at causing harm rather than mere profit (consider the case of Tiziana Cantone, revenge porn, etc.) to be risky, as they express a disvalue that is not lesser (indeed generally greater) than the latter. We therefore requested the Government to evaluate the opportunity to include in the subjective element underlying the offenses referred to in Articles 167, 167-bis, and 167-ter, the intent to cause harm. In order to avoid unjustified disparities in treatment and an unreasonable limitation of the subjective scope of application of the crime of unlawful communication and dissemination, we suggested configuring it not as a specific crime but as a common crime, similar to the other incriminating norms provided by the Code and by Legislative Decree 51/2018. It remains understood, of course, that possible active subjects of the crime can only be those who, by virtue of their role concerning the processing, are required to comply with specific provisions, the violation of which, characterized by intent, constitutes the elements of the crime.

We finally invited consideration of the questionable reasonableness of the repeal of the crime of non-compliance with the measures of the Guarantor, in light of the simultaneous introduction of a corresponding crime in Legislative Decree 51/2018, limited to processing carried out for criminal justice and police purposes. This disparity in treatment raises concerns regarding compliance with the principle of equality-reasonableness, since the same conduct, harmful to the same legal asset (the full effectiveness of the functions of the Guarantor), would be subject to two extremely different sanctioning regimes, solely due to the subjective nature of the holder and the context in which the processing is carried out (police activity or criminal justice, or any other area). These elements are insufficient to justify, by themselves, such differing sanctioning regimes. We therefore suggested – should the decision be made to maintain the validity of Article 170 – to identify the specific measures of the Guarantor whose non-compliance constitutes the elements of the crime, in order to confer greater specificity to the offense.

Regarding administrative offenses, we suggested including among the sanctionable conducts also: a) the failure to carry out the impact assessment and the failure to activate, where necessary, the prior consultation of the Authority, in cases where the processing of health data for medical, biomedical, and epidemiological research is carried out without the consent of the data subjects; b) the further processing of data for scientific research or statistical purposes, in the absence of prior authorization from the Guarantor or in violation of the same; c) the failure to respond to requests for information or the presentation of documents to the Guarantor (which is already sanctioned today). These are important obligations, the omission of which – capable of undermining the control powers of the Authority essential for the legitimacy of the processing – should be sanctioned also for deterrent purposes, in accordance with the systematic provisions of current legislation.

We also requested to include, among the conduct to be sanctioned (and currently sanctioned but which otherwise, due to normative references to repealed norms, would no longer be), the carrying out of processing different from those provided for in the statistical research project, the undue communication to third parties, the retention beyond the duration of the project, and the dissemination of the data themselves.

As for the regulatory provisions – which pursuant to Article 2-sexies represent one of the possible legal bases for the processing of sensitive, genetic, biometric data for reasons of significant public interest – we requested to foresee, in accordance with current legislation and utilizing the margins allowed by the GDPR for the recognition of additional guarantees, the favorable opinion of the Guarantor on the relevant regulatory schemes, as well as the integration of the subject matter of such normative acts with reference to the specific measures for the protection of data subjects required by European legislation.

In order to allow scientific research on genetic data while ensuring the necessary guarantees for the data subjects, we requested to permit the further processing of such data for research purposes, based on the authorization (even general) of the Guarantor.

Finally, we requested to extend to the judiciary in the exercise of jurisdictional functions other than criminal ones the obligation to designate the data protection officer, consistently with the choice made during the transposition of Directive 2016/680, regarding processing carried out by the judiciary in criminal matters.

**3. The Critical Issues Identified During Parliamentary Examination**

Regarding the critical issues of the decree, identified in previous sessions of the Commission, I would like to emphasize how some of them coincide with certain observations contained in our opinion. 

For example, the reference, not entirely clear, to the date of March 21 as the deadline for the facilitated definition of sanctioning proceedings (Article 18) should, in our view, be more correctly identified as the date of actual application of the Regulation (May 25, 2018).

We also suggested, in the opinion, the opportunity – shared by some interventions – to replace mere communication with notification of the administrative violation contestation (Article 166, paragraph 6).

As for the doubt that paragraph 5 of Article 166 imposes a necessarily...
**Unique for the exercise of prescriptive and sanctioning power**, it can certainly be clarified that the adoption of the relevant measures can be joint or separate. However, the necessity to prepare a procedure that allows for the separate application of prescriptions and sanctions is explicitly stated and made necessary by the regulation referred to in Article 83, paragraph 2, which, while admitting the possible alternation between prescriptive (or prohibitory) and sanctioning measures, also includes compliance with the former among the parameters for evaluating the "whether" (as well as the "quantum") of the sanction.

Regarding the introduction of additional conditions for the processing of biometric, genetic, and health-related data, this is expressly permitted by Article 9.4 of the GDPR, which for this sub-category of "special" data allows for further enhanced protection, which has been utilized by most member countries.

In the opinion, we requested clarification that consent (as an additional measure of guarantee for the data subject (in the sense accepted by the WP Art. 29 in the opinion on the limitation of purpose)) can be provided—due to the peculiarities of the treatment relevant from time to time—only for genetic data, due to the particular characteristics of such information (predictive, shared by family branch, and revealing extremely delicate aspects of existence, such as paternity).

Finally, it could be considered to expressly include, within the category of data related to criminal convictions and offenses, data related to the applicability of preventive measures, which are currently included within judicial data and certainly deserving of enhanced protection, equal to that recognized for other data referred to in Article 10, with an extensive interpretation of its application scope to measures that not all member states recognize.

Regarding the objections raised by some of the audited subjects, I will limit myself to highlighting some of the most frequently discussed aspects.

In relation to the incriminating norms, beyond the improvements suggested in our opinion, I focus on the criticisms expressed regarding the requirement used in a selective function, in the cases referred to in Articles 167-bis and ter, of the "relevant number of people" whose data are processed. 

Although it can certainly be improved in terms of determinacy, it nonetheless has precedents in the legal system, not only as an aggravating circumstance (Article 80, paragraph 2, consolidated text on narcotics, large quantity) but also as a constitutive element of the offense, in Article 501-bis of the Penal Code, concerning the "relevant quantities" of goods. 

This is, after all, a useful element—like others, such as specific intent or harm as an event of the crime—to narrow the scope of application of the incriminating norm to conduct characterized by greater social disvalue. This is in compliance not only with the principles of fragmentation and subsidiarity of criminal norms but also with the ne bis in idem, thus avoiding the risk of overlap with administrative offenses.

Regarding the procedure for the imposition of administrative sanctions, some of the audited subjects raised the absence of contradiction, which is, however, expressly provided (as it is today) with the possibility of submitting briefs and requesting hearings, as provided by Article 166, paragraph 7. A similar possibility for the presentation of defensive briefs is expressly provided by Article 18, paragraph 4, within the procedure to be followed, in a transitional phase, for the facilitated resolution of violations contested prior to the date of entry into force of the decree.

As for the lack of provision for a minimum penalty (noting that it is not at all new to the legal system, and beyond any assessment of its compatibility with the GDPR), in the systematic framework of the Regulation, a continuum is outlined between prohibitory, prescriptive, and monitoring measures on one side and monetary administrative sanctions on the other. Therefore, the minimum measure applicable in the face of an offense is to be identified in those referred to in Article 58, paragraph 2, according to the gradation indicated there, while the actual amount of the monetary administrative sanction that should be imposed will be determined based on the parameters specifically indicated by Article 83, paragraph 2, GDPR.

In this sense, there is no constitutional illegitimacy as the guaranteeing function of the penalty framework (aimed at circumscribing the discretion of the body called to impose the sanction) remains unaffected, since the choice of the Guarantor on the "whether" and the "quantum" of the sanction (thus also on the infra-penal framework) is strictly bound by the stringent parameters of Article 83, paragraph 2 of the Regulation.

Moreover, the provision of a minimum penalty in the face of such a high maximum (designed specifically to apply to big tech) risks being either excessively high (and thus disproportionate for offenses characterized by modest disvalue but for which non-monetary measures are nonetheless insufficient) or sufficiently reduced but such as to excessively widen the gap of the penalty framework between minimum and maximum.

In any case, the lack of provision for a minimum penalty does not result in the absence of reward measures for those who wish to access reduced payment, because Article 166, paragraph 9, allows for the resolution of the dispute through compliance with the Guarantor's prescriptions and payment of half of the imposed sanction. Therefore, the amount to be paid to extinguish the procedure is fixed not with reference to the abstract penalty framework but to the amount actually imposed, thus allowing for a better adjustment of the sanction to be applied in a facilitated manner to the peculiarities of the specific case.

**The allocation to the fund**
for the functioning of the Authority of half of the proceeds derived from the application of monetary sanctions, in the absence of the current earmarking requirement (particularly in favor of inspection activities), has become necessary due to the need for self-financing of the Guarantor (which is, moreover, peacefully recognized by other Authorities), whose increasingly numerous functions have been provided without an increase in burdens, thus in the absence of the necessary funding. Naturally, if alternative resources were identified to be allocated to the Guarantor, there would be no objection to reintroducing the earmarking of the sums derived from the collection of sanctions, although the expansion of the possibilities for using the proceeds from sanctions allows the Authority to also enhance functions other than those related to inspections, including adopting guidelines or prescriptive measures suitable for facilitating compliance with the GDPR.

However, one cannot hypothesize a possible distortion of the exercise of sanctioning power for instrumental purposes and mere financial interest, not only due to the criminal relevance of such conduct but especially because of the rigor and sense of responsibility with which this Authority has always exercised its sanctioning power.

Regarding the absence of technical defense in the administrative procedure aimed at resolving complaints, I would like to clarify that, although not mandatory, it is always possible, thus it does not entail any limitation of the right to defense just as it does not today with respect to appeals, which are equally alternative to judicial protection.

On the contrary, the provision of necessary technical defense would constitute a limitation on the possibilities of exercising rights that is incompatible with the regime of broad accessibility to means of protection, as established by Article 77 of the GDPR, as well as an additional burden for citizens and businesses.

Regarding the request for the repeal of paragraphs 1022 and 1023 of the budget law, we propose in the opinion instead to restrict their application, in the manner provided for in Article 36 of the GDPR, to only those processing activities functional to the authorization for changing the personal details of minors, deserving of further guarantees, thus making such norms fully compliant with the Regulation.

The invoked period of abstention from the exercise of sanctioning power would entail a sort of legitimization of even very serious violations that is certainly incompatible with the guarantees provided by the Regulation and, more generally, with the principle of legality.

However, we will certainly value the criteria of graduality and proportionality established on this point by the Regulation in order to guide inspection and sanctioning activities towards violations that are most deserving of attention, in terms of concentration and nature of the data.

Finally, regarding the norms applicable to micro, small, or medium enterprises, within the framework of the Wp art. 29 (the coordinating body of the data protection authorities of the Member States, now replaced by the European Data Protection Board), we had proposed an extensive interpretation of the exemptions to the rule on the register of processing activities, which, however, was not accepted.

The possibility of indicating, by the DPAs, simplification measures in favor of these categories has been admitted, which the Guarantor intends to enhance, within the spaces allowed by the norm, pursuant to Article 22, paragraph 10, of the decree.

But, as suggested by some of those heard, a path to follow could be to identify, with codes of conduct, specific methods of applying the obligations within the relevant category, enhancing the contribution provided by trade associations and thus facilitating compliance with obligations by these business realities.

The text can, therefore, certainly improve, but it is important that it is approved as soon as possible, in order to provide the data protection regulation with the necessary completeness and legal certainty, the lack of which risks otherwise disorienting companies, administrations, and citizens themselves.
