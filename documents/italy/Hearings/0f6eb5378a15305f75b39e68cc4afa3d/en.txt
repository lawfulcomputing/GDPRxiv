Informal hearing of Antonello Soro, President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, on Bill No. 920, containing measures for the effectiveness of actions by public administrations and the prevention of absenteeism, before the 11th Committee on Public and Private Employment, Social Security of the Senate of the Republic (November 27, 2018)  
(text of the intervention)  

1. Necessity and proportionality in the limitation of fundamental rights  
I thank the Committee for this opportunity for discussion on a topic whose complexity we have already had the chance to highlight in the opinion provided at the request of the Government. In this context, it will be possible to further explore (in particular, Article 2), aimed at ensuring full compliance of Member States' legislation with European regulations on data protection, which is precisely the purpose of the Authority's opinion on legislative acts, as mandated by the General Data Protection Regulation (hereinafter: Regulation) and, for the field of criminal justice and police, by Directive 2016/680.  
This is an important provision that demonstrates how the legislative power itself is bound by the observance of the principles established in the field of data protection by European sources. The rationale for the mandatory opinion of the Authority on primary legislation that may affect the right to personal data protection lies precisely here. In the need, that is, to ensure that the national legislator respects the necessary conditions and limits, according to the European standard, to guarantee the proper balancing between the right to data protection and the legal goods that may be relevant from time to time.  
As is always the case with fundamental rights, this balancing must be conducted according to the principle of proportionality, which, particularly in the field of data protection, has assumed a central role precisely due to the need to guarantee that essential "social function" referred to in Recital 4 of the Regulation.  
In the context of data protection, in fact, the principle of proportionality assumes a dual function. On one hand, it serves as an essential regulatory criterion in the performance of processing by the data controller, particularly concerning the choice of the quantity and manner of the data processed. On the other hand, the principle of proportionality represents a general legitimacy parameter for the limitations of the right to data protection, to be observed - in accordance with the standards set forth in Article 52 of the Charter of Nice - also in the exercise of legislative power.  
Pursuant to the aforementioned Article 52, paragraph 1, limitations on the exercise of the rights and freedoms recognized by the Charter and, consequently, on the exercise of the right to personal data protection, are indeed admissible only if they are:  

- provided for by law;  
- respectful of the essential content of the right to data protection;  
- necessary in accordance with the principle of proportionality;  
- responding to objectives of general interest recognized by the Union or to the need to protect the rights and freedoms of others.
As for the Council of Europe, Article 5, paragraph 1 of Convention No. 108 of 1981, as recently amended, establishes a requirement of proportionality for the processing of personal data in relation to the legitimate purpose pursued, demanding a fair balance between the interests at stake, at all stages of processing. This means that “personal data that would be adequate and relevant, but would imply a disproportionate interference with the rights and fundamental freedoms at stake, must be considered excessive” (1).

The Regulation also relates the principle of proportionality to the very legal provision of processing, which must indeed be proportionate to the legitimate objective pursued, based on a principle established by a consolidated jurisprudence of the Court of Justice and the European Court of Human Rights (Article 6, paragraph 3, letter b); see Court of Justice, November 9, 2010, joined cases C-92/09 and C-93/09, Volker and Markus Schecke; April 8, 2004, C-203/12 and C-594/12, Digital Rights Ireland; December 21, 2016, Tele2 Sverige, C-203/15 and 698/15, as well as, regarding the European Court of Human Rights, concerning the principle of proportionality in general, see, in particular, Section II, judgment of November 28, 2017, application no. 70838-13; Grand Chamber, September 5, 2017, Bărbulescu v. Romania (no. 61496/08).

These provisions thus articulate within the Regulation the criterion of “necessity in compliance with the principle of proportionality” to which Article 52 of the Charter of Nice subordinates the legitimacy of limitations on the rights it enshrines, among which is the right to the protection of personal data.

Also concerning the legislative proposal under examination, it is therefore crucial to ascertain whether the balance outlined between data protection and conflicting interests (in this case, the legitimate needs to combat absenteeism in public administrations) can be considered compliant with the principle of necessity and proportionality and such as not to infringe the essential content of that fundamental “freedom” right, as prescribed by Article 52 of the Charter of Nice.

2. Biometric Data in the Regulation

In light of this parameter, the provision of Article 2, paragraph 1, letter d, must be read, which imposes on all public administrations the obligation to introduce - except for personnel under public law and for agile work (2) - biometric identity verification systems and video surveillance in place of the various automatic detection systems currently in use, for the purpose of verifying compliance with working hours and, therefore, combating the phenomenon of false attendance at work.

The implementation modalities of the regulation are entrusted to regulations to be adopted (with a decree from the Ministry of Education for school personnel, with a Prime Ministerial Decree for any other administration), subject to the opinion of the Data Protection Authority on the modalities of processing biometric data, in compliance with the provisions of Article 9 of the Regulation and the guarantee measures defined by the Authority pursuant to Article 2-septies of the Code on the protection of personal data (Legislative Decree 196/2003, as amended by Legislative Decree 101/2018, which adapts the domestic legal system to the GDPR). As specified in the Report, these regulations will also establish the specific technology to be used for the intended purposes.

The regulation therefore provides, through the simultaneous (and not alternative) use of the relevant systems, for the processing of both personal data such as the image of the person (through the use of video surveillance tools) and biometric data. This type of personal data is classified by the new European legal framework among the special categories of personal data that are granted enhanced protection, due to the risks (of discrimination, in particular) to which their improper use may expose the data subject.

The balance between the needs for certain identification for various purposes and the protection of such significant personal data has been a topic on which the Authority has had the opportunity to express itself, thus far, in various contexts, recalling in any case the necessity to respect the principles of proportionality, non-excessiveness, necessity of the processed data, and verifying the possibility of adopting less invasive alternative solutions to achieve the intended result.

However, the new European legal framework has made this scrutiny of proportionality and legitimacy, in a broader sense, of the processing even more stringent, bringing - with the implications that we will illustrate - biometric data into those special categories of personal data, which are recognized as having greater guarantees.

For these data, in fact, the GDPR generally establishes a prohibition on processing, which can only be overcome in the presence of certain conditions, including, indeed, the necessity for the data controller to fulfill a legal obligation or to perform a task in the public interest or related to the exercise of public powers or, specifically for biometric data, the necessity of processing for the fulfillment of obligations and the exercise of specific rights (of the data controller or the data subject themselves) in labor law, to the extent that it is authorized “by the law of the Member States,” in the presence of appropriate safeguards for the fundamental rights and interests of the data subject (Article 6, paragraph 1, letters c) and e), 3, and Article 9, paragraph 2, letter b), Regulation).

The same Regulation then provides for a specific national legal reserve for the regulation of employment relationships, allowing each Member State to provide for “more specific rules” in this area, including “appropriate and specific measures to safeguard human dignity, legitimate interests, and the fundamental rights of the data subjects” (Article 88, paragraphs 1 and 2, Regulation).

In general terms, therefore,
**The Legitimacy Requirements for the Processing of Biometric Data**

The legitimacy requirements for the processing of biometric data, including (but not limited to) in the workplace, relate to the existence of a specific regulatory provision (of legislative or regulatory rank depending on the case), the necessity of the processing for achieving the legitimate purposes pursued, as well as compliance with appropriate safeguards. This latter requirement has been emphasized by the Italian legislator, in the context of alignment with the GDPR, through the provision of a general measure from the Guarantor that outlines the necessary safeguards for the legitimacy of the processing of genetic, biometric, and health-related data, exercising the flexibility margin granted by the European legislator.

**3. The Provisions of the Draft Law in Light of the GDPR**

In this framework, the proposed regulation in the draft law must be assessed for its compatibility with the provisions of the Regulation, which constitute, as is known, parameters of legitimacy for domestic legislation in accordance with the European legal order, also for the purposes of the review under Article 117, first paragraph, of the Constitution. 

The legislative provision for processing, detailed by the implementing regulations, along with the obligation to comply with the safeguards established by the Guarantor under Article 2-septies, meets the requirements set forth by the Regulation, in terms of the abstract suitability of the source to introduce such limitations on the right to data protection. However, in substance, the provision does not appear compatible with the principles of necessity and proportionality.

From one perspective, in fact, the requirement for the simultaneous mandatory use of two systems to verify compliance with working hours (collection of biometric data and video surveillance) exceeds the limits imposed by the strict necessity of processing concerning the pursued aim. If, indeed, the premise for introducing such an invasive attendance verification system as the biometric one is its perceived effectiveness and reliability, it necessarily follows that the concurrent use of video surveillance is superfluous, as it could add nothing in terms of countering evasive phenomena. In this regard, therefore, the need for an alternative provision of these systems for verifying compliance with working hours is reiterated.

On the other hand, the hypothesized systematic, generalized, and undifferentiated introduction of biometric identification systems for attendance tracking across all public administrations does not appear to comply with the principle of proportionality—as defined by European jurisprudence—due to the constraints imposed by the European legal order on this matter, owing to the invasiveness of such forms of verification and the implications arising from the particular nature of the data.

For the purposes of respecting the principles of necessity and proportionality (which are applicable even in the presence of a legislative or regulatory provision for processing), the use of such biometric detection systems must be made residual, applicable only where other attendance detection systems are not suitable for the pursued objectives.

The necessity of limiting measures on the right to data protection is, in fact, a principle that the Court of Justice (as well as the European Court of Human Rights) has repeatedly emphasized, allowing for more invasive measures only in the face of the inadequacy of less restrictive systems for the purpose, since "derogations and restrictions" on fundamental rights must occur "within the limits of what is strictly necessary" (CJEU, C-362/14, Maximillian Schrems v. Data Protection Commissioner [GC], October 6, 2015). In the Schecke ruling, for example, the Court of Justice partially annulled a European regulation that mandated the publication of the names of beneficiaries of EU agricultural subsidies and the amounts they received for violating the principles of proportionality and necessity, as equally effective but less invasive measures were conceivable.

Compliance with the principle of proportionality—understood as the justification for limiting the right to data protection in the presence of concrete needs in this regard—also requires anchoring the use of such detection systems to the existence of specific risk factors or particular prerequisites, such as the size of the entity, the number of employees involved, and the recurrence of critical situations that could also be influenced by the environmental context. The specification of such factors could be delegated to the implementing regulations, on which the Guarantor will need to express its opinion.

The AIR (Regulatory Impact Assessment) highlights, in this regard, the need to counter a phenomenon such as false attendance reporting, which is undoubtedly serious but for which there does not seem to be consistent data regarding its systematic and generalized spread in public administrations. In fact, it is noted that only 10% of disciplinary dismissals in the last year stemmed from false attendance reporting; a figure that is certainly significant but not indicative of the general pervasiveness of the phenomenon or sufficient to justify the adoption of such an invasive attendance detection system in each public administration.

Therefore, the abstract, generalized, and undifferentiated legal presumption of the existence, for all public administrations, of risk factors that would make biometric systems the only means capable of ensuring compliance with working hours does not appear compatible with the principle of proportionality.

To achieve the commendable goal of combating absenteeism and false attendance reporting, it should...
therefore, to resort to measures that are less restrictive of the right to data protection, using biometric detection systems, in the presence of specific risk factors, when less invasive solutions must reasonably be considered inadequate for the purpose.
