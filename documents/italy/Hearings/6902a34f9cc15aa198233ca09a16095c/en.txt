**Informal Hearing of Antonello Soro, President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data, in the context of the examination in the relevant committee of the bill C. 1189 Government, concerning “Measures to combat crimes against public administration and regarding the transparency of political parties and movements” before the Joint Committees I Constitutional Affairs and II Justice of the Chamber of Deputies (October 10, 2018) (text of the intervention)**

1. **General Profiles**  
I thank the Committees for the opportunity for this discussion, which will focus solely on the second Chapter of the bill, as it is the subject of more specific competence of the Authority.  
The text regulates a theme that is both complex and of primary importance for democracy, such as the transparency of the financing of political formations and their activities.  
Transparency is necessary for the fairness of political competition and for the informed exercise of the right to vote and the freedom of political association by citizens, who have the right to know of any co-interests between parties and various entities that support their actions.  
This awareness is, moreover, a natural implication of the necessarily democratic character of political association protected by Article 49 of the Constitution, precisely because, through parties, citizens can participate “in determining national policy,” using a democratic method.  
And democracy is, first and foremost, as is well known, the governance of visible power.  
Opacity in the financing of parties has represented, moreover, one of the main factors of their delegitimization and, therefore, of the crisis of trust of citizens in representative institutions and in democratic mechanisms, of which political association is a necessary prerequisite.  
Norms aimed at promoting the necessary degree of transparency in the internal life of parties and, in particular, their sources of financing, are therefore undoubtedly appropriate. However, such regulation must reconcile, in the fairest possible manner, this need with respect for the right to the protection of personal data, enshrined as fundamental by primary European law and articulated in a legal framework (General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and Directive (EU) 2016/680 on police and criminal justice) that, due to the very nature of the source, constrains in many parts the same internal legislative discretion.  
It is therefore necessary to delineate the limit beyond which the publicity of the subsidies granted to political formations undermines the very core of the fundamental right to the protection of data, which is inalienable due to the express general provision of the Charter of Nice (Article 52).  
The right to the protection of personal data is, moreover, a necessary prerequisite for democracy as it allows for the free expression of personality even in the social formations where it takes place, to cite Article 2 of the Constitution to which dignity and the right to privacy have traditionally been linked, the former being functional.  
What comes into play in the matter under examination is, moreover, a personal data that is subject to special protection. Information about contributions made to a specific political party or movement (or, as we will see later, also to politically oriented foundations) can indeed express, to some extent, the political orientation of the individual concerned, their affinity to certain political formations.  
Therefore, the data relating to the contribution made to parties and movements has also been considered, in the past, as sensitive data and as such deserving of the enhanced protection granted to this category of data by the legislation in force for over twenty years: Directive 95/46, Law 675/96, Legislative Decree 196/2003 (hereinafter: Code) and now, indeed, the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (hereinafter: Reg. 679).  
The rationale for this enhanced protection must be identified, moreover, in the need to allow for the free exercise of democratic freedoms, shielded from the risk of discrimination or stigmatization motivated, precisely, by political or ideological aversion, etc., which could be fueled by the indiscriminate publicity of information about the beliefs of individual citizens.  
Not by chance, Directive 95/46 and the Code before, as well as the Regulation (Article 9, paragraph 2, letter d) now, have always allowed the intra-associative circulation of such sensitive data, even disregarding the consent of the individual concerned, with the only limit, indeed, of dissemination or communication to the outside, precisely to avoid the risks of discrimination inherent in the indiscriminate publicity of the data in question.  
Naturally, this does not mean that the data protection regulation generally opposes the publicity of information on the financing of parties.  
However, precisely because of the particular nature of such personal data, the relevant regulation must be able to strike the balance, at the highest point, between the transparency needs of the (financial) management of parties and the right to the protection of the data of the donor. Which - it is worth reiterating - is protected, to the extent that it is intended to be established, only if a natural person, since legal entities do not represent subjects of law, in the strict sense, for the purposes of data protection regulation.  
In this perspective, the confidentiality of the activist must therefore be protected, who could - for fear of retaliation or discrimination, perhaps even in the workplace, resulting from the indiscriminate publicity of the data on the subsidy granted - be induced to refrain from such a form of contribution.  
Naturally, the threshold must be established to distinguish the modest donation of the activist aimed at contributing to the life of the political formation to which they feel they belong, from the systematic, or otherwise significant, financing of parties, which deserves...
**instead advertising to ensure transparency regarding relationships in the broad sense of “debtors” and the co-interests that may influence the same political line followed.**  
It remains understood that, in any case, where it concerns ascertaining any illicit activities in the provision of contributions, for example in the form of money laundering of proceeds from crime, the judicial authority will certainly not encounter the limits imposed by transparency regulations.  
Also in the investigative context, moreover, data protection guarantees are ensured by the obligation – also imposed on investigative bodies – to comply with the provisions of Directive 2016/680 (implemented by us with Legislative Decree No. 51 of 2018), which has significantly regulated, from a privacy perspective, not only police and judicial cooperation (as per the previous framework decision) but also the very internal investigative scope.  

**2. The proposed regulation**  
Examining, therefore, from a forward-looking perspective, the provisions of the draft law, we can formulate in extreme summary these indications.  
The planned disclosure, on the website of the beneficiary party, as well as in some cases of Parliament, of the identity of the author of donations exceeding the threshold of 500 euros, as well as the amount of the contribution, constitutes a processing (in the more invasive form of online dissemination) of data belonging to the special categories referred to in Article 9 of Regulation 679. From the perspective of the legal basis, it can be traced back to the category referred to in Article 9, paragraph 2, letter g), that is, the necessity of processing for reasons of significant public interest, based on an express provision contained, also, in national (legislative or regulatory) law (in this sense, the reference to the irrelevance of consent should be removed, as it is extraneous and misleading).  
Such a regulatory provision - specifies Article 9, paragraph 2, letter g) - must be proportionate to the pursued aim (the principle of proportionality is, that is, related to the same normative presupposition), must respect the essence of the right and provide appropriate and specific measures to protect the rights of the data subject (see, in this sense, also Article 2-sexies of the Code and in particular paragraph 2, letter f).  
If the relevance (required pursuant to the aforementioned Article 2-sexies) of the public interest pursued – that is, the transparency of party financing, in view of the democratic control to be ensured – is in this case undeniable, from the perspective of proportionality concerning the pursued aim, the norm could be refined.  
One of the possible solutions could be to modulate differently the methods of fulfilling this publication obligation. In this sense, a shorter deadline for publication on the internet could be expressly indicated, in contrast to a much longer retention of the register kept by the party, containing the annotations of donations with the same information.  
Still from the perspective of proportionality, regarding the identification of the threshold beyond which the obligation of publication is triggered: it implies a discretionary assessment by the legislator, who must verify within which limits the confidentiality of the donor should be protected. To this end, it may be useful to consider, for example, that EU Regulation 2014/1141 (as amended by Regulation 2018/673), regarding the transparency of financing of European parties, outlines in this sense a gradation of publicity obligations proportional to the amount of the contribution.  
In particular, the regulation qualifies as small donations (and thus exempt from the obligation of publication) those of an amount less than 1,500 euros. For contributions exceeding this amount but less than 3,000 euros, online publication (on a dedicated portal of the European Parliament) is instead subject to the obligation of consent, while a strict obligation of publication, regardless of the consent of the data subject, is established only for grants exceeding 3,000 euros.  
The aforementioned regulation then provides specific obligations regarding data retention (possible for a maximum of two years from the start of publication), prohibition of use for other purposes, adoption of technical and organizational measures suitable to prevent any form of unlawful processing, compensation for damage from unlawful processing.  
Regarding the provision of appropriate and specific measures to protect the data subject, it should therefore also be provided, in the draft law, for a mandatory publication deadline strictly commensurate and not exceeding the pursued aims, as well as methods of fulfilling the publicity obligation in a non-indexable form and protected from the risk of alteration, copying, etc.  
Such guarantees could also be provided by an implementing regulation to be issued upon the opinion of the Guarantor.  
The proposed extension to foundations, associations, or committees of a political nature of the transparency and reporting obligations provided for parties can be considered reasonable, to the extent that it subjects entities, such as political foundations, which often actively support party initiatives, to the same transparency regime provided for parties. In this sense, the aforementioned European Regulation also supports this, which extends the publicity obligations provided for parties to “political” foundations, which are, however, subject to specific registration with the Authority for European political parties and foundations.  
If, therefore, this expansion of publicity obligations seems justifiable due to the identity of the pursued aim, the scope of application of the norm must, however, be adequately circumscribed, selecting – within the varied associative realm – the entities that actually play an active supportive role for parties and movements.

**1. Registration and Legal Certainty**

The registration, the necessary certainty of law in relation to the obligations of transparency to be observed, minimizes the risk of an excessively broad interpretation of the norm, which would end up subjecting to the prescribed publicity obligations even foundations lacking actual political nature. 

**2. Definition of Political Connection Indicators**

Therefore, also in order to ensure the necessary respect for the principle of proportionality, it is appropriate to better define the indicators of connection with politics that, according to the norm, allow for attributing political nature to foundations, associations, committees (and thus extending to them the obligations of transparency). In this sense, in particular, it should be clarified whether the character of certain criteria, described with broad formulas, is mandatory or merely exemplary, or otherwise more precisely define certain parameters, such as, for example, the membership of a party by the holders of institutional positions within the entity.

**3. Mandatory Disclosure of Criminal Records**

The provision for the mandatory dissemination, on the party's website, of the criminal record of candidates in electoral competitions, except for those in municipalities with fewer than 15,000 inhabitants, constitutes a processing of data "related to criminal convictions and offenses" (art. 10 Reg. 679; 2-octies Code) which, like judicial data under the previous regulation, enjoys enhanced protection due to the particularly stigmatizing nature of the information it reveals. 

**4. Legitimacy of Data Processing**

A necessary requirement for the legitimacy of such processing is not only the provision based on legislative or regulatory norms (also) internal (which is present in this case), but also the respect for the principle of proportionality and the provision of appropriate guarantees imposed, precisely, for such categories of data by art. 10 Reg. 679. 

**5. Proportionality and Ineligibility**

From the first perspective (proportionality), it is first appropriate to establish a connection between this provision and the regulation of ineligibility for the specific electoral competition in question. Since a definitive conviction for various offenses (district, against public administration, non-negligent with a conviction exceeding two years) is an obstacle to candidacy itself, it is evident that the criminal records of the admitted candidates can only report, as possible entries, only convictions (definitive) for offenses deemed by the legislator not to be obstacles to candidacy. 

This circumstance creates the paradoxical effect whereby parties are required to establish a sort of "electronic criminal record," concerning offenses deemed by the legislator irrelevant for the purposes of ineligibility. The rationale of this provision would then consist in providing the citizen with a more complete picture of the candidate's conduct, the proportionality of which must, however, be carefully evaluated. This provision would indeed impose a significant limitation on privacy for the needs of publicity concerning offenses deemed not to constitute a specific case of "moral unworthiness" of the candidate. 

**6. Scope of Criminal Record Publication**

If, on the other hand, the obligation to publish the criminal record is understood to refer to proposed candidates and not only to those who are already officially admitted to candidacy by the electoral office, it would be an excessively broad hypothesis and, as such, of dubious proportionality. For the purpose of the informed exercise of the right to vote, the requirements of "morality" of only those candidates actually admitted to the electoral competition are relevant. 

In any case, a modulation of the breadth of such publication could be considered based on the different characteristics of the regulation on ineligibility for each type of election.

**7. Appropriate Guarantees for Rights and Freedoms**

From the second perspective (appropriate guarantees for rights and freedoms), it is necessary to assess whether the type of publication hypothesized (disclosure on the websites of parties and movements) is, as such, compatible with such guarantees, due to the risk of reproduction, alteration, indiscriminate circulation that necessarily accompanies dissemination online. 

It should then be provided for selective access (with credentials issued to anyone interested and upon specific request) to such data, made available in a format protected from the risk of copying or alteration, for a time proportionate to the pursued needs (for example, that of the electoral campaign). 

Such guarantees could also be provided by an implementing regulation (cf. art. 2-octies), to be issued upon the opinion of the Guarantor. 

In any case, the same legal norm should be supplemented with reference to the phase of collection, by the party, of the criminal record provided to it by the candidate.
