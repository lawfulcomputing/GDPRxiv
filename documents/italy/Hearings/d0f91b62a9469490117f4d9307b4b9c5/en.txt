THE VIDEO OF THE HEARING

Hearing, via videoconference, of the President of the Data Protection Authority in the context of the exploratory investigation on the simplification of citizens' access to services provided by the National Health Service
Parliamentary Commission for Simplification
(May 25, 2020)

I thank the Commission for this opportunity to discuss the relationship between the right to health and data protection: a topic of great relevance, particularly in the current emergency context. 

These two fundamental rights present, in fact, unexpected analogies due essentially to the underlying dialectic between freedom and dignity, the individual and society. In both, in particular, freedom is expressed as self-determination. These rights exist in a constant dialectic between individual guarantee and social protection, often realizing the former in balance with the latter, sometimes even in synergy. 

The relationship between freedom and dignity, the individual and society, underlying these two rights, becomes even more complex due to technology, which, while offering extraordinary possibilities, also induces new vulnerabilities to which we expose ourselves through our data. 

Thus, in the healthcare sector, digitalization is an essential component of clinical governance efficiency, ensuring personalized healthcare based on the informed participation of the patient in the therapeutic process. The pandemic has demonstrated how digital means—through electronic prescriptions and telemedicine—can allow for the continuation of care even under social distancing conditions. 

Yet, poorly governed technology can exponentially increase clinical risk, reflecting, in this context, cybersecurity risk, where, for example, the data on which a diagnosis is based may be altered. On the other hand, the exfiltration or unauthorized access to health data can violate, sometimes irreversibly, the right to the inviolability of one’s private life, which constitutes the oldest root of privacy. 

On an individual level, indeed, the knowledge of such “sensitive” data as genetic or health information can lead to discrimination (consider employment or insurance relationships) or, in any case, significant prejudices for the individual concerned. But the cybersecurity risk, in the healthcare sector, also has important effects from a collective perspective. 

On a public level, in fact, attacks on the information systems of healthcare facilities—a significant part of cyber attacks in our country—can have devastating effects on all citizens, preventing the provision of healthcare services or, in the case of data alteration of patients, causing large-scale clinical errors. 

The vulnerability of healthcare systems, therefore, risks causing even very serious service disruptions, generating diagnostic or therapeutic errors or paralyzing care activities. This is a problem that particularly concerns our country. 

Recent research (Clusit, first half of 2019 report) has indicated the healthcare sector as one of those exposed to the greatest risks in terms of cybersecurity due to the lack of a comprehensive security and protection plan, which would instead be essential in this field, especially in light of the increasing use of cloud computing and artificial intelligence. 

The challenge today, then, is to make digitalization in the healthcare sector an organic, forward-looking, and secure process, thus promoting the efficiency of the system and, with it, the effectiveness of the right to health, overcoming the vulnerabilities of technology and minimizing individual and collective risks. 

The Electronic Health Record (EHR) is, in a certain sense, the emblem of this challenge, as an indispensable element of innovation and efficiency in diagnostic and therapeutic activities, as foreseen by the Italian and European Digital Agenda, the Health Pact, and indicated as an "enabling platform" by the three-year plan of Agid. 

However, entrusting the entire clinical history of millions of patients to an IT infrastructure also represents a significant source of vulnerability if it lacks adequate protections to prevent unauthorized access, exfiltration, or data alteration. 

This explains the need—previously urged by us—for a regulatory framework that equips such an indispensable yet delicate tool with all the necessary safeguards, in terms of security measures as well as the overall architecture of the system. 

Legislative provisions have also allowed for the alignment of heterogeneous regional initiatives within a framework of uniform guarantees, ensuring equal treatment of citizens. 

Since 2009—three years before the introduction of the relevant regulations—the Authority has provided some important indications on the electronic health record (as well as on the health dossier), particularly noting the need to ensure:
- full freedom for the patient regarding essential choices related to the record, including those concerning its scope and the possibility of obscuring certain clinical events,
- selective and differentiated legitimization of the personnel authorized to access it and the patient's right to verify the accesses made—thus restoring centrality to the individual in the process of managing their data—and
- the obligation for the data controller to report any data breaches occurring in their structure to the Authority, even when the obligation to notify data violations was not yet general, in order to contain with timely measures the individual and collective damages that could arise from violations in this area. 

These indications have significantly improved the quality of services provided, laying the necessary conditions to promote citizens' trust in an essential diagnostic tool but still...

This observation, along with the constant monitoring of the application of the regulations, has allowed for the proposal, also in light of the subsequent European Regulation, of simplification measures and, at the same time, enhancement of the electronic health record, which were then introduced by the relaunch decree.  
In particular, it was deemed appropriate—and shared by the Authority—to eliminate the consent for the feeding of the Record, while confirming the consent (authentically expressive of informational self-determination) related to consultation by healthcare professionals.  
This modification significantly simplifies the process of establishing the electronic health record, making it automatically available regardless of individual expressions of will, while confirming the patient's consent as the source of legitimacy for access to data by the healthcare professional.  
The scope of the record is expanded to include all health and socio-health documents related to the services provided, whether or not covered by the National Health Service (SSN), thus including among the authorized subjects for feeding the generality of healthcare professionals who follow the patient.  
The planned additional feeding of the electronic health record with data available on organ donation choices, vaccinations, and appointments will then promote the effectiveness of health services if and to the extent that it ensures the alignment of databases and, therefore, the accuracy and updating of information.  
Furthermore, a significant strengthening is anticipated—which will need to be subject to careful monitoring—of the Portal and the national infrastructure for interoperability, necessary for the provision of 'mobile' health services for citizens, that is, in regions different from their residence.  
It is therefore expected that the National Registry of consents and related revocations will be established, to be associated with the beneficiaries listed in the relevant registry, also including data related to any delegations (for example, for decisions concerning minors).  
The enhancement of the function and the extension of the scope of the electronic health record, provided for by the relaunch decree also based on the indications given by the Authority, certainly represents an important challenge for the healthcare sector, to be pursued thoroughly without underestimating in any way the risks associated with entrusting, to an IT platform, the data on the clinical history, potentially, of all beneficiaries.  
On one hand, in fact, the cybersecurity risk (which can inevitably translate into clinical risk) must be countered with the strictest adherence to the principle of accountability and the criteria of privacy by design and by default, rationalizing the information assets and the very architecture of processing, following its dynamics throughout the entire supply chain.  
The purpose limitation must also be understood in a strict sense, avoiding access to electronic health record data, for which complete anonymization cannot be guaranteed, for the purposes of health programming by administrations holding different competencies.  
On this aspect, I trust that misunderstandings with the Ministry of Economy and Finance (MEF) will be resolved, also because that ministry already holds relevant fiscal data for monitoring expenditure even in the health sector.  
Moreover, the Ministry of Health already uses the electronic health record for purposes (in addition to research, also for) health programming, verification of the quality of care, and evaluation of healthcare (governance purposes), without, however, using identifying data, according to levels of access and data processing methods defined in accordance with the principles of proportionality, necessity, and indispensability.  
On the other hand, the risk of unauthorized access to health record data, made possible by inadequate definition of the perimeter and the legitimacy profiles of the same healthcare professionals, should not be underestimated.  
There have been numerous cases, brought to our attention, of consultations of health records by personnel lacking the necessary qualifications, for retaliatory purposes or simple, pathological curiosity.  
The violation of confidentiality resulting from such conduct could be even more significant if the electronic health record contains data of particular relevance concerning existential choices, such as those on organ donation or, where provided, on advance treatment directives.  
The protection of data from unauthorized access and the precise definition of the subjects authorized to consult constitute, moreover, an unavoidable objective, especially in the overall healthcare context.  
Consider the sensitivity of data, such as those (although not present in the electronic health record) concerning heterologous fertilization, for which it must be ensured, through a coding system, both the traceability of the path of gametes from the donor (anonymous) to the born child and vice versa, to allow re-identification in case of adverse events, as well as the confidentiality of mothers.  
The management of such delicate and constantly evolving data as health data requires adequate guarantees to ensure its quality.  
In this sense, data protection rules are a prerequisite for healthcare efficiency, contributing to the guarantee of accuracy and updating of data, in a field, such as the one under examination, where reliance on outdated or altered information can sometimes lead to even lethal harm to the patient.  
Constant updating must, moreover, also concern the expressions of will of the beneficiary, to ensure their effective respect in their current state and evolution.

The process of digitalization in the healthcare sector – of which the medical record is a significant component – raises, moreover, other relevant issues from the perspective of data protection, which we will briefly touch upon while slightly expanding the scope of our discussion today. 
The increasingly frequent use of artificial intelligence for research purposes in the medical field, as well as for diagnostic purposes, highlights the need to ensure the accuracy of the analytical process based on data, where algorithmic choices are made possible by the self-learning capabilities of the machine based on the input information. 
The "intelligence" of their choices depends on the accuracy of the data used in configuring the algorithms. 
If the classification of reference cases provided to the algorithm to decide, for example, the nature of a pathology or to evaluate a marker is incorrect, then the resulting diagnosis will also be wrong, with potentially fatal effects for the patient. 
Data protection, therefore, is a prerequisite for the effectiveness of big data analytics and should not be combined with a regression but, on the contrary, with further development of "user-friendly" technologies that incorporate guarantees of security and confidentiality of data. 
Privacy by design and by default measures – to be applied rigorously, for example, in relation to the increasingly widespread telemedicine – are, in this sense, a decisive example of how technology, if supported by a forward-looking "vision" in social as well as legal terms, can represent the solution rather than the problem and strengthen citizens' trust in the healthcare system. 
This solution is all the more necessary in light of research increasingly based on data and algorithms capable of cross-referencing vast amounts of information, with a high risk not only of re-identification (which leads one to consider data effectively anonymous only in marginal cases) but also of discrimination against groups (a risk that is even higher when the datasets on which the algorithmic decision is based are not representative or otherwise underlie biases related to gender, ethnicity, social conditions, or indeed health, etc.). 
Data protection can represent both a guarantee for the individual and a factor of appropriateness for overall healthcare governance. 
In this respect, this regulation offers important guarantees, particularly requiring transparency, contestability, and non-discrimination of the algorithmic process, as well as a general approach aimed at risk prevention, with the provision of precautionary measures and the adoption of an overall strategy aimed at holding the data processing actors accountable. 
We have recalled these principles also in relation to increasingly frequent healthcare assistance models based on proactive medicine and, therefore, on health risk profiling, which, although aimed at the "personalization" of medicine and the improvement of therapeutic offerings, nonetheless involve extremely delicate aspects from an existential point of view. 
Hence the need not only for consent as an expression of self-determination (both informative and health-related) concerning automated processing that is not strictly necessary for immediate care purposes of the individual concerned, but also for adequate supervision of profiling, which, if based on inaccurate data or inferences, risks causing serious harm to the individual and significant errors in the overall clinical governance. 
Guarantees of correctness must also be ensured regarding the use – promoted by the relaunch decree – of predictive methodologies for healthcare needs, for which data protection rules can represent a valuable prerequisite for effectiveness. 
These examples thus demonstrate how the synergy between innovation, healthcare governance, and data protection will play an increasingly decisive role for our societies, a challenge we must strive to win in the name, once again, of the centrality of the person and their dignity: those bonds that, as Aldo Moro explained in the Constituent Assembly, even the collective interest in public health cannot override.
