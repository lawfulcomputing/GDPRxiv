Hearing of the President of the Authority for the Protection of Personal Data in the context of the examination of the legislative proposals C. 1056 and related bills, concerning the establishment of a parliamentary inquiry commission on the intentional, serial, and massive dissemination of false information (so-called fake news)  
Joint Committees IX (Transport) and VII (Culture) of the Chamber of Deputies  
(March 3, 2020)  

I thank the Committees for their willingness to analyze the complex phenomenon of fake news also from the perspective of data protection: a discipline that is increasingly central to the governance of digital society.  
The scope of action of the proposed Commission appears well-defined in all proposals, addressing both the themes of disinformation and misinformation in their main implications of an individual and collective nature.  
From the first perspective, in fact, the phenomenon can be considered primarily in its inciting value of discriminatory, defamatory, or otherwise harmful conduct to individual dignity.  
Misinformation, on the other hand, represents a complex phenomenon to be analyzed also as a potential threat to democracy, with potentially negative repercussions on sovereignty.  
In fact, social bombing carried out with propaganda messages or even just the sending of unilateral news, targeted based on the voter profile created by the algorithm—such as in the case of Cambridge Analytica—can have extremely distorting effects on the correct formation of consent, thus steering the political choices of the electorate in the desired direction.  
It is therefore agreed to extend the object of the investigation in these terms, to avoid reducing a significant democratic issue such as "post-truth" to a mere criminal or technological matter.  
Now, it is clear that neither disinformation nor misinformation originated with social media.  
Certainly, what is amplified exponentially by the internet is the virality with which even blatantly false news circulates online.  
And people end up believing it to be true due to that self-confirming mechanism that leads to the reliability of the news depending not on its verifiability but on the number of shares it has received: based on its diffusivity and not on its intrinsic truthfulness.  
It is the logic of the audience: the news, however formed, ends up conferring authority to its source based on the number of readers-viewers it attracts, and the mechanism of advertising remuneration, correlated to the number of visitors to the site, certainly does not help.  
Fact-checking has thus been replaced by narrative; source verification by mass consensus as the only parameter for evaluating news.  
But above all, what makes the most news is generally what is furthest from reality, appearing as a truth finally unveiled and removed from the attempts at mystification by power.  
All this is further aggravated by the fragmentation of information centers, which leads to a sort of informational autism: the tendency to seek information only from sources inclined to confirm our prior beliefs, hindering the possibility of verification on false news or beliefs.  
This can only further weaken the critical sense essential for selecting truthful information from the mass of news offered by the web.  
What to do? Of the various proposed solutions, none is, in itself, conclusive, but may prove at least partially useful if combined with others.  
From the essentially public perspective of combating disinformation, the technological shortcut—namely, software capable of downgrading false news by placing it at the end, prioritizing verified information—does not seem to represent, at least by itself, a convincing solution.  
Because it feeds on the same flawed logic it attempts to remedy: delegating to the algorithm an activity, such as verifying sources and news, that can only be human and evaluative.  
The automation of fact-checking, if devoid of adequate human support, would indeed contribute to further depressing the already weak critical sense and the habit of dialectical verification.  
What may instead prove useful is the adoption of measures, including technical ones, aimed at signaling possible distortions of informational content due to their robotized origin.  
Distinguishing freedom of expression from algorithmic amplification can indeed help provide users with greater tools to navigate consciously in what risks, otherwise, being mere informational entropy (and not richness).  
In this sense, the path—already partially undertaken experimentally by some operators—of technical solutions aimed at signaling to the user, based on objective criteria—for example, the massiveness of the sends—potentially unreliable content, should be pursued, thus also stimulating the public's critical sense.  
Using technology to promote rather than limit rights can, in this sense, be one of the best solutions to help make the internet that extraordinary democratic tool it was meant to be.  
But combating informational distortion can also benefit from valuable tools offered by data protection legislation, whenever the manipulative intent of the conveyed content is based on its targeting, made possible by the psychometric segmentation of the audience due to profiling.  
This is what happened with Cambridge Analytica, but it is reasonable to think it occurs due to the broader extension of the profiling method from the commercial to the political sphere.  
The sending of content specifically tailored to the way of being, thinking, acting, deducible from the online behavior of the user detected by the algorithm.
, it can indeed have a manipulative effect on one's thinking that is incomparable to any monopoly on information because it is capable of adapting so perfectly to the thoughts of the "target" that it severely limits their self-determination. The contrast to such distorting phenomena – which have a devastating impact on primary democratic guarantees such as informational pluralism, freedom of expression, and the right to information – primarily involves combating the unlawful exploitation of user data that underlies it, for which, for example, we have sanctioned Facebook. The significance of this method of counteraction is such that European regulations now expressly sanction the unlawful use of personal data to influence electoral outcomes, often, as we have seen, even by foreign powers. It is better to enhance these tools than to yield to the recurring temptation of criminal law: misleading because it is reductionist and controversial in democratic terms, as it ultimately assigns to the criminal judiciary the role of a truth tribunal, in a context where accuracy should instead be pursued through pluralistic and dialectical confrontation. And with the inviolable limit of respecting the dignity of others, which is supported in case of violation by the rules (which are indeed criminal) on incitement to discrimination, defamation, and unlawful processing of data, which delineate the boundary beyond which freedom of expression cannot extend without unduly violating the dignity of others. However, within this boundary, there exists and can be further configured a wide range of tools also born from the right to personal data protection, which can better contribute to combating inaccurate information that harms dignity. In this sense, it is very important not only to rectify defamatory news but also to update (even from Google snippets!) outdated news that has been overtaken by subsequent facts, as often happens in judicial reporting. This is a doubly beneficial tool, as it is useful not only to protect the dignity of the interested party but also to enhance the quality of information, which is inevitably improved by the stimulus for updating and completeness of the news. This is a distinct and additional aspect compared to the already significant issue of "delisting," since updating is functional not to the "oblivion" of a past crystallized in a news item but to the integral representation of identity, even in the diachronic evolution it may undergo and in its social projection, completing its developments a past that otherwise would no longer correspond to us. And if these tools can already be used today to combat defamatory campaigns and incitement to hatred, further measures can certainly be introduced on the dual front of remedial protection and user accountability, as well as that of platforms, albeit within the limits imposed by the prohibition of their surveillance over disseminated content. In the first respect, the system for removing unlawful online content should be strengthened, limiting its further dissemination and holding the provider accountable, while reserving the final decision to the public authority. As demonstrated by the case of cyberbullying, the mechanism based on the request to the provider for removal and the subsequent appeal to the Authority in case of inaction or dispute is a useful tool for protecting online personal rights, as it combines the need for prompt removal of content with the reservation of the final decision to the public authority, in the adversarial context of the parties. This represents an overall balancing that responds to the needs emphasized by the Court of Justice and the European Court of Human Rights; the latter in particular with some rulings that have even established, for States, a positive obligation to ensure measures suitable for protecting personal dignity. In this sense, especially for combating hate speech, it would be useful to have a regulation that governs, also from a temporal perspective, the procedure that providers are required to follow upon receiving removal requests, with prompt timelines as provided for cyberbullying and with the right of the opposing party to appeal to the Authority in case of dispute. Naturally, in cases where personal data is not at issue but the disseminated content is unlawful because aimed at carrying out disinformation campaigns, a system similar to the German one could also be imagined, in which the reporting of fake news is decided by independent entities (an Authority, the journalists' association, or others) and determines, if founded, the obligation for the provider to remove the news and communicate the correction to users who have "interacted" (through sharing, liking, or commenting) with the false news. All this with the possibility of appealing to a judge in case of dispute over the decision, thus reserving to the public authority the final decision on fundamental rights. A second level of intervention – modeled on the current French law – could concern the regulation of the currently anomalous and retaliatory use of anonymity online, making it truly reversible, that is, allowing for the identification of the author of unlawful content at the request of the judicial authority. One could think, therefore, of an obligation to provide the social platform provider, at the time of opening a profile, with an identification document, which, however, is legally required to be inaccessible to the provider, under penalty of constituting a crime that could indeed be introduced ad hoc. The provider would instead be obliged to allow access only to the judicial authority where a criminal proceeding is underway against the author of the unlawful content or a civil proceeding for...
**Compensation for Damage Suffered Due to the Same Contents.**

This measure, which is not without its criticisms—it is important to clarify—would never be entirely conclusive in the absence of an international standardization of the regulation of anonymity online that, by balancing freedom of expression and dignity, allows for effective protection of victims of online offenses, which is currently hindered by the use of various methods of identity masking.

The set of tools outlined above, aimed at countering violations of both personal rights and essential democratic safeguards, perpetrated through the distorted use of the internet, must inevitably leave a residual sphere of free expression, in which we must learn to accept the risk of inaccuracy as a counterpart to pluralism and tolerance.

A "cost" of democracy, if we wish to define it that way, but certainly a reality we must contend with not so much because the web exists, but because there are no certifying bodies of truth, which can only be the result of a dialectical process of comparison and verification.

A task that is certainly more complex and laborious, to be conducted under the banner of the ethics of doubt and a sense of limits, but cannot be replaced by any Tribunal of Truth.
