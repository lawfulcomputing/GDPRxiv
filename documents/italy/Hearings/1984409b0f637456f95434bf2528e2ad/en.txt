**THE VIDEO OF THE HEARING**  
Memorandum of the President of the Data Protection Authority - Constitutional Profiles of the Possible Introduction of a "Vaccination Passport" for Citizens Who Have Received the Anti-SARS-COV2 Vaccine  
1st Commission (Constitutional Affairs) of the Senate of the Republic  
(April 8, 2021)  

1. **The Context**  
I thank the Commission for this opportunity to discuss a topic of pressing relevance, which your sensitivity allows us to address also from the increasingly significant perspective of privacy. The centrality of this right in the pandemic context is such that, from the very first measures adopted by the Government (and by Governments, not only European) for the purpose of containing infections, it was necessary to first understand how to reconcile public health needs with individual privacy. However, the pandemic has been able to demonstrate, testing it day by day, the social function of data protection, as emphasized by the GDPR since its initial Recitals.  
It has become even clearer how this is a non-tyrannical right, capable of continuous balancing with other legal goods at stake and of modulations that can adjust the intensity of protection according to the context, the relevance of the data to the most intimate characteristics of the person, and thus to the risks of discrimination connected to any improper use, as well as in light of the expected or possible implications of the particular technologies used.  
The issue of tracking apps is particularly relevant in this regard. The whole world has had to manage the possibility of using a potentially very invasive technology for the purposes of containing infections and strengthening diagnostic capabilities, as it is capable of reconstructing—through the initially proposed geolocation—the entire pattern of citizens' movements.  
However, also thanks to the contribution offered by Data Protection Authorities, Europe has been able to oppose such an invasive form of tracing with a much more contained, yet no less effective, solution aimed at tracing contacts, not individuals, based on a voluntary choice of the subject, but taken in the spirit of social responsibility and solidarity, especially towards the most vulnerable segments of the population, exposed to the most dramatic consequences of contagion. Hence the option—in particular, but not only in Italy—in favor of a voluntary adherence system, managed by state authorities, based on the collection of data not of geolocation but of mere proximity of devices, with data retention limited to the strictly necessary period for reconstructing the contact chain and an overall security architecture effective in preventing the improper circulation of information.  
This result has also been possible thanks to the loyal and constant cooperation between Parliament (before whose Committees several important hearings have been held), the Government, and the Authority—coordinated at the European level with other homologous Authorities in the specific Committee—both in the phase that we could define as "ascending" in preparing the regulatory framework, and in the "descending" phase of its implementation and modification. Thus, even from a regulatory perspective, data protection has demonstrated its capacity for democratic "mediation," in a Kelsenian sense, offering itself as a cornerstone of a constant balance between public and private, also thanks to a flexible exercise of its functions by the Authority, in synergy with the actions of other institutions.  

2. **Vaccination Passports, Between Freedom of Care and Public Health Needs**  
After tracking apps, another important test for the right to privacy is, indeed, the relationship between freedom of movement and the health condition of the individual, with the latter being an element capable of overcoming any restrictions imposed on the former for epidemic containment purposes.  
If a year ago, in China, there was discussion about the creation of a true health passport, as part of a more complex and invasive biosurveillance system, with the first (although still insufficient) availability of vaccines and in light of the ongoing pandemic situation, the debate has opened, albeit with different tones, also in Europe and within the same Member States.  
In general terms, and aside from what will be discussed in more detail shortly, the main concerns raised regarding this solution, in its various forms, relate primarily to the non-univocal implication—discussed in scientific circles—between the presence of neutralizing antibodies and permanent or sufficiently lasting immunity. In the absence of such a correlation, the very purpose of this certification would be significantly weakened, as it would not be possible to ascertain for significant periods the actual incapacity of the subject to become a vector for possible contagion. Furthermore, specifically regarding the vaccination passport, the limited availability of the antidote and, on the other hand, its incompatibility with certain health conditions risks generating discrimination against those who cannot or, for personal reasons, do not wish to benefit from it (cf. resolution of January 27 by the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe). Such discrimination would, moreover, be all the more incisive the more the certificate (almost like the maritime health certificates used between the 16th and 17th centuries) is conceived as a prerequisite not only for free movement but also for access to certain places or services.  
On the other hand, however, the needs for containing infections are still so strong that they suggest, even from a solidarity perspective (and thus to protect the most vulnerable segments...
of the population) particularly cautious measures to resume certain activities, including international travel, which have been significantly restricted for over a year, with understandably severe consequences for the affected economic and productive sectors. 

These reasons, in particular, have motivated a push, developed also within our country and primarily supported by the Presidents of some Regions, in favor of "vaccine passports" or similar certificates of negativity, as prerequisites for mobility within the national territory or access to certain services. 

We have witnessed the elaboration of very different proposals, each of which would deserve specific treatment, but focusing only on the common aspects, we can first observe, in method, how the impact of such measures on the right to freedom of movement as well as on the right to therapeutic self-determination requires, at the internal level, a state law that complies with the respective reservations (see Constitutional Court, ruling 5/2018 on the legitimacy conditions of the vaccination obligation, as well as, regarding the state law reservation in vaccination matters, see the order of the Labor Judge of Messina dated December 12, 2020). Only a state law, in fact, could condition the exercise of certain rights or freedoms to a prophylactic measure that would otherwise be optional, thus making it, at least to some extent, indirectly mandatory. 

In substance, this regulatory provision should also ensure an adequate synergy between public health needs and individual dignity, according to the "spirit" underlying Article 32, as outlined by Aldo Moro's intervention in the Constituent Assembly regarding its final formulation. The concrete modalities of implementing these measures, their proportionality and non-exceedance, also from a privacy perspective, in relation to the (exclusively) public health objective pursued, the temporal limitation to the pandemic context, and the possibility of fulfilling the vaccination requirement by those who could not or did not want to access it, with equivalent conditions such as, for example, the diagnostic verification of negativity, albeit with its different temporal effectiveness, also contribute to delineating such synergy. 

These reasons are also underlying the "warning" that the Guarantor intended to issue, even in light of the announcement of apps, albeit for voluntary use, certifying vaccination for access to certain places or services. The use of such heterogeneous solutions would indeed risk creating, for the multiple managers, a sort of vaccination registry of their users, in the absence of any of the guarantees and necessary uniformity prescribed by the state law reservation, established not only for mandatory health treatments (or those that indirectly become so) but also for the right to data protection (Constitutional Court, ruling 271/05). The impact of these measures on individual privacy is, in fact, far from marginal. Even if they do not contain explicit health data such as those on serological negativity or the fact of recovery, vaccine passports in the strict sense could imply the subject's vulnerability status in cases where, for example, they are vaccinated despite not being in the age groups eligible for recruitment at that particular stage. 

It is evident, therefore, that the introduction of such a measure requires a uniform regulation not only to satisfy the state law reservations provided for in this matter but, above all, to prevent disparities in treatment or even just the centralization, in the hands of various private entities such as app managers or other similar technologies, of data deserving of the utmost protection as they could lead, if fraudulently used, to the worst stigmatizations. 

3. The proposals under discussion 
In this context, the choice of the European Union to uniformly regulate (in a regulatory form, moreover) the legitimacy conditions for the use of health passes (not limited to vaccination status) to promote intra-European mobility is certainly commendable, both in method and in substance. 

In method, indeed, the very variety (also from a technological point of view) of possible solutions makes the definition of a common standard highly appropriate, allowing for adequate comparison between homogeneous data types and ensuring a fair balance between freedom of movement (on which the regulatory provision, in Article 21 TFEU, is based) privacy, and health needs. Already concerning tracking apps, the necessity to promote freedom of movement within the Union had made the provision for their interoperability appropriate: a topic we discussed in October. 

But above all, in substance, the provision of the essential coordinates within which these measures must be framed and within which the member states themselves must operate, once again characterizes the European choice as profoundly identity-forming, that is, expressive of a precise conception of the relationship between public and private, authority and freedom, technique and human factor. The system outlined by the proposals (Com (2021) 130 and 140(1)) marks, in fact, a profound difference, first of all axiological, compared to other solutions, such as the Chinese one, which is underpinned by radically different political-institutional options. There, in fact, the vaccination pass is not only part of a capillary biosurveillance system, but is also correlated to the multifunctional Wechat platform, which the Chinese government accesses extensively, with an additional increase in state control power, not only in the health field. 

The attempt undertaken by the EU is, instead, to make the green pass an instrument not of control but of facilitation...
**rollo, but of promoting freedoms, minimizing primarily (but, of course, not only) its impact on privacy, as a social legitimization element of (and citizens' trust in) the measure itself, ensuring proportionality and non-discrimination.** (2) To this end, the Commission has stated that it has planned the collection and processing of only the data necessary to verify the authenticity, validity, and integrity of the certificates, with a decentralized verification system that does not involve the retention of the results of the verification and has designed the system as temporally limited to the duration of the emergency condition as declared by the WHO. The data controllers are the competent Authorities issuing the certificates. States will have to establish a digital infrastructure of the “trust framework” (the terminology is significant!) for the issuance, verification, and interoperability of the certificates (specifically vaccination, recovery, testing) according to the technical specifications identified by the Commission with an implementing act. 

States will decide which health restrictions to exempt travelers from, but they must apply such exemptions equally to holders of green passes (whether they are for vaccination, testing, or recovery) and must notify, with adequate justification, any additional restrictions that may be introduced by the States. 

**4. The joint opinion Edps/Edpb**  
The proposals overall outline adequate guarantees for privacy; however, in a joint opinion (4/21 of March 31), the European Data Protection Supervisor and the European Data Protection Board suggested—particularly regarding proposal no. 130, but with arguments extendable to 140—further improvements that will be illustrated. 

First of all, to avoid discrimination based on a person's health condition, it is requested to clarify the obligation for Member States to accept all three types of certificates included in the green pass, as well as the illegality of any use of the pass that could lead to, even de facto, discriminatory treatment or otherwise disproportionate in relation to the pursued aim. 

To ensure an effective proportionality assessment and meet the requirements prescribed by Article 52.1 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (3) for rules limiting fundamental rights, it is emphasized that national rules that may legitimize further uses of the green pass should precisely delineate the scope and purposes of the processing. 

It is then requested that the European regulation better define the purpose of the green pass and introduce a mechanism for monitoring its use by Member States, in addition to adequate technical and organizational measures against the falsification of certificates and any other type of abuse, especially regarding data belonging to “special” categories and those subject to automated decisions (in this regard, the reference to CJEU La Quadrature du Net a.o, Joined Cases C-511/18, C-512/18 and C-520/18, 6.10.2020, ECLI:EU:C:2020:791, p.13 is useful). 

It has also been requested to further clarify, including with a specific sunset clause, the illegitimacy of access to the data contained in the certificates once the pandemic emergency has ceased and to exclude, in Article 15, that the green pass system can be reactivated by a delegated act of the Commission following suspension due to ceased needs, in the presence of a further declaration of the existence of a pandemic from Sars-Cov-2, its variants, or similar infectious diseases with epidemic potential. It is requested to clarify whether the creation of the certificate itself is subject to a request from the individual or only its issuance and, to exclude discriminatory effects of the digital divide, it is suggested to obligate States, with a strengthened regulatory wording compared to the current one, to issue the certificate in both paper and digital form, as well as to limit, where technically possible, the verification techniques to those that do not involve the transmission of personal data. 

Regarding the categories of data used, it is requested to better justify, in the preamble, the reasons that make the collection of detailed information necessary, such as the vaccine product used, the holder of the marketing authorization of the vaccine, or the manufacturer and the number of vaccinations or vaccine doses administered, specifying that the changes that may be made by delegated acts of the Commission should be limited to operating within the subcategories of the data catalog provided in the annex. 

It is also considered preferable to delegate to an executive act of the Commission the identification of only the technical details of the measures for the security of processing, indicating already in the proposal, thus raising its source, the obligation for controllers and processors to adopt technical and organizational measures appropriate to the risk involved in the processing. Similarly, in light of the possibility left to the Commission to introduce, with its own executive act, specifications on the relationship between controllers and processors for the purpose of allocating the related responsibilities, it is hoped that Member States will make public a list of the subjects involved in the processing, in order to allow the effective exercise of rights by the data subjects, in compliance with the principle of transparency. 

It is also hoped for a more precise definition of the maximum retention periods of data functional to the issuance of the pass, or alternatively the criteria by which to define them, not exceeding the end of the pandemic. 

Finally, it is suggested to better clarify the conditions of legitimacy and the guarantees for the transfer of data abroad, ensuring their use exclusively for the purposes sanctioned by the Proposals. 

These are observations aimed at an overall,
further refinement of proposals that, however, already imply an adequate balance between privacy, public health needs, and freedom of movement, demonstrating once again how data protection regulation represents an increasingly relevant prerequisite for sustainable governance of both innovation and emergency.
