**Observations of the President of the Guarantor for the Protection of Personal Data, Antonello Soro - A.C. 491 “Provisions on Transparency in the Relationships Between Producing Companies, Entities Operating in the Health Sector, and Health Organizations”**

before the 12th Committee on Social Affairs of the Chamber of Deputies (November 5, 2018)

**General Profiles**  
The proposed law addresses a particularly complex issue, namely the transparency of the interests between companies producing health goods and services, professionals operating in the health sector, and organizations providing health services.  
In general terms, the aim underlying the bill—to combat corrupt practices in the health sector by promoting transparency in the relationships among the main operators in the field—is commendable.  
However, the methods by which this objective is pursued must be adequately weighed, balancing the need for transparency in the relationships among health sector operators with the right to personal data protection—enshrined as a fundamental freedom by Article 8 of the Charter of Nice and Article 16 of the TFEU—which is inevitably compromised by such invasive publicity as internet disclosure.  
In compliance with the requirements set forth by the European Court of Justice in the Schecke ruling of 2010, it is therefore necessary to delineate the limit beyond which the publicity of the interests between companies and professionals in the health sector infringes upon the essential core—non-compressible under Article 52 of the Charter of Nice—of the right to data protection of the individuals involved.  
The publicity obligations to be introduced must particularly ensure compliance with the principles of proportionality, fairness, and minimization, as well as the limitation of the online permanence of information, as per the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679.  
The same national regulation on access to documents held by public entities for the execution of public interest tasks must—according to the European Regulation—reconcile the need for public knowledge with the right to personal data protection (see Article 86 and Recital 154).  
To ensure compliance with these principles and, in particular, with the principle of proportionality, existing informational obligations and behavioral norms (including ethical ones) should be adequately considered so as not to duplicate the required compliance.  
Particularly noteworthy is the regime of mandatory communication and public knowledge of external assignments held by public employees (including doctors and professionals of the National Health Service), the reporting obligations imposed on health operators regarding participation in conferences and training events, as well as, more generally, the telematic data collection system on health services (prescribed and provided) that allows for monitoring expenditure through the network connection of doctors and public and private facilities contracted with the National Health Service.  
Moreover, in assessing the appropriateness of introducing new transparency obligations, consideration should be given to the existing limitations regarding the publicity of medicines, the prior communication procedure to AIFA required for conferences organized by pharmaceutical companies, as well as the constraints related to the delivery of free samples of medicines by medical representatives.  
New compliance obligations should therefore be graduated based on relevant criteria, such as the degree of risk of fraudulent behavior by the operator, the context in which they operate, the amount, frequency, and type of financial transaction or interest relationship, based on a reasonable assessment of the functionality of the data to be published in relation to the actual needs for public knowledge.  
It should also be noted that a certain interaction between public and private interests, articulated in relationships among producing companies, professionals, and health organizations, is somewhat inherent to this sector. Consequently, it may be difficult for the public to distinguish between initiatives that could improperly influence the practices of sector operators and legitimate relationships that are useful for stimulating innovation, training, improving clinical practice, and advancing research.  
In this context, indiscriminate publicity of such information could lead to distorting effects on the behavior of the professionals themselves, who might be induced to adopt excessive precautions to prevent even the appearance of conflicts of interest, limiting their relationships with producing companies to the detriment, for example, of their updates on new technologies or their participation in clinical research.  

1. **The Proposed Regulation, Critical Issues, and the Guarantor's Recommendations.**  
In this perspective, the following are some indications regarding the individual provisions of the proposed law, in order to align them with the requirements set forth by Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and the national adaptation legislation (Legislative Decree No. 101 of 2018).  

1.1. **Entities Subject to Publicity Obligations.**  
First of all, the definitions identifying the entities subject to online publicity obligations need to be clarified. The subjective scope of application appears to be outlined with broad and, at times, indeterminate formulas. Also to ensure the necessary respect for the principle of proportionality, it is appropriate to precisely delineate the scope of the publication obligations, adequately selecting within the article...
**1.1. Entities and subjects in the healthcare sector**

In the healthcare sector, the entities and subjects for whom the risk of abuse, improper practices, and conflicts of interest is such that widespread control by the community is essential, and therefore justifies a limitation on the confidentiality of the interested parties.

**1.2. Content of the communication obligations to the Ministry of Health**

Similar considerations should be made regarding the definition of the content of the communication obligations to the Ministry of Health and, therefore, of publicity, also in light of the sanctions provided for in case of non-compliance (which are also subject to online dissemination in a specific section of the electronic register). In this sense, always in light of the principle of proportionality, the threshold for the provision beyond which the obligation of publication on the web is triggered should be appropriately reconsidered, in order to avoid overloading the Register with insignificant information that could obscure, due to excess, other much more relevant information, thus frustrating the pursued goal of transparency.

To this end, it may be useful to consider, for example, that the Code of Conduct for Public Employees (D.P.R. No. 62 of 2013) qualifies gifts and other benefits under 150 euros as of "modest value," if made occasionally and within the scope of normal courtesy relations.

Furthermore, the provisions that extend the obligation to communicate to the Ministry of Health the shareholdings, bonds, and proceeds from industrial or intellectual property rights held by the spouse, cohabitant, or relatives "up to the second degree" of "subjects operating in the health sector" should be carefully reassessed. 

There is a concrete risk that such provisions may prove difficult to apply, considering that the recipients of the aforementioned obligations may not be aware of being in the conditions provided for by the regulation, for example, if they do not have any relationship with their relatives (also up to the second degree, i.e., including siblings, grandparents, and grandchildren).

**1.3. The notion of "direct or indirect interest relationship"**

Again, from the perspective of proportionality, the notion of "direct or indirect interest relationship" should be precisely delineated. In this regard, consideration should be given to excluding from this notion the "gratuitous or honorary" participations in conferences and training events, in relation to the actual relevance of such situations in terms of possible improper practices, as well as the risk that professionals wishing to update themselves may refrain from doing so to avoid having these aspects of their professional life become freely accessible online. It should also be considered whether it would be more appropriate to make the necessary amendments to the current provisions, which require Regions to regulate the methods by which SSN operators are obliged to disclose their participation in initiatives promoted or funded by pharmaceutical companies and medical device suppliers (art. 48, d.l. No. 269/2003), in order to make this mechanism more efficient and avoid burdening the sector with additional obligations.

To prevent an excess of irrelevant information from obscuring more significant ones, it could be provided that only certain types of benefits and interest relationships - which present a higher risk in terms of abuses, depending on the context and their frequency and magnitude - be included in the public electronic register, as opposed to others - of more limited relevance - which could be subject to simple communication obligations to the Ministry, without any online dissemination. In this regard, it could be evaluated to delegate to the implementing decree that the Ministry is required to adopt, after consulting the Guarantor, the task of appropriately grading the methods of publicity.

**1.4. Data to be published in the Register**

Regarding the details of the data to be published in the register, in compliance with the principle of minimization, the publication of the tax code and VAT number of the beneficiary of the benefit or the counterpart of the interest relationship appears excessive, considering that the professional domicile of the interested party is in any case required to be published.

It should also be clarified how the nature of the benefit or interest relationship differs from its cause.

**1.5. Rights of the interested parties and the guarantee measures for their protection**

The provisions that empower interested parties to request the Ministry of Health to rectify or delete data concerning them contained in the electronic register need to be refined in accordance with the European regulatory framework on personal data protection. The limitation of the possibility to exercise such rights only to cases of presumed inaccuracy of the published data or the identification of the subject to whom the data refers does not seem in line with Articles 15 and following of the Regulation. In fact, European legislation entitles the interested party to exercise rights beyond those of obtaining rectification and deletion, such as, for example, the integration of their data if found to be incomplete, the limitation of processing, or the deletion of data that has proven to be excessive, collected unlawfully, or not removed from the web upon the expiration of the publication term indicated by law.

It is therefore appropriate that the provisions be amended with express reference to Articles 15 and following of the European Regulation EU 2016/679.

Furthermore, the provisions aimed at empowering interested parties to appeal to the ordinary judicial authority, pursuant to Article 152 of the Personal Data Protection Code, should also be refined, in cases where their rights are violated.
**1.6. Publication Terms.**  
The mandatory publication term for data in the Register should be strictly proportional to the pursued objectives. In this regard, it is appropriate to indicate a shorter term for publication on the Internet compared to the five years provided, at the end of which the data must be deleted.  
In this case, after the (shorter) term of their online presence has expired, the possibility of storing them in an internal archive of the Ministry of Health could be evaluated, where they could be kept for a longer period. In this context, it may be useful to note that the code of conduct of the European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associations provides that information regarding value transfers between pharmaceutical companies, operators, and health organizations is disseminated on the corporate websites of the former for a period of three years.  
Rome, November 5, 2018.  

**Regarding the above, it should be noted that this provision applies only to natural persons and not to legal entities (and, therefore, to health organizations), to which the data protection regulation does not grant protection except in the field of electronic communications.**  
Furthermore, both forms of protection, of a judicial and administrative nature (before the Authority), provided by European legislation must be recognized to the data subject. If the data subject believes that the rights granted to them by the data protection legislation have been violated, they have the right to lodge a complaint with the Authority, in addition to the right to appeal to the ordinary judiciary.  
Regarding the specific measures to protect data subjects, the proposed law should be supplemented by clarifying that the producing companies are required to provide specific information to health sector operators, towards whom they make disbursements or maintain relationships of interest. This is particularly relevant given that such information will be published online on the Ministry of Health's website, in order to enable data subjects to consciously regulate their behaviors.  
Among the guarantee measures to protect data subjects, it should be provided that the data published in the Public Online Register can only be reused under the conditions set forth by the European legislation on the reuse of public data (Directive 2003/98/EC, transposed into our legal system by Legislative Decree No. 36 of 2006) and in any case, where it concerns information related to natural persons, in terms compatible with the original purposes for which they were collected by the Ministry.  
Due to the indiscriminate availability online of the information contained in the Register, which could lead to negative consequences for data subjects, the indexing of personal data published on the Ministry's website by general search engines should also be expressly excluded (allowing however the search through those internal to the site) to avoid the decontextualization of the data itself.  
It is also appropriate to delegate to the implementing decree the definition of technical measures suitable to prevent the risk of reproduction, deletion, or alteration of the data made public through the Online Register.
