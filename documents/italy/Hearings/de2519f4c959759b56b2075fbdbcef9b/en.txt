Senate of the Republic - Extraordinary Commission on Intolerance, Racism, Antisemitism, Incitement to Hatred and Violence  
Hearing of the Vice President of the Guarantor for the Protection of Personal Data, Prof. Ginevra Cerrina Feroni, on the phenomenon of hate speech  
(15 February 2022)  
- THE VIDEO OF THE HEARING  

I would like to thank the President, the Vice President, and all the honorable Senators for the invitation to share some reflections on very significant and relevant themes both from an institutional and legal perspective, as well as from a political and public opinion standpoint, to which this illustrious Commission directs its interest. The work you are doing is truly important, spanning various directions in terms of interdisciplinary approaches and examined themes.  
Summary: 1) Methodological Premise. – 2) Definition of the Subject. – 3) Hatred as a Criminal Offense. – 4) An Assessment of Freedom of Expression. – 5) Hate Online and the Role of Platforms.  

1. Methodological Premise.  
I would like to try to shape this intervention around three nodes that I consider crucial, leaving space for questions and debate afterward.  
1) Is hatred legally definable?  
2) How does the hypothesis of a possible regulation of hatred impact freedom of expression?  
3) What role do platforms play regarding the issue of hate speech?  
I will reason using exclusively the tools of the jurist and, in my case, of the constitutionalist, to try to confer a logic as neutral as possible, akin to that of scientific and, in particular, legal reasoning. Because, you see, the training of a jurist requires adherence to precise standards. We could say that for a discourse to be purely legal, emancipating itself from ideological or psychological temptations, it needs to be brought back within well-recognizable categories, framed by certain, objective, indisputable, and preferably factual parameters. Therefore, what should concern us preliminarily is to bring the broadly expansive terms of hatred and intolerance back within such parameters, which are often so broad that they can be quite elusive. And where such parameters are not objectively discernible, it will be necessary – so to speak, sine ira et studio – to attempt to construct them.  
The themes that this Commission is addressing are uncomfortable and far from theoretical: they evoke painful and often tragic facts, touching on very sensitive chords. Therefore, every judgment, comment, and technical evaluation – such as that which may be asked of a constitutionalist – truly imposes a great deal of caution. But be careful. The planes must be distinct. One thing is the dimension of moral and ethical reproach; another is the translation of the galaxy of the category "hatred," "intolerance," "discrimination" into behaviors that are criminally sanctionable.  

2. Definition of the Subject.  
What is hatred? For a Florentine, the theme of hatred cannot be foreign... Just think of the fractures caused by the long-standing and almost legendary hatreds between Ghibellines and Guelphs and, within the latter, between the factions of the Whites and the Blacks, which exemplify the historical animosity of so much of Italian civilization that is both transversal and trans-historical.  
In general terms, hatred – as a motive and not as a crime in itself – is the prejudice, the mental conditioning that inspires action, or the verbal or physical abuse directed at an individual or group due to their perceived belonging to a certain set, usually defined by race, gender, religion, sexual orientation, disability, class, ethnicity, nationality, age, gender identity, political affiliation, or any other distinguishing trait that is considered a paradoxically intrinsic or innate responsibility. In these terms, hatred (or intolerance), as a potentially valid motive, is certainly not episodic but representative of a community that recognizes itself in specific symbols through which its members consider themselves superior to individuals belonging to another.  
But if this is hatred – demonstrating the slippery nature of the theme – there are nonetheless several extenuating circumstances that have been sought in anthropological and political terms, whether functionalist or skeptical. For example, Eco (Building the Enemy, La Nave di Teseo, 2020) wrote that the identification of a common enemy serves a society as an essential unifying element, without which it would never be able to find its radical unifying moment. And one of the fathers of sociology, Simmel, in his essay On Conflict (The Conflict of Modern Civilization, first Italian edition of 1925), considered hatred to be the inextinguishable negative attitude, pure irreducible enmity, so that where the element that serves as the object of contention does not exist or is extinguished, it is rather invented or reinvented in order to keep alive a pretext, whatever it may be, to rekindle an unquenchable conflict. Hatred would thus be the feeling without ifs and buts, but above all without a why; gratuitous, just as love is, after all. The Scottish philosopher Hume, in Book II of the Treatise of Human Nature (1739), dedicated to passions, contemplates hatred as one of the four main passions generated by sympathy, in the Greek sense: “to feel passions with others.” The only rationality of human action would be the “transmission of sympathy,” the continuous exchange among men of emotions and feelings that profoundly influence them, among which is indeed hatred. Even in some pages on democracy by Kelsen, those defined as the most skeptical, we find hatred as an indispensable element of discussion in the axiological incoherence of tired, saturated, exhausted political regimes.  
Hatred is, therefore, an element of the human dimension itself.  
What interests us for our purposes...
**3. Hate as a Criminal Offense.**

Legally, what limits should be placed on manifest intolerance? What limits should be imposed on hate speech? First and foremost, it is necessary to pay attention to a basic distinction: for the phenomenon of hate to be legally relevant, it must be objectively denoted as such and not merely the result of a partial or prospective connotation. Let me clarify: to lead to legal consequences, the reference cannot be what is merely perceived and not real, substantiated by recognizable events or phenomena.

Positive legislation—and that which is cyclically proposed for introduction—specifically refers to incitement or encouragement of hate, often duplicating the evaluation frameworks. Hate is incitement to hate... With definitions that become, evidently, tautological, thus showing all the expressive limits and intrinsic contradictions. This is evidenced by the (commendable) attempts made in the context, for example, of the Council of Europe with the recommendation of the Committee of Ministers dated October 30, 1997, on hate speech and March 21, 2016, No. 15, against racism and intolerance. It is no coincidence that, in literature, the idea is expressed that “hate” is more of a “definitional container that includes forms of expression that are quite different from one another” (Spigno, Hate Speech, 2018). It is also no coincidence that at the European level, a legislative definition of hate has not been reached, preferring to leave to the States the task of adopting the necessary measures to criminalize public incitement to violence and hate (see Framework Decision of the Council of November 28, 2008) or, in any case, deferring the task to soft law instruments (see Code of Conduct to Counter Illegal Hate Speech Online adopted in 2016 to combat the proliferation of racist and xenophobic hate speech with the help of Facebook, Microsoft, YouTube, Twitter). These instruments pose significant issues regarding the framing of sources of law, especially on matters of this caliber.

The Masters of criminal law teach us that we must always operate within the principles of specificity and determinacy of the offense (Palazzo, The Principle of Determinacy in Criminal Law, 1979), as well as materiality and offensiveness of the crime (Mantovani, Criminal Law - General Part, 3rd ed., 1992). Respect for the principle of determinacy in criminal law (Article 25, second paragraph of the Constitution) has been elevated to a supreme principle of the legal system (Constitutional Court 115/2018). When determinacy is invoked, one is indeed invoking one of the cornerstones of the rule of law, namely the principle that the legislator, when drafting criminal offenses, must adhere to a standard of lexical clarity and structural transparency, conducting a scrupulous selection of terminology that reduces the spaces for creative invasion by the judge. Thus, the guarantor spirit of the principle of determinacy and its intimate connection with the principle of separation of powers, foundational to the rule of law, emerges clearly.

The problem then arises, in practice, when reasoning specifically about Article 604 bis of the Penal Code, which punishes— as we know—propaganda and incitement. The first offense is difficult to delineate, and the second is also problematic in its criminal significance, regarding the actual probability of violence occurring. Incitement, in short, makes violence possible in itself, but how probable is it?

Comparative experience does not help. French law, precise to the point of pedantry in the design of the sanctioning and corrective system, is decidedly ambiguous in the concrete definition of offenses (I refer to the discipline provided by the Loi contre les contenus haineux sur internet, known as the Loi Avia, last adopted with Loi n° 2020-766 of June 24, 2020, after the ruling of the Conseil Constitutionnel n° 2020-801 of June 18, 2020). As for British law on hate incitement offenses, it is noteworthy that it shows indifference to the intentions of those committing them (Section 66, Sentencing Act 2020). Hate crime cases are, in fact, defined based on the victim's perception. But if hate becomes a matter of mere perception and not of ascertainable intent, and even the context is considered irrelevant, how can one defend against instrumental, pretextual, or emulative use of the law and, on the other hand, against the abuse of censorial power?

Concrete elements, however, come from U.S. jurisprudence, which has long managed to anchor prohibitions on hate and discriminatory content only where it generates, considering the context, a direct, specific, and imminent danger (this is the so-called emergency test, with the obligatory reference to the case Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969)).

It is for this reason that I take the liberty of addressing this esteemed Commission with the suggestion to formulate the offense it intends, if necessary, to regulate in precise terms. Let it be a clear offense! Unquestionable! The best approach for a correct construction (or reconstruction) of the offense...
**Criminal Law and Interpretation**

Criminal law can only be established through a preventive operation or a series of hermeneutic operations. Interpretation must occur preliminarily, in this legislative context, and not in a subsequent application phase. This limits the discretion of interpreters and, above all, judges in the a posteriori determination of criminal cases. 

Starting from the recognition of our own preconceptions in approaching such a complex subject. A complexity that can find in this Commission the highest place of synthesis and, therefore, objectification. Every pre-understanding related to themes of discrimination, intolerance, incitement to violence, every lived experience, ethical-behavioral codes, personal contacts with hatred becomes a richness in the determination, indeed, pre-determination of the coherent, complete criminal offense.

Hatred itself, after all, is a hermeneutic concept in itself, to be dissected and examined from every possible perspective, including the opposite one, that of the enemy, of the hater himself. Thus, to understand, from the other side, who we are or how we are seen. Hatred as a prism and not as a demarcation line that is itself discriminatory, not as a new and further object of a meta-discrimination. The grim bulk of the Ministry of Love that Orwell erected in his 1984 still looms large in all our imaginations. That terrible Authority that chose who and how to hate and punished "thought crimes," against which stood the complementary Ministry of Truth.

Personally, I have always believed that the criminalization of hate speech, already abundantly provided for by the legal system, is not the most effective path to follow. Rather, it seems necessary to monitor the ever-increasing (and concerning) pressure aimed at assigning to criminal law a role that is not its own: not as a last resort, but as a pedagogical-ethical-moral function that should, reasonably, be entrusted to different tools, primarily educational, formative, cultural, starting from language, in families and schools from childhood.

**4) An Assessment of Freedom of Expression**

An assessment of freedom of expression is an unavoidable step to contextualize the issue of hatred. 

We are certainly far from the absoluteness of the cultural model of freedom of expression as that of the First Amendment of the United States, which states, "Congress shall make no law... abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press." A provision centered on the prohibition for the federal legislator to intervene by limiting freedom of expression and, therefore, constructed as a limit against public powers. In Europe, the model is obviously different, also because hate speech finds its dimension in the necessary balancing between freedom of expression and the dignity of the individual (prohibition of discrimination, Article 14 ECHR, Article 21 Charter of Rights). Freedom of expression is not absolute (Article 10 ECHR, Article 11 Charter of Rights), but encounters limitations provided by law and proportional to the achievement of objectives.

Yet, to "take seriously" the freedom of expression of thought is no small matter even in Europe. In our legal system, starting from the foundations, it must indeed be recognized as the first among freedoms, belonging to the "hard core" of inviolable human rights and, as a supreme principle of the legal system, removed from constitutional revision. In liberal-democratic systems, freedom of expression is placed at the base of the constitutional fabric, upon which political representation rests, the fruit of the free contest of ideas: that is to say, the distribution of power, political direction choices, public opinion control. This is in essential contrast to what happens in forms of autocratic state. In this perspective, one correctly speaks of a total state (according to Forsthoff's formula, Der totale Staat, 1933), where the unconditional conformation of everything to the dominant ideology prevails, whether secular or religious in nature. The close connection between freedom of expression and liberal democracy has been widely argued by political writers and legal doctrine. Freedom of thought is thus, in the test of resistance, freedom of dissent and criticism, the right to differentiation from everything anchored to the fabric of established values or, in any case, referable to the dominant power structures understood in a broad sense (Pacillo, Crimes Against Religious Confessions after Law No. 85 of February 24, 2006, 2007). And so it carries with it another essential element of liberal democracy: namely, the guarantee of minorities and dissent, so much so that - it is said - an alarm bell must sound when dissent is silent (Chiodi, Tacit Dissent, Turin, 1990).

Yet, despite our Constitution containing as the only expressed limit to freedom of expression the good morals, the Constitutional Court has extracted from the constitutional text a series of legal situations deserving of protection capable of supporting limitations on the freedom of expression. In particular, from the perspective of a systemic protection of rights, the freedom of expression may be subject to limitation in cases where it causes an immediate and definitive injury to constitutionally guaranteed values. In pursuing this systemic protection of rights, the Constitutional Court has identified as constitutionally protected goods capable of supporting limitations on freedom of expression: the right to honor, decorum, respectability, privacy, reputation (decisions 122 of 1970, 38 of 1973, 86 of 1974), the protection of minors (decisions nos. 9 and 25 of 1965 and 16 of 1981), religious sentiment (decision no. 188 of 1975), the defense...
**5. Hate on the Internet and the Role of Platforms.**

Let us then take this entire discussion and bring it directly to the universal issue of the present, namely the internet, where the theme of hate explodes. In the situation of regulatory anarchy regarding what constitutes hate, platforms enter the scene with a range of actions based on the evident contractual asymmetry, which ranges from the subsequent removal, in practice punitive, of content to the preventive measure of closing the accounts of repeat offenders or algorithmically presumed haters.

The constitutional freedom of expression online is today defined by the platforms that assess the ethically and legally acceptable boundaries, deciding what can and cannot be said in the digital agora. The issue is very serious and must start from an examination of reality. The involvement of platforms in regulating freedom of expression has become a necessity that seems difficult to overlook. While in some respects they represent the most direct and effective channels, in others, they prove inadequate to conceive a strategy in line with the constitutional parameters of modern democracies. This is the consequence of the very institutional design of the Internet, conceived as a fluid transnational space that currently makes these actors perhaps the only subjects capable of effectively regulating digital content and shaping the reality of online expression. However, in doing so, online freedom of expression ends up depending on the platforms, taking on a role alternative to that of the State, as it is now established that Facebook, YouTube, Twitter, etc., are not (any longer) a simple information intermediation service, but have become governance structures of the system themselves. Consequently, any regulation policy of the Internet pursued by the platforms necessarily entails a reduction of the area of means of reaction in the hands of the State.

The platforms' approach to the issue of hate speech currently highlights several weaknesses in the process of moderating freedom of expression, including: a) the absence of clear and knowable evaluation parameters in community standards; b) the absence of effective legal reasoning and procedural safeguards leading to a summary and careless procedure; c) the approximation of the examples of community standards; d) the use of algorithms.

In summary, regarding the first aspect, it should at least be noted that community standards are prepared by content policy teams and concern content moderation policies in light of the general conduct criteria contained therein. These rules established by the platforms serve as a true norm that impacts fundamental personal rights, even though no public actor is involved in their drafting. Their content is often vague, and the standard of protection for freedom of expression is highly discretionary. Facebook, for example, largely delegates its moderation policy to internal guidelines whose content and formation process are not always linear. There is also a lack of transparency and accountability regarding the procedures for removing posts, factors that lead to an infringement not only of the individual manifestation of thought but also of the very legal outcomes underlying the principle of equality.

Regarding the second aspect, namely the absence of effective legal reasoning and procedural safeguards, it should be highlighted that the moderation phase of potential hate speech begins with a report, which can come either from users or from algorithmic results. Various selection teams (content policy teams) or the algorithm are tasked with evaluating the unlawfulness of content in a matter of seconds, regardless of the type of illegality. And here lies the first problem, because for more blatant violations, co...
into their decision-making process? The limitations of algorithms in understanding human nuances and the complexities of language can lead to significant errors in judgment, especially in sensitive areas such as hate speech and content moderation.

1. **Immediate and Intuitive Decisions**  
   In cases like child pornography and terrorism, the decision-making process is more immediate and intuitive. However, for other categories, such as the fragile boundaries of hate speech, legal expertise and more time for reflection would be required.

2. **Qualifications for Content Policy Teams**  
   To be part of Facebook's content policy teams, which should consist of presumed "experts" on issues such as terrorism, incitement to hatred, and child pornography, no specific legal qualifications or even specialization are required.

3. **Communication of Content Removal**  
   When the (alleged) illegal content is removed, the complete removal of the content and its justification is not always communicated to the user. This represents another problematic aspect.

4. **Opaque Appeal Procedures**  
   Currently, there is an opaque procedure for contesting removed posts: in the notification that communicates the removal of content, there is an option to appeal for a second review, but the user is not given the opportunity to provide a detailed argument in defense of the post in question. A second reviewer will decide simply within 24 hours whether to confirm the removal. Thus, there is a lack of a true adversarial process.

5. **Identification of Hate Conduct**  
   A third problematic aspect concerns the concrete identification of hate conduct. Each platform has proceeded independently, sometimes describing and other times exemplifying expressions potentially attributable to hate speech. For example, Twitter remains generally vague on this matter, considering prohibited: "content that wishes, promotes, or hopes for death, serious bodily injury, or serious illness to an entire protected category and/or to people who may be part of it"; "humiliating or insulting content," or "dehumanizing speech." A dozen "case studies" are included, illustrative but not exhaustive: "I will kill you," "you should get cancer," "all [members of a religious group] are terrorists," etc. These expressions certainly exceed the limits of free speech in every respect.

6. **Dual Evaluation Criteria**  
   For Twitter, there would thus be a dual criterion for evaluation: one concerning the recipient of the expression (protected groups or groups that have suffered genocides) and another, alternative or cumulative, concerning the content: violence or wish, hope or invocation of serious harm to a person or a group of people. A more in-depth analysis, however, highlights the presence of cases whose offensiveness is ambiguous or difficult to assess and, in any case, does not seem to contain the necessary prerequisites to constitute a true hate speech.

7. **Facebook's Community Standards**  
   This is the case with Facebook's community standards, although its exemplification is much richer and more detailed compared to Twitter. Facebook divides the types of prohibited content into three levels (it is unclear whether these levels also correspond to a climax of offensiveness or are distinct only for the nature of the offenses; nor is the response graded according to the severity of the hatred expressed by the statement). However, some of the examples are perplexing due to their inherently harmless nature. It would not even be possible to refer to someone belonging to a minority community with the following adjectives: "liar," "ignorant," "crazy," "incompetent," "useless." 

8. **Explicit Exclusion and Judgments**  
   Moreover, according to level 3 ("Content directed at individuals or groups of individuals"), one cannot express judgments on the "explicit exclusion" of individuals or groups of individuals: in particular, using the verbs "expel" or "not allow." It must be presumed that a statement like: "All undocumented immigrants must be expelled" would be contrary to the company's rules. Furthermore, one cannot speak of "denial of access to social services." Thus, it is not only about insults but also about arguments that express one's view on politically and institutionally relevant issues.

9. **Distinguishing Hate Crimes from Positions**  
   But in a conversation on such topics, how can one distinguish a hate crime from a position, however contestable, of someone who intends, for example, to outright exclude immigrants from social services? And how to evaluate the position of someone who argues that only regular foreigners should be allowed to enjoy social and health benefits, while irregular ones should not? What role should the context in which the statement is made (for example, in a joking atmosphere among friends) play? And how is it evaluated?

10. **Imperfect Algorithmic Decisions**  
    A final aspect concerns the imperfection of algorithmic decisions. Millions of illicit contents are removed every month thanks to the use of algorithms. The use of algorithmic selection has become an essential variable, for example, for Twitter and Google, which delegate a large part of the reports to the tool. The attempt to accompany decisions concerning general interests or fundamental rights through a dehumanized mathematical method aims, among various aspects, at two primary objectives: to minimize the margin of error and to ensure total impartiality and transparency regarding the limitation of freedom of expression. 

11. **Problems with Algorithms**  
    The problematic aspects of the algorithm concern precisely its lack of accuracy with reference to context and the intrinsic biases that characterize it. This stems from a well-known fact, namely that at the base of the algorithm there is always knowledge conditioned by human programming activity. The power to steer the algorithm lies in the hands of those who hold the algorithm's code. Those who possess the code use it against other subjects, and there is no symmetry in this operation. But, above all, algorithms are incapable of interpreting context: to what extent can algorithms (even those of machine learning) read our true intentions, the "style" with which we share a comment or a photo? How much can they read the psychological constructs of each user and incorporate them into their decision-making process?
In the specific context of reference, also composed of equally psychological constructs of the different users? 

On all these issues, the recent approval of the Digital Service Act by the European Parliament certainly represents a significant step forward in terms of measures to combat the spread of illegal content online. It is not irrelevant that effective guarantees for users have been considered, including the possibility to challenge the content moderation decisions of platforms and the introduction of transparency measures for the platforms themselves, including regarding the algorithms used. However, it is striking, especially in light of the reflections on the definitional problems proposed, that throughout the text there is never a reference to hate as a definition of the offense, but only generic references in the Report to the text (pages 4 and 6, moreover with reference to the aforementioned Code of Conduct on Online Hate Speech of the European Commission) and in the Recitals (n. 12, n. 57, n. 69). This is not a minor omission for a text that aims to become the European cornerstone in the fight against hate speech online. From this perspective, I believe it should be taken into account in an Italian regulatory context. 

Thank you for your attention.
