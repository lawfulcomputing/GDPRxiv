**The Family Court is Again Subject to Serious Criticism**  
Date: 02-07-2024  
**Decision**  
Public Authorities  
Serious Criticism  
Supervision / Self-initiated Case  
Processing Security  
Unintentional Disclosure  

The Data Protection Agency has once again expressed criticism of the Family Court for a lack of processing security, which has led to several instances of unintentional disclosure of protected information and information about stays at crisis centers.  
**Journal Number:** 2023-432-0003  

**Summary**  
The Family Court has reported 28 breaches of personal data security to the Data Protection Agency during the period from January 6, 2023, to June 30, 2024. The breaches consist of the Family Court unlawfully disclosing information about protected names and addresses or other information that could potentially reveal a person's whereabouts, including stays at crisis centers. In most cases, the information was disclosed to the other party in the case, which typically could be the reason for the choice of name and address protection or stay at a crisis center. The unintentional disclosures are due to human errors resulting from employee inattention. The Data Protection Agency has previously dealt with two similar cases regarding the Family Court's unintentional disclosure of the same types of information. The Data Protection Agency made decisions in these cases on March 4, 2021, and September 6, 2022. In both cases, serious criticism was expressed, and in the 2022 case, the Family Court was also ordered to conduct a renewed risk assessment and, based on this, to establish necessary organizational or technical security measures to reduce the risk of new similar breaches.  

**Serious Criticism for Failing to Meet the Requirement for Appropriate Security**  
The Data Protection Agency can ascertain that the Family Court has, over the years, initiated a number of security measures to reduce the risk of this type of breach. However, the Data Protection Agency finds grounds to once again express serious criticism that the Family Court has not met the requirement for appropriate security. In light of the still relatively high number of breaches and the very serious consequences that this type of breach can have for the affected individuals, it is the Data Protection Agency's opinion that the Family Court must continue to make even very significant efforts to avoid unintentional disclosure of particularly information about protected names and addresses and stays at crisis centers, etc. Furthermore, it is the Data Protection Agency's opinion that the Family Court has not yet implemented sufficient security measures—or ensured that the identified measures have had the necessary effect—to reduce the risk that employees, due to errors, misunderstandings, or inattention, unintentionally disclose information about registered individuals to unauthorized recipients, including particularly the opposing party in the case, which is often the reason for the need for protection.  

Thus, the Family Court has not sufficiently ensured that employees have exercised the necessary care in processing citizens' personal data, including in connection with the additional control that, in certain cases, is performed by another caseworker, nor have sufficient organizational or technical security measures been implemented to otherwise mitigate the unintentional disclosures of personal data that have continuously occurred as a result of misunderstandings, errors, or inattention among employees.  

However, the Data Protection Agency has noted that the Family Court continues to work on strengthening existing measures and implementing new measures aimed at reducing the risk of similar breaches, as well as implementing processes for ongoing control and evaluation of the effectiveness of existing security measures, including in connection with the identification of new breaches. The Data Protection Agency finds grounds to continue to closely monitor the development in the number of such breaches. In this regard, the Data Protection Agency has requested that the Family Court provide detailed information in the coming year—regarding the information already provided in connection with the reporting of the breach to the Data Protection Agency—concerning any future breaches of the same type. This includes, among other things, a detailed description of what the assessment of the breach has led to in terms of reducing the risk of similar breaches.  

Repeated breaches of personal data security should generally prompt data controllers to reflect on already conducted risk assessments. Breach types attributed to personal errors or isolated incidents should, upon repetition, lead to the introduction of additional effective control measures, new guidelines, and/or technical support that minimizes the now known and updated risks.  

**Decision**  
1. **Background**  
The Data Protection Agency hereby returns to the case[1], where the Data Protection Agency on September 6, 2022 – in a case that the agency had initiated on its own regarding the Family Court's unintentional disclosures of protected name and address information – expressed serious criticism that the Family Court's processing of personal data had not been conducted in accordance with the rules in the General Data Protection Regulation[2] Article 32, paragraph 1. The Data Protection Agency also found grounds to issue the Family Court an order to conduct a renewed...
Risk assessment based on the breaches addressed in the decision. The Family Law House was also required, based on the risk assessment, to establish necessary organizational and/or technical security measures. If the Family Law House assessed that the risk to the rights and freedoms of the registered individuals was high, the order also included that the Family Law House should prepare a consequence analysis, cf. Article 35 of the General Data Protection Regulation. The deadline for complying with the order was January 6, 2023. The Data Protection Agency requested, by the same date, to receive confirmation that the order had been complied with, as well as information on what changes in the technical and organizational measures had been implemented.

The case concerned 37 reports of breaches of personal data security received from May 27, 2021, to August 16, 2022. The breaches involved the disclosure of protected addresses, protected names, phone numbers, email addresses, information about where the children attended school or institution, the residence of one party, including names of crisis centers, first name and job title of the leader of a crisis center, city or municipality, which could potentially reveal the party's residence to the unauthorized recipient. In most cases, the information had been disclosed to the opposing party in the case, who was typically the person responsible for the party having name and address protection. The reason for wanting name and address protection in relation to the opposing party could be to prevent the opposing party from obtaining information about the residence, e.g., due to concerns about child abduction, violence, etc., or because the party did not wish to have contact via email or phone with the other party, who may also have a restraining order. In some cases, one parent had also changed their name to hide from the other parent.

The information had been disclosed in connection with responses to requests for access to documents, sending decisions, information letters, and party hearings. The disclosures were primarily due to human errors in the form of caseworkers not being attentive to the information, which is why the necessary anonymization of the information had not been carried out before decisions, etc., were sent to the opposing party.

The Data Protection Agency stated in connection with the criticism decision that the agency could ascertain that the Family Law House – following the agency's decision of March 4, 2021 – had implemented significant organizational measures to avoid unintentional disclosure of protected name and address information. However, it was the Data Protection Agency's opinion that new and repeated breaches of personal data security or incidents that should be recorded on the list required under Article 33, paragraph 5 of the General Data Protection Regulation should have prompted the data controller to reflect on already conducted risk assessments. Breach types attributed to personal errors or isolated incidents should – upon repetition – have led to the introduction of additional effective control measures or technical support that minimized the now known and actualized risks.

The Data Protection Agency found that the Family Law House – by having identified and reported several similar breaches of personal data security, without having reconsidered the existing measures to prevent future breaches of the same type – had not taken appropriate organizational and technical measures to ensure a level of security that was appropriate to the risks associated with the Family Law House's processing of personal data, cf. Article 32, paragraph 1 of the General Data Protection Regulation. The Data Protection Agency placed particular emphasis on the fact that the Family Law House had not sufficiently had the necessary procedures for regular verification, assessment, and evaluation of the effectiveness of the already established measures.

The Data Protection Agency also expressed serious criticism of the Family Law House on March 4, 2021. The Data Protection Agency also initiated a case on its own initiative against the Family Law House on October 1, 2020, as the agency could ascertain that the Family Law House had reported 158 breaches of personal data security from May 2018 until September 27, 2020, and that 134 of these reports concerned unintentional disclosure of personal data. Of the 134 breaches, 34 cases were found to involve individuals with protected names and addresses.

The Data Protection Agency made a decision in the case on March 4, 2021. The Data Protection Agency found, among other things, that the Family Law House had not sufficiently ensured that employees had exercised the necessary care in processing citizens' personal data, including protected name and address information. It was the Data Protection Agency's assessment that the human errors could have been avoided with the necessary care from the employees' side, just as the extra control performed by another caseworker was evidently not sufficiently effective.

On that basis, the Data Protection Agency found grounds to express serious criticism that the Family Law House's processing of personal data had not been carried out in accordance with the rules in Article 32, paragraph 1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

**Family Court's Statement of September 15, 2023, on the Effectiveness of Security Measures**  
As part of the assessment of whether the Family Court has complied with the order of September 22, 2022, the Data Protection Authority reviewed the similar breaches that the Family Court reported to the Data Protection Authority during the period from September 22, 2022, to July 31, 2023. In total, there were 28 breaches, of which 13 were reported after January 6, 2023.  
Of the 13 breaches, 10 were reported in June and July 2023, respectively.  
On this basis, the Data Protection Authority requested by letter dated August 18, 2023, that the Family Court inform whether it is assessed that the additional established security measures have had an effect in reducing the risk of unintentional disclosure of, among other things, protected name and address information, and whether the Family Court expects a further effect in light of the fact that not all planned measures have been implemented yet.  
The Family Court informed on September 15, 2023, that there was a strong focus on avoiding data breaches in the approximately 150,000 cases processed annually. The Family Court had thus initiated a number of initiatives and in this connection had particularly implemented awareness measures, reviewed self-service solutions aimed at data minimization, and improved workflows to minimize the number of data breaches. Additionally, the Family Court was engaged in work to ensure an even greater focus on precision in obtaining information and on the basis for assessing which information can and should be exempted from party hearing and access to documents.  

The Family Court further pointed out that there were (already) the following special case processing steps to address this type of security breach:  
- If one of the parties in the case is residing at a crisis center, it is registered in the case title. This ensures that the caseworker can clearly see that one of the parties has or has had residence at a crisis center or has name and address protection.  
- In connection with party hearings and access to documents, where a party resides at a crisis center, it is stated in the Family Court's guidelines that information about residence at a crisis center must be removed so that the opposing party cannot become aware of where the other party resides, unless it is specifically stated that it is not necessary.  
- In cases where one of the parties has name and address protection, this is also added to the case title, ensuring that in party hearings and access to documents, it is considered whether name and address protection indicates a situation where such conditions are present that decisive considerations for private interests dictate that the information must be kept confidential under the provisions of the Public Administration Act § 15 b, no. 5.  
- When a caseworker has exempted information from party hearing/access to documents, the party hearing/access must always be double-checked by a colleague to catch any errors in anonymization.  
- If the task of providing access to documents is delegated to the unit that handles access requests, it must be noted on the access request that specifically...
**To clarify whether the opposing party and possibly the child/children have name and address protection or are at a crisis center. In this context, it must be clarified whether the person requesting access to documents may be informed of the opposing party's/child's/children's name, address, and contact details.**

It was the assessment of the Family Law House that there had been a positive development in the number of data breaches in general, but also for data breaches concerning information about name and address protection. However, the development over the summer of 2023 was excluded from this, which the Family Law House would investigate the background for. The Family Law House thus closely monitored the development with a view to being able to implement appropriate measures if needed.

Furthermore, it was stated that the Family Law House was in the final phase regarding the decision on the choice of anonymization tool for purchase and implementation, which is expected to reduce the number of data breaches in the form of overlooked individual disclosures of otherwise confidential information. 

Finally, the Family Law House stated that a new case management system would not be purchased, but that the existing system would instead be improved.

**4. Justification for the Data Protection Authority's Decision**

The Data Protection Authority assumes that the Family Law House has reported 28 breaches concerning the unintentional disclosure of information about protected names and addresses or other information that could potentially reveal the party's whereabouts during the period from January 6, 2023, to June 30, 2024. The information has typically been disclosed to the opposing party, which may be the reason for the choice of, among other things, name and address protection. There was particularly a larger number of breaches during two shorter periods – specifically in the summer of 2023 and around the turn of the year 2023/2024.

The Data Protection Authority further assumes that the unintentional disclosures of the information are due to human errors resulting from employee inattention. 

Additionally, the Data Protection Authority assumes that the Family Law House has, over the years, taken the initiative to implement a series of security measures to reduce the risk of this type of breach. 

It follows from Article 32, paragraph 1 of the General Data Protection Regulation that the data controller, taking into account the current technical level, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing, as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to these risks.

Thus, the data controller has an obligation to identify the risks that its processing poses to the data subjects and to ensure that appropriate security measures are implemented to protect the data subjects against these risks. The General Data Protection Regulation does not prescribe which specific security measures must be implemented to meet the regulation's requirements for processing security, as the focus is instead on a risk-based approach. However, it can generally be stated that the requirements for processing security are heightened the higher the risk is for the affected individuals whose information an organization processes.

It is the opinion of the Data Protection Authority that the requirement under Article 32 for appropriate security will normally imply that, as a data controller, one must ensure that information about data subjects, including particularly confidential and sensitive information, does not come to the knowledge of unauthorized persons, and that, therefore, appropriate quality control of the content in sent documents should be performed to avoid disclosures of personal data to unauthorized parties, and that handling of confidential and sensitive personal data generally imposes greater demands on employees' diligence in connection with the sending of personal data, including ensuring that correct information is sent to the correct recipient.

Furthermore, it follows from Article 32, paragraph 1, letter d of the regulation that the data controller, as relevant, must implement a procedure for regular testing, assessment, and evaluation of the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure processing security.

This requirement entails, among other things, that repeated breaches of personal data security should prompt the data controller to reflect on already conducted risk assessments. Types of breaches attributed to personal errors or isolated incidents should, upon repetition, lead to the introduction of additional effective control measures, new guidelines, and/or technical support that minimizes the now known and updated risks.

The Data Protection Authority can ascertain that the Family Law House – following the Authority's decision of March 4, 2021 – has implemented significant organizational measures to avoid unintentional disclosure of protected name and address information. 

The Data Protection Authority is also aware that the Family Law House processes a very large number of cases each year, including cases involving protected names and addresses, and that security breaches due to human errors can be difficult to completely avoid in these cases, where the disclosure of information is seen to depend on manual processes and assessments.

Given the continued relatively high number of breaches and the very serious consequences that this type of breach can have for the affected individuals, it is, however, the opinion of the Data Protection Authority that considerable ongoing efforts must still be made to avoid unintentional disclosures, particularly of information about protected names and addresses and residence at crisis centers, etc., and that the Family Law House has not yet implemented sufficient security measures – or ensured that the identified measures have had the necessary effect – to reduce the risk that employees, due to errors, misunderstandings, or inattention, unintentionally disclose information about data subjects to unauthorized recipients, including particularly the opposing party in the case, which is often the reason for the need for protection.

Thus, the Family Law House has not sufficiently ensured that employees have exercised the necessary care in the processing of citizens' personal data – particularly information about protected names and addresses and other information that could potentially reveal a person's whereabouts with serious consequences.

The Data Protection Agency finds, based on the above, that there is again grounds to express serious criticism of the Family Law Agency's processing of personal data not being in accordance with the rules in Article 32, paragraph 1 of the General Data Protection Regulation.

The Data Protection Agency has further noted that the Family Law Agency has reported only 4 breaches of this type during the period from February 2024 to and including June 2024. One of the breaches occurred in January, which was also during one of the shorter periods when a larger number of breaches occurred. It is noted in this context that the Data Protection Agency assumes that the Family Law Agency clarifies whether there is also a need to implement additional measures to avoid a larger number of breaches in connection with the holiday period at the turn of the year 2024/2025, similar to what occurred at the turn of the year 2023/2024.  
As stated above, the Data Protection Agency finds grounds to continue closely monitoring the development in the number of breaches of the type concerning this case. The Data Protection Agency therefore looks forward to receiving the detailed information specified in section 2 regarding any new breaches of this type.  
5. Final Remarks  
The decisions of the Data Protection Agency cannot be appealed to another administrative authority, cf. Section 30 of the Data Protection Act. However, the Agency's decisions can be appealed to the courts, cf. Section 63 of the Constitution.  
The Data Protection Agency hereby considers the case with case number 2023-432-0003 to be concluded. However, the Data Protection Agency looks forward to receiving detailed information about any similar breaches, as mentioned above.
