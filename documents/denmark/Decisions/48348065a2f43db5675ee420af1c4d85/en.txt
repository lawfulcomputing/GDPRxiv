The Data Protection Agency has now made a decision in the sixth and final supervision case regarding the processing security in AULA, which the agency initiated in the autumn of 2021 concerning six selected municipalities. At the Data Protection Agency's contact committee meeting in May, the work on processing security in AULA formed the basis for discussions on risk assessments and impact analyses.  
Journal Number: 2023-423-0005.  

Summary  
In December 2023, the Data Protection Agency made decisions in five out of six supervision cases initiated in the autumn of 2021 regarding the selected municipalities' processing of personal data in the IT system AULA. The supervisions focused on compliance with the data protection regulation's rules on processing security and impact analysis.  

Copenhagen Municipality Criticized for Deficiencies in Impact Analysis  
The Data Protection Agency has now made a decision in the supervision case involving Copenhagen Municipality, which is the sixth and final of these cases. The Data Protection Agency has expressed criticism, as the agency has assessed that the impact analysis does not meet all the minimum requirements for an impact analysis. The decision also contains some recommendations for the municipality's further work on the planned update of the impact analysis.  

Regarding the municipality's risk assessment, the Data Protection Agency has recommended that Copenhagen Municipality, in their revised risk assessment concept, structure the information so that it becomes possible to compare the risk assessments before and after the implementation of the measures, and that the connection between the measures and the individual risks is clearly presented. The Data Protection Agency found that the municipality had not fully demonstrated that appropriate measures had been implemented to reduce all the risks identified by the municipality.  

However, Copenhagen Municipality has worked seriously and purposefully to reduce the identified risks. In particular, the municipality has focused on reducing the risk that the use of UNI-login entails, according to the municipality's assessment.  

The supervision case involving Copenhagen Municipality contains more elements than the other five supervision cases. The Data Protection Agency became aware of an internal supervision report from Copenhagen Municipality's data protection advisor, which among other things concerned the municipality's use of AULA's module "Secure File Sharing," based on a press mention in the spring of 2023. Therefore, in this case, the Data Protection Agency has examined the supervision report and requested the municipality to answer some questions.  

Based on this, the Data Protection Agency has recommended in the decision that Copenhagen Municipality – possibly in dialogue with the other municipalities, KOMBIT, and KL – clarify the purpose of processing personal data in AULA in the municipality's impact analysis. The Data Protection Agency has further noted that it is relevant to clarify which document types "Secure File Sharing" in AULA is used for, and what types of personal data these document types typically contain.

1. Written supervision of the City of Copenhagen regarding the processing security in AULA
The City of Copenhagen is among the municipalities that the Data Protection Agency selected for supervision in the autumn of 2021 under the General Data Protection Regulation[1] and the Data Protection Act[2]. The Data Protection Agency's supervision is a written oversight with a particular focus on the technical and organizational security measures that have been observed to comply with the requirement for an appropriate level of security for the processing activities in the IT system AULA. The supervision is a continuation of the Data Protection Agency's previous supervision of Kombit A/S (hereinafter KOMBIT) in December 2019, where the supervision during a site visit could ascertain that KOMBIT is not to be regarded as the data controller for AULA under Article 4, No. 7 of the General Data Protection Regulation. Therefore, the Data Protection Agency informed KOMBIT that KOMBIT must ensure that all municipalities are informed of their data responsibilities and that KOMBIT provides the municipalities with all necessary materials and information in this regard. The Data Protection Agency expects that KOMBIT has informed the municipalities about this immediately after the site visit in December 2019.

2. Decision
After a sample review of KOMBIT's risk assessment materials and a review of the City of Copenhagen's risk assessment as well as the other materials submitted, the Data Protection Agency finds that the City of Copenhagen has demonstrated that it has identified the most significant risks that the municipality's processing of personal data in AULA poses to the data subjects. The City of Copenhagen appears to have documented how the municipality has assessed the risk to the data subjects from the relevant processing activities, as well as how the municipality relates to KOMBIT's risk assessment. The City of Copenhagen seems to have worked very seriously and purposefully to reduce the identified risks. In this context, the City of Copenhagen is particularly noted to have worked on reducing the risk that the use of UNI-login, according to the municipality's assessment, entails. However, it is the Data Protection Agency's assessment that the City of Copenhagen has not fully demonstrated that the municipality has implemented appropriate measures to reduce all identified risks, including the risks outlined in the risk assessment materials from KOMBIT. The Data Protection Agency has noted that the City of Copenhagen is working on a revised risk assessment concept. Therefore, the Data Protection Agency recommends that the City of Copenhagen structures the information in the revised risk assessment concept in such a way that it is possible to compare the assessments of risks before and after the measures have been implemented, and that the relationship between measures and individual risks is clear. In this way, an overview can be created that can help ensure that all identified risks have been assessed and managed through the implementation of appropriate security measures. After reviewing the City of Copenhagen's impact assessment and the underlying risk assessment forms, the Data Protection Agency finds that the City of Copenhagen, regarding the timing of the preparation of their impact assessment, has acted in accordance with Article 35, Paragraph 1 of the General Data Protection Regulation. However, it is the Data Protection Agency's assessment that the City of Copenhagen has not conducted a sufficient impact assessment, cf. Article 35, Paragraph 1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, as the submitted impact assessment does not meet the minimum requirements set out in Article 35, Paragraph 7, letters a-d of the Regulation.

On this basis, the Data Protection Agency finds grounds to criticize that the City of Copenhagen's processing of personal data has not been carried out in accordance with Article 35, Paragraph 1 of the Regulation. Finally, the Data Protection Agency finds that the rollout of AULA to the daycare institutions has not resulted in such a change in the risk posed by the processing activities that the City of Copenhagen should have conducted a renewed review of the impact assessment in accordance with Article 35, Paragraph 11 of the General Data Protection Regulation. The Data Protection Agency has noted that the City of Copenhagen had planned an update of the impact assessment in the autumn of 2023 based on the municipality's new concept for risk assessments, including impact assessments. The Data Protection Agency recommends that the municipality, in this context, updates the information in the impact assessment so that it accurately reflects that AULA is also used in the municipality's daycare institutions. Furthermore, the Data Protection Agency recommends that the City of Copenhagen – possibly in dialogue with other municipalities, KOMBIT, and KL – clarifies the purpose of the processing of personal data in AULA in the municipality's impact assessment. In this context, it is also relevant to clarify in the impact assessment which document types Secure File Sharing is used for, and what types of personal data these document types typically contain.

3.1. General Information on the Decision and Development of AULA  
The Danish Municipalities' Association (hereinafter KL) states on their website that the municipal acquisition of AULA is part of the User Portal Initiative, which is a political initiative from 2013.[3] It further states that AULA aims to support communication and thereby collaboration regarding children's learning and well-being in a secure and user-friendly manner.  
KL also states that all 98 municipalities in Denmark jointly participated in a common tender for a collaboration platform – the later AULA – for the school sector in 2016. AULA was put out to tender by the municipalities' IT community, KOMBIT A/S (hereinafter KOMBIT), at the beginning of 2017, and the choice fell on the supplier Netcompany – IT and Business Consulting, which has since developed AULA in collaboration with the companies Frog, EduLab, and Advice.  
KOMBIT provides information about AULA on the website aulainfo.dk. It appears from this that the majority of public schools began using AULA in 2019 and daycare services in the winter of 2020/2021.[4]  
The Data Protection Agency assumes that the individual municipalities are independently responsible for their processing of personal data in AULA, and that KOMBIT, which is the municipalities' data processor, has entered into a data processing agreement with Netcompany, which is thus a sub-processor.[5]

Regarding the messaging module in AULA, it is stated, for example, in KOMBIT's risk assessment that they have identified 10 threats, three of which are deemed most likely. The three threats are marked in red, and the Data Protection Agency understands from the material that KOMBIT assesses these as high risks.

One of the threats concerns user behavior that leads to mismanagement of personal data in the system, carried out by an authorized user through unintentional disclosure of personal data in the application. The consequence of loss of confidentiality is categorized as "devastating," and it is noted, among other things, that children may suffer significant reputational damage, which can lead to social losses. The likelihood is categorized as "expected," and it is noted in this context that it is anticipated that there will be cases where a user mistakenly sends a message containing personal data to one or more users, for example, by sending to a group.

Another threat concerns unintentional process errors that lead to incorrect handling due to incorrect or missing implementation of data classification in the solution. It is further described as a leak of sensitive data due to incorrect (too low) or missing data classification. The consequence of loss of confidentiality is categorized as "very serious." It is noted that the reason for this is that sensitive personal data (e.g., messages regarding a child's illness/health) can be accessed or altered by unauthorized individuals internally, and that registered individuals are exposed internally to unauthorized access. Furthermore, it is stated that registered individuals are exposed in a way that harms social relationships, both internally among colleagues/students. The likelihood is categorized as "expected." It is noted in this context that it is users who must classify data, and human errors are anticipated. As an example, it is stated that it is possible for a user to send a message containing sensitive personal data without marking it as "sensitive message," resulting in lower security.

The third threat concerns unintentional process errors where there are incorrect accesses due to unclear or lacking policies. The consequence of loss of confidentiality is categorized as "very serious." The material provides the same description of the consequence as for incorrect handling due to incorrect or missing implementation of data classification in the solution. The likelihood is categorized as "expected." It is noted in this context that all municipalities must assign rights to many users/groups, which is why errors must be expected. As an example, it is stated that a group may have members who no longer belong to the group and who have not been removed from the group.

The City of Copenhagen describes in a submitted background note regarding the work on security in AULA in the Children and Youth Administration that the municipality's risk assessment differs from KOMBIT's risk assessment, as KOMBIT assesses that AULA has strong functional separation and strong user authentication, and therefore KOMBIT assesses that data leaks as a result are unlikely. 

The Data Protection Agency thus assumes based on this note that the City of Copenhagen, in addition to the risks identified in KOMBIT's risk assessments, has also identified high risks concerning unauthorized access to AULA and data due to insufficient user authentication and unauthorized access to data due to lack of or insufficient functional separation in system accesses.

3.4. Reported breaches of personal data security regarding AULA  
The background for the Data Protection Agency's initial decision to supervise the municipalities with a particular focus on the technical and organizational security measures that have been observed to meet the requirement for an appropriate level of security for the processing in AULA is that the agency has seen (and continues to see) a number of reported breaches of personal data security in connection with the processing of personal data in AULA.  
In AULA, personal data is processed, including
confidential and sensitive personal data about vulnerable registered individuals, including children. Furthermore, the system's users are often individuals who do not primarily work with data protection (including educators, pedagogical staff, as well as students and parents), which, in the opinion of the supervisory authority – in light of the many reported breaches from the vast majority of the country's municipalities – may imply that stricter requirements should be imposed on the data controllers' observance of both technical and organizational measures, cf. Article 32, as well as mitigating measures for compliance with the regulation's Article 25. A large portion of these reports of breaches of personal data security in AULA concern instances where personal data has been sent to one or more incorrect recipients in AULA. For example, where a message or secure file regarding a child has been sent to another child's parents, or where a message has been mistakenly sent to a group of recipients instead of a specific recipient. The Data Protection Authority has also seen cases where, when sending documents to one or more parents, an incorrect document containing information about another child has been attached. Thus, there have been several unintended disclosures of personal data about children in AULA, including descriptions of children's concentration difficulties, settings for pedagogical-psychological assessments, information about dyslexia, school statements, as well as action plans and assessments regarding educational readiness. Upon an immediate search in the Data Protection Authority's ESDH system, the authority can ascertain that it has received at least 35 reports of breaches of personal data security from the Municipality of Copenhagen under the data protection regulation's Article 33, where AULA is mentioned in the report. The majority of the reports concern incidents where a message in AULA has been sent to one or more incorrect recipients. For instance, there are cases where a pedagogical psychological assessment was sent to another child's parents, and where a message containing information about a child's dyslexia was sent to another pair of parents in the same class. Additionally, there is a case where a parent group of 72 individuals was mistakenly added to an AULA message thread for staff, which included meeting minutes from staff meetings containing information about children in the institution and their families. There is a similar incident where a teacher intended to send a message to a pair of parents regarding their son but mistakenly sent the message to 94 unrelated recipients, including staff from other schools in the municipality. Furthermore, the Data Protection Authority has received a report from the municipality regarding unauthorized access to information about whether contacts in AULA (parents) have custody over a child or not. The Data Protection Authority has also received three reports from the municipality concerning unauthorized access to information in AULA, where unauthorized individuals have logged into some AULA users' profiles, as they in two of the cases have gained access to login information.

4.1.1. Relevant Rules  
The General Data Protection Regulation requires, on several points, that a data controller addresses the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects and can document the considerations and conclusions this has led to. It follows directly from the provisions in Articles 5, paragraph 1, letter f, 24, 25, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, and 39, that the data controller must address the risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects. Furthermore, it follows from both Article 5, paragraph 2, regarding the principles in Article 5, paragraph 1, and Article 24 regarding compliance with the entire regulation, that a data controller must be able to demonstrate compliance with data protection rules. Among other things, Articles 25, 35, and 36 require that considerations regarding the involved risks to the rights of the data subjects must take place before the processing is initiated. It follows from the General Data Protection Regulation's Article 5, paragraph 1, letter f, that personal data must be processed in a manner that ensures adequate security for the personal data in question, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, using appropriate technical or organizational measures.
**4.1.1. Responsibilities of the Data Controller**

The data controller must also be able to demonstrate that the fundamental principles are adhered to, as stipulated in Article 5, paragraph 2 of the Data Protection Regulation. According to Article 32, paragraph 1 of the Data Protection Regulation, the data controller must take appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a security level that is appropriate to the risks associated with the data controller's processing of personal data. 

It follows from Article 24, paragraph 1 of the Data Protection Regulation that a data controller must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure and to be able to demonstrate that the processing is in compliance with the regulation. The data controller has an obligation to identify the risks that the data controller's processing poses to the data subjects and to ensure that appropriate security measures are implemented to protect the data subjects from these risks. 

Furthermore, the data controller must be able to demonstrate that personal data is processed in a manner that ensures adequate security for the individuals to whom the data relates. In order for the supervisory authority to assess whether an appropriate level of security has been ensured, it must be documented and explained which risks the data controller has identified and what mitigating measures have been taken to reduce these risks.

**4.1.2. General Requirements for Risk Assessment**

The Data Protection Authority notes that the Data Protection Regulation does not specify how detailed the risk assessment should be. It is the opinion of the Data Protection Authority that the data controller must establish an appropriate level considering the risks that are relevant to the data controller's processing of personal data. 

If a supplier, data processor, or available analyses determine or indicate specific risk scenarios, it is the opinion of the Data Protection Authority that data controllers should take these assessments into account, particularly when processing activities in a system are deemed to involve high risks for the data subjects. At a minimum, there should be a substantiated assessment of why the matter is not relevant in the processing of personal data that occurs with the data controller.

It is not explicitly stated anywhere in the Data Protection Regulation how the documentation should look, what the specific content should be, or what the frequency of updates and revisions should be. The Data Protection Authority finds that the documentation obligation implies that the supervisory authority – from the total material that the data controller can present – can assess whether the processing is in compliance with the Data Protection Regulation. 

Thus, it should be evident from the submitted material how and on what basis the data protection considerations have been made. This includes, among other things, the legality of the processing, the ability to ensure compliance with the rights of the data subjects, adherence to the data protection principles, and ensuring an appropriate level of security. 

Additionally, it should be clear what considerations, choices, and omissions have been made to ensure that the processing of the information is in accordance with the Data Protection Regulation. In this context, it should be possible to timestamp when and with what content relevant considerations and actions have taken place. 

It is the opinion of the Data Protection Authority that a risk assessment should include an evaluation of the consequence (e.g., high, medium, low) for the data subjects in the event of loss of confidentiality, availability, and integrity. Subsequently, the risk assessment should identify what threats exist regarding the loss of confidentiality, availability, and integrity, as well as the likelihood (e.g., high, medium, low) that the threat will materialize. Finally, the risk assessment should map the existing security measures and their contribution to reducing the risk. Based on this, the data controllers can assess the risk and determine whether it is an acceptable risk or if further measures need to be implemented. By addressing these elements in a risk assessment, the data controllers can document how they have assessed the risk to the data subjects from the relevant processing activities, as well as on what basis they believe that the implemented measures have reduced the risk.

4.2. **Impact Analysis**
4.2.1. Requirement for a Consequence Analysis  
It follows from Article 35, paragraph 1 of the Data Protection Regulation that if a type of processing, particularly through the use of new technologies and by virtue of its nature, scope, context, and purposes, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller shall carry out an analysis of the intended processing activities' consequences for the protection of personal data prior to the processing. A single analysis may encompass several similar processing activities that involve similar high risks.  
In the guidelines on consequence analysis regarding data protection from the Article 29 Working Party (now the European Data Protection Board, EDPB)[6], criteria are established that can help identify the processing activities that will require a consequence analysis. According to the guidelines, a data controller can generally assume that processing which meets two of the criteria must be subject to a consequence analysis regarding data protection.  
The processing of personal data in AULA meets two of these criteria, as sensitive and confidential personal data about vulnerable data subjects, including children, is processed. Furthermore, it concerns a system used for collaboration and communication between schools/daycare institutions and children and their parents. In the system, personal data is exchanged in messages, among other things, and there are many users associated with the system. The Data Protection Authority believes that this communication entails a risk that, due to human errors, personal data may be inadvertently disclosed to incorrect users. This is particularly relevant considering that the users consist of both municipal employees as well as children and their parents, and that – regardless of guidance – human errors will occur for which there are no immediate mitigating measures.  
Generally, based on the types of information processed in AULA, the volume of information, the number of transactions (exchange of information in connection with communication), and the diversity of users, there is not only a potential risk that the risk scenario will occur but also a real one.  
The consequences for the rights of the data subjects can, in the opinion of the Data Protection Authority, range across almost the entire spectrum of severity of consequences, from the less intrusive, such as receiving unintended messages, to more serious ones, such as significant loss of reputation, to the extreme consequence where a data subject's life is endangered by the disclosure of a concealed address, to mention just a few possible consequences.  
The Data Protection Authority notes that any risk that involves a high consequence for the rights and freedoms of data subjects – even with relatively low probabilities of the risk materializing – will likely entail a high risk for the rights of the data subjects, which triggers the obligation to conduct a consequence analysis regarding data protection, cf. Article 35, paragraph 1 of the Regulation.  
Furthermore, based on reports of personal data security breaches concerning AULA, the Data Protection Authority has generally found that several of these would likely entail a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Additionally, the Authority has noted in several instances that municipalities have deemed the conditions in Article 34 to be met and have informed the data subjects about breaches of personal data security in AULA. The municipalities in question have thus assessed that the breaches likely pose a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.  
Based on the above, the Data Protection Authority agrees with the assessment of the City of Copenhagen that the processing of personal data in AULA entails a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.  
The Data Protection Authority therefore finds that the processing activities carried out in AULA are subject to the requirement for a consequence analysis regarding data protection prior to the implementation of AULA.  

4.2.2. Timing for the Preparation of the Consequence Analysis  
It follows from Article 35, paragraph 1 of the Data Protection Regulation that a consequence analysis regarding data protection must be conducted prior to the processing.  
The Data Protection Authority assumes that the City of Copenhagen's consequence analysis was prepared on March 3, 2020, as this date is indicated on the front page of the consequence analysis. 4.2.3. Minimum Requirements for Consequence Analyses

Article 35 of the General Data Protection Regulation contains a number of requirements that a consequence analysis must meet at a minimum. These requirements follow from paragraph 7 and include the following:

1. a systematic description of the planned processing activities and the purposes of the processing, including, where applicable, the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller
2. an assessment of whether the processing activities are necessary and proportionate to the purposes
3. an assessment of the risks to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects as referred to in paragraph 1, and
4. the measures that are envisaged to address these risks, including safeguards, security measures, and mechanisms that can ensure the protection of personal data and demonstrate compliance with this regulation, taking into account the rights and legitimate interests of the data subjects and other affected persons.

The rules of the General Data Protection Regulation regarding consequence analysis are intended to ensure that no processing, where there is an inherent high risk to the rights and freedoms of the data subjects, is initiated without the data controller having worked on and mitigated such risks.

A consequence analysis regarding data protection is thus a tool that enables the data controller to systematically address the risks that a processing activity may entail.

The General Data Protection Regulation provides data controllers with the flexibility to determine the exact structure and form of the consequence analysis regarding data protection. However, regardless of the form, a consequence analysis regarding data protection must contain the above minimum requirements and thus be a proper assessment of risks that enables the data controllers to take measures to mitigate them.

The Data Protection Agency has reviewed the City of Copenhagen's consequence analysis in relation to the minimum requirements in Article 35, paragraph 7 of the General Data Protection Regulation. In this context, the Data Protection Agency has particularly looked at the content of the latest submitted version of the consequence analysis from April 2021 and compared it with the previous version from March 2020.

The Data Protection Agency has noted that the City of Copenhagen had planned an update of the consequence analysis in the autumn of 2023 based on the municipality's new concept for risk assessments including consequence analyses.

It is the Agency's overall assessment that the City of Copenhagen has not conducted a sufficient consequence analysis, according to Article 35, paragraph 1 of the General Data Protection Regulation, as the submitted consequence analysis does not meet the minimum requirements for this, according to Article 35, paragraph 7, letters a-d. The consequence analysis does not, among other things, contain assessments of necessity and proportionality.

Below follows the Data Protection Agency's assessment of the consequence analysis in relation to the individual minimum requirements that follow from Article 35, paragraph 7, letters a-d.

Ad a) Systematic description of processing activities and purposes

It is the opinion of the Data Protection Agency that the requirement for a systematic description of the planned...
**Processing Activities and Purposes of Processing**  
pursuant to Article 35, paragraph 7, letter a of the Data Protection Regulation, implies that the data controllers must carry out a systematic description of the various forms of processing, e.g., collection, registration, disclosure, etc., to which personal data will be subject, as well as a clear description and definition of the personal data being processed. Furthermore, the requirement entails that the impact assessment must include a description of the purpose of the processing. 

Additionally, it is the opinion of the Data Protection Authority that a systematic description should include information about the legal basis for the processing (authority), which categories of data subjects the information pertains to, the number of data subjects, information about where the data comes from, who—within and outside the organization—can access the data, how long the data is stored, whether data is transferred to third countries (and on what basis), and whether new technologies are used for the processing of personal data. 

Thus, the systematic description should contribute to the data controller's assessment of the specific likelihood and severity of high risks, taking into account the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the sources of risk. In this connection, the Data Protection Authority refers to recital 90[7]. 

The Data Protection Authority can ascertain that the impact assessment from the Municipality of Copenhagen includes, among other things, information about various forms of processing activities that personal data undergoes in AULA, the purpose of processing personal data, the number of data subjects, information that the data originates from KMD student (school), UNI-login, and KMD institution (daycare), and how long the data is stored. 

Furthermore, it appears from the impact assessment that information about school students, employees, and parents/contact persons is processed. The Data Protection Authority has understood from the impact assessment that the Municipality of Copenhagen assesses that there are no special citizen considerations in the processing of personal data in AULA. It is the opinion of the Data Protection Authority that there are special citizen considerations present in the municipalities' processing of personal data in AULA, as information about children, who are vulnerable physical persons, is processed. The Data Protection Authority notes in this connection that children and young people have the right to special protection of their personal data according to the rules in the Data Protection Regulation. The Data Protection Authority refers in this connection to recital 38[8] of the regulation. 

The impact assessment also contains information about which types of personal data are processed. However, it is not stated which categories of personal data are involved, including whether they pertain to special categories of personal data (sensitive personal data), ordinary personal data, and/or confidential personal data. However, the Data Protection Authority can ascertain from the listing of the types of data that there are both ordinary, confidential, and sensitive personal data involved. 

Regarding the legal basis for the processing in AULA, the following is stated in the impact assessment: "The Folkeskole Act 45(2)(2)." However, it does not appear what the legal basis in the Data Protection Regulation is for the processing of personal data. 

Furthermore, it does not appear from the impact assessment who—within and outside the organization—can access the data, whether there is a transfer of data to third countries (and on what basis), or whether new technologies are used for the processing of personal data. 

In light of this, it is the assessment of the Data Protection Authority that the Municipality of Copenhagen has described their processing activities in AULA and the purpose of the processing, but that the description is not sufficiently detailed, as it does not contain the aforementioned information. Thus, the minimum requirement in Article 35, paragraph 7, letter a is not met.

4.2.5. Renewed Impact Assessment
It follows from the Data Protection Regulation's Article 35, paragraph 11, that the data controller must conduct a renewed review if necessary to assess whether processing has been carried out in accordance with the impact assessment regarding data protection, at least when there is a change in the risk posed by the processing activities. 

It is the Data Protection Authority's opinion that a renewed review of the processing activities must be conducted to assess whether the processing has been carried out in accordance with the original impact assessment when the risk landscape changes, or the data controller assesses for another reason that it is necessary. This may, for example, be the case when such a change occurs that the risk posed by the processing activities becomes higher. 

Furthermore, it must also be assessed whether there is a need for a renewed review when changes occur that mean that the purpose of the processing changes, and/or that other personal data than those currently processed will be processed in the future. This may be relevant, for example, if sensitive personal data will be processed in a system where only ordinary personal data has previously been processed.

4.4. General remarks from the Data Protection Agency  
In system landscapes where multiple data controllers use the same systems and perform approximately...
the same processing of personal data, there can be a significant synergy in making joint assessments regarding risk factors, potential impact assessments, and mitigating measures. The Data Protection Agency notes that it may be relevant to conduct a joint impact assessment if multiple data controllers are planning to implement a common IT system or processing platform, for example, within the municipal sector. For multiple data controllers to collaborate on preparing a joint impact assessment, it is a prerequisite that the same type of system is involved, the same processing activity of the same personal data, and that the processing activities entail similar high risks.

It is not crucial that there is full identity between the systems and processing activities, but that the system, data, and particularly the processing activities do not deviate significantly from one another. Otherwise, a supplement to the joint impact assessment must necessarily be prepared. 

Thus, it is the opinion of the Data Protection Agency that it would be sufficient for several municipalities to prepare one impact assessment regarding data protection for the processing of personal data in the same system, provided by the same supplier, if the same processing activities are performed in the system, the same types of personal data are processed, and the processing activities entail the same high risks.

It is the data controllers who specifically assess whether a joint impact assessment can be conducted for the processing activities in the systems. The data controllers in a joint impact assessment each bear the responsibility for conducting the impact assessment. 

The Data Protection Agency believes that in relation to the municipalities' processing of personal data in AULA, the municipalities use the same type of system for the same processing activities of the same types of personal data, and that the municipalities' processing activities entail similar high risks. Furthermore, the system is provided by the same supplier. Thus, the municipalities have the opportunity to prepare a joint impact assessment regarding data protection for their processing of personal data in AULA.

In associations and bodies that represent categories of data controllers, it may also be beneficial to develop a code of conduct in accordance with Article 40 of the General Data Protection Regulation to specify the application of the General Data Protection Regulation. 

A code of conduct can provide correct and practical guidance on how to organize oneself in connection with one or more processing activities to comply with data protection rules. This could, for example, involve establishing procedures that must be followed for a specific processing activity. A code of conduct would therefore be a useful tool to assist in complying with data protection rules for the authorities that have subscribed to the code. This facilitates compliance with the rules but does not relieve the individual data controller of the obligations and tasks that rest upon them as data controllers. This is particularly significant in situations where the data controller chooses to deviate from the assumptions in the common concepts and processing, for example, by using a system for other purposes.

In this context, the Data Protection Agency encourages consideration across municipalities and possibly in collaboration with KL and KOMBIT to develop a code of conduct in accordance with Article 40 of the General Data Protection Regulation regarding the municipalities' processing of personal data in AULA. This may also be relevant to consider in relation to other processing activities in the municipalities, where the municipalities use the same system for processing the same types of personal data for the same purposes.
