Consent to facial recognition in fitness center was valid  
Date: 03-07-2024  
Decision  
Private companies  
Criticism  
Complaint  
Access control  
Processed by the Data Council  
Fundamental principles  
The Data Protection Agency has made a decision in a case regarding a fitness center's use of facial recognition, including whether the fitness center's other access options could be considered sufficient alternatives.  
Journal number: 2023-31-0028.  
Summary  
The Data Protection Agency received a complaint from a citizen regarding the fitness center Sporting Health Club (SHC) implementing facial recognition in connection with SHC's fitness centers. The citizen pointed out, among other things, that there were not sufficient and equivalent alternatives to facial recognition.  
It emerged from the case that users of the fitness center who do not wish to consent to facial recognition can be let in by the reception during staffed opening hours. Outside of staffed opening hours, users can contact the 24/7 support, which can either remotely unlock the door or generate a code for the door.  
In a previous case regarding FysioDanmark Hillerød, the Data Protection Agency assessed that the use of facial recognition as an access option to fitness centers does not in itself violate the fundamental principles of the General Data Protection Regulation's Article 5, including the requirements for proportionality.  
The present case primarily raised questions about the requirements for consent, including whether the possible alternatives in the specific case could be considered a real alternative.  
The Data Protection Agency found – after the case was processed at a meeting of the Data Council – that SHC can obtain valid consent, which can constitute an exception to the prohibition on processing special categories of data, provided that SHC ensures that the consent is informed and correctly obtained.  
The Data Protection Agency expresses criticism  
However, the Data Protection Agency found grounds to express criticism of the consent that the fitness center specifically attempted to obtain from the complainant, as the complainant had been informed that there were no alternatives to facial recognition.  
Decision  
The Data Protection Agency hereby returns to the case, where the complainant on February 19, 2023, complained to the agency about Sporting Health Club Scandinavia ApS's (SHC) use of facial recognition, including SHC's collection of consent for the processing of personal data.  
The Data Protection Agency notes for the sake of good order that the agency, with this decision, has only addressed the questions mentioned in the decision.  
1. Decision  
The Data Protection Agency finds – after the case has been processed at a meeting of the Data Council – that SHC, if SHC ensures that the consent obtained by SHC is informed, obtains valid consent, which under the General Data Protection Regulation's Article 9, paragraph 2, letter a, can constitute an exception to the prohibition on processing special categories of data.  
However, the Data Protection Agency finds that there are grounds to express criticism that the consent that SHC specifically attempted to obtain from the complainant was not voluntary and thus not valid.

**3. Justification of the Data Protection Agency**  
**3.1. Relevant Legal Provisions**  
The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies, according to Article 2, paragraph 1, to the processing of personal data that is wholly or partly carried out by means of automated data processing, and to other non-automated processing of personal data that is or will be contained in a register.  
According to Article 6, paragraph 1 of the GDPR, processing is only lawful if and to the extent that at least one of the conditions in point (a) of the provision applies. For example, processing is lawful, according to point (a) of the provision, if the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data for one or more specific purposes.  
According to Article 9, paragraph 1 of the GDPR, there is a prohibition on the processing of special categories of data, including the processing of biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person.  
Biometric data is understood, according to Article 4, number 14 of the GDPR, as personal data that, as a result of specific technical processing concerning the physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics of a natural person, enables or confirms the unique identification of that person, e.g., facial images or fingerprint data.  
Consent as referred to in Article 6, paragraph 1, point (a), and Article 9, paragraph 2, point (a) of the GDPR is understood, according to Article 4, number 11, as any voluntary, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes, by which the data subject, by declaration or clear confirmation, agrees to the processing of personal data concerning them.  
Article 7 of the GDPR contains a number of conditions for consent. It states:  
**Paragraph 1.** If processing is based on consent, the data controller must be able to demonstrate that the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data.  
**Paragraph 2.** If the data subject's consent is given in a written declaration that also concerns other matters, the request for consent must be presented in a manner that is clearly distinguishable from the other matters, in an easily understandable and accessible form, and in clear and plain language. Any part of such a declaration that constitutes a breach of this regulation is not binding.  
**Paragraph 3.** The data subject has the right to withdraw their consent at any time. The withdrawal of consent does not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal. Before consent is given, the data subject must be informed that consent can be withdrawn. It must be as easy to withdraw consent as it is to give it.  
**Paragraph 4.** In assessing whether consent is freely given, the greatest possible consideration shall be given to, among other things, whether the fulfillment of a contract, including a service, is made conditional on consent to the processing of personal data that is not necessary for the fulfillment of that contract.  
Any processing of personal data must – in addition to having a basis in Articles 6 & 9 of the GDPR – be carried out in accordance with the principles for processing personal data in Article 5. This means that personal data must:  
- be processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner in relation to the data subject ("lawfulness, fairness, and transparency")  
- be collected for specified, legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; further processing for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, shall not be considered incompatible with the original purposes ("purpose limitation")  
- be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ("data minimization")  
- be accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; every reasonable step must be taken to ensure that personal data that is inaccurate in relation to the purposes for which it is processed is erased or rectified without delay ("accuracy")  
- be kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed; personal data may be stored for longer periods if the personal data are only processed for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research purposes, or for statistical purposes in accordance with Article 89, paragraph 1, provided that appropriate technical and organizational measures are implemented as required by this regulation to safeguard the rights and freedoms of the data subjects ("storage limitation")  
- be processed in a manner that ensures adequate security of the personal data in question,
under protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage, using appropriate technical or organizational measures ("integrity and confidentiality").

3.2. The Data Protection Authority's Practice  
In the spring of 2022, the Data Protection Authority made a decision regarding a fitness center's use of facial recognition (Data Protection Authority case no. 2021-431-0145). In the decision, the Data Protection Authority stated, among other things:  
"When personal data in the form of images is processed in connection with facial scanning, there is processing of biometric data, cf. Article 4, no. 14 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). FysioDanmark has stated that the system operates in such a way that a camera is set up at the entrance to the fitness center, which can scan the customer's face, after which the result is compared to images already uploaded in the system.  
Thus, there is a comparison of biometric information about the relevant customer (collected at the time of identification) with a number of biometric templates stored in a database. There are thus one or more matching processes, and therefore, it is a matter of processing biometric data with the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person.  
As a general rule, it is prohibited to process such information, cf. Article 9, paragraph 1 of the GDPR, unless an exception to this prohibition can be identified in paragraph 2 of the provision. The Data Protection Authority agrees with FysioDanmark that the customer's explicit consent, cf. Article 9, paragraph 2, letter a of the GDPR, is the most appropriate exception to the prohibition, as none of the other exceptions in Article 9, paragraph 2 of the GDPR appear to be applicable.  
The Data Protection Authority assumes that FysioDanmark, by using the customer's explicit consent, will observe the obligations under Article 7 of the GDPR, and as a result, the Authority finds no basis to override FysioDanmark's assessment that the consent given by the data subject in this context complies with the rules for consent in the GDPR, including the requirement that consent must be voluntary as stated in Article 4, no. 11 of the GDPR.  
In this regard, the Data Protection Authority has emphasized the design and content of the submitted consent declaration, and that it is optional for the customer whether they wish to use facial recognition as access control, as the customer – if they do not wish to use facial recognition – can instead use access cards and codes."  
The Data Protection Authority's decision can be found in its entirety on the Authority's website by searching for "FysioDanmark."

3.3. Guidance from the Data Protection Authority and the European Data Protection Board (EDPB)  
3.3.1. Data Protection Authority's Guidance on Consent  
According to the Data Protection Authority's guidance on consent from May 2021, which is based on the EDPB's guidelines 5/2020, it is stated regarding the requirement of voluntariness that:  
"A consent must be voluntary. The purpose of consent is to give data subjects a choice and, not least, control over personal data about themselves. Consent is therefore not considered to be given voluntarily if the data subject does not have a real or free choice. For example, consent must not be given under duress. This applies regardless of whether it is the data controller or others who exert pressure on the data subject.  
Any form of inappropriate pressure or influence on the data subject's free will renders the consent invalid."  
Furthermore, it is stated regarding the requirement of voluntariness that:  
"A data controller can to some extent motivate data subjects to give consent by offering a benefit associated with giving consent. Enrollment in a business's loyalty program may, for example, involve discounts that motivate the customer to consent to receive promotional material from the business. The discount or benefits that consent to a loyalty program entails do not exclude that the consent can be considered voluntary.  
However, it is important to be aware of whether a lack of consent results in negative consequences for the data subject who does not wish to give consent – for example, in the form of additional costs..."  
Regarding the requirement of informed consent, it is stated that:  
"A consent must be informed. This means that the data subject must be aware of what they are consenting to. The data controller must provide the data subject with a number of information that ensures that the data subject can make their decision on an informed basis. There are no formal requirements for how this information must be provided. It is thus possible to meet the requirement both in writing, orally, or digitally."  
The Data Protection Authority's guidance on consent can be found in its entirety on the Data Protection Authority's website.

3.3.2. EDPB Guidelines on the Use of Video Equipment for Processing Personal Data  
According to the EDPB's guidelines 3/2019 on the use of video equipment for processing personal data, it is stated regarding consent for the processing of biometric data:  
"Finally, it applies that when consent is required under Article 9 of the GDPR, the data controller must not make access to its services conditional on the acceptance of biometric processing. In other words, and particularly when biometric processing is used for authentication purposes, the data controller must offer an alternative solution that does not involve biometric processing – without restrictions or additional costs for the data subject. This alternative solution is also necessary for individuals who do not meet the requirements for the biometric equipment (registration or reading of biometric data impossible, disability complicates its use, etc.),
and if the biometric equipment is not available (e.g., due to a malfunction of the device). In such cases, an "alternative solution" must be used to ensure the continuity of the intended service, which is, however, limited to extraordinary use. In special cases, there may be a situation where the processing of biometric data is the central activity in a service contract, for example, if a museum organizes an exhibition demonstrating the use of a facial recognition system. In that case, the registered individuals will not be able to refuse the processing of biometric data if they wish to visit the exhibition. The consent required under Article 9 remains valid in such cases if the requirements of Article 7 are met.

3.4. The Data Protection Authority's assessment of the specific case  
The case concerns the fitness center SHC implementing facial recognition as access control to the company's fitness centers, including SHC's obtaining of consent for the processing of personal data in this connection.  
The Data Protection Authority has previously assessed in the case concerning FysioDanmark (indirectly) that the use of facial recognition as access control to fitness centers cannot in itself be considered contrary to the fundamental principles of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) Article 5, including the requirements for proportionality.  
Consequently, the Data Protection Authority finds that SHC – provided that the conditions for lawful processing are otherwise met – may similarly use facial recognition as access control to its fitness centers. The Data Protection Authority has also emphasized that the processing occurs based on consent, as discussed below, and that the registered individual therefore has a choice regarding – and control over – whether facial recognition occurs and the associated processing of information.  
The case raises a number of general questions regarding the requirements for consent, including whether the possible alternatives in the specific case can be considered a genuine alternative. Furthermore, the case raises questions about consent in relation to complaints.

3.4.1. Is the consent generally valid?  
When personal data in the form of images is processed in connection with facial scanning, there is processing of biometric data, cf. GDPR Article 4, No. 14.  
It appears from the case that the system operates in such a way that a camera is set up to scan the customer's face, after which the result is compared with images already uploaded in the system.  
Thus, there is a comparison of biometric information about the specific customer (collected at the time of identification) with a number of biometric templates stored in a database. Therefore, one or more matching processes occur, and there is thus processing of biometric data with the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person.  
As a general rule, it is prohibited to process such information, cf. GDPR Article 9, paragraph 1, unless an exception to this prohibition can be identified in paragraph 2 of the provision.  
The Data Protection Authority has noted that SHC has arranged the system so that it must be activated for a scan to take place, e.g., by pressing a button, and that therefore there is no (illegal) processing of information about individuals who have not consented to facial recognition.  
The Data Protection Authority agrees with SHC that explicit consent, cf. GDPR Article 9, paragraph 2, letter a, is the most appropriate exception to the prohibition, as none of the other exceptions in GDPR Article 9, paragraph 2, appear to be applicable.  
For explicit consent under GDPR Article 9, paragraph 2, letter a, and Article 6, paragraph 1, letter a, to be valid, it must, according to GDPR Article 4, No. 11, be, among other things, voluntary.  
Consent cannot be considered voluntary if the procedure for obtaining consent does not allow the registered individual to give separate consent for different processing purposes regarding personal data, thereby forcing the registered individual to consent to all purposes. Consent must therefore be granular (divided).  
It has been stated in the case that SHC – in addition to processing biometric data in connection with access control – collects information via the system for statistical purposes, including check-in times for customers. In this connection, SHC has stated that this processing occurs based on the so-called balancing of interests rule in GDPR Article 6, paragraph 1, letter f.  
The Data Protection Authority agrees with SHC that the information about when a customer checks in is, in itself, information that is solely covered by GDPR Article 6.  
However, the Data Protection Authority is of the opinion that this information has the character of "derived" information, as the information (under certain circumstances) is provided using facial recognition.  
Therefore, for this processing as well, there is processing of biometric data with the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person covered by the prohibition in GDPR Article 9, paragraph 1.  
For consent to be valid, SHC must thus have granularized the consent so that consent can be given both for the processing of biometric data as part of access to the fitness center and for the use of facial scanning to conduct the mentioned statistics.  
The Data Protection Authority has noted that SHC provides the option for individuals to consent to both "use of facial scanning as an access method" and "use of facial scanning to conduct statistics."
the use of the fitness center."  
The Data Protection Agency finds that the consent obtained by SHC is sufficiently granular. However, SHC should consider clarifying in the consent declaration what "statistics on the use of the fitness center" encompasses.  
In continuation of this, the Data Protection Agency finds reason to note that SHC – regardless of whether SHC bases its processing on Article 6, paragraph 1, letter f – cannot use "derived" information obtained through facial recognition unless SHC has explicit consent for this in accordance with Article 9, paragraph 2, letter a.  
When it comes to the use of consent in connection with facial recognition, it is furthermore the Data Protection Agency's opinion – in accordance with what is stated in the EDPB guidelines 3/2019 – that the requirement for voluntariness in a case like this implies that data controllers – in this case SHC – must offer an alternative solution that does not involve the processing of biometric data.  
This alternative does not have to be identical but must not involve non-negligible limitations or costs for the data subject.  
It is the Data Protection Agency's assessment that the ability to check in at the reception through personal service during the reception's opening hours cannot in itself be considered a corresponding alternative, as this limits access to the center compared to if one consents to facial recognition.  
As SHC has explained, SHC provides the option, for those who do not wish to consent to facial recognition, to – in addition to the possibility of checking in at the reception through personal service – call support, which can open the door, and that one can generate a code by calling support.  
Since the alternative options (generating a code or calling support) provide the same access to the center as consenting to facial recognition, and since the alternative options are not associated with significant limitations or financial costs, the Data Protection Agency finds that SHC offers sufficient alternatives.  
The Data Protection Agency assumes in this context that these are real alternatives, including that one should not have to wait an unreasonable amount of time to get through to support to generate a code or be let in.  
As the case regarding the question this case concerns (access for customers) does not give the Data Protection Agency reason to question the voluntariness of the consent, the Data Protection Agency finds that SHC obtains voluntary consent.  
The Data Protection Agency notes in this context that the question of any consent from employees is not part of this case, and therefore the Agency has not taken a position on this. The Data Protection Agency can refer to the Agency's decision in the case concerning FysioDanmark for considerations regarding this.  
In addition to being voluntary, consent must also be informed. This means that the data subject must be aware of what they are consenting to. This entails that the data controller must provide the data subject with a range of information to ensure that the data subject can make their decision on an informed basis.  
In a situation such as the present, it is the Data Protection Agency's opinion that the requirement for consent to be informed implies that the data subject must be informed about the alternatives to consenting to facial recognition.  
The Data Protection Agency has found that SHC has made certain efforts to do this, among other things on the information screens at the entrance to the fitness centers and in the company's privacy policy.  
As the case stands, however, it is the Data Protection Agency's assessment that the information about the alternatives that SHC provides appears to be somewhat fragmented. For example, the data subject themselves must find further information about the possible alternatives in the privacy policy, just as the data subject must deduce what "code" and the reference to support cover on the information screens at the entrance to the fitness centers.  
In any case, it is the Data Protection Agency's opinion that it would be most consistent with the requirements for informed consent if it were stated in connection with obtaining consent – already in the consent declaration itself – that there are alternatives, and where one can read more about these. It is noted in this context that this appears to be a relatively simple approach.  
Based on the above, the Data Protection Agency concludes that SHC, if SHC ensures that the consent obtained is informed, obtains valid consent, which under Article 9, paragraph 2, letter a of the General Data Protection Regulation can constitute an exception to the prohibition on processing special categories of data.  
Consequently, the consent can also serve as a basis for processing under Article 6, paragraph 1, letter a of the General Data Protection Regulation.  
What has otherwise been stated regarding the case, including whether the refusal to give consent in itself is a special category of personal data, cannot lead to a different assessment of the validity of the consent or the legality of the processing in general.  
3.4.2. Consent in relation to complaints?  
It is – notwithstanding the above regarding the general voluntariness of consent – the Data Protection Agency's opinion that the circumstance that a complaint from an employee was informed that “[i]f [a] member does not wish to give consent to facial recognition, it is only possible to train in our centers during staffed opening hours,” must be assumed to have given the complainant a justified impression that there would be no alternatives to facial recognition.  
It is, in the Data Protection Agency's assessment, equivalent to...
med, that there would be no alternatives. This means that the consent that was attempted to be obtained from the complainant at that time would not be valid. Under these circumstances, the Data Protection Authority finds grounds to express criticism that the consent which SHC specifically attempted to obtain from the complainant was not voluntary and thus not valid. However, the complainant must be considered to be sufficiently informed about the possible alternatives at this time, which means that valid consent can be obtained.
