Notice from the National Commission for Data Protection regarding
Bill No. 8335 on security, safety, order, and video surveillance in public transport and amending the law of February 5, 2021, on public transport.
Deliberation No. 25/AV10/2024 of May 2, 2024.

1. 
In accordance with Article 57.1.e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation) (hereinafter referred to as the "GDPR"), to which Article 7 of the law of August 1, 2018, on the organization of the National Commission for Data Protection and the general regime on data protection refers, the National Commission for Data Protection (hereinafter referred to as the "National Commission" or the "CNPD") "advises, in accordance with the law of the Member State, the national parliament, the government, and other institutions and bodies regarding legislative and administrative measures relating to the protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons with regard to processing."

Furthermore, Article 36.4 of the GDPR states that "[m]ember states consult the supervisory authority in the context of the preparation of a legislative proposal to be adopted by a national parliament, or a regulatory measure based on such a legislative measure, which relates to processing."

2. 
Although not directly seized by the Ministry of Mobility and Public Works, the National Commission nonetheless wishes to express its opinion on Bill No. 8335 concerning security, safety, order, and video surveillance in public transport and amending the law of February 5, 2021, on public transport (hereinafter referred to as the "bill").

3. 
According to the explanatory memorandum, there has been an "increase in inappropriate behaviors and assaults in public transport, towards the driving staff, particularly bus drivers and train accompanying staff, as well as towards the equipment," while "operators lament, for some time now, that the legislative and regulatory texts and the measures currently in place are not sufficient."

The present bill therefore aims to establish "a better framework for determining rules for the use of public transport, and to enable the detection and sanctioning of offenses, particularly against behaviors that hinder the proper functioning of public transport or even compromise its safe execution."

4.  
The CNPD wishes to emphasize from the outset that the analysis of the rules of use and behavior by users in public transport, that is to say in any bus, tram, or train executing a public transport service, and in public transport infrastructures including railway and bus stations, park-and-ride facilities (P+R), and stops served by public transport services, as well as the very principle of expanding the powers of public transport service agents does not fall within its scope of competence. However, the processing of personal data that said agents may, where applicable, implement in this context, as well as the provisions regarding the installation of surveillance cameras in public transport means and infrastructures raise questions related to the protection of personal data.

1.  
Regarding the processing of personal data carried out by public transport service agents

5.  
According to Article 24 of the draft law titled "Processing of Personal Data," the agents referred to in Article 12, namely service agents and licensed service agents, may, in the performance of their duties, collect the name, surname, date of birth, and address of the person who has committed an offense liable to be punished by one of the measures or sanctions in Chapters 5 and 6 of the draft law. It should be noted in this context that the draft law indeed distinguishes between the "service agent," that is to say, any "person employed for the provision of public transport services," on the one hand, and such an agent who is "licensed" by the minister responsible for Transport (hereinafter referred to as the "minister") in accordance with Article 8 of the draft law, on the other hand. Furthermore, the public transport services refer to those provided by trains, buses, and trams as defined in Articles 2 and 4 of the law of February 5, 2021, on public transport.

a. Preliminary Remarks

6.  
First of all, the CNPD wishes to emphasize that it understands that any potential processing of personal data in the context of the detection of offenses referred to in the aforementioned Article 9 of the draft law can only be carried out by service agents who are "licensed." It therefore recommends that the authors of the draft law adapt Article 24, second paragraph, accordingly.

**e. Regarding Identity Control**

16. According to Article 7.4 of the bill, authorized service agents, members of the Grand Ducal Police within the police framework, and ADA agents are permitted, in the event of findings of offenses leading to an administrative sanction, to check the identity of users and to request an identity document for this purpose. The commentary on the articles only specifies that "identity is necessary to trigger the procedures provided for in the related articles."

17. The CNPD is surprised that the legislator has chosen to extend the prerogatives of judicial police officers and agents in the context of identity checks provided for in Article 45 of the Code of Criminal Procedure to other actors, especially since these latter may, where applicable, be employees hired by private companies. 

18. Furthermore, even though Article 7.4 of the bill provides that "the refusal to present an identity document is punishable by a fine of 25 to 250 euros," the CNPD questions what coercive means authorized service agents can invoke in the event of a user's refusal or inability to produce an identity document. Indeed, the detention of a person in such a situation or their transport to the police station for identity verification purposes constitutes a power granted by the Code of Criminal Procedure solely to the Grand Ducal Police.

Based on the aforementioned provisions, the CNPD assumes that no copy of the said report will be kept by the authorized service agent. 22. In this case, it believes that clarifications should be inserted into the text of the draft law.

II.  
Regarding Video Surveillance  
24.  
Article 21.1 of the draft law provides for the establishment of video surveillance "by installing cameras on board and outside public transport vehicles, as well as in public transport infrastructures [...]." As mentioned in point 4 of this opinion, "public transport vehicles" refer to buses, trams, and trains providing public transport services, while "public transport infrastructures" include railway and bus stations, park-and-ride facilities (P+R), and stops served by public transport services. According to the commentary on the articles, this article aims to create "a legal basis for the installation of surveillance cameras in and on public transport vehicles and in public transport infrastructures and defines their purposes."  
25.  
The capture and recording by such surveillance cameras of images of identifiable persons or vehicles whose license plates are clearly visible in the images constitute processing of personal data within the meaning of the GDPR. In general, the CNPD welcomes the fact that the draft law intends to provide a legal basis for the processing of data carried out in the context of the installation of the aforementioned cameras. It notes that these cameras pursue various purposes listed in Article 21.1 of the draft law, such as "securing access to public transport," "ensuring the safety of persons and protecting property," or "allowing competent authorities and operators to take appropriate and necessary measures in the event of accidents and incidents." Their installation in public transport vehicles and infrastructures could therefore correspond to a public interest mission in accordance with Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR. Nevertheless, various conditions must be met in this context, which the CNPD wishes to remind in its preliminary remarks before analyzing in more detail the substance of the provisions in this matter contained in the draft law.  
a. Preliminary Remarks  
26.  
The CNPD first considers that Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR, read in conjunction with Article 6.3 of the GDPR, provides in this context a particular constraint related to the lawfulness of data processing necessary for the performance of a public interest mission or related to the exercise of public authority vested in the data controller. In this case, the basis, that is to say, the public interest mission in question, and notably the purposes of the data processing must arise either from European Union law or from the law of the Member State to which the data controller is subject, and this basis must meet "a public interest objective and is proportionate to the legitimate objective pursued."

In a recent ruling, the Court of Justice stated, for example, that the "determination of the purposes and means of processing and, where appropriate, the designation of the responsible party by national law can be not only explicit but also implicit. In the latter case, it is nevertheless required that this determination arises with sufficient certainty from the role, mission, and responsibilities assigned to the person or entity concerned."

28.  
In domestic law, it should be noted that Article 31 of the revised Constitution, applicable since July 1, 2023, provides that "[e]very person has the right to informational self-determination and to the protection of personal data concerning them. These data may only be processed for purposes and under conditions determined by law."

29.  
Therefore, taking into account that the protection of personal data is a matter reserved for law by the Constitution, the essence of the normative framework must result from the law, including the purposes, conditions, and modalities under which less essential elements may be regulated by regulations and orders issued by the Grand Duke. Indeed, according to the jurisprudence of the Constitutional Court, Article 45.2 of the Constitution requires that in these matters, "the setting of the objectives of the implementing measures must be clearly stated, as well as the conditions to which they are, if applicable, subject. The guidance and framework of the executive power must, in any case, be consistent, precise, and readable, the essence of the relevant provisions being required to be included in the law."

30.  
Next, it must be taken into account that fundamental rights are not absolute prerogatives since the possibility of interference or limitation is provided for both by Article 8.2 of the European Convention on Human Rights and by Article 52.1 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, as well as by Article 37 of the revised Luxembourg Constitution, which states that "[a]ny limitation on the exercise of public freedoms must be provided for by law and respect their essential content. In compliance with the principle of proportionality, limitations may only be imposed if they are necessary in a democratic society and effectively meet objectives of general interest or the need to protect the rights and freedoms of others."

31.  
The National Commission therefore considers that if the data processing carried out by entities in the context of fulfilling their public interest missions constitutes an interference in the private life of citizens, such processing must be specifically framed by a law in the formal sense. Indeed, an interference or limitation in the exercise of public freedoms can only be justified if it is provided for by a law accessible to the persons concerned and predictable in its repercussions, that is to say, formulated with a clarity that allows individuals to understand its implications.

d. Regarding the fields of vision and the number of cameras  
44.  
Without prejudice to its previous comments on the notion of data controller, or even on the purposes pursued by the processing operations carried out by the cameras that may be installed in and outside public transport means and infrastructures in accordance with Article 27.1 of the draft law, the CNPD wonders whether the data controller(s) have only the option to install such cameras or whether, on the contrary, the future law would impose an obligation on them to set them up.  
45.  
Similarly, it is not clear from the text of the draft law, nor from the commentary on the articles, whether cameras will potentially be installed at all stations, parking lots, and stops served by public transport services, or only at strategic points that meet certain conditions (for example, locations particularly exposed to risks of accidents, damage, assaults, thefts, or threats to the safety of persons or property). The CNPD wishes to emphasize in this context that the installation of cameras throughout the public transport network could lead to nearly the entirety of public space in Luxembourg being under video surveillance, which risks being in contradiction with the principles of proportionality and necessity, combined with the principle of data minimization. It therefore considers it important to establish in the body of the draft law objective criteria governing the implementation of surveillance cameras in order to avoid any disproportionate collection of personal data, while respecting the principles of predictability and quality of law as provided by European Union law.  
46.  
Furthermore, similar to what is provided in Article 43b/s, paragraph (5), subparagraph 2, of the amended law of July 18, 2018, on the Grand Ducal Police, the CNPD recommends that the authors of the draft law include in the text of the draft law a provision stating that “[t]he use of facial recognition techniques is excluded.”  
47.  
Finally, the CNPD wishes to emphasize that employees of the various public transport operators generally have the right not to be subjected to permanent and continuous surveillance, especially through cameras installed inside public transport means. To avoid such surveillance, the data controller must limit the field of vision of the cameras to only the area necessary to achieve the intended purposes.

e. Regarding the information of the data subjects  
48.  
Article 22.2 of the draft law provides that data subjects are "informed of the existence of a video surveillance system by the placement of pictograms and display panels at the entrances of public transport means, as well as at stops and platforms."  
49.  
The CNPD wishes to emphasize that "data subject" refers to any identified or identifiable natural person.23 In this case, this targets not only individuals using public transport, as well as pedestrians crossing public roads, but also vehicle owners whose license plates are clearly readable, even if the individuals themselves do not appear directly within the field of vision of the cameras. This obligation to inform the aforementioned data subjects falls to the data controller in accordance with Article 13 of the GDPR. The commentary on the articles specifies that data subjects are informed "by the placement of posters and an information notice in the relevant locations, as well as on the website of the Public Transport Administration or others." While it can only generally support the idea of informing in two levels to facilitate the understanding of data subjects regarding the data processing carried out during the use of a video surveillance system, it notes that the terminology used in the commentary on the articles ("on the website of the Public Transport Administration or others") reinforces the issue of defining the data controller as detailed in points 33 to 37 of this opinion.  
50.  
Regarding the information in two levels more specifically, such an approach consists of communicating - in the first instance - a series of information to the data subjects, for example through display panels as mentioned by the authors of the draft law, and then - in a second instance - to communicate via other means, such as a website, all the information required under Article 13 of the GDPR. f. Regarding the retention period of images  
56. Based on Article 23 of the draft law, recordings containing personal data are deleted after six months, unless they are used in the context of a judicial, administrative, or disciplinary procedure.  
57. The CNPD first asks whether a 24/7 real-time viewing of the images is planned to ensure the preventive effect in achieving the purposes outlined in Article 21, points 4° and 6° of the draft law, or if the recordings are only viewed a posteriori in the event of an incident.  
58. Regarding the retention period, Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR obliges the data controller not to retain personal data for longer than necessary for the purposes for which they are processed. The commentary on the articles specifies in this context that “in view of the procedures and the various actors involved (establishing an offense, filing a complaint, preparing a report, seizure order by the investigating judge...) or in the case of a late complaint from the victim, and in consultation with the Public Prosecutor's Office, [the retention period] is set at 6 months to allow for the prosecution of offenses by public authorities.”  
59. The CNPD generally considers that for data processing carried out by a video surveillance system necessary for the legitimate interests of the data controller, a retention period of 8 days seems appropriate, except in exceptional cases justifying a duration of 30 days.  
60. In the specific case and due to the arguments mentioned by the authors in the commentary on the articles, it understands, however, that a retention period of 8 days may not be sufficient to achieve the intended purposes. However, by analyzing other video surveillance systems targeting public roads and regulated by national legal texts, the CNPD notes that the retention periods are shorter. For example, Article 43ter, paragraph 7, of the amended law of July 18, 2018, on the Grand Ducal Police provides for a retention period of 28 days for images recorded by the Grand Ducal Police's body cameras, while Article 436/'s, paragraph 1 of the aforementioned law regulating the VISUPOL system defines a storage period of two months.
