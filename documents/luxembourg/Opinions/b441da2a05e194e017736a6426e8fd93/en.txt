Deliberation No. 10/AV5/2024 of February 23, 2024

In accordance with Article 57.1.e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation) (hereinafter referred to as the "GDPR"), to which Article 7 of the law of August 1, 2018, organizing the National Commission for Data Protection and the general regime on data protection refers, the National Commission for Data Protection (hereinafter referred to as the "National Commission" or the "CNPD") "advises, in accordance with the law of the Member State, the national parliament, the government, and other institutions and bodies regarding legislative and administrative measures relating to the protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons with regard to processing."

Furthermore, Article 36.4 of the GDPR states that "[m]ember states consult the supervisory authority in the context of the preparation of a legislative proposal to be adopted by a national parliament, or a regulatory measure based on such a legislative measure, which relates to processing."

By letter dated September 11, 2023, the Minister of Energy and Spatial Planning invited the National Commission to express its opinion on Bill No. 8250 concerning sub-metering and the allocation of costs for heating, cooling, and hot water (hereinafter referred to as the "Bill") which had already been submitted on June 14, 2023, to the Chamber of Deputies and for which priority processing had been requested from the CNPD.

3. The draft law aims to transpose into national law Directive (EU) 2018/2002 of the European Parliament and of the Council of December 11, 2018, amending Directive 2012/27/EU on energy efficiency, which establishes a common framework of measures for the promotion of energy efficiency within the Union. It follows from the explanatory memorandum of the draft law that Directive (EU) 2018/2002 imposes "on Member States the obligation to create a new legal framework that makes the metering of the consumption of certain types of energy at the individual units of a collective building mandatory." The objective pursued is to encourage end users, by regularly providing information on their actual energy consumption, to achieve energy savings through behavioral change.

4. This opinion will not comment on the aforementioned directives but will limit itself to examining the provisions of the draft law as well as the draft grand-ducal regulation that concern their concrete implementation in national law with an impact on the protection of personal data.

1. General Observations

5. It should be recalled that the information collected by thermal energy meters constitutes personal data if it can be linked to an identified or identifiable natural person. Already during the national deployment of smart meters, the National Commission had, while recognizing the advantages brought by these meters, warned against the risks that could arise for the protection of privacy2. For example, the analysis of the collected data allows for the deduction of a large number of information about the living habits of the individuals concerned, such as wake-up and bedtime, periods of absence, the number of people present in the dwelling, or even, in specific cases, the type of devices used.

II. 
On the installation of measuring devices

According to the understanding of the CNPD, the draft law provides for the deployment of three different types of measuring devices, namely:
1. The meters referred to in point 1° of Article 2.1, which are installed by the network operator at the connection point of each end customer;
2. The individual meters referred to in point 2° of Article 2.1, which are installed in collective buildings by the owner or, in the case of a co-ownership, by the property manager at the connection point of the private units. These individual meters are installed if the occupants of the private units do not have a direct contractual relationship with the energy supplier and are therefore not end customers;
3. The heating cost allocators referred to in Article 5, which are installed in collective buildings by the owner or, in the case of a co-ownership, by the property manager if the installation of individual meters is not technically feasible or economically justified.

To the knowledge of the CNPD, energy contracts and the associated meters are generally signed and assigned in the name of the tenant or the end occupant, including in the case of co-ownership. We do not know if any exceptional cases might still exist on the territory of the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg, particularly since the installation and deployment of smart meters.

9. If the draft law clearly indicates who is responsible for the installation of the various measuring devices, it remains silent regarding the different data processing operations that will be carried out under the text. Thus, the draft law does not provide any indication of the person who carries out the readings of the various measuring devices nor of the person considered responsible for the different resulting data processing.

10. Regarding the installation of meters by network managers, it follows from paragraph 3 of Article 3.1 of the draft law that these managers must provide end customers with information "regarding the collection and processing of personal data in accordance with data protection and privacy legislation." The CNPD assumes that this provision refers to the obligation of transparency incumbent upon the data controller under Articles 12 and following of the GDPR, so it can be inferred that network managers are to be considered as data processors. However, for reasons of legal certainty, it would be preferable for the draft law to expressly determine who is the data controller. Indeed, the notion of data controller plays an important role in the application of the GDPR as it determines who is responsible for the various rules regarding data protection, such as the security of processing, as well as how data subjects can exercise their rights. In the absence of clarity regarding the designation of the data controller, the question arises regarding the attribution of responsibility for implementing the technical and organizational measures as provided for by Article 24 of the GDPR.

11. In this context, it should be noted that the obligation to provide information regarding the processing of personal data, during the installation of meters, is only stated for network managers, and not for owners or property managers in the case of a co-ownership, during the installation of individual meters as mentioned in Article 3.2 of the draft law. This approach raises questions, given that owners and property managers could then be considered responsible for the processing concerning the data collected by individual meters and heating cost allocators if one adheres literally to the text of the draft law and the scenarios such as mentioned.

12. The text under review is also silent on the categories of data collected, the purposes pursued, the duration of retention, or the recipients to whom the data may be communicated. This lack of precision is all the more regrettable given that the data collected by the various measuring devices is likely to provide a very detailed view of the personal consumption habits of the individuals concerned. More specifically, in the absence of a clear determination of the purposes, there is an increased risk that the owner may use the collected data related to the energy consumption of the occupants—when the latter are not directly contractually linked to the energy supplier, assuming that such an exceptional legal situation may exist in Luxembourg—for purposes that are not compatible with the objective pursued by the directive that the draft law intends to transpose. To recall, this relates to the allocation of energy costs based on actual consumption and the regular provision of information on actual energy consumption so that occupants can achieve energy savings through behavioral change. Thus, the principle of purpose limitation enshrined in Article 5.1.b) of the GDPR generally opposes the owner using this data for purposes other than those related to mere information regarding the energy efficiency of the occupants.

III. On the provision of information regarding consumption and billing

13. According to Article 2.3 of the draft law, “[s]uppliers as well as owners or, in the case of co-ownership, property managers are subject to obligations to provide data and information related to billing and consumption in accordance with the provisions of Article 11.” As for the meters installed by network managers, the obligation to provide the data lies with the supplier and not with the network manager, which seems to imply a transmission of data between the network manager and the supplier.

Regarding the information note referred to in Article 11.3 of the draft law, the minimum information to be included is listed in Article 7.2 of the draft Grand-Ducal Regulation, including notably the comparison of current energy consumption with "the consumption of the private unit for the same period during the previous year (...)." It follows that this comparison may contain information relating to a third party, namely the previous occupant of the private unit. The CNPD expresses doubts about the compliance of this provision with Directive 2012/27/EU as amended by Directive (EU) 2018/2002. According to point 3.c) of Annex VII bis of this directive, Member States ensure that final users have "the comparison of the current energy consumption of the final user with their consumption for the same period during the previous year (...)." Recommendation (EU) 2019/1660, in its point 9.3.1., is clear: "Given the requirements regarding data protection and privacy (see also point 7.5), this requirement should be interpreted as applying only to information relating to the energy consumed by the current occupant, namely the same final user who must be able to access the information." Therefore, Article 7.2.3°a) of the draft Grand-Ducal Regulation risks being contrary to both Directive 2012/27/EU as amended by Directive (EU) 2018/2002 and the principle of data minimization established in Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR.

18. Similarly, Article 7.2.3°b) of the draft Grand-Ducal Regulation may not respect the spirit of the European reference text while raising issues related to the protection of personal data. Thus, in such cases, the property manager transmits this note to the co-owners who will then forward it to the tenants of the concerned private units, in other words, the occupants. Furthermore, the CNPD questions whether the provision that the property manager transmits the information note to the co-owners, who in turn will forward it to the occupants, might not contradict the terms of Article 2.3 of the bill. In the view of the National Commission, the bill presents some ambiguity regarding the person responsible, in the case of co-ownership, for communicating consumption data to tenants and, consequently, regarding the determination of the data controller and the obligations arising from this qualification. In order to limit the number of actors having access to consumption data and to avoid any potential inconsistency in the text, the CNPD wonders whether it would not be more appropriate to uniformly provide in the text that the property manager directly transmits the information note to the concerned final occupant, as is also the case for the assessment note referred to in Article 11.2 of the bill.

22. Upon reading Article 11.4, paragraph 1, of the draft law, it must first be noted that only the mention of a "secured" personal space on the Internet is provided without any further indication in this regard, particularly concerning the person or entity responsible for its establishment or regarding security measures. The CNPD then understands that by referring to paragraph 2, the authors of the draft law also intend to confer the possibility of opting for this means of electronic communication to the occupants of the private units receiving consumption information from the owner or the property manager. This raises the question of whether, in cases where the occupants are not directly contractually linked to the energy supplier—assuming this legal situation still exists at the national level—it is the responsibility of the property manager, or even the owner, to set up such an online platform, which would constitute a considerable burden.

In the comments on Article 11 of the draft law, it is indicated that the wording "through a secured personal space on the Internet" has been borrowed from the French Energy Code, and more specifically from Article L741-2 of the latter. However, this same article regulates the provision of invoices on a durable medium other than paper, notably through a secured online space. This is ensured by the supplier within the framework of a subscription contract to a heating and cooling network with its subscriber, who must also be informed of this. This contractual relationship as referred to by the French text is therefore distinct from that in which the occupant, who is not the final customer, receives information regarding their consumption from the property manager or the owner—assuming this exceptional situation still exists at the national level—hence the CNPD's questioning regarding the reference in Article 11.4, paragraph 1, to paragraph 2 of Article 11.

23. Furthermore, the National Commission questions the extent of the information referred to by this space and, a fortiori, the type of personal data concerned. Upon reading Article 11.4 of the draft law, it seems that the information note as mentioned by Article 11.3 is not included.

24. Even if no specific obligation regarding data protection is added by the directive to be transposed, the recommendation (EU) 2019/1660 still emphasizes the importance of complying with the provisions already in force, such as the GDPR. However, it seems that the draft law does not meet the requirements related to the application of the GDPR rules, particularly the provisions of Article 6.3. In this case, the processing of consumption data would be necessary to comply with a legal obligation created by the draft law under review, in accordance with Article 6.1.e) of the GDPR. Article 6.3 of the GDPR, which specifically concerns this legal basis, provides that "the purposes of the processing are defined in this legal basis" and that specific provisions may be included "to adapt the application of the rules of this regulation, among others: the general conditions governing the lawfulness of the processing by the data controller; the types of data subject to processing; the data subjects; the entities to which personal data may be communicated and the purposes for which they may be communicated; the limitation of purposes; the retention periods; [...]". The CNPD regrets that the draft law does not contain such provisions.

25. The National Commission finally notes that certain legislations from other Member States in the energy sector particularly emphasize the importance of data protection, such as the Walloon Belgian decree of October 15, 2020, concerning the organization of the thermal energy market and thermal energy networks, which provides a section entirely dedicated to the protection of privacy. This section specifically qualifies the various actors involved based on the processing and emphasizes the importance of respecting the chosen purposes and the limited retention period.
