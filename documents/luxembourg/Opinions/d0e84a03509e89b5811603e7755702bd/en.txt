4. It appears from the explanatory memorandum that the draft law under review aims to replace the law of June 24, 2008, concerning the control of travelers in accommodation establishments, a system established in 1929 and which has undergone several adaptations since. The proposed amendments, stemming from the recommendations of a working group composed of representatives from various entities such as the Directorate General of Tourism, the Ministry of Internal Security, the Grand Ducal Police, and the CTIE, aim to increase the efficiency of the current system both for operators, meaning any natural or legal person providing, for a fee, accommodation services, and for the recipients of this data, which are the Grand Ducal Police and the National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies of the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg (hereinafter referred to as "STATEC"). The CNPD includes a commentary on the articles related to Article 1 of the draft law, which explains that "It does not matter, with regard to this law, whether the person carries out this activity habitually or not and whether or not they are required to have an establishment authorization. Thus, the obligations imposed by this law also apply to persons who, under the new provisions regarding establishments (draft law No. 7989), would not be required to hold an establishment authorization due to the fact that their activity falls below the threshold at which such authorization is required," and that natural persons who rent, even occasionally, their private accommodation through an online commercial platform must comply with the provisions of the draft law under review.

5. The National Commission has taken note of the submission of a draft Grand Ducal regulation concerning the repeal of the Grand Ducal regulation of August 5, 2015, relating to the registers to be maintained by lessors operating a tourist accommodation service. The latter was enacted to implement the aforementioned law of June 24, 2008, in order to specify the modalities and deadlines for the communication of accommodation registers and data, as well as the model of these registers, the information to be provided therein, the method of their preservation, and the processing of data by the Grand Ducal Police. All these issues are now addressed by the draft law under review.

6. The National Commission will express in this opinion its observations regarding the issues related to the protection of personal data raised by the draft law.

I.  
Preliminary Remarks  
7. Current legislation requires anyone who hosts a person in a collective accommodation establishment or private tourist accommodation to complete or have completed a form containing certain information about the hosted individuals and to transmit it to certain authorities.  
8. The accommodation form system aims to satisfy a dual purpose. Under Article 45 of the Convention implementing the Schengen Agreement (hereinafter referred to as the "CISA"), Luxembourg is obliged to take measures to ensure that foreigners, nationals of the European Union and third countries, who are hosted in a tourist accommodation establishment complete and sign a declaration form and prove their identity. Furthermore, Luxembourg is required by Regulation (EU) No. 692/2011 of the European Parliament and of the Council of July 6, 2011, concerning European statistics on tourism and repealing Council Directive 95/57/EC to provide the statistical office of the European Commission (EUROSTAT) with statistics on the occupancy of accommodation establishments. Therefore, the establishment and transmission of accommodation forms to the respective authorities cover both security and statistical purposes.  
9. Regarding the security aspect, the National Commission notes that the draft law under review provides for a non-targeted transmission of accommodation forms for each traveler and for each stay by the operator to the Grand Ducal Police. This systematic and generalized transmission results in a massive centralization of personal data with the Grand Ducal Police, which raises significant questions regarding the right to data protection as enshrined both in Article 31 of the Constitution and in supranational texts. In this context, it should be emphasized that automatic transmission of travelers' personal data by the operator to police services has been abolished in all our neighboring countries. Indeed, neither Germany, nor France, nor Belgium currently has a systematic and generalized transmission of data of all travelers to police services. On the other hand, the aforementioned legal orders provide for the retention of accommodation forms by the operator itself for a specified period. If requested, the operator or their representative must make the recorded data available to police officers so that verification is possible. Furthermore, France has limited access to the data by requiring police officers to base their request on one of the three legal grounds provided for by the Code on the Entry and Stay of Foreigners and Asylum Law, namely for the purposes of

**II. The responsible parties for the various processing activities**

11. It is important to recall that the notion of the data controller plays a significant role in data protection. Firstly, the determination of the data controller can have implications for the applicable legal regime. Indeed, data processing carried out for criminal or security purposes by competent authorities, such as the Grand Ducal Police or the State Intelligence Service (hereinafter referred to as the "SRE"), is subject to the law of August 1, 2018, concerning the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data in criminal matters as well as in matters of national security, while other processing activities, such as those carried out by operators or STATEC, fall under the common law regime, namely the GDPR. Secondly, the determination of the data controller allows for the identification of which entity is responsible for the various rules regarding data protection as well as how the concerned individuals can exercise their rights.

12. Under Articles 3 and 6 of the draft law, it is the responsibility of the operator to establish the accommodation records and to transmit to the Grand Ducal Police as well as to STATEC the personal data listed in the respective applicable annexes. It follows that the operator is the data controller for the collection and subsequent transmission of travelers' data, through the accommodation records, to the Grand Ducal Police and to STATEC. Similarly, STATEC, the Grand Ducal Police, and the SRE are to be considered as responsible for the processing activities they carry out subsequently.

13. Consequently, the CNPD can align itself with the developments made by the authors in the commentary on Article 6, according to which the different actors are not joint controllers of personal data processing but independent and successive controllers.

III.  
The collection of data by operators  
14. The bill under review establishes a legal obligation for operators to process a certain number of personal data. Thus, the collection of personal data from travelers by operators finds its legal basis in Article 6.1(e) of the GDPR, under which processing is lawful if it is "necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject." In this case, Article 6.3 of the GDPR provides a specific constraint related to the lawfulness of processing, in that the basis and purposes of the processing must be defined either by Union law or by national law. Furthermore, this legal basis must pursue a public interest objective and be proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued.  
15. It follows from Article 6.3 as well as Recital 45 of the GDPR that the legal basis may contain specific provisions aimed, among other things, at determining the data controller, the data subjects, the categories of data being processed, and the retention period.  
1. The operator as the data controller  
16. As outlined in points 11 to 13 of this opinion, operators are to be considered as responsible for the processing related to the collection and transmission of personal data from travelers. Thus, compliance with the principles set out in Article 5 of the GDPR is imposed on operators as data controllers. This opinion will focus more specifically on the principles of transparency, on the one hand, and the principle of integrity and confidentiality, on the other.  
A. The principle of transparency  
17. The principle of transparency is enshrined in Article 5.1(a), while Articles 12 to 14 of the GDPR outline the practical requirements regarding the information of data subjects. Thus, the information referred to in Article 13 of the GDPR must be provided by the data controller, that is to say, the operator, at the time of data collection in a concise, transparent, comprehensible, and easily accessible manner. The requirement of comprehensibility implies that this information should be accessible to the majority of the targeted public, and therefore a translation into one or more languages should be provided if the individuals targeted by the data controller speak other languages.  
18. Furthermore, Article 13.1(e) of the GDPR requires data controllers to provide data subjects with information regarding the recipients or categories of recipients of the data.

**B. The Role of the CTIE Regarding the Principle of Integrity and Confidentiality**

19. The commentary on Article 6 of this draft law specifies that "the transfer of data and information from the operator to the competent authorities is carried out through the IT solution hosted by the CTIE, which, in terms of the General Data Protection Regulation, will have the status of a processor for the operators and the Police." However, the qualification retained by the authors of the draft law raises several observations from the CNPD.

20. First of all, it should be recalled that Article 4.8 of the GDPR defines the processor as "the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of the controller." However, it follows from Article 1.6 of the draft law that the IT solution is hosted within the infrastructure of the CTIE on behalf of the Minister responsible for Tourism. This wording suggests that the CTIE would act as a processor for the Minister, whereas, according to the understanding of the CNPD, the latter does not intervene within the framework of the system provided for by the draft law. The National Commission believes that the explanations contained in this regard in the commentary on Article 1 do not provide the necessary clarifications.

21. Furthermore, in accordance with Article 28.3 of the GDPR, processing by a processor must be governed by a contract or another legal act under Union law or the law of a Member State, concluded between the controller and the processor, which must contain certain clauses. Thus, the qualification of processor has the consequence that operators must conclude a processing agreement with the CTIE in the sense of Article 28.3 of the GDPR. However, the conclusion of such a contract does not affect the responsibility of the operators regarding data protection. The CNPD reminds that the operator, as the controller, remains responsible for implementing appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure that the processing is carried out in accordance with the GDPR (Article 24) and must be able to demonstrate this. Therefore, the National Commission questions whether the qualification of the various actors, as envisaged by the authors of the draft law, is adapted to the reality of the activities actually carried out. 2. The Data Subjects

22. The current system provides that a lodging form must be completed by each traveler, with the exception of the person accompanying the traveler and children under the age of 15 who are included on the same form. Furthermore, Article 1 of the law of June 24, 2008, concerning the control of travelers in accommodation establishments, as well as the implementing regulation, which has been in force since August 14, 2015, only requires, for persons traveling in groups, the obligation to establish a form for the main traveler and to indicate on this form the number of accompanying travelers. Consequently, no personal data concerning the "accompanying" persons is transmitted to the Grand Ducal Police.

23. The authors of the bill under review now wish to impose on operators the obligation to establish a lodging form for each client aged 15 or older and for each stay of the client. The CNPD takes note of the authors' explanations regarding the reasons for wanting to expand the circle of concerned persons. According to their explanations, the current system would be ineffective in that the Police do not receive any information about the identity of the various members of a group, and it is not easy for the operator, when several people present themselves at their establishment without being part of an organized trip, to determine who is the "main" traveler whose data they are supposed to record and who the accompanying persons are.

24. At the European level, Article 45.1.a) of the CAAS explicitly excludes spouses or minors accompanying the main traveler or members of travel groups from the obligation to complete a lodging form. Thus, the National Commission wonders whether it is really necessary to expand the circle of concerned persons in light of the aforementioned provision.

25. Furthermore, Article 1.5° of the bill under review defines a traveler as "any person aged 15 or older who stays in tourist accommodation for a duration not exceeding three consecutive months." As a result, minors aged fifteen to eighteen are required to complete a lodging form for each stay in tourist accommodation, and their personal data is also transmitted to the Grand Ducal Police. Considering that these are vulnerable persons, the CNPD finds the generalized and systematic transmission to the Grand Ducal Police to be all the more problematic. It should be noted that regarding the processing of personal data in criminal matters, "measures taken by the data controller should include the restoration and implementation of specific safeguards intended for the processing of personal data."

**3. Categories of Data**  
27. Travelers must provide on the accommodation form the personal information specified in Annex 1 of this bill. The National Commission understands that this includes, in particular, the number of the identification document presented by the traveler. According to Article 5.2 of the bill under review, the operator is obliged to verify the data submitted to them based on the traveler’s identity card or passport. For this purpose, the operator may request the traveler to present an identification document solely for the purpose of verifying the information provided by them. This is an obligation that is also provided for by Article 45.1.a) of the CAAS. The National Commission wishes to emphasize that operators are rightly entitled to request an identification document to verify the traveler’s data, but they do not have the right to make a copy of the document presented, in accordance with the principle of minimization provided in Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR, which stipulates that data collection must be limited to what is necessary for the purposes for which it is processed.

IV. 
Transmission of data to the Grand-Ducal Police
29. According to the explanatory memorandum, one of the main reasons for the proposed reform lies in the fact that the current retention period of seventy-two hours, during which the Grand-Ducal Police can retain data transmitted by operators, is deemed too short. Thus, any investigation into a suspect who later appears in a judicial file would be impossible, meaning that the use of this tool is almost exclusively limited to the search for reported persons. The authors of the draft law argue that the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg could in the future be increasingly used as a meeting place prior to the preparation of a terrorist attack or to facilitate escape, and consequently propose to extend the retention period to six months. However, the National Commission notes that the centralization of data with the Grand-Ducal Police as provided for in the draft law under review, and as it currently exists, represents a singularity compared to our neighboring countries. 

30. Indeed, there is currently a hybrid system in the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg. The data from the accommodation records of all travelers must be transmitted within a maximum period of twelve hours by the operators to the Grand-Ducal Police, while the originals of the accommodation records are retained by the operator themselves. In this second scenario, these records must be presented upon any request by the agents of the Grand-Ducal Police. Therefore, the draft law under review explicitly provides in its Article 8.1 that "the originals of the accommodation records established on paper must be retained by the operator for a period of six months and must be presented upon any request from the Grand-Ducal Police." Thus, the authors of the draft law now wish to give police officers the possibility to requisition the accommodation records from the operator for a period of six months, which represents the common procedure in our neighboring countries.

V. 
Transmission of Data to STATEC

35. In order to develop reliable statistical data to record and study the evolution of tourist flows and/or to study the economic competitiveness of the tourism sector, the operator is obliged to transmit to STATEC, for each traveler and each stay, the information listed in Annex II of the draft law under review. As mentioned above, Article 9 of Regulation (EU) 692/2011 of the European Parliament and of the Council of July 6, 2011, concerning statistics on tourism and repealing Council Directive 95/57/EC, obliges Member States to provide the statistical office of the European Commission (EUROSTAT) with various statistics on the occupancy of accommodation establishments within their territory.

36. From the commentary on Article 6 of the draft law under review, it appears that "(...) insofar as STATEC does not receive any personal data, the processing of the information received is not subject to data protection legislation." The authors of the draft law consider that the data transmitted to STATEC constitutes anonymized data whose processing is not subject to the GDPR regime. The CNPD must express reservations regarding this reasoning. Indeed, Recital (26) of the GDPR specifies that there is no need to apply the principles relating to data protection to anonymous information, namely information that does not relate to an identified or identifiable natural person, nor to personal data rendered anonymous in such a way that the data subject is not or no longer identifiable. However, the National Commission believes that in this case, a combination of the postal code and the year of birth, data that are transmitted to STATEC, could allow for the indirect identification of a person in specific cases.

37. Under the principle of minimization, provided for in Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR, personal data must be limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed. According to the aforementioned Regulation (EU) 692/2011, only the country or geographical area of residence of the visitor should be transmitted to the CNPD.
