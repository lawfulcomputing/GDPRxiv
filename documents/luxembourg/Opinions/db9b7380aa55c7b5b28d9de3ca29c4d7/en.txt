**Security and Freedom**  
1. The Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter the "CJEU" or the "Court") has increasingly detailed its position regarding the possibility for telecommunications operators or providers of electronic communications services (hereinafter "operators and providers") to retain traffic and location data and for the competent national authorities to access the data thus retained since its first ruling on the matter in 2014.  

4. The Court raises the point that such retention by operators and providers contradicts the principle of confidentiality enshrined in Directive 2002/58/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council of July 12, 2002, concerning the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector (hereinafter the "Privacy and Electronic Communications Directive"). Thus, traffic and location data must, in principle, be erased or rendered anonymous when they are no longer necessary for the transmission of a communication.  

5. However, this principle of erasure is subject to exceptions, particularly concerning the retention of data required for billing and payments for interconnection, or even the possibility, with the consent of the data subject, to process this data for commercial purposes or for the provision of value-added services. Another exception arises from Article 15 of this directive, which grants Member States the possibility to limit the scope of rights and obligations in certain cases.  

6. The case law regarding data retention thus primarily focuses on the Court's interpretation of the possible exceptions under Article 15 of the Privacy and Electronic Communications Directive. In the rulings that followed the first ruling "Digital Rights Ireland" of April 8, 2014, the Court increasingly refines this interpretation and provides indications regarding the conditions that must accompany these admissible exceptions to the principle of confidentiality.

7.  
The CJEU determines that Article 15 of the Directive on Privacy and Electronic Communications, interpreted in light of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, does not oppose national legislative measures providing for both the retention of traffic and location data and access to such data, provided that they are limited in terms of the objectives pursued as well as delineated in terms of the persons targeted, the means of communication concerned, the categories of data retained, and the duration of retention, and that they "ensure, through clear and precise rules, that the retention of the data in question is subject to compliance with the relevant material and procedural conditions and that the individuals concerned have effective safeguards against the risks of abuse."  

8.  
Over the past few years, the CJEU has been able to analyze, in its rulings related to data retention, various legislative measures for retention and access that have attempted to meet the requirements raised in its case law. Consequently, it has been able to increasingly specify what legislative measures for retention and access must comply with in order to be consistent with the legal framework regarding the protection of personal data. The explanatory memorandum extensively describes the majority of the requirements raised by the CJEU.  

9.  
In general, the CNPD wishes to remind that to ensure compliance with the principle of proportionality, the CJEU has repeatedly expressed that retention must be limited to what is strictly necessary to achieve the objectives. In its rulings, the CJEU systematically analyzes the proportionality of the measures.  

10.  
Thus, first, it determines the objectives pursued by the legislative measures for retention or access. This first step is essential, not only because the objectives are exhaustively listed in Article 15 of the Directive on Privacy and Electronic Communications, but also because the importance of the objective pursued impacts the tolerated interference with the privacy of the individuals concerned. It is evident from reading the CJEU's rulings on data retention that the more important the objectives, the more the interference of the retention and access measures in the privacy of the individuals concerned is tolerated.  

11.  
It follows that the extent of the legislative measures for retention and access largely depends on the objective pursued. Thus, generalized and indiscriminate retention of traffic data may be tolerated, subject to certain conditions, for the objectives of safeguarding national security. 1. **The measure of access to identification data based on Article 48-27.1 of the Code of Criminal Procedure**

157. The draft law aims to adapt the wording of Article 48-27 of the CPP to reflect changes in telecommunications law, notably the introduction of Article 10ter.1, which establishes the legal obligation for operators and providers to retain users' identification data. Thus, the new wording of Article 48-27.1 of the CPP will grant the investigating judge and the public prosecutor the ability to access this identification data.

158. Regarding the objectives for which access to retained data is possible, it should be noted that the provision under review does not limit applicability to specific offenses. Therefore, the use of this access measure is possible for both the safeguarding of national security and the fight against serious crime, as well as for the fight against crime in general.

159. In the ruling Ministerio Fiscal of 2018, the CJEU was able to rule on such a measure of access to identification data only. It particularly clarifies the question of for which objectives (national security, serious crime, simple crime) access to identification data alone is possible.

160. It is clear from this ruling that the Court determines the severity of the interference in the private lives of the individuals concerned based on the nature of the accessed data. Thus, when data is accessed that does not allow for precise conclusions regarding the private lives of the individuals concerned, the interference is not classified as serious. In the specific case of the ruling Ministerio Fiscal, the data only allowed for the correlation, during a specified period [12 days], of the activated SIM card(s) with the stolen mobile phone and the civil identity of the holders of these SIM cards. Without cross-referencing with the data related to the communications made with said SIM cards and the location data, this data does not allow for knowledge of the date, time, duration, and recipients of the communications made with the SIM card(s) in question, nor the places where these communications took place or the frequency of such communications with certain individuals during a given period. The Court concludes by stating that if access is therefore aimed at civil identity data, that is to say, the name, surname, and, where applicable, the address, as well as the only phone numbers corresponding to these SIM cards, this interference cannot be classified as serious.
