Deliberation No. 103/AV43/2023 of December 8, 2023  

1. In accordance with Article 57.1.e) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of April 27, 2016, on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data, and repealing Directive 95/46/EC (General Data Protection Regulation) (hereinafter referred to as the "GDPR"), to which Article 7 of the law of August 1, 2018, organizing the National Commission for Data Protection and the general regime on data protection refers, the National Commission for Data Protection (hereinafter referred to as the "National Commission" or "CNPD") "advises, in accordance with the law of the Member State, the national parliament, the government, and other institutions and bodies regarding legislative and administrative measures relating to the protection of the rights and freedoms of natural persons with regard to processing."  
Furthermore, Article 36.4 of the GDPR states that "[m]ember states consult the supervisory authority in the context of the preparation of a legislative proposal to be adopted by a national parliament, or a regulatory measure based on such a legislative measure, which relates to processing."  

2. On November 29, 2021, the CNPD notified the draft bill No. 7674 concerning the organization of access to knowledge of one's origins in the context of adoption or medically assisted procreation with third-party donors.  

3. By letter dated May 11, 2023, the Ministry of Justice invited the National Commission to express its opinion on the government amendments related to the aforementioned draft bill that were approved by the Government Council in its session of April 28, 2023 (hereinafter referred to as the "amendments").  

4. Amendment No. 1 aims to modify the title of the draft bill to "Draft Bill on the Organization of Access to One's Origins in the Context of Adoption or Medically Assisted Procreation with Third-Party Donors" (hereinafter referred to as the "draft bill"). The CNPD notes that the other amendments primarily aim to address the formal objections raised by the Council of State in its opinion of July 16, 2021. At the same time, it regrets that almost all of its considerations, concerns, and questions raised in its initial opinion have not been taken into account by the authors of the amendments. Therefore, it takes the liberty of reiterating all of its observations made in its aforementioned opinion, while emphasizing certain points in this opinion, and even issuing additional recommendations regarding the amendments. **Preliminary Remarks**

The CNPD proposes to follow the new numbering of the articles of the draft law following the amendments.

1. Preliminary Remarks  
1.1. Regarding the roles of the different stakeholders  
5. Article 3.1 of the draft law expressly designates the "competent minister," that is, the minister responsible for Children's Rights (hereinafter referred to as the "minister"), as the data controller. However, as criticized in its initial opinion, such clarity does not exist for the other actors mentioned in the draft law, such as, for example, hospital establishments or any other health professional involved in the birth under secrecy, or even for medically assisted procreation (hereinafter referred to as "MAP") the fertilization centers or the doctor responsible for implementing MAP, or for other national adoptions, international adoptions, judicial authorities, or the central authority for adoption.  
These actors play a crucial role in the collection of personal data and their subsequent transmission to the data controller, and it is important to determine in the draft law their function, that is, whether they are to be considered as independent data controllers, as joint data controllers, or even as processors. These qualifications are essential for the application of the GDPR as they determine who is responsible for compliance with data protection rules and how the data subjects can exercise their rights.

1.2. Regarding categories of personal data  
6. It should first be recalled that the GDPR applies to any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Recital (26) of the GDPR specifies that "[t]o determine whether a natural person is identifiable, it is necessary to consider all means reasonably likely to be used by the data controller or by any other person to identify the natural person directly or indirectly [...]."  
7. Furthermore, under the principle of data minimization, enshrined in Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR, personal data must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed.  
8. The CNPD notes in this context that, despite the modifications provided by the amendments, the draft law still contains numerous uncertainties regarding the categories of personal data.

9. The entirety of these formulations is particularly vague and requires clarification. Indeed, it is necessary to question what the authors of the draft law intended to convey by "information about the child's origins, the circumstances of the birth as well as any other information," "information relating to identity," "information relating to the origins of the adopted child," or even "any other information available on the donor(s)" or "any other information, file, and material object left by the birth parent(s)," as well as the "identity declarations."

10. More specifically, it is not clear from the text of the draft law whether the terms used refer to personal data or not. The CNPD wishes to point out in this regard that there is a risk of (re)identification of a natural person through a combination of information, even if qualified as "non-identifying" by the authors of the draft law, which results in the application of the rules regarding data protection. In other words, if it were, for example, possible for the minister to use and cross-reference all the data obtained in the context of the draft law and link it to a specific natural person, thereby allowing for the indirect identification of that person, in that case, the GDPR would be applicable.

11. Nevertheless, in light of the imprecision of the formulations used by the authors of the draft law and the resulting legal ambiguity, it must be noted that the draft law under review does not meet the requirements of clarity, precision, and predictability that a legal text must fulfill, in accordance with the jurisprudence of the Court of Justice of the European Union and the European Court of Human Rights.

12. Similarly, in its current state, the CNPD is unable to assess whether the aforementioned principle of data minimization is being respected. It therefore recommends that the authors of the draft law enumerate in a single article, depending on the scenarios (anonymous childbirth, national adoption, international adoption, assisted reproduction), the categories of personal data that would, if applicable, be collected and processed. Alternatively, it could be considered to include at the beginning of each chapter a list of the categories of data that would be processed during the implementation of the relevant chapter.

13. Finally, the CNPD wishes to emphasize that from a data protection perspective, there is nothing to prevent a child from accessing non-personal data, that is to say, data that cannot be linked to a specific natural person. This includes, for example, medical information about the parent's health that does not identify them but would allow the child to at least access information related to their genetic heritage and potential health risks. It refers, in this context, to the considerations of the Consultative Commission on Human Rights expressed in its opinions of July 6, 2022, and July 3, 2023.

16. Furthermore, the obligation of the data controller to ensure an appropriate level of security relative to the risks to the rights and freedoms of the individuals concerned by the processing of personal data, in accordance with Article 32 of the GDPR, deserves particular attention in this case, as unauthorized access to data, data leaks or losses, or unwanted modifications could cause serious harm to the individuals concerned.

17. In the event that an IT tool is used to process the data in question, which seems to be the case given the financial sheet of the initial draft law, it would be necessary to provide for an access logging system. In this regard, the CNPD recommends that logging data be retained for a period of five years from their recording, after which they are deleted, unless they are subject to a control procedure.

18. Finally, as expressed in its initial opinion11, the National Commission considers it essential, given the sensitivity of certain processed data, to provide for the modalities for implementing this principle of integrity and confidentiality in a legislative or at least regulatory text.

19. However, none of the other observations of the CNPD have been taken into account by the authors of the amendments.

**3. As for Article 4 of the draft law (former Article 51)**

23. **Article 4.2 of the draft law enumerates the various information that a hospital establishment or any health professional who assisted in the birth that took place outside a hospital must communicate to the birth parents in the event of a secret birth. Following amendment No. 7, paragraphs 1 and 3 of Article 4 of the draft law specify the format of this communication, namely that the hospital establishment or the aforementioned professional must transmit the relevant information "based on a pre-established document made available by the competent minister, which must be signed by the parent(s). In the event that this communication is not possible or if the signature of the document is refused by the parent(s), this must be noted on the document."**

24. **It emerges from the commentary on said amendment No. 7 that this document will serve as proof of the communication of the relevant information, on the one hand, and that the signature "can obviously consist of non-identifying signs of the identity of the parent(s)," on the other hand. While understanding the possibility of signing in such a manner to maintain the confidentiality of the parents' identity, the National Commission, on the other hand, has difficulties in grasping how this document would then serve as proof of communication in such a specific situation. Similarly, it wonders whether the signed document is to be transmitted and retained by the CNPD.**

**4. Regarding Article 9 of the draft law (formerly Article 10)**

26. Article 9 point 5° of the draft law still provides that the minister is to receive “[t]he identity declarations made by the ascendants, descendants, and collateral relatives of the birth parents.” The CNPD wishes to refer in this context to the section “1.2. Regarding the categories of personal data” of this opinion, while emphasizing that neither the initial draft law nor the amendments provide additional details or explanations regarding the categories of personal data that would appear on said “identity declarations.”

27. It is also deemed essential to reiterate its questions posed in its initial opinion regarding how this data would be collected: “Is it the responsibility of the ascendants, descendants, and collateral relatives to address themselves directly to the minister? How will the minister ensure that the identity declarations are attributed to the concerned child, given that only the first names and sex of the child as well as the date and place of birth (and not the identity of the birth parents) are mentioned outside the envelopes referred to in Article 6 of the draft law? Will the minister verify if there is indeed a familial link between these individuals and the birth parents?” The Council of State has also expressed similar concerns in its opinion.
