II. In Law 
II.1. On the grounds for the decision 
A. On the quality of the controlled entity and the determination of the data controller 
1. On the principles 
17. In accordance with Article 4.7 of the GDPR, the data controller is "the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing [...]". 
18. The European Data Protection Board (hereinafter referred to as the "EDPB") has provided further explanations in its guidelines 07/2020 on the concepts of data controller and processor. It clarified that "there is no limit to the type of entity that can assume the role of data controller, but in practice, it is generally the organization itself and not an individual within it (such as the CEO, an employee, or a board member) that acts as the data controller"7. 
2. In this case 
19. The head of the investigation identified the controlled entity in point 6 of his statement of objections, clarifying that it is the company "A", and added that the controlled entity "is to be considered as the data controller, within the meaning of the GDPR, [...] regarding the video surveillance system implemented"8. 
20. In its written observations on the statement of objections, the controlled entity expressed doubt regarding the identity of the controlled entity because it seems to have understood that the "controlled entity" would be the natural person present on the day of the on-site visit by the CNPD agents9. 
21. The Restricted Formation wishes to clarify that the "controlled entity" is the company "A" (as specified in point 3 of this decision) and not the individual(s) present on the day of the on-site visit by the CNPD agents. The fact that the manager of the controlled entity was not present on the day of the on-site visit but that another person answered the questions posed by the CNPD agents does not change the identity of the "controlled entity". Furthermore, the fact that said person was unable to answer all the questions from the agents and/or could not respond correctly to these questions will not prejudice the controlled entity. An unannounced on-site visit is precisely not conducted in anticipation of meeting on-site the individuals responsible for data protection and/or the legal representative of the controlled entity but rather to be able to take stock of the situation. 
22. It also confirms that the controlled entity is indeed the "data controller" within the meaning of Article 4.7 of the GDPR because the controlled entity determines the purposes and means of the processing, and it recalls that even if a particular natural person is designated "to ensure compliance with data protection rules, this person will not be the data controller but will act on behalf of the legal entity [...] which, in its capacity as data controller, will be ultimately responsible in the event of a breach of the rules"10.

c) the processing is necessary for compliance with a legal obligation to which the data controller is subject;

It should be noted that the principle of lawfulness is distinct from the principle of purpose, the latter providing that personal data must be "collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes; […] (purpose limitation)" (Article 5.1.b) of the GDPR). The processing of personal data must therefore pursue a certain predetermined objective, and it is generally not permitted to use the data collected for any purpose other than that which was defined before the processing began. It is only once the purpose of the processing, that is to say its objective, has been defined that its determined, explicit, and legitimate nature can be assessed.

The Restricted Formation first recalls that there is a difference between the notion of "legal basis" provided for by Article 6.1 of the GDPR and the notion of "purpose" provided for by Article 5.1.b) of the GDPR, and that the controller has conflated these two notions on several occasions. Thus, in its response to the statement of objections, the controller maintained its assertions that, in this case, the video surveillance system was covered by three legal bases, namely "Security of property, Security of persons, Consent of employees"41, whereas only the consent of the data subjects is listed among the legally enumerated bases in Article 6.1 of the GDPR. The security of property and the security of persons may constitute legitimate interests of a data controller for which a video surveillance system may appear necessary. Thus, the appropriate legality condition would therefore be the legitimate interest according to Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR. 
35. 
Regarding the consent of the data subject (Article 6.1.a) of the GDPR), the Restricted Formation recalls that according to Article 4.11 of the GDPR, the consent of the data subject means, among other things, "any freely given indication of wishes." It is therefore necessary to determine whether consent can be given freely if there is an imbalance of power relations, as is the case in an employment relationship between employer and employee. The Restricted Formation does not exclude that an employer may rely on consent as a legal basis for data processing by video surveillance concerning its employees, but only in strictly exceptional situations outside of workplace monitoring (for example, for one-off processing and in a situation where the employee had a real choice and where the employer can demonstrate that the employee does not have to fear negative consequences in case of refusal; cf. the example given by the EDPB in its guidelines 5/2020 on consent under Regulation (EU) 2016/67942). In the case of systematic and permanent video surveillance in the workplace, the Restricted Formation is of the opinion that employees are not able to freely give their consent and, for this reason, it finds that the legal basis of consent put forward by the controller was not valid.

Regarding the legal basis for compliance with a legal obligation (Article 6.1.c) of the GDPR), the Restricted Formation is of the opinion that the security obligation imposed on the employer towards its employees as provided in Article L. 312-1 of the Labor Code does not impose any obligation to carry out video surveillance of employees. The video surveillance system as designed in this case does not appear to be the most appropriate means to protect employees. Regarding the legal basis of legitimate interest not explicitly invoked by the controlled entity but analyzed by the head of the investigation in his communication of grievances, it fully aligns with his analysis and notes that the video surveillance device may, at least partially, rely on said legal basis. It also shares the head of investigation's view that the fundamental rights and interests of the concerned individuals (in this case: employees of the controlled entity) prevail over the legitimate interests of the controlled entity concerning camera no. 1 and camera no. 2.

Thus, the Restricted Formation finds that the legal basis of legitimate interest (Article 6.1.f) of the GDPR was not valid concerning camera no. 1 and camera no. 2 and that consequently, the processing of personal data carried out by these two cameras was unlawful.

E. On the breach related to the principle of data retention limitation (Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR) and the obligation of data protection by default (Article 25.2 of the GDPR) 
1. On the principles  
59. 
Article 5.1.e) of the GDPR provides for the principle of data retention limitation and states the following:  
“1. Personal data must be: […] 
e) kept in a form which permits identification of the data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the data are processed; personal data may be stored for longer periods insofar as they will be processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, for scientific or historical research, or for statistical purposes in accordance with Article 89(1), provided that the appropriate technical and organizational measures required by this Regulation are implemented to safeguard the rights and freedoms of the data subject (data retention limitation);.” 
60. 
According to Recital (39) of the GDPR “personal data should be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for the purposes for which they are processed. This requires, in particular, ensuring that the duration of data retention is limited to the strict minimum. Personal data should only be processed if the purpose of the processing cannot be reasonably achieved by other means. To ensure that data is not kept longer than necessary, deadlines should be set by the data controller for their deletion or for periodic review […].” 
61. 
Article 25 of the GDPR introduces two general obligations for the data controller and/or the processor, namely data protection by design (Article 25.1 of the GDPR) and data protection by default (Article 25.2 of the GDPR). Article 25.2 states more specifically:  
“2. The data controller shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure that, by default, only personal data which are necessary for each specific purpose of the processing are processed. This applies to the amount of personal data collected, the extent of their processing, their retention period, and their accessibility. In particular, these measures ensure that, by default, personal data is not made accessible to an indefinite number of natural persons without the intervention of the data subject.” 
62. 
In its Guidelines 4/2019 on Article 25 of the GDPR63, the EDPS specifies that “[i]n its common definition in computing, the term ‘by default’ refers to the pre-existing or pre-selected value of a configurable parameter that is assigned to a software application, computer program, or computing device. These parameters are also referred to as ‘presets’ or ‘factory settings’, particularly for electronic devices”64.  
The EDPS further clarifies that “the term ‘by default’, in the context of personal data processing, refers to making choices regarding the configuration values or processing options defined or prescribed in a processing system, such as a software application, service, or device, or a manual processing procedure, which affect the amount of personal data collected, the extent of their processing, the duration of their retention, as well as their accessibility”65.  
To ensure that only personal data which are necessary for each specific purpose of the processing are processed, the EDPS emphasizes that “the fundamental requirement is that data protection is integrated by default into the processing”66.
