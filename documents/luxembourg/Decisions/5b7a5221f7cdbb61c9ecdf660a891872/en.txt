II. In law  
II.1. On the grounds for the decision  
A. On the status of the parties concerned and the determination of the data controller  
1. On the principles  
23. In accordance with Article 4.7 of the GDPR, the data controller is "the natural or legal person, public authority, service, or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing [...]".  
24. The European Data Protection Board (hereinafter: "EDPB"), in its guidelines 07/2020 on the concepts of data controller and processor, specifies that "in principle, there is no limit to the type of entity that may assume the role of data controller."  
25. The EDPB further states that the concept of data controller is a functional concept and that "it is therefore based on a factual rather than formal analysis. [...] In most cases, the 'body that determines' can be easily and clearly identified by reference to certain legal and/or factual circumstances from which 'influence' can normally be inferred, unless other elements indicate otherwise."  
26. Finally, the EDPB specifies that "[i]n the absence of control arising from legal provisions, the designation of a party as data controller must be established based on an assessment of the factual circumstances in which the processing takes place. All relevant factual circumstances must be taken into account to determine whether a given entity exerts a determining influence on the processing of the personal data in question."

B. On the breach related to the principle of accountability (Article 5.2 of the GDPR)
1. On the principles
30. Article 5.2 of the GDPR states that "[t]he data controller is responsible for compliance with paragraph 1 and is able to demonstrate that it is complied with (accountability)." Paragraph 1 of this article states in particular that "[p]ersonal data must be: a) processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently in relation to the data subject (lawfulness, fairness, transparency)."
31. Recital 74 of the GDPR notably states that "it is particularly important that the data controller is required to implement appropriate and effective measures and is able to demonstrate compliance of processing activities with this Regulation, including the effectiveness of the measures."
32. Furthermore, the Court of Justice of the European Union (hereinafter: the "CJEU") specifies that "[u]nder paragraph 2 of Article 5 of the GDPR, the data controller, in accordance with the principle of 'accountability' set out in that provision, is responsible for compliance with paragraph 1 of this article and must be able to demonstrate that it complies with each of the principles set out in that paragraph 1, such proof being thus placed on its shoulders"16.
33. Article 6.1, f) of the GDPR states that "processing is necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the data controller or by a third party, unless the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data prevail, in particular when the data subject is a child."
34. Recital (47) of the GDPR states in particular that "the legitimate interests of a data controller (…) may constitute a legal basis for processing, unless the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject prevail, taking into account the reasonable expectations of the data subjects based on their relationship with the data controller."
35. Thus, as noted by the CJEU, the provision of Article 6.1, f) of the GDPR "provides for three cumulative conditions for the processing of personal data it addresses to be lawful, namely, first, the pursuit of a legitimate interest by the data controller or by a third party, second, the necessity of processing personal data for the realization of the legitimate interest pursued, and third, the condition that the interests or the freedoms and fundamental rights of the data subject concerning the protection of data do not prevail over the legitimate interest of the data controller or a third party"17.

Therefore, under the principle of accountability, the data controller relying on the legal basis of legitimate interest must be able to demonstrate that the aforementioned cumulative conditions are met. Thus, it is particularly incumbent upon them to show that the balancing test has been carried out appropriately and that the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not outweigh the legitimate interests they pursue.  
2. In this case 
37. During the on-site visit, the High School informed the agents of the CNPD that the legal basis for the processing implemented by the video surveillance system is legitimate interest, namely access control and property protection in order to detect break-ins, theft, and/or vandalism18.  
38. It also indicated that no balancing between its legitimate interests and the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subjects had been carried out19. By acting in this manner, the High School failed to meet one of the three cumulative conditions set out in point 35 above, namely to conduct the balancing test and demonstrate that the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject do not outweigh the legitimate interests it pursues. The High School was therefore unable to provide evidence that this condition was met. However, this burden of proof rests with it under Article 5.2 of the GDPR. 
39. Consequently, the Restricted Formation agrees with the opinion of the head of investigation and considers that non-compliance with Article 5.2 of the GDPR was established on the day of the on-site visit by the agents of the CNPD.

C. On the breach related to the principle of transparency (Article 5.1.a) of the GDPR) and the information obligations (Articles 13.1 and 13.2 of the GDPR) 
1. On the principles 
40. The communication to data subjects of information regarding the processing of their data is an essential element in the context of compliance with the general obligations of transparency under the GDPR.  
41. Indeed, under Article 5.1.a) of the GDPR, personal data must be processed, among other things, in a transparent manner with respect to the data subjects (principle of transparency). Article 12 of the GDPR provides that “the data controller shall take appropriate measures to provide any information referred to in Articles 13 and 14 (…) regarding the processing to the data subject in a concise, transparent, comprehensible, and easily accessible manner.”  
42. Furthermore, when the data subject requests it, the information may be provided orally20. The guidelines of the Article 29 Working Party on transparency under Regulation (EU) 2016/679, adopted on April 11, 2018, state that “[t]he data controller should (…) ensure that a written record is kept, and ensure that it is able to prove it (for the purposes of compliance with the accountability requirement), of: i) the request for information made orally, ii) the method by which the identity of the data subject was verified (…) and iii) that the information was provided to the data subject”21.

2. In this case 
45. During the on-site visit, the agents of the CNPD noted that the only signage displayed at the entrances of the Lycée24 regarding video surveillance consisted of three panels featuring a camera pictogram, as well as the mention "area under video surveillance," without any further information25. 
46. However, the Restricted Formation recalls that the CNPD's guidelines on video surveillance state that “[i]n order to inform the individuals concerned of the presence of a video surveillance system, the CNPD recommends communicating, for example via display panels, a first level of information containing: the identity and contact details of the data controller; the purpose(s) of the processing; the existence of the rights of the individuals concerned; the mention that more comprehensive information exists (second level of information) and the means to access it (for example, a hyperlink directing to the website of the data controller, the use of a QR code, a phone number to call, or the indication of the location where this more detailed information is available)”26. 
47. The Restricted Formation therefore notes that the first level of information (the panels), as observed on the day of the on-site visit, did not contain any of the aforementioned information, nor did it refer to a second-level document that would contain the missing information. 
48. Furthermore, the Lycée stated during the on-site visit that information regarding video surveillance had been provided orally to students and teaching staff27.  
49. According to the Lycée, this information would have been provided to students by the class regents, as indicated in their administrative tasks28. However, the Restricted Formation agrees with the opinion of the Chief Investigator who “believes that the excerpt of administrative tasks does not demonstrate that information in the sense of the GDPR was actually provided to the students”29. 
50. Regarding the information provided to teaching staff, the Lycée fails to provide evidence of any such information30, having stated that there is no record of such communication31. In any case, a mere oral assertion by the Lycée would not have proven that the requirements of the GDPR were met32. 
51. Moreover, no information appears to have been provided regarding individuals outside the Lycée (for example, parents of students) other than the students or teaching staff33. 
52. Thus, in the absence of any form of information, the Restricted Formation considers that the Lycée has failed to comply with the requirements of Articles 5.1.a), 13.1, and 13.2 of the GDPR.  
53. Additionally, the Lycée does not justify that the oral provision of this information was requested by the individuals concerned, in violation of Article 12.1 of the GDPR34.  
54. In light of the above, the Restricted Formation aligns with the opinion of the Chief Investigator and concludes that non-compliance with Article 5.1.a) related to Articles 13.1 and 13.2 of the GDPR was established on the day of the on-site visit by the agents of the CNPD.

68.

F. On the breach related to the security of processing (Article 32.1 of the GDPR)  
1. On the principles  
78. Article 32.1 of the GDPR states that "[t]aking into account the state of the knowledge, the costs of implementation, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing as well as the risks, the degree of probability and severity of which varies, for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the data controller and the processor shall implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk (...)" and in particular "means to restore the availability of personal data and access to it within appropriate timeframes in the event of a physical or technical incident" and "a procedure to regularly test, analyze, and evaluate the effectiveness of the technical and organizational measures to ensure the security of processing."  
79. Article 32.2 of the GDPR states that "[i]n assessing the appropriate level of security, particular account shall be taken of the risks presented by the processing, resulting in particular from the destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of personal data transmitted, stored, or otherwise processed, or from unauthorized access to such data, whether accidental or unlawful."  
80. The EDPS in its aforementioned guidelines 3/2019 specifies that "[a]ccess control ensures that only authorized persons can access the system and the data (...)." The CNPD's guidelines on video surveillance state that "access to the data must be secured (for example, through a strong password and an identifier) and each person with access to the data must have an individual access account. An access log must also be available, so that it is possible to trace the individuals who have accessed the data."

2. In this case 

81. It emerges from the first on-site visit that the images captured by the video surveillance system could be viewed in real-time by the six members of the technical team of the Lycée from the computers installed in booths A and B59. The members of the technical team therefore only had access to the images in real-time60. Only the principal and the deputy principal of the Lycée had access to the recorded images via an individual user account61. 

82. Furthermore, each member of the technical team had individual access to the computer but accessed the video surveillance system's operating software via a shared account62. Indeed, the lead investigator noted that access to the operating software of the video surveillance system “is secured by a common username and password shared among the six members of the technical team, who thus use a single user account”63. 

83. Moreover, the agents of the CNPD observed during the on-site visit that access to booths A and B is physically secured by a locked door, as well as by a door secured by an access badge system64. 

84. Finally, during the on-site visit, the Lycée stated that no traceability of access to the operating software of the video surveillance system was possible65. 

85. Like the lead investigator, the Restricted Formation believes that access to a video surveillance system via software should, in principle, be secured through individual user accounts that allow for proper traceability of access and actions on the system, such as, for example, the deletion or modification of video recordings. Furthermore, a password, in accordance with basic rules regarding information system security, must, to be effective, remain secret and individual. This rule is not respected in the case of a shared account. 

86. Consequently, the Restricted Formation holds that in order to comply with the requirements of Article 32.1 of the GDPR, the Lycée must implement a logging system for access and events, as well as a periodic review of log files to detect any anomalies and ensure that the members of the technical team of the Lycée authorized to access the video surveillance system each have an individual account with a unique password in order to access the operating software of said system. 

87. In light of the above, the Restricted Formation concludes that non-compliance with Article 32.1 was established on the day of the on-site visit by the agents of the CNPD.
