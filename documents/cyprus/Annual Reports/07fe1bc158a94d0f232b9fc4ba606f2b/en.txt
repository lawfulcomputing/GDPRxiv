FOREWORD  
The year 2023 was marked by my honorable election as Vice-President of the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) for a five-year term, during the elections held on the anniversary of the five years of implementation of the General Data Protection Regulation, that is, on May 25, 2023. This election sends many messages, including that small-sized Supervisory Authorities, such as my Office, can play a decisive role in European affairs through hard work and dedication.  

At the same time, the Vice-Presidency automatically entails more and demanding obligations both within and outside Cyprus, as representing an institutional body of the European Union requires focus, as well as continuous and substantial presence and participation.  
In carrying out my duties as Vice-President of the EDPB, I chaired a session of the EDPB Plenary, five sessions of the EDPB Strategic Advisory expert subgroup, and handled 20 requests received by the EDPB for public access to EDPB documents. In addition, I actively participated in all EDPB sessions, while the officials of my Office had uninterrupted participation and presence in the meetings of expert subgroups and special teams of the EDPB.  

However, apart from European obligations, the demands for overseeing compliance with the legal framework for data protection in our country are becoming increasingly pressing. In 2023, my Office received 448 complaints and 110 notifications of personal data breach incidents. A total of 67 Decisions were issued, of which 11 resulted in an administrative fine totaling €120,250.  
The new data necessitate the strengthening of my Office with technical means, as well as with human resources trained to meet the tireless effort of safeguarding the personality and privacy of citizens.  

Eirini Loizidou Nikolaidou

1. INTRODUCTION  
1.1. The legal framework for the protection of personal data in the EU – General Data Protection Regulation, (EU) 2016/679  
On May 25, 2018, the General Data Protection Regulation, (EU) 2016/679 (hereinafter referred to as the "Regulation"), came into effect, establishing an enhanced data protection framework across all member states of the European Union and the European Economic Area. The Regulation is also designed to address the technological and social changes that have occurred over the past two decades, particularly due to the continuous evolution of the internet and the new services it provides, the development of the digital economy, and the widespread use of social media.  
The Regulation also applies to organizations based outside the EU if they offer goods or services to EU citizens or monitor the behavior of EU citizens. It is noted that many organizations, particularly online businesses, which were not subject to the European Directive 95/46/EC on data protection that was repealed with the implementation of the Regulation, are now subject to the Regulation.  
The Regulation promotes transparency, strengthens the rights of data subjects, introduces the principle of accountability in the sense of proving compliance with the provisions of the Regulation to the competent Supervisory Authority (Data Protection Commissioner), enhances the security of personal data, and introduces strict and deterrent administrative penalties.  

1.2. The Law on the Protection of Natural Persons Against the Processing of Personal Data and the Free Movement of Such Data of 2018, Law 125(I)/2018  
On July 31, 2018, the Law on the Protection of Natural Persons Against the Processing of Personal Data and the Free Movement of Such Data of 2018, Law 125(I)/2018, was published in the Official Gazette of the Republic. This Law was enacted for the more effective implementation of certain provisions of the Regulation.  

1.3. The Data Protection Commissioner  
The Data Protection Commissioner is an Independent Administrative Authority, responsible for overseeing the implementation of the Regulation and national legislation on personal data and other regulations concerning the protection of individuals from the processing of personal data. The Commissioner exercises the powers conferred upon them by the Regulation and national legislation or any other Law.  
The Commissioner represents the Republic of Cyprus in the relevant Bodies and Committees of the European Union, the Council of Europe, as well as other International Organizations.  

Key Responsibilities of the Data Protection Commissioner  
- Monitors and enforces the implementation of the Regulation.  
- Is a member of the European Data Protection Board.  
- Promotes public awareness and understanding of the risks, rules, safeguards, and rights related to processing. Special attention is given to activities specifically targeting children.  
- Advises the national parliament, government, and other bodies and organizations on legislative and administrative measures related to the protection of the rights and freedoms of individuals against processing.  
- Provides information to data subjects regarding the exercise of their rights under the Regulation.  
- Investigates complaints lodged by the data subject or by an entity or organization or association active in the field of protecting the rights and freedoms of data subjects concerning the protection of personal data.  
- Conducts inquiries regarding the implementation of the Regulation.  
- Maintains internal records of violations of the Regulation and the measures taken.  
- Approves certification criteria and approves or reviews certificates.  
- Provides accreditation to certification bodies.  
- Issues opinions on drafts of codes of conduct and approves them.  
- Approves binding corporate rules.  
- Approves standard contractual clauses.  
- Imposes administrative penalties, including administrative fines.  
- Collaborates with other Data Protection Authorities.

Additional Responsibilities of the Commissioner for the Protection of Personal Data  
  
In the context of harmonizing Cypriot legislation with Community law, the provisions of Directive 2002/58/EC regarding the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector have been transposed into Part 14 of the Law on the Regulation of Electronic Communications and Postal Services of 2004, Law 112(I)/2004, as amended.  
According to the provisions of Article 107 of Part 14 of this Law, additional responsibilities are granted to the Commissioner beyond those provided by the Law.  
Specifically, the Commissioner has the authority to handle cases and complaints regarding potential violations of Articles 99, 100, 101, 105, and 106, which concern the confidentiality of communications, traffic and billing data, location data, subscriber directories, and unsolicited calls (for the purpose of direct commercial promotion). Additionally, for the implementation of Article 98A, the Commissioner collaborates with the Commissioner for Communications.  
Law 112(I)/2004 has been amended by Law 23(I)/2022, which was published in the Official Gazette of the Republic with No. 4876, Annex (I) on March 4, 2022. Part 14 of Law 112(I)/2004 transposes the provisions of the well-known e-Privacy Directive, Directive 2002/58/EC, regarding the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector. For the purposes of legislative consistency, the amending Law 23(I)/2022 updated every reference in the Law to the repealed Law on the Processing of Personal Data (Protection of the Individual) of 2001, Law 138(I)/2001, and replaced all relevant references with references to the Regulation, as well as to the implementing Law 125(I)/2018.  
With the passage of the amending Law 23(I)/2022, the actual content of Articles 99 - 106 of Law 112(I)/2004 was not altered. However, a significant change lies in the fact that the responsibilities and powers granted to the Commissioner, as well as the prescribed sanctions, were specified and explicitly mentioned.  
In the context of the implementation of Article 98A, a memorandum of understanding and joint action was signed in 2017 by the Commissioner and the Commissioner for the Regulation of Electronic Communications and Postal Services (the previous title of the Commissioner for Communications). On July 25, 2023, a new Memorandum of Cooperation was signed regarding matters related to the joint or separate responsibilities of the Commissioner for the Protection of Personal Data and the Commissioner for Communications, in accordance with the existing and new legislation, and came into immediate effect.  

  
In December 2017, the Law on the Right of Access to Information from the Public Sector, Law 184(I)/2017, was passed, which came into effect in December 2020. The purpose of this Law is to enhance the principles of Transparency and Accountability in the Public and broader public sector. Based on Article 35, the supervision of the implementation of this Law is entrusted to the Commissioner for the Protection of Personal Data, who also acts as the Information Commissioner.  
This Law enshrines the right of citizens to request and receive, under certain conditions, information held by Public Authorities for the purpose of monitoring the Acts and Decisions of the Administration. At the same time, it creates an obligation for these Authorities to publish certain information on their websites, so that citizens do not need to submit requests to obtain this information.  
For the actions of the Information Commissioner, a separate Annual Report is prepared, which, pursuant to Article 37 of this Law, is submitted to the Council of Ministers and communicated to the Minister of Justice and Public Order and the House of Representatives. Additionally, this Report is made public on the website of the Office of the Information Commissioner.

3. COMPLAINTS  
Summaries of the main complaints from data subjects that occupied my Office during the year 2023 are provided, which are not included in Chapter 5 Commissioner Decisions. It is noted that where it is not specified against which entity the complaint was directed, this means that my Office did not communicate with that entity or that the complaint was directed against a natural person.

3.1. Rights of Data Subjects  
Right of Access  
3.1.1. Complaint regarding the non-satisfaction of the right of access by the Department of Population and Migration Records  
The complaint concerned the non-satisfaction of the right of access to an employee's personal file by the Department of Population and Migration Records. As was evident from the investigation I conducted, the employee had never requested access to his personal file and, therefore, the Department had never refused to satisfy his request. Based on the above, the complaint was deemed unfounded. The complainant contacted my Office again, forwarding a relevant response received from the Department regarding his request for a copy of his file. Among other things, I explained to the complainant that if the Department follows a specific procedure regarding the right of access, then that procedure must be followed. I also mentioned that under Article 15 of the Regulation, justification for the request for access and provision of a copy is not necessary, while any positions and opinions he may have should first be submitted to the Department for consideration and then to my Office. Furthermore, I noted that in the case of a refusal to provide or a finding of a violation, my Office would examine the incident taking into account the positions of the Department. 3.2.23. Complaint regarding the publication of photographs on social media  
The complainant submitted a complaint regarding the publication of her photographs on social media accounts (Facebook and Instagram).  
According to Recital 18 of the Regulation, concerning social media, as well as Article 2(2)(c) of the Regulation, it is evident that the Regulation does not apply when the processing of personal data is carried out by an individual in the context of personal or household activities and is not related to any professional or commercial activity. In cases of exemption for household activities, the Regulation applies to data controllers or processors who provide means for such personal or household activities (e.g., online platforms, social media platforms).  
Additionally, in the Judgment of the Court of the European Union, C‑73/07, Satakunnan Markkinapörssi and Satamedia, dated December 16, 2008, it was stated that processing that is exposed to an indeterminate number of persons does not fall under personal or household activities.  
Following an investigation by my Office, it was found that the two accounts maintained on Facebook by the individual against whom the complaint was directed were private, meaning that beyond the profile picture, users who were not friends could not see other photographs. Regarding the accounts maintained by this individual on Instagram, one of them was private, therefore someone who did not follow this user could not see what was posted, while the second account had no posts.  
I took into account the data before me, as well as the fact that the photographs were posted in the context of household activities and concerned private and closed accounts (i.e., someone could only see the posts if they were friends or followers of this individual), and I did not discern valid reasons for further action.  
I informed the complainant accordingly, who did not respond.

3.3.12. Complaint regarding the recording of a conversation by a natural person  
As reported by the complainant to my Office, unbeknownst to her and without her consent, a natural person proceeded to record a private conversation she had with the complainant. During this conversation, the complainant referred to her personal, family, and financial details. The person against whom the complaint was directed sent the recording of the conversation to third parties, for which, however, there was no evidence. In fact, as the complainant stated, one of these third parties was a member of the complainant's family.
63

In Recital 18 of the Regulation, it is clarified that "This Regulation does not apply to the processing of personal data by a natural person in the course of purely personal or household activities and thus without a connection to any professional or commercial activity. Personal or household activities could include correspondence and the maintenance of address books or social networking and online activities carried out in the context of such activities. However, this Regulation applies to data controllers or processors who provide the means for processing personal data for such personal or household activities." Similarly, Article 2(2)(c) of the Regulation states that the Regulation does not apply to the processing of personal data by a natural person "in the course of purely personal or household activities." 

Therefore, according to the above, the Regulation does not apply when the following conditions are cumulatively met: (a) the processing of data is carried out by a natural person, (b) in the context of personal or household activities, and (c) which is not connected to any professional or commercial activity. Regarding the above, in the Judgment of the Court of the European Union, C-73/07, Satakunnan Markkinapörssi and Satamedia, dated December 16, 2008, it was stated that personal or household activities do not include processing that is exposed to an unlimited number of persons. 

Taking the above into account, as well as the evidence I had before me, I informed the complainant that it did not appear that the recorded conversation under investigation had been disclosed to an unlimited number of persons, but rather to a specific number of contacts determined by the individual against whom the complaint was directed. As a result of the above, the said processing fell within the exception of Article 2(2)(c) of the Regulation and, therefore, I could not proceed with the investigation of the complaint.

(α) the provisions of Article 27 of the Statute of the Church of Cyprus, according to which the preparation of the voters' list for the vacant Metropolis and the electoral process for the election of a tripartite is conducted by the analogous application of the procedure for the election of the Archbishop,

4.8.  Disclosure of Personal Data of Permanent Public Employees to the Statistical Service  
The question submitted to my Office by the Public Service Commission concerned a request from the Statistical Service for the disclosure of personal data of public employees for the purposes of a European salary survey, which was conducted based on Regulation (EC) No. 1738/2005 of the Commission, amending Regulation (EC) No. 1916/2000 regarding the definition and transmission of information on the structure of salaries.  
The Statistical Service supported the validity of the request on the provisions of Article 6(1)(c) of the Regulation, namely that the relevant processing was necessary for compliance with a legal obligation.  
However, upon examining all the elements and details, and insofar as the public sector does not fall within the scope and/or subject of the said survey and/or is excluded from it according to the relevant Regulation (EC) 1738/2005, I concluded that the provisions of Article 6(1)(c) of the Regulation could not constitute a valid legal basis for the relevant processing, namely the disclosure of data by the Public Service Commission and the collection of data by the Statistical Service. I also noted that the data requested by the Statistical Service went beyond the mandatory variables of the relevant Regulation and, therefore, in such a case, it would need to justify the necessity of their collection and carry out the necessary balancing to ensure the principles of necessity, proportionality, and minimization.  
In light of the above, directions were given to the Statistical Service that in order to extend the scope of the salary survey to the Public Sector, it would need to support another legal basis within the framework of the Regulation and, furthermore, to limit the content of the survey to the mandatory variables defined by Regulation 1738/2005.  
For this purpose, I specifically pointed out certain aspects for compliance with the principle of minimization, e.g., instead of the year of birth, the Statistical Service appeared to be collecting and processing the date of birth, which is a personal data that can directly identify a natural person, even without combination with another element, a practice that did not seem to align with and/or contradicted the principle of data minimization. Additionally, the element of managerial or supervisory position constituted information that is not a mandatory variable, despite the fact that this data was requested by the Statistical Service. Furthermore, considering that the Statistical Service already possessed data on the salaries of public employees from the General Accounting Office, which should not include both the identity card number and the social security number, but should be limited only to the latter, I deemed the scale of the position in the Public Sector as excessive information.  
It was also significant to indicate that the Statistical Service could receive information/personal data, but not without limits, rather within the legal framework of the existing legislation for the protection of personal data.  
Beyond the above, I incidentally addressed the role of data protection officers and the manner of exercising their duties, as points of contact with my Office, and emphasized that their positions and recommendations should not be submitted for validation to my Office, but should be accepted, respected, and taken into account by the respective data controllers, unless there are reasons that argue otherwise or if it constitutes a recommendation from the data protection officers.

4.9. Rent Against Installment Scheme – Role Attribution

4.10. Cooperation Protocol of Social Welfare Services – KENTHEA within the framework of the European Program "Make the Difference"  
In the context of providing advice to Public Authorities, my Office was called upon to comment on the draft cooperation protocol between the Social Welfare Services and the Center for Information and Treatment of Addicted Persons (KENTHEA). The implementation of the cooperation protocol would result in the collection and processing of personal data, including special categories of data by KENTHEA, that is, by a non-governmental organization.  
The Services and KENTHEA participate in the European Program "Make the Difference," the implementation of which required the development of a Guide/Protocol for the cooperation of prevention and treatment services for addictions, as a result of which the relevant protocol was prepared and submitted to my Office. The aim of the program is to prevent factors that endanger the well-being of children living in households with addiction issues. Cooperation takes place in various ways, including multidisciplinary teams/coordinating meetings, where professionals from various public or private entities may be invited, and minutes will be prepared for each session, which will be shared with each participant.  
Reasoning – Considerations – Comments:  
The Services, acting as the data controller, collect and process the personal data of individuals/families, with the legal basis being the relevant primary and secondary legislative provisions or the ratifying laws of international treaties.
82

The KENTHEA, as a non-governmental organization of a voluntary nature operating in the field of prevention and treatment of addictions, has the ability, acting as the data controller, to collect and process personal data of individuals who approach it, with the legal basis being their consent. Relevant are the provisions of Article 9 of the Regulation. 

In any case, the cooperation protocol, as a non-legally binding document, did not constitute a legal act in itself and, therefore, could not serve as a valid legal basis, nor could it legally frame the collection and processing of personal data or sensitive personal data by KENTHEA, or the flow of personal data from the Services to KENTHEA or vice versa, or from KENTHEA to all participants of the multidisciplinary groups. 

Under the circumstances, the only appropriate legal basis that could support the collection, processing, exchange, and sharing of personal data to and from KENTHEA could be considered consent, in the sense and under the conditions attributed to this term by the provisions of Article 7 of the Regulation. 

However, consent does not generally constitute a valid legal basis for the processing of data by Public Authorities in the usual exercise of their duties as a result of the exercise of public authority, due to the imbalance of power in the relationships between public authority and the governed. Relevant is Recital 43 of the Regulation. 

According to the provisions of Article 7 of the Regulation, for consent to constitute a valid legal basis, it must be the result of free and genuine will, following proper and thorough information of the data subjects. Additionally, it must be revocable at any time, relate to each of the separate processing operations, and the data controller must be able to demonstrate that the data subjects have consented. 

Notwithstanding the above, explicit consent could potentially constitute a valid legal basis regarding the processing operations of the Services as a Public Authority, namely the sharing of personal data from the Services to KENTHEA, but also further for the collection and processing of personal data by KENTHEA and the maintenance of a relevant registry, as well as the flow of data / sharing of minutes with the participants of the multidisciplinary groups (provided that such was absolutely necessary for the purposes of the program). 

Regarding the case of the Services concerning the sharing of personal data from the Services to KENTHEA, the Services, as the data controller, would need to prepare a consent form that describes in detail at least the purpose, the processing operations, the categories of data concerned, the recipients (KENTHEA), the possibility of revocation at any time, the exercise of rights, the assurance that referral to KENTHEA was optional, and that in the event that individuals did not consent, they would not be deprived of an equivalent level of service from the Services. This form would need to be signed by both parents. 

Therefore, the form prepared by the Services required appropriate modifications to serve as a satisfactory legal basis. 

A similar consent form should also be prepared by KENTHEA, as it would be a new and separate data controller. 

A serious issue that the parties were called upon to recognize is whether, in their case, the status of joint data controllers applied. Such cases occur when more than one party is involved, and the Regulation establishes specific rules and defines the framework governing their relationships. The primary criterion for the existence of joint processing responsibility...
83

is the joint participation of two or more entities in determining the purposes and means of each processing activity. Joint participation may take the form of a common decision made by two or more entities or arise as a result of converging decisions by two or more entities. The provisions of Article 26 of the Regulation are relevant. In the event that this status were to be recognized, a necessary condition was the drafting of a relevant agreement that would duly reflect the respective roles and relationships of the jointly responsible processors towards the data subjects. Another important chapter was the security of data during the transfer of data to KENTHEA, as well as during the retention of data in a registry by KENTHEA, that is, by a private non-governmental organization, which would have to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure an adequate level of security against the risks associated with the sensitive categories of data that it would collect and process. This level, in any case, should not be lower than the level of security and data protection ensured by the Services. Therefore, KENTHEA would have to ensure strict compliance with the fundamental principles of lawful data processing (Article 5(1) of the Regulation), including the principle of data minimization, as well as that the storage of data takes place on servers in Cyprus. Beyond this, among the minimum measures that had to be taken by KENTHEA, in its role as the data controller, were the following: implementation of encryption, implementation of a security policy with organizational, technical, and physical security measures, audit trails/log files for detecting user access, changing passwords quarterly, passwords with a recommended minimum length of 8 characters consisting of lowercase letters, uppercase letters, numbers, and symbols, allocation of roles and access levels according to the need to know/tasks, implementation of firewalls and antivirus programs, establishment of procedures for satisfying the rights of data subjects, designation of a data protection officer whose details should be published on KENTHEA's website, provision of a data protection policy, and conducting penetration tests on KENTHEA's information systems, in the case that they are accessible from the internet.

5.1. Complaint against the newspaper "Politis" for the publication of names and photographs of police investigators on duty at Larnaca Airport

5.2. Complaint regarding the operation of Closed Circuit Television (CCTV) in a store

5.3. Complaint regarding receiving unwanted calls

5.6. Complaint Against the Council of Registration of Hairdressers and Barbers of Cyprus  
A complaint was submitted against the Council of Registration of Hairdressers and Barbers of Cyprus regarding the installation of Closed-Circuit Television (CCTV) inside the office and the recording of the entire space, as well as the corridor outside it, resulting in the continuous recording of employees and third parties visiting the area.  
During the investigation of the complaint, the Council provided my Office with consent forms, through which it had obtained the consent of the employees for the installation of the CCTV.  
Considering that consent must be given freely and the existence of a power imbalance between the Council as the employer and its employees, I deemed it unlikely that the latter were in a position to express their refusal to the installation of the CCTV without fear of potential negative consequences. Therefore, the Council's staff was unlikely to have responded freely to the employer's request for consent. Consequently, the legal basis could not and should not be the consent of the employees, and thus, the consent forms could not constitute sufficient evidence of the employees' agreement to the processing of their personal data.  
In light of the above, I concluded that the specific processing, namely the operation of the CCTV, lacked legality and constituted a violation of Articles 5(1)(a) and 6 of the Regulation.  
Based on the above finding, I issued Orders to the Council of Registration of Hairdressers and Barbers of Cyprus, as follows:  
Order 1: To permanently cease the operation of the CCTV and uninstall the camera, or in the case that the cost of its uninstallation is disproportionate, to cover it in such a way that it gives the impression that it is not operational,  
Order 2: To immediately delete any recordings obtained from the CCTV, if applicable, and send me relevant proof,  
Order 3: To notify my Office, within an exclusive deadline of 2 weeks from the receipt of this, of all actions taken to implement the Orders.  
The Council of Registration of Hairdressers and Barbers of Cyprus has complied with the Orders of my Office.

5.28. Complaint regarding the Statistical Processing Methods of the scores of candidates who participated in the Written Examinations 2019 for Registration and Ranking on the Lists of Appointable Candidates

I examined a complaint regarding the processing of personal data during the application of statistical processing methods of the scores of candidates who participated in the written examinations 2019 for registration and ranking on the lists of appointable candidates of the New Appointment System in Education. 

Taking into account the Public Educational Service Law of 1969, Law 10/1969, I determined that the Examination Service (hereinafter referred to as the "Service") of the Directorate of Higher Education of the Ministry of Education, Sports and Youth is the data controller for the processing of personal data that takes place within the framework of the statistical processing of the Examination results. 

For registration on the list of appointable candidates, it is required that candidates record their qualifications and submit certificates/evidence, so that they can receive relevant scoring. For a candidate to be included on the list of appointable candidates, success in the Examinations is required. In each specialty, a list of appointable candidates is created, which includes successful candidates from various years. At specified intervals, the list of appointable candidates for each specialty is updated, with the addition of successful candidates from the new Examinations and/or with the differentiation of the scoring of qualifications and/or the scores of each successful candidate. The application of statistical processing methods aims to form the index that reflects the degree of difficulty of the 2019 Examination in relation to the degree of difficulty of the corresponding Examination of 2017. However, the Service assigned the application of statistical processing to a data processor without conducting a relevant assignment contract, as provided in Article 28(3) of the Regulation. Consequently, I issued a Warning for the violation of the aforementioned articles. 

Since the Written Examinations for Registration and Ranking on the Lists of Appointable Candidates Regulations of 2017, as amended, provide for statistical processing for the purposes of a unified ranking of candidates, which requires the processing of personal data, the Service was obliged to conduct prior consultation with my Office before including the relevant provision. Based on the provisions of Article 58(3)(b) of the Regulation, I have the authority to issue, on my own initiative or at the request of others, opinions to the national parliament, the government, or other bodies and organizations, as well as to the public, on any matter related to the protection of personal data.  
In 2023, I issued the following Directive, which is presented in full:  

6.1. Guidelines for direct marketing  
1. Introduction  
Direct marketing aims to advertise and involves the processing of personal data. This promotional practice is considered, in principle, a lawful and legitimate activity provided that specific conditions are met as arising from the institutional and regulatory framework for the protection of personal data.  
Home address, email address, and phone number are some of the most common personal data processed during communication for the purpose of direct marketing.  
The legislative framework that protects personal data during direct marketing of goods or services is based, depending on the means of communication chosen for the marketing, as will be explained below, either on the provisions of the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679, hereinafter referred to as "the Regulation," or on the provisions of Article 106 of the Law on the Regulation of Electronic Communications and Postal Services Law 112 of 2004, as amended, hereinafter referred to as "Law 112(I)/2004."  
Natural or legal persons conducting the above processing in the context of promoting their products or services are considered data controllers according to the interpretation given to this term in Article 4 of the Regulation and must comply with the provisions of the relevant legislation.  
It is understood that in cases where part of the processing is delegated to a processor (an external natural or legal person who is not an employee of the Data Controller), according to the Regulation (Articles 4(8) and 28), there must be a relevant processing agreement, and the processor must comply with all obligations provided by the Regulation and act according to the instructions and directions of the data controller.  

2. Direct marketing of goods or services via electronic means  
In the case of direct marketing via electronic means, the Law (Law 112(I)/2004), as amended, which has transposed into national law the provisions of Directive 2002/58/EC (commonly known as the "e-Privacy Directive" regarding the protection of privacy in the electronic communications sector, applies as a more specific regulation.
**106**

Article 106 of this Law titled "Unsolicited Calls" regulates the sending of advertising messages for the purposes of direct marketing through electronic means. Direct marketing through electronic means is primarily carried out through the following:

• automated calling systems without human intervention  
• mobile messaging services (SMS, MMS)  
• email messaging services  
• facsimile services  

The general principle established by Article 106 (1) is that communication for the purposes of direct marketing through electronic means is only permitted in the case of individuals who have given their prior consent, the so-called "opt-in."  

However, Article 106 (2) introduces the following essential exception:  
In the case where a natural or legal person obtains from its customers their email contact details in the context of the sale of a product or service, according to the provisions of the Regulation, it may use such details for the direct marketing of its own similar products or services, provided that the customers have clearly and distinctly the opportunity to object, free of charge and easily, to this use of electronic contact details at the time of their collection, or subsequently at any time a message is sent (in case the user initially did not object to this use).  

Also, by the same article, the practice of sending electronic messages for the purpose of direct marketing, which conceal or hide the identity of the sender or the person on behalf of or for the benefit of whom the message is sent, or without valid contact details to which the recipient can request the termination of this communication free of charge, is prohibited.  

Specifically, senders are obliged to provide in all direct marketing messages they send the recipients of the messages with an email address (in the case of messages via email) or a toll-free number (in the case of SMS, MMS messages), so as to allow for the possibility of opting out without charge. Each message sent must include instructions explaining to the recipient how they can request the termination of the receipt of messages. For example, at the end of the message, it should state "To unsubscribe call 8XXXXXXX" or "SMS STOP send XXXX to 8XXX." The provision of the option to unsubscribe must accompany every message sent. It is also clarified that the provision of the option to unsubscribe from the list of numbers/addresses to which messages are sent (opt-out) does not in itself legitimize the sending of messages, but prior consent from the recipients must either have been obtained or they must be customers.  

The above restrictions apply to subscribers who are natural persons. To safeguard the legal interests of subscribers who are not natural persons regarding electronic communications for the purpose of direct marketing, the provisions of the Law on Legal Entities (Safeguarding Legal Interests regarding Unsolicited Communications) Decree of 2005 issued by the Commissioner for Electronic Communications and Postal Regulation under Articles 106(3) and 152 of the above Law, published in the Official Gazette of the Republic on January 28, 2005, apply, according to which consent must also be obtained from legal entities.
107

3. Direct commercial promotion of the sale of goods or the provision of services via postal mail and face-to-face calls  
For direct commercial promotion through postal mail and/or face-to-face calls/human intervention, the provisions of the Regulation apply. In addition to the fundamental principles (Article 5) and the conditions for lawful processing (Article 6) that must be applied to all forms of processing, the recitals of paragraphs 47 and 70 of the preamble and Article 21 of the Regulation are significant for direct commercial promotion.  
The legal basis for the aforementioned unsolicited communication may be:  
• The consent of the data subject [Article 6(1)(a)],  
• The legitimate interest pursued by the data controller [Article 6(1)(f)].  
In the event that the Data Controller claims that the processing is carried out based on the legitimate interest pursued, they must conduct an assessment/balancing of their legitimate interest against the rights of the data subjects. The Data Controller could, according to recital 47 of the preamble of the Regulation, invoke such a legitimate interest when there is a relevant and appropriate relationship between the data subject and the Data Controller, such as when the data subject is an existing customer of the data controller.  
The data subject has the right at any time to exercise the right to object (Article 21 of the Regulation) regarding the processing of personal data that takes place for the purpose of achieving direct commercial promotion, including profiling, if it is related to such direct commercial promotion (Article 21).

4. Clarifications applicable to all cases of unsolicited communication  
a. Consent  
The consent of the data subject is interpreted in all cases in the sense attributed to it by the Regulation. Therefore, it must:  
• be free, explicit, and contain an explicit statement of will from the data subject themselves (and not a third party) to the data controller that they agree to have their personal data processed for a specific purpose  
• be expressed through positive action and clarity  
• be obtained after the data subject has been duly informed  
• be revocable  

b. Burden of Proof  
i. The burden of proof for obtaining consent lies with the Data Controller. Individuals or companies engaged in direct marketing must record details such as the date, time, and method by which consent was obtained and maintain a relevant register so that in cases of audits or complaints, they can demonstrate that they have obtained the consent of the recipients.  
ii. The burden of proof that the data subject is an existing customer or that the legal basis of legitimate interest is established lies with the Data Controller.

108

c. Commercial promotion includes any form of promotion of purposes and ideas  
From the European legislative framework, it is clear that the concept of direct promotion is interpreted broadly, and "commercial or advertising purposes" include purposes pursued by either commercial entities or charitable organizations or other associations or unions, e.g., of a political nature.

d. Political communication  
Unsolicited political communication is now typically encountered in every electoral campaign. As previously mentioned, it is clarified that the restrictions and obligations of the legislative framework apply in this area regardless of whether political communication is characterized as commercial. More information can be found in the Guidelines of the Commissioner for Personal Data Protection titled "Sending messages and making calls of political content/promotion of candidacies" dated 14/10/2022.

e. Other clarifications  
• The claim that the messages sent are merely informative and not advertising is incorrect. If the messages contain the name of a company or a product or service, they are generally considered advertising.  
• Silence or inaction from the data subject is not considered consent.  
• The existing commercial relationship pertains only to communication for the promotion of similar products or services of the Data Controller. For example, if a customer has purchased an electrical appliance, it is generally not justified to subsequently send them messages advertising jewelry.  
• The Data Controller, when a company, and creates a customer relationship that allows communication for commercial promotion does not automatically and voluntarily extend the right to communicate with the customer to subsidiaries or parent companies or other members of a corporate group. They must first adequately investigate whether a legitimate interest is indeed established.  
• Phone numbers randomly generated through software tools constitute personal data because the holder can easily be identified; therefore, the practice of communication either through electronic means or via face-to-face calls to holders of phone numbers generated in the above manner is prohibited.

Following my previous Announcements regarding the audits conducted by my Office on websites concerning the use of cookies, I inform you that approximately 30 audits have been completed, primarily concerning news and other related public information websites. It is reminded that cookies are small data files stored by a website on the visitor's computer or mobile device, for which, under the legislation, user consent is required (except for those that are strictly necessary for the service provider of the information society to provide the service explicitly requested by the user). The administrators of the websites that were audited received letters with the findings of the audit, which they were called upon to rectify. Given that these specific audits were the first of their kind, and that websites are continuously upgraded with the use of new technologies, a significant timeframe for compliance was granted to the website administrators, and numerous letters were sent with suggestions/clarifications regarding issues that arose during the audits. 

The main issues identified during the audits are as follows:
1. Most websites did not provide information regarding the purposes of cookie usage.
2. Websites that had information about the use of cookies did not obtain the explicit consent of users for the use of cookies, or the method of obtaining consent did not meet the requirements for valid consent (free, revocable, etc.).
3. Some cookies, such as those for measuring website traffic, were incorrectly categorized as "strictly necessary."

Following our suggestions, we have found that there has been compliance with the provisions of the legislation and the objectives of the audit. The audits were conducted within a specific timeframe, and the compliance of the websites will be re-evaluated. 

In response to the frequently asked question regarding how other Supervisory Authorities address the use of cookies, I would like to mention that they have conducted and are conducting similar audits and are taking various actions aimed at compliance with the legislation.

8.13. Announcement of the Commissioner for the Protection of Personal Data regarding the EU-U.S. Data Protection Framework, dated July 11, 2023  
The European Commission adopted yesterday, July 10, 2023, the executive Decision for the "EU-U.S. Data Protection Framework." Under the new data protection framework, data transfers to the U.S. can be carried out from Union countries to companies in the U.S. without the need for any other transfer tool, relevant guarantees, or supplementary measures. However, U.S. companies will be able to join this framework after committing to comply with a detailed set of obligations for privacy protection.  
The new framework is the third adequacy Decision of the European Commission for data transfers to the U.S. Since July 26, 2000, data transfers to the U.S. were covered by the European Commission's Safe Harbor Decision, which was invalidated by the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) on October 6, 2015, following a complaint by Max Schrems. Subsequently, from July 12, 2016, the European Commission's Privacy Shield Decision came into effect, which was also invalidated on July 16, 2020, by the CJEU, following another complaint filed by Max Schrems.  
Yesterday's Decision was adopted after extensive consultations between the Commission and the U.S., and following a relevant Opinion from the European Data Protection Board. According to the Commission's Announcement, safeguards are provided to limit U.S. intelligence authorities' access to the data of European citizens to what is necessary and proportionate for national security protection. Additionally, enhanced oversight of U.S. intelligence activities is provided. Finally, the establishment of a Data Protection Review Court is deemed important, which will investigate and resolve complaints from Union citizens regarding U.S. national security authorities' access to their data.

The new framework will be subject to periodic reviews by the European Commission in cooperation with representatives of the Union's Data Protection Authorities. Specifically, the first review will be conducted within a year from the entry into force of the adequacy Decision.

Decision: Violation of Personal Data at the Open University of Cyprus Network
On March 30, 2023, a Notification of Personal Data Breach was submitted to my Office on behalf of the Open University of Cyprus (hereinafter referred to as the "University"). A group of "hackers" (hereinafter referred to as the "intruder") declared via the social networking platform Twitter that they were responsible for the attack and a deadline was given to the University for the payment of ransom for the return/non-disclosure of the files that had been leaked from the attack. When the deadline for the payment of the ransom had passed, the stolen data was published by the intruder and made available on the dark web. 
After a full investigation of the incident, it was found that the leaked data concerns students, graduates, and other subjects (contracting parties of the University) that were temporarily stored on an affected server and were used for processing tasks by the employees. 
Eleven complaints have been submitted to my Office from data subjects alleging that their personal data has been leaked due to the incident under examination, which were taken into account during the investigation of the incident. 
The University also sent me a list of actions it will take to enhance the security of its systems. These actions will be implemented gradually based on a program that has been developed, starting from now, with a completion date set for 2026, depending on the criticality, cost, and prerequisites for their implementation. 
After a legal and technical examination of all the above, it was found that there was a violation of the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 due to the failure to implement appropriate security measures and a breach of the principle of "accountability." 
Taking into account all the facts of the case, the technical and organizational measures taken by the University prior to the attack, and the mitigating factors mentioned by the University, as well as the fact that the University is part of the broader public sector, an Administrative Fine of forty-five thousand (€45,000) euros was imposed on the University. 
An Order was also given to the University, within six months: 
(a) to appoint a security officer, even temporarily/deputy, who will oversee the implementation of the measures that the University intends to take, 
(b) to inform me about the progress of the implementation of the measures of which it informed me that it intends to take.

10.  INTERNATIONAL ACTIVITIES

10.1.  European Data Protection Board  
The European Data Protection Board (EDPB) was established pursuant to Article 68 of the Regulation and is an institutional body of the European Union with legal personality and the ability to adopt legally binding decisions. The aim of the EDPB is to ensure the consistent application of the Regulation throughout the European Economic Area, as well as to promote cooperation among the Supervisory Authorities that comprise it.  
The EDPB consists of the Head of the Supervisory Authority of each Member State and the European Data Protection Supervisor. It is understood that if there is more than one Data Protection Supervisory Authority in a Member State, a common representative is designated in accordance with the law of that Member State.  
In 2023, the EDPB held 15 plenary sessions, of which 5 were in-person sessions in Brussels, 9 sessions were held remotely, and one hybrid session in which EDPB members could participate either in person in Brussels or remotely. It is noted that my Office actively participated in all EDPB sessions.

10.1.1.  Leadership of the European Data Protection Board  
The President and the two Vice-Presidents of the EDPB are elected by secret ballot of the EDPB members for a five-year term. Due to the expiration of the term of the President and one Vice-President of the EDPB on May 25, 2023, elections were held for the vacant positions.  
The EDPB members elected Ms. Anu Talus, the Commissioner of Finland, as President of the EDPB, and elected me as Vice-President of the EDPB.  
My election as Vice-President of the EDPB demonstrates that despite its small size, our country can serve European bodies from elected positions.  
No elections were held for the other Vice-President position of the EDPB, which is held by the Commissioner of the Netherlands, Mr. Aleid Wolfsen, as his term expires in 2024.  
In 2023, in my capacity as Vice-President of the EDPB, I chaired one plenary session of the EDPB and five sessions of the Strategic Advisory expert subgroup. The Strategic Advisory subgroup meets at a high level, as it is called to discuss and provide guidance on strategically significant issues concerning the EDPB. Additionally, this subgroup resolves questions and determines the course on critical issues that cannot be resolved within the framework of the work of other subgroups and special groups of the EDPB.  
Also, as Vice-President of the EDPB, with the assistance of the EDPB Secretariat, I handled 20 requests for access to EDPB documents, pursuant to Regulation (EC) 1049/2001 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of May 30, 2001, regarding public access to documents of the European Parliament, the Council, and the Commission. The purpose of Regulation 1049/2001 is to establish the principles, conditions, and limitations, for reasons of public or private interest, governing the right of access to documents of the European Parliament, the Council, and the Commission. Furthermore, this Regulation establishes rules to ensure the easiest possible exercise of this right of access and promotes good administrative practice regarding access to documents.

It is noted that the Ministry is, among other things, responsible for electronic governance in the public service. The responses received, after being initially evaluated by my Office, were subsequently sent to the EDPB, in order to extract aggregated results.  
In January 2023, the EDPB published on its website the Report for this action, which includes aggregated findings based on the responses received from the Supervisory Authorities participating in the action.  
Specifically, the Report analyzes the following challenges:  
•  
lack of conducting Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) for the relevant processing,  
•  
roles and responsibilities that are not properly defined, resulting in difficulties in complying with the respective obligations of the Regulation, both for the provider and for the public authorities,  
•  
difficulty for public authorities to determine the purposes and means of processing personal data during the contract and to fulfill the obligations under the Regulation, due to the existing power imbalance,  
•  
most public authorities appear to have limited control and cannot effectively oppose the use of subprocessors or changes to subprocessors, without risking a potentially critical loss of service,
144

• when the information available to public authorities, in their capacity as data controllers, and to the Supervisory Authority is not sufficiently clear, it may be difficult to accurately assess which categories of data are shared, in which locations they are shared, and for what purposes,  
• risk of access by governments of third countries when using providers that are located outside the Union and store data in the EEA,  
• lack of knowledge regarding the processing of diagnostic/telemetry data by providers, failure to conduct assessments prior to the commencement of use, and lack of clarity and precision in contracts regarding which data may be collected,  
• failure to conduct targeted audits, in general, by public authorities concerning the activities of providers.  
The Report presents a series of pioneering practices and points of caution for public authorities using cloud services. These elements receive particular attention and are utilized by the Supervisory Authorities when auditing the relevant processing activities conducted within their territory, whether these are audits that were ongoing during the coordinated action or audits that were conducted or will be conducted after the publication of the Report.

10.2. Supervision of national large-scale information systems  
Various large-scale information systems operate within the European Union, allowing the competent authorities of the member states to exchange information for the effective execution of their duties and responsibilities. These systems are supervised, at a central level, by the European Data Protection Supervisor and, at a national level, by the Data Protection Supervisory Authorities. For the effective cooperation of the European Supervisor with the national Supervisory Authorities, there are established Coordinated Supervisory Teams (CSTs), in which my Office participates, either as a member or as an observer.  
The role of my Office, as the competent Supervisory Authority for the national systems described below, is to ensure that the competent Authorities of the Republic process personal data in accordance with the relevant European and national legislation and that the rights of citizens are exercised in accordance with these legislations. Interested parties can exercise their rights of access, rectification, erasure, and restriction of data concerning them before the relevant competent Authorities of Cyprus. The exercise of these rights may be subject to restrictions provided by the relevant Union or national legislations or that may be permitted by the Commissioner.  

EUROPOL  
EUROPOL is a system that allows the exchange of information between the Law Enforcement Authorities of the member states, with the aim of their mutual cooperation for the prevention and combating of
146

serious forms of crime that affect two or more Member States, terrorism, and forms of crime that harm common interests covered by Union policy. The competent authority in Cyprus is the national EUROPOL unit of the Police. My Office participates as a member in the Cooperation Board of EUROPOL, established under Article 45 of Regulation (EU) 2016/794 of the European Parliament and of the Council, of May 11, 2016, on the European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Cooperation (Europol) and repealing and replacing Council Decisions 2009/371/JHA, 2009/934/JHA, 2009/935/JHA, 2009/936/JHA, and 2009/968/JHA.

EUROJUST  
EUROJUST is a system that allows for the exchange of information between judicial and prosecutorial authorities of the Member States, with the aim of enhancing coordination and cooperation among national authorities for the investigation and prosecution of serious crimes that have implications for two or more Member States or require prosecution on common grounds, based on operations conducted and information provided by the authorities of the Member States, EUROPOL, the European Public Prosecutor's Office, and OLAF. The competent authorities in Cyprus are the Legal Service and the relevant Courts. As Commissioner, I am a member of the Joint Supervisory Body (JSB) of EUROJUST. In November 2018, the new Regulation governing the operation of EUROJUST came into force, which was implemented in December 2019.

EURODAC  
EURODAC is a system that allows for the exchange of information between the Asylum Services of the Member States, for the purpose of determining the Member State responsible for examining the application for international protection submitted by a third-country national, and for the comparison of fingerprint data of asylum applicants with the data stored in the EU central system, for law enforcement purposes. The competent authorities in Cyprus are the Asylum Service and the Police. My Office is a member of the Coordinated Supervision Group of EURODAC.

SIS II  
The second generation Schengen Information System (SIS II) allows for the exchange of information between competent authorities of the Member States for immigration purposes and effective border control, as well as for law enforcement purposes. It contains information on third-country nationals, wanted persons with a European Arrest Warrant, missing persons, persons monitored by the Police or who have been banned from entering the Schengen area, stolen vehicles, and stolen or lost travel documents, such as passports and identity cards. My Office participates in the meetings of the Coordinated Supervision Group of SIS II as an observer.  
In 2022, the Law on the Operation and Use of the Second Generation Schengen Information System (SIS II) in the Republic came into force, Law No. 12(I)/2022, on which my Office submitted comments during the formal consultation process.  
SIS has been operational in the Republic since July 25, 2023, following a relevant Decision of the Council of the European Union. Based on a supervisory plan that I submitted and was approved by the European
147

Before the operation of the SIS commenced, I conducted an audit to ascertain the readiness of the Police to fulfill its obligations regarding this system. The readiness of the Police was positively assessed, and I issued an Audit Report which I sent to the relevant supervisory bodies of the EU. In September 2023, I conducted a second audit to determine how the SIS operates in practice and whether appropriate technical and organizational security measures were taken during the input or output of information to or from the SIS, and whether the procedures stipulated by the Law on the Operation and Use of the Second Generation Schengen Information System (SIS II) in the Republic, Law No. 12(I)/2022, were being followed. The audit was conducted through on-site visits to Larnaca Airport, the Strovolos Police Directorate, and the Agios Domettios checkpoint, where a demonstration of the system's operation took place, and various questions were posed and answered. The operation of the SIS was positively evaluated, and this was reflected in the second Audit Report that I issued and sent to the Police. This information regarding the audits of the SIS is also included in subsection 7.2.

VIS
The European Visa Information System (VIS) allows for the exchange of information between the competent authorities of the member states for the purpose of issuing visas to third-country nationals. However, Cyprus's national VIS is used only for managing visa applications submitted to the Embassies and Consulates of the Republic and, exceptionally, at the points of entry. The competent authority for the VIS is the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The national VIS is not connected to the central VIS, and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs does not have the capability to exchange information with the corresponding authorities of other member states. My Office participates in the meetings of the VIS Coordinated Supervision Group as an observer.

CIS
The Customs Information System (CIS) allows for the exchange of information between the Customs Authorities of the member states for the purposes of preventing, verifying, and prosecuting criminal offenses related to Community Law on customs and agriculture, as well as for mutual assistance and cooperation among these authorities to ensure the proper application of customs and agricultural regulations. In Cyprus, the competent authority for the CIS is the Customs Department.

IMI
The Internal Market Information System (IMI) enables competent authorities of a member state implementing Community legislation on Internal Market issues to identify the corresponding competent authorities of other member states and exchange information through standardized queries within their competencies for the effective implementation of Community legislation. The IMI serves a number of Community Acts, such as the Directive on the Recognition of Professional Qualifications, the Services Directive, the Directive on the Posting of Workers, the Regulation on Cross-Border Transport of Cash, the Directive on Patients' Rights, cross-border complaint resolution through the "SOLVIT" network, and the Directive on Electronic Commerce.

148

In Cyprus, the national coordinator for the IMI is the Ministry of Energy, Commerce, and Industry. I hold, as Commissioner, the status of a member of the IMI Coordinated Supervision Group.

12.1.1. Unsolicited advertising messages (spam)  
For the sending of advertising messages aimed at direct commercial promotion via electronic means, including email, SMS, and fax, the provisions of Article 106 of the Law on the Regulation of Electronic Communications and Postal Services of 2004 (Law 112(I)/2004), as amended, apply, according to which prior consent from the recipients of the messages is required. The Law allows the sending of messages to customers provided that they are given the opportunity to object to receiving the messages easily and at no cost.  
There is a specific complaint submission form Type C (for unsolicited advertising messages (Spam)) posted on my Office's website, which can also be obtained from the stores of CYTA, EPIC (formerly MTN), and the Citizen Service Centers, and which the public can fill out and send for the investigation of their complaint.  
For advertising/promotional calls made in person (with human intervention), the provisions of the Regulation apply.  
Advertising messages also include messages of a political nature (campaign promotion).
