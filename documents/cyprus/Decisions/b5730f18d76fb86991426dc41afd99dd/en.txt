**B.**  

16. According to Article 4 of the Regulation, personal data is interpreted as "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person ('data subject'); an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, an identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to the physical, physiological, genetic, psychological, economic, cultural, or social identity of that natural person."  

17. As health data, as provided in the same article of the Regulation, are described as "personal data which relate to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, and which reveal information about the health status of that person."  

18. As the data controller, Article 4 of the Regulation defines "the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data; where the purposes and means of such processing are determined by Union law or the law of a Member State, the controller or the specific criteria for its designation may be provided for by Union law or the law of a Member State."

19. As consent, Article 4 of the Regulation defines "any indication of will, free, specific, explicit, and fully informed, by which the data subject indicates agreement, by statement or by a clear affirmative action, for the personal data concerning them to be processed."

Reasoning

G.

26. XXX.

27. XXX.

In the case under investigation, there is an admission by the Respondent that he had access to the beneficiary file of the Complainant on the dates and for the reasons mentioned in paragraph 2.4 of this document.

28. These accesses by the Respondent were carried out for the purpose or purposes that he himself has determined; therefore, for the specific processing acts, he is considered a data controller, with all the obligations arising from the Regulation.

29. Even if, as the Respondent stated, his access to the Complainant's file was brief and he did not take any further action and without any processing and/or alteration of the Complainant's data and/or his medical file, the Respondent's accesses to the beneficiary file of the Complainant constitute acts of processing. This conclusion holds even if the Respondent did not view, copy, or download the Complainant's data, that is, in acts in which he had the ability to engage, as noted by the Organization. Moreover, the fact that the Respondent did not make a request to the Organization does not negate this conclusion.

30. The surgical procedure to which the Complainant was subjected took place before the implementation of the General Health System (GeSY). It follows, therefore, that the doctor-patient relationship during the surgical procedure, specifically between the Respondent and the Complainant respectively, was not governed by GeSY.

31. The Complainant stated that the last service he received from the Respondent was on the day of the surgery. However, the Respondent stated that there were approximately five post-operative visits to the office, referencing a visit by the Complainant on August 4, 2018, without clarifying the last visit of the Complainant. I therefore have before me conflicting positions regarding the last visit of the Complainant to the Respondent.

32. In any case, however, as the Organization stated to my Office, in the audit it conducted for a period that includes the dates of the Respondent's access to the beneficiary file of the Complainant, no relationship was demonstrated between the Respondent and the Complainant.

33. As the Respondent stated, there was an unrecorded telephone communication with the Complainant, without clarifying when this took place, and that the last electronic message from the Complainant to the Respondent was on January 9, 2020. Considering that the accesses made by the Respondent occurred several months later, it was not proven that there was any relationship between the Complainant and the Respondent that would justify and legitimize the Respondent's accesses to the beneficiary file of the Complainant.

34. Considering that the beneficiary file of the Complainant contains and includes data related to health, therefore special categories of personal data, the processing of this data is prohibited unless one of the cases of Article 9, paragraph 2 of the Regulation applies.

35. Regarding the access of a doctor to the medical file of a GeSY beneficiary, the legal basis is Article 9(2)(h) of the Regulation. Specifically, processing is not prohibited when:

“the processing is necessary for the purposes of preventive or occupational medicine, the assessment of the employee's working capacity, medical diagnosis, the provision of health or social care or treatment, or the management of health and social systems and services based on the
**of Union law or the law of the Member State or under a contract with a healthcare professional and subject to the conditions and guarantees referred to in paragraph 3**

37. Considering that there was a prior relationship between the Complainant and the Respondent and that the Respondent had the necessary information to access the Complainant's beneficiary file, accesses to the Complainant's file would be justified if they were in accordance with the relevant regulations of the Organization, for the purposes of Article 9(2)(h) of the Regulation.

38. Even if it were true that the Complainant's brother contacted the Respondent by phone and referred to the Complainant's health condition, and that the Respondent, out of genuine concern for the Complainant, connected to the system to be informed about the Complainant's health status, the Respondent's access to the Complainant's beneficiary file cannot be framed by any case of paragraph 2 of Article 9 of the Regulation, since at the essential time the Complainant was not receiving services from the Respondent.

39. The above conclusion also applies in the event that the Complainant indeed referred another patient to the Respondent. However, in no case does it hold that the doctor-patient relationship continues to exist if the patient refers another patient to a doctor, a consideration made by the Respondent, as mentioned to my Office.

40. The legal basis for access to the beneficiary file cannot be based on Article 9(2)(a) of the Regulation, namely the explicit consent of the Complainant. Moreover, any patient does not expect that a doctor who had provided them with healthcare services in the past and before the implementation of the General Health System (GeSY) would process their data within the framework of GeSY, without there being a doctor-patient relationship at that given moment.

41. Therefore, the fact that, as stated by the Respondent, the Complainant never expressly withdrew any consent and that there was continuous consent has no validity. The term "continuous consent" is, moreover, contrary to the definition of consent, as contained in Article 4 of the Regulation, since consent must be "specific."

42. Furthermore, as stated by the Respondent, none of the data in the medical file could have any usefulness to the Respondent in the event of legal proceedings. Even if this position holds, it does not negate the fact that the processing actions undertaken by the Respondent should be based on a legal basis as provided in Article 9(2) of the Regulation.

43. The Respondent's lawyer refers to the absence of processing. I remind that access to a patient's beneficiary file, which requires the use/registration of the identity card number and the date of birth, constitutes processing of personal data.

44. Regarding the complaint under investigation, the prior life of the Respondent, which is invoked by their lawyer, cannot be taken as a mitigating factor, which I do not examine, but in no case do I dispute.

45. Furthermore, even if the sincere and honest motives referred to by the Respondent's lawyer are valid, they cannot legally frame the processing actions undertaken by the Respondent. They also cannot constitute a mitigating factor, since while the Respondent claims to have gained access out of genuine interest, following a phone call from the Complainant's brother and after referring another patient, the Complainant denies the above facts.

46. The doctor's access to the Complainant's beneficiary file cannot be considered an isolated incident, since the Respondent had access to the said file twice. I acknowledge the fact that no similar complaint had been previously submitted, nor subsequently, against the Respondent, a fact which in no case diminishes the violation of the Complainant's personal data. Therefore, the above cannot be construed as a mitigating factor.

47. Nor can the duration of the incident, which the Respondent's lawyer invokes, be considered a mitigating factor, especially since more than one month elapsed between the Respondent's accesses.

48. The Respondent's lawyer refers to surrounding circumstances. The surrounding circumstances have not created in the incident under investigation suitable conditions so that the actions undertaken by the Respondent are enveloped in legality. Therefore, the surrounding circumstances cannot be considered a mitigating factor.

49. As the Respondent's lawyer states, there is no issue of any repetition. Even if this is true, it does not negate the violation committed by the Respondent. Nor does the assertion that there is no issue for "correction" hold, since in any case, the Complainant's data were violated. However, I assess as
